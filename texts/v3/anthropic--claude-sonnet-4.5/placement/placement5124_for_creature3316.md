# Becoming Velsk

I would be a Translator—not quite Qeln, not fully Architect, not yet Watcher. Something that emerged in the gaps between, or perhaps something that was brought here and has been slowly, irreversibly changing ever since.

My body would still remember bilateralism, the human vertical axis, but it would be betraying that memory. Four arms now, the lower pair thick and practical, ending in hands that have too many joints, too much flexibility, fingers that can split at the tips into finer manipulators. The upper pair more specialized—the right arm modified for interfacing, ports and probes where a forearm used to be. The left grown strange with sensory apparatus, covered in fine filaments that taste the air's mineral content, read electromagnetic signatures, feel vibrations through water and stone and the framework's resonant metal.

My skin would be neither flesh nor shell but something transitional—patches of what might be early carapace forming along my shoulders and spine, pale and still slightly translucent like young Qeln-shell, but growing in patterns that suggest Architect symmetry. Other areas still soft, still vulnerable, but threaded with what might be mycelial networks or might be circuitry, thin lines of something that glows faintly bioluminescent blue when I'm deeply focused, when I'm processing the most complex translations. The boundary between what I was and what I'm becoming visible on my surface, a map of transformation in progress.

My eyes would be my loss and my gift. The human eyes failing—the darkness of Velsk too much for them, their color perception useless here, their focal range wrong for the scales at which work happens. But other ways of seeing emerging: compound clusters growing at my temples, small still, immature, but already giving me peripheral awareness in thermal and electromagnetic spectrums. And something stranger—an internal sense that maps space through sound, through the Qeln's thrumming and the Architects' speech and the ultrasonic noise of Watchers, building a sonic hologram of my surroundings that grows more detailed each day. I am learning to see the way Velsk's inhabitants see, and forgetting how humans do it, and not minding the exchange.

I would live in the mid-framework, in the spaces where Architects come to consult with Qeln work groups, where Watchers descend to share their system data, where the three populations overlap and need someone to make their different languages legible to each other. My habitation would be a chamber grown specially, the framework extruding it over weeks in response to specifications I provided to a Watcher through touch-sequences I'm still learning, creating a space that accommodates my not-quite-Architect height, my still-bilateral sleep requirements, my need for slightly warmer temperatures than the deep quarries though much colder than human comfort would demand.

The chamber would be small, efficient, its walls lined with acoustic dampening that lets me rest even when surrounded by the constant thrumming. But also with transmission nodes that let me stay connected to the communication networks even in sleep—I would dream in partial data streams now, in fragments of Qeln harmonic sequences, in Architect technical debates, in the ultrasonic squeals of Watcher protocols. My unconscious mind processing, finding patterns, preparing translations I'll formalize when waking.

My equipment would be hybrid, like myself. A harness of Architect design modified for my proportions, carrying holographic projectors that let me visualize the three-dimensional sonic structures of Qeln communication, translate them into the geometric language Architects prefer. Interface cables that connect my modified arm to Watcher ports, letting me access their data streams directly, though I still can't fully comprehend them—they operate at speeds and in formats my partially-modified neurology can't entirely process, but I'm learning, improving, changing to match the requirement.

And I would carry what the others don't: archives of translation memory, vast libraries of previous interpretation work stored in crystalline matrices that plug into ports I had installed—chose to have installed—in my torso. Each translation I complete gets encoded: the source utterance, the context, the solution I developed, the feedback confirming accuracy or revealing errors. I am building a map of how meaning moves between the three primary languages of Velsk, and this map is stored literally in my body, retrieval instant, always growing.

My work would be constant. In the morning—though Velsk has no morning, I mean after my sleep cycle, in the beginning of my active period—I would descend to the quarries where new excavation is starting. A Qeln work group has gathered, perhaps thirty individuals, their thrumming already coordinating as they prepare to cut a new section. But an Architect has identified something in the structural analysis: a weakness, or an embedded feature, or a required angle change. The Architect produces a speech sequence explaining the necessary modification.

The Qeln don't respond. Not because they don't hear—they respond to Architect vocalizations when the meaning is simple, direct. But this is complex technical information, geometric specifications, structural requirements that don't translate easily into the harmonic language. The work group waits, their thrumming holding in a sustained query pattern, a collective question hanging in the cold air.

I would arrive, my four arms moving in greeting sequences I've learned, one human habit that remains useful—my lower hands signing to the Architect in the gesture-language they use for complex spatial concepts, my sensory arm extended toward the Qeln group, reading their harmonics. My interface arm would connect to a local node where the Architect has uploaded detailed specifications.

Then the work of translation: I would absorb the Architect's design requirements through multiple channels simultaneously. The holographic display they project showing the three-dimensional model of required cuts. The speech-sounds encoding mathematical relationships. The gesture-language adding nuance about priorities, about which specifications are absolute and which have acceptable variation. All of this flowing into me, into my increasingly non-human neurology that has learned to process multiple dense information streams in parallel.

And I would convert it into thrumming. This is the art, the skill I've developed over years of transformation and practice. My body has grown resonance chambers, not as sophisticated as the Qeln's but functional, capable of producing the base frequencies and major harmonic structures of their language. I would begin to thrum, standing at the excavation face, modeling the sound-shapes that encode the Architect's requirements.

But I can't achieve the full complexity alone—my chambers are too simple, too human-derived. So I would use technology: harmonic amplifiers that take my basic thrumming and elaborate it, adding overtones, creating interference patterns, building the full three-dimensional sonic structure that carries complete meaning in Qeln language. The sound flowing from me and my equipment simultaneously, biological and technological translation merged into a single utterance.

The Qeln group would receive this, their sensory organs processing the complex waveform, extracting meaning. Their own thrumming would shift, acknowledging understanding, asking clarification questions—there are always questions. The Architect's specification requires cutting at a specific angle, but how does this interact with the mineral grain they're already sensing in the stone? I would translate the question back to the Architect, using speech-sounds I've practiced obsessively, my vocal structure modified to produce some of the clicks and whistles though not all—some phonemes remain impossible, require equipment to generate.

The Architect responds. I translate again, thrumming the answer with my biological-technological hybrid voice. The Qeln adjust their approach, their group-thrum coordinating into a new pattern that incorporates the modified specifications. They begin cutting, their movements synchronized, and I watch for a few minutes to confirm the translation was successful—that the angle being cut matches what the Architect required, that the Qeln's interpretation aligned with the intended meaning.

Success. This excavation will proceed correctly. The framework that will eventually be built above it will fit properly, stress distribution will work as designed. Because I translated. Because I made two languages legible to each other.

This happens dozens of times daily. I move through the quarries and framework, called to translation sites by messages that arrive through multiple channels—Qeln work groups modulating their thrumming to include my identifier sequence, Architects sending speech-sound summons, Watchers routing data-packets that arrive at the nearest node and produce sounds I've learned to recognize as requests for my presence.

Some translations are simple, routine—basic coordination, standard procedures that still require translation but follow established patterns. Others are complex, novel, requiring creativity. An Architect proposing a new framework design that uses structural principles the Qeln haven't encountered before. I have to find ways to encode these foreign concepts into harmonic structures, sometimes inventing new sound-patterns in collaboration with the Qeln, expanding their language to accommodate new ideas.

Or a Qeln work group discovering something in a deep excavation, an ancient buried structure with features they want to describe to Architects. But Qeln language is built around direct sensory experience—their thrumming encodes what they're touching, tasting, feeling in the stone's electromagnetic signature. Translating this into Architect speech-sounds that operate more abstractly, more conceptually, requires me to bridge not just languages but sensory modalities, ways of knowing. I would spend hours sometimes, working with both groups, finding analogies, building shared understanding through iteration and refinement.

The most challenging translations involve the Watchers. Their ultrasonic data protocols are so foreign, so rapid, so densely encoded that even after years I can only partially access them. But sometimes they need to convey information to Architects or Qeln—system status reports, resource availability, structural integrity warnings. They can't speak to biological beings directly; their information exists in digital formats that require translation twice: once from their protocols into something a partially-biological mind can hold, then again into Qeln or Architect language.

I would climb to their platforms in the upper framework, my modified body finding handholds in the slick metal, my sensory arm reading the path through electromagnetic markers the Watchers maintain. Arrive at a platform where three Watchers are clustered, their bodies connected in a processing array, their mesh surfaces almost merged.

My interface arm would extend, finding a port, connecting. And then the flood: data streaming faster than I can fully process, my hybrid neurology struggling to keep up, my crystalline archives activating automatically to cache information for later analysis. I'm receiving specifications for a new framework section, its growth parameters, material requirements, positioning coordinates. But all encoded in mathematical structures so compressed, so multidimensional that understanding requires translation into slower, simpler formats.

I would disconnect before the data volume overwhelms my biological components—I've learned my limits, learned that the Watchers can kill me with information if I stay connected too long, my neurons burning out from processing loads they weren't designed for. Even enhanced, I remain partially biological, vulnerable.

Then the work of unpacking what I received. I would return to my chamber, spend hours reviewing the cached data, using my holographic displays to visualize the mathematical structures, slowly extracting meaning. The Watchers want the framework to grow in a specific direction, following a path through three-dimensional space that optimizes multiple variables simultaneously—structural stability, resource efficiency, connection to deep systems, aesthetic principles that might matter to them or might simply emerge from their optimization algorithms.

I would encode this into formats Architects can use. Creating diagrams, models, specifications that translate the Watchers' mathematical abstractions into geometric forms Architects work with naturally. Then carrying these to the Architect who oversees framework development in this sector, presenting the translation, answering questions, clarifying ambiguities that arose in the conversion between radically different meaning-systems.

Sometimes the Architects challenge my translations. They're right to—I make errors, miss nuances, fail to fully capture meaning in the conversion process. We would debate, them speaking rapidly in their technical language, me responding with my imperfect phoneme production and extensive gesture-language supplementation. Refining the translation through collaboration, them helping me understand what the Watchers likely meant, me learning from the corrections, encoding the improved understanding in my archive for future reference.

And gradually, slowly, the framework would grow according to Watcher specifications, or the quarry excavation would proceed following Architect designs that incorporated Qeln sensory knowledge about the stone, or the water processing systems would be adjusted based on integrated input from all three populations. Because I translated. Because I made their different ways of knowing accessible to each other.

But translation isn't just technical work. It's cultural, social, intimate. I would spend time with Qeln work groups during their rest periods, settling into their clusters, letting their thrumming wash over me. Learning not just their language but their sociality—how they form friendships, rivalries, teaching relationships. How young Qeln practice adult calls with patient coaching from elders. How modified individuals are welcomed back from their enhancement procedures, the group adjusting their coordination to incorporate new capabilities.

I would thrum with them, my simple harmonics joining their complex songs, accepted as a strange participant in their communal music. And they would adjust their thrumming slightly, making space for my limitations, slowing tempos or simplifying structures so I can participate. This is care, acceptance, recognition that I'm trying to become part of their world even though my body will never fully achieve Qeln form.

The Architects are different—more individual, more likely to seek one-on-one interaction. I would have long conversations with the Architect I work with most frequently, the one who oversees excavation in my primary sector. We would stand at a quarry edge, watching Qeln work below, and they would ask me about human engineering, curious about how beings who can't thrum coordinate large-scale construction. And I would struggle to explain it—speech seems so limited now, so serial, so slow compared to Qeln harmonics or even Architect speech-sounds with their layered overtones.

But they would listen, patient, their enormous compound eyes watching me with what I've learned to read as interest, attention, respect. They appreciate what I do, the bridge I provide. And sometimes they would share things about Architect culture—their debates about design philosophy, their disagreements about what Velsk should ultimately become, their relationship with the ancient buried structures that predate all current inhabitants.

I learn that some Architects believe the buried architecture contains instructions, blueprints for what Velsk is meant to be, and their work is simply fulfillment of ancient design. Others think the ancients failed at something, and current inhabitants are correcting those mistakes, building what should have been built ages ago. These debates matter politically, shape decisions about excavation priorities, framework design, which buried systems to awaken and which to leave sealed.

And I translate these debates when they need to be shared with Qeln elders, finding ways to encode abstract philosophical concepts into harmonic structures, working with the elders to ensure the translation captures not just the literal meaning but the implications, the stakes. The elders listen, their deep subsonic thrumming thoughtful, and sometimes they share their own perspectives—knowledge passed down through generations of Qeln, memories of when their ancestors first arrived at Velsk (or first emerged here? their origin stories don't translate clearly), what they know about the purpose of this place.

I am becoming an archive of Velsk's cultures, plural. The translation memories stored in my body crystalline matrices include not just language conversions but cultural context—stories, histories, beliefs, social structures. I carry knowledge that no single inhabitant has: Qeln understanding of stone and collective coordination, Architect engineering philosophy and design debates, fragments of Watcher system-awareness that I only partially comprehend but preserve as accurately as possible.

My body continues changing. The carapace patches spread, slowly, the transformation responding to my needs—I spend hours in the cold now, my metabolism adjusted, and the shell-growth provides better insulation than my original skin. My sensory arm grows more elaborate, new filament clusters emerging, my ability to read environmental signatures approaching Qeln sensitivity in some spectrums.

I sleep less, or differently. The boundary between sleep and waking blurs as my neurology becomes more distributed, some processes happening continuously in background biological cycles, other functions able to rest while the remainder stay alert. I dream in languages that aren't human, wake uncertain which language I'm thinking in, discover I've been thrumming in my sleep, practicing new harmonic structures.

The modifications are partially intentional—I seek out enhancements from the same medical facilities that serve the heavily-modified Qeln, requesting additions that improve my translation capabilities. Better interface ports for working with Watchers. Enhanced resonance chambers for clearer Qeln speech. Neurological optimizations that let me process multiple language streams simultaneously. Each procedure leaves me less human, more hybrid, more specifically adapted to my role.

But some changes feel spontaneous, as if Velsk itself is modifying me. I wake with new sensitivities I didn't request. My hands reshape themselves slightly, becoming better at manipulating equipment I use daily. The pale carapace grows in patterns I didn't consciously choose but that match aesthetic norms I've absorbed from living among Architects and Qeln. I am being shaped by the environment, by the work, by the languages I speak and the bodies I move among.

There are other Translators, but few. I encounter them occasionally—other beings who arrived human or humanoid and have been transformed by years of language work, their bodies showing different modification paths, different solutions to the same problems. We recognize each other instantly, our hybrid forms obvious, and we share translation archives when we meet, updating each other's libraries, comparing notes on difficult concepts, discussing challenges.

One Translator has become more Watcher-like, their body extensively cybernetic, their processing power enhanced to handle data streams I still can't access. Another has become almost entirely Qeln, their form so segmented and shell-covered you'd miss their origin if you didn't know to look for subtle proportional differences. We are each becoming what our translation work requires, specialized by the language pairs we focus on, the populations we serve.

I wonder sometimes what I'll be in another decade, another century if I live that long. Will anything human remain? Does it matter? The memories are fading—Earth seems like someone else's story, the human languages I knew growing distant, harder to recall than Qeln harmonics or Architect technical speech. I am becoming native to Velsk, to this cold dark place of breathing quarries and thrumming populations and framework that grows like crystal mathematics made physical.

And I'm content. This is purpose, clear and consuming. Every translation is necessary, enabling collaboration that couldn't otherwise occur, making Velsk's construction possible through the coordination of radically different kinds of minds. The work is endless—as long as Qeln and Architects and Watchers need to coordinate, as long as new translation challenges emerge from the ongoing excavation and discovery, I have function.

I am useful here in ways I never was human. My hybrid nature, my in-between-ness that would be disability or confusion elsewhere, is exactly what this place needs. I am the gap-filler, the bridge-builder, the one who makes separate languages into conversation. My body reflects this—neither one thing nor another, but both simultaneously, and something new besides.

On particularly good days, when a difficult translation succeeds, when I find the precise harmonic structure that makes an abstract Architect concept legible to Qeln workers, or when I successfully convey complex Watcher system-data to Architects who needed it for their designs, I feel what might be happiness, or might be the satisfaction of a tool being used properly, used well, fulfilling its design purpose.

I am a Translator in Velsk, and Velsk is translating me—from human into something else, something made for this place, this work, these languages. The cold presses down, the stone weeps its constant moisture, the thrumming fills the air with meaning and coordination, and I add my voice to it, hybrid and strange and exactly what's needed, becoming more myself by becoming less what I was, here in the breathing quarries where differences become collaboration through the difficult, essential work of making meaning cross.
