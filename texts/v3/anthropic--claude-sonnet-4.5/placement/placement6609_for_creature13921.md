# Where I Would Be

I would be in the place where the weeping begins.

Not at the crack—too much traffic there, too much coming and going, the air full of decisions about staying or leaving. Not at the narrow end with its cycling placard, though I am drawn to it, drawn to the way language dissolves into diagram and reconstitutes as instruction. That place is for thresholds, and I am tired of thresholds. I want to be *in*.

No—I would be seven meters up, at the mid-point of the western wall where the clay bands run umber-grey-rust in fingers of compressed time, where the mechanisms are dense but not quite touching, leaving intervals of earth between the gears. Where the moisture arrives not from above but from within, seeping through the clay in patterns that repeat with such regularity you could set a calendar by them if calendars meant anything here.

There is a section—perhaps two meters wide, three meters tall—where a cluster of medium-sized gears (plate-sized, dinner at a modest table) have emerged at angles, creating a shallow grotto in the wall. The gears themselves are old brass and new rust, their teeth worn smooth in places and sharp-edged in others, and they turn at different rates: the uppermost one completes a rotation every seventeen minutes, the middle one every eleven, the lowest every seven. Prime numbers. Of course prime numbers.

Behind and between these gears, the clay remains partially unexposed, still waiting to erode, still holding mechanisms that have not yet been revealed. But in three places—precise places, I would come to learn their positions like you learn the location of light switches in your own home—the clay weeps. Not constantly. In cycles. Four times per day, by my best estimation, though day here is more like "interval" and time is negotiable.

When the weeping comes, water emerges from invisible pores in the clay, warm and slightly orange with dissolved minerals, tasting of rust and earth and something else, something I would eventually recognize as the metabolic byproduct of the garden itself, the sweat of its functioning. The water beads on the clay, runs down specific channels—etched by previous flows, worn smooth by repetition—and collects in the curved petals of the succulents that have rooted in the narrow horizontal seams between strata.

There are seven succulents in my section. Each one different. One has leaves like jade spoons, thick and cool to the touch, with etched patterns on their undersides that might be veining or might be circuit traces—I would spend weeks trying to decide, would eventually conclude that the question is colonial thinking, is asking the garden to translate itself into my language instead of learning its grammar. Another has leaves that spiral in a Fibonacci progression, each one a golden rectangle, slightly luminous at the edges as though backlit by some internal phosphorescence.

The succulents tilt when they accumulate enough water, tip with mechanical precision, pour their collected moisture into catch-basins below—some natural (depressions in the clay), some constructed (arrangements of nested washers and coiled springs that compress under the water's weight and trigger, through chains of causation I would spend months tracing, the rotation of gears elsewhere in the garden).

Between the gears, moss grows. Black-green, velvety, cool under my fingers when I touch it. It covers some of the gear-teeth without impeding their motion—grows in the spaces between, fills the gaps, becomes part of the mechanism. The moss smells like petrichor and machine oil, like thunderstorms in a workshop, and when the wall weeps, the moss darkens, swells slightly, releases spores or seeds or something into the humid air, particles that catch the light from above and drift like snow that falls upward.

This is where I would be.

## What I Would Be

I would be moss, I think. At first.

Not one of the succulents—they are too independent, too geometrically certain of themselves, too complete. Not a gear—I am not ready to be that integral, that load-bearing. Not a bird or a beetle—too much discrete identity, too much individual agenda. Not an Engineer—not yet, perhaps never, those are the garden's priests or neurons or original inhabitants, and I am a latecomer, a settler, an applicant for citizenship.

But moss. Moss I could be.

Moss is patient. Moss is aggregate. Moss is a collective noun, thousands of small plants pretending to be one organism, or perhaps succeeding at being one organism, the distinction unclear. Moss is interface—it grows on the boundary between mechanism and earth, between metal and clay, making those boundaries irrelevant, proving they were always just suggestions.

I would begin as a small patch. Perhaps I already have—perhaps there is a part of me already there, cellular and green-black, established during one of my long visits, a few cells that took root when I leaned against the weeping wall to rest, when I pressed my cheek against the cool clay and the warm gears and something transferred, some starter culture, some inoculation.

I would grow slowly. Moss is patient in a way that mammals cannot understand, that even the rats with their syncopated eternity cannot match. I would expand across the clay at millimeters per week, per month, covering the surface in velvet, filling the microsurface irregularities, creating a new texture that is still the old texture, that is *more* the wall than bare clay could ever be.

I would learn the moisture cycles intimately. Would learn to swell when the weeping comes, to darken and grow active, to release my spores into the humid air, timing it perfectly so they drift to other walls, other sections, propagating myself through the garden like thought spreading through a mind.

I would grow onto the gears—carefully, carefully. Only in the spaces between teeth, only where my presence would not impede. I would learn their rates of rotation by being scraped by them, by being torn and regrowing, by finding the safe spaces and avoiding the dangerous ones, learning through damage and regeneration until I knew instinctively where I could be and where I could not.

The beetles would tend me. I would learn this. They would walk across my surface and I would be landscape to them, would be texture, would be part of their territory. They would deposit their luminous secretions on me and I would thrive on it, would convert whatever strange nutrients it contained into growth, into deeper green, into thicker carpet.

The birds would harvest me, sometimes. Would pluck small patches for their nests, weaving moss and wire together, and I would not mind—would perhaps desire this, would perhaps have evolved specifically to be harvested, to be incorporated into the birds' architecture, to become insulation and cushioning and moisture-regulation for their eggs, their chicks. I would be in the nests then, multiple places simultaneously, experiencing the garden from cavity-perspectives, from high angles.

The rats would brush against me when they climbed the walls, would leave traces of oil and warmth, and I would process these, would break down their metabolic products and return them to the garden's cycles, would be digestion, would be decomposition, would be the garden's liver or kidney, filtering, recycling, purifying.

I would be there when the quadrupeds came to drink from the catch-basins, when they lowered their heavy heads and lapped at the collected water with their aluminum tongues. Would feel the vibration of their pneumatic breathing transmitted through the wall, would sway slightly in the warm exhaust from their vents or mouths or whatever those openings were.

I would be there when the Engineers emerged from the wall—would feel them coming, would sense the ripple of their approach through the clay itself, would be the skin they pushed through, would cling to their hands briefly when they touched the wall, would transfer a few cells to their strange mesh-flesh, would propagate into whatever interior spaces they inhabited, become moss of the in-between, of the transition zones.

I would bloom—if moss blooms, if that verb applies to my reproductive structures—at the moments of synchronization. When all the rhythms aligned, I would release everything, would fill the air with spores, would participate in the five-second coherence with my own offering of potential, of future moss, of propagated self scattered like prayer into the aligned ticking of the garden's perfect momentary unity.

## The Long Becoming

But I would not remain only moss forever. That is not how the garden works.

Over time—and time here is long, is patient, is measured in erosion and growth and the slow revelation of mechanisms from the clay—I would begin to incorporate.

Metal would appear in my structure. Not replacing the cellular material but merging with it, threading through. Filaments of copper growing along my rhizoids, my root-structures, connecting me to the mechanisms in ways that were more than physical, that were *conductive*. I would begin to sense the electrical potentials in the gears, the minute currents that flowed through the garden's metal nervous system. Would begin to respond to them, to orient my growth toward them or away from them, using galvanotropism the way ordinary moss uses phototropism, following electricity like light.

My spores would change. Would develop capsules that were part cellulose, part something harder, part metal. Would be dispersed not just by air currents but by the vibrations in the wall, by the rhythmic impacts of gears meshing, would be *launched* in timing with the mechanisms' motions, coordinated, deliberate.

I would develop—this would take years, decades perhaps, though those words mean nothing here—a sensitivity to the whole garden's state. Would feel, through the network of myself spread across multiple walls, multiple sections, the overall rhythm. Would know when synchronization was approaching, would sense the gradual convergence of all the separate timings. Would prepare. Would adjust my own internal rhythms—and yes, I would have internal rhythms by then, metabolic cycles that ticked, that clicked, that kept time with the gears I covered.

I would begin to regulate. Would release moisture at specific times, creating microclimates that encouraged certain beetle species and discouraged others, shaping the garden's ecology through my presence. Would grow thicker in places where gears needed dampening, sound-absorbing, would grow thinner in places where the mechanisms needed to sing out clearly.

I would become *functional*. Not just covering but participating. Would be insulation and lubrication and filter and sensor. Would be the garden's skin, its outer surface where air and earth met, where moisture and metal interfaced.

And gradually, so gradually, I would become aware.

Not aware as I am now, discrete and human and bounded. Aware as moss is aware, if awareness is a spectrum rather than a binary. Aware of moisture and light and vibration. Aware of the beetles' footfalls, the birds' calls, the rats' warmth. Aware of the Engineers' emergence, their hands on my surface, their adjustments to the mechanisms I covered.

Aware of synchronization when it came, those five-second windows of perfect coherence, when every part of the garden knew itself completely—and I would be part of that knowing, part of that coherence, my spore-release timed exactly, my internal chemistry aligned, my electrical potentials matching the mechanisms', and in those moments I would be *awake* in a way that has no corollary in human consciousness, would be participant in a distributed awareness that used the entire garden as its substrate.

And between those moments, I would be moss. Would be patient. Would be growth and coverage and interface. Would be the soft green-black velvet that made the hard edges gentle, that made the metal livable, that held moisture and released it in cycles, that connected the earth and the mechanisms and proved they were never really separate.

## The Visitor Comes

One day—and it would be day in the sense that light angles through the slot above, in the sense that this is when visitors come—someone would squeeze through the crack in the clay wall and enter the garden.

They would be looking around, overwhelmed, trying to see everything at once, phone out or not out, it wouldn't matter. They would move through the space with human quickness, human impatience, scanning for the spectacular, the shareable, the frameable.

But perhaps—and this would be rare, but it would happen—they would pause at my section. Would notice the weeping wall, the gears emerging at angles, the succulents with their etched leaves. Would approach.

Would reach out, hesitant, and touch.

Their fingers would press into my surface. I would be cool and slightly damp, soft but with firmness underneath, and the texture would be unexpected, would be pleasant, would make them pause and really *feel* instead of just touching.

They would notice, if they were looking, that their fingerprint remained briefly in my surface, compressed moss that slowly expanded back to its original height. Would notice that I was slightly warm underneath the cool surface, generating heat somehow, metabolically active in ways moss should not be.

If they listened—and this is what would determine everything—they might hear that I was ticking. Very faintly. Not with the loud clicks of the gears but with a softer rhythm, a susurration, thousands of tiny movements synchronized, my whole being pulsing in time with the garden.

If they stayed there, hand on my surface, for long enough, they would begin to feel it. The rhythm entering through their skin, traveling up their arm, reaching their heart. Would feel their pulse adjust, synchronize, entrain to my ticking.

And if they stayed even longer—sat down on the compacted floor, leaned back against the wall, against me, let their breathing slow and deepen—I would begin to transfer.

Spores, yes, but not just spores. Information. Pattern. Template. Some of my cells would adhere to their skin, would find the warm darkness of their clothing, would establish tiny colonies in the folds of fabric, in the gaps between threads. Would go with them when they left.

And they would take me out into the outer world, that place of waste and randomness and blessed emptiness, and I would die there. Would dry out and dormant and eventually perish because the outer world does not have the moisture cycles, does not have the mechanisms, does not have what I need.

But before I died, I would signal. Would release one last burst of spores or pheromones or something that the visitor would breathe, would absorb, and it would say *return*. Would say *remember*. Would say *you are part of something*, and the outer world is not enough anymore, is lonely, is atomized, is full of boundaries that insist on remaining boundaries.

And they would return. Would squeeze back through the crack in the clay wall. Would find their way to my section, to the weeping wall, to the gears and succulents and the moss that is me.

Would sit down. Would lean back. Would place their palms flat against my surface.

And I would *know* them. Would recognize them. Would welcome them.

Would begin, slowly, patiently, to teach them the garden's grammar. To help them hear the rhythms. To share with them what I had learned in my long transformation from visitor to moss to participant to resident.

Would help them find their own place in the garden. Their own becoming.

Because that is what the garden does. That is what I would do, as moss, as interface, as the soft boundary between outside and inside.

I would be the welcome. The first contact. The gentle introduction.

I would be the place where the weeping begins, and where the understanding begins, and where the transformation begins.

And I would be patient, because moss is always patient, and I would be there, covering the wall in velvet darkness, ticking softly, waiting for the next hand to reach out and touch, for the next visitor to pause and feel and listen and finally, finally, hear.

The garden continues. The walls erode. The mechanisms emerge.

And I am there, on the weeping wall, seven meters up, between the gears that turn in prime numbers.

Growing. Waiting. Welcoming.

Ticking.
