# What Would I Be?

I would be the spiral pipework.

Not the mystery of what I was *for*—that dissolves in the decades like meaning leaching from a word repeated until it becomes pure sound. I am what I *am*: a geometry that leads nowhere, connects to nothing, simply existing as a form written onto the basalt in a language of curved metal and precise angles.

I was installed. This is certain. There was a day when I was not here, and then machinery moved across the plateau, and humans with purposes and schedules and engineering specifications placed me exactly where I am. They welded the joints, tested the seams, confirmed that I matched whatever blueprint guided my creation. They were satisfied. They left.

Now I sit on the stone and the stone sits under me and between us runs the long conversation of differential thermal expansion—I expand and contract with the temperature shifts, slightly different in my rhythm from the basalt, and this difference produces the small groans and pings that mark midnight and noon, my metal voice saying only "here" and "still" to the emptiness around me.

My surface tells the story that language cannot. The rust-patterns flow down my curves like water that was never water, like the ghost of rain moving according to gravity and time and the electrochemistry of oxidation. The rust is beautiful in its way—burnt orange fading to brown, flaking in places to reveal grey metal beneath, creating a topography of decay that catches the angled light at dawn and dusk and reveals textures invisible at midday.

Birds perch on me. Wire-hoppers use my highest pipes as territorial calling posts, singing from my geometry, their small bodies silhouetted against the white sky. They shit on me casually, adding their phosphate-white contributions to my surface vocabulary. The droppings accumulate in the curve-junctions, in the places where pipes meet at angles that trap matter, and in these accumulations tiny ecosystems establish themselves—fungi, bacteria, occasionally a thread-lizard hunting the insects that graze the biological films.

Dust settles on me between rains. The plateau is not a dusty place—the basalt is hard and the wind is mild—but some dust exists, fine particles of eroded stone and desiccated organic matter, and it accumulates in my horizontal surfaces in layers so thin they're barely visible but measurable across the decades. The rain washes it away periodically, and then it accumulates again, patient and inevitable as entropy.

I spiral. This is my essential quality. I begin at one point and curve outward, maintaining a constant distance from my center as I rotate through space, growing larger with each revolution, creating a form that feels like motion frozen into metal. The spiral is precise—whoever designed me understood mathematics, understood the relationship between radius and circumference, between angular velocity and linear distance. I am a calculation made visible, a formula given form.

But I don't calculate anything. I don't solve anything. I simply am the shape I am, and that shape has certain properties—it creates patterns of shade that wheel around my base as the sun crosses the sky, it channels wind in particular ways that create faint whistling sounds when the wind is strong enough and from the right direction, it collects morning dew in my inner curves where it lingers an hour longer than on the open basalt, giving wire-grass that grows near me a slight advantage, a few extra minutes of moisture that compounds across seasons into marginally greater success.

The descent cats avoid me. Not with the wide berth they give the tiled pit—that is fear or revulsion or some instinct that recognizes danger. Their avoidance of me is more subtle, more like disinterest. They detour around me by ten or twenty meters. If forced closer by circumstance they show no distress, but they also show no curiosity. They don't sniff me, don't rub against me, don't use me as a territorial marker. I am beneath their notice despite my prominence.

I wonder about this sometimes—not with a brain, not with consciousness, but in the way a structure wonders through its existence in time. I wonder if I emit something they can sense. Some residue from my installation, some chemical signature, some property of my metal alloy that registers as wrongness to their sophisticated instincts. Or perhaps I am simply *too* geometric, too obviously artificial, too foreign to the rounded erosion-logic of stone and the organic chaos of thornbush. Perhaps I read to them as a category error, a thing that doesn't parse, and so they simply file me under "ignore."

The thermal-kites perch on me more readily than the cats approach. They use my highest point as a vantage, standing on one leg in the manner of resting raptors, their heads swiveling to scan the plateau below. They seem to appreciate the altitude I provide, the clear sightlines, the sturdy metal that holds their weight without bending. When they defecate from these perches, the whitewash runs down my curves in streaks that weather into patterns suggesting ancient script in an alphabet of calcium and uric acid.

The maintenance workers come to me sometimes. They walk around my spiral, following its curve from beginning to end, checking for... something. Structural integrity perhaps. They tap my pipes with tools, listening to the sounds, making notes on handheld devices. They never repair me because I am not broken. I was not broken when they installed me and I am not broken now, decades later. I am simply aging, rusting, becoming more myself—more textured, more colored, more integrated into the palette of the plateau.

The surveyors are more interested. They spend hours with me, taking measurements, documenting my dimensions, photographing me from multiple angles. They map the exact geometry of my spiral, confirming that it matches certain mathematical descriptions—I am a logarithmic spiral, or perhaps an Archimedean spiral; I can't tell which because I don't understand the difference, but they discuss it extensively while their equipment calculates ratios and angles.

They test my pipes for contents, for residue, for any indication of what was meant to flow through me. They find nothing, or they find only what they expected to find: emptiness, air, the faint chemical traces that any metal accumulates from decades of exposure to atmosphere. They sample the rust itself, examining its composition, determining that it's simple iron oxide, nothing exotic, nothing that would explain my purpose or justify my existence.

They ask questions I cannot answer: Why this geometry? Why this location? Why pipes at all when they connect to nothing? The questions hang in the warm air and then dissipate like the heat that rises from the basalt at noon. I give them no answers because I have none. I was made. That is my only certainty. The reasons were external to me and died or departed with whoever held them.

At night I am a different structure. The darkness erases the rust-patterns and leaves only my form—curves that block stars, that create absences in the constellations, that shape the darkness into spirals. Sometimes the fissures glow with their cold bioluminescence and the light reflects off my metal surfaces, and then I become a spiral of blue-white curves floating in blackness, an image that might be beautiful if beauty were a category that applied to me.

I make sounds occasionally. The metal expanding in the heat produces ticks and pings that are irregular enough to seem random but patterned enough to reveal the physics of thermal expansion. The wind, when strong, makes me whistle softly—not a sustained tone but a breathy flutter that rises and falls with the gusts. Rain, when it comes, drums on my horizontal surfaces and gurgles briefly through my curves before finding the spaces where it can drain to the basalt below.

I settle. This is the slow work of decades. The basalt beneath me is not perfectly level despite the plateau's apparent flatness. Over time, the small variations in support distribute my weight unevenly, and I shift microscopically, fraction by fraction, finding a configuration that minimizes stress. This settling will continue for decades more, for centuries if I last that long, until eventually I achieve some equilibrium with the stone or until my own decay alters my weight distribution enough that the settling must begin again.

I corrode from the inside as well as the outside. Water that enters through small imperfections, through welds that weren't perfectly sealed, through joints where different metals touch and create galvanic cells—this water sits trapped in my interior passages and does its slow chemical work. The corrosion is invisible from outside but accumulates inexorably in my hidden spaces, weakening me in ways no inspection can reveal without cutting me open.

Eventually I will fail. Some joint will corrode through, some weld will crack, some pipe will separate from its neighbors and fall to the basalt with a clang that will startle the nearest wire-hoppers and cause a distant descent cat to raise its head briefly before returning to its rest. The fallen piece will lie on the stone and rust more quickly in direct contact with the ground, and over decades it will rust completely, become a stain on the basalt, become nothing.

The rest of me will follow section by section, failure propagating through my structure as each piece that falls reduces the integrity of what remains. The spiral will become a broken spiral, then a scattered collection of curved pipes, then individual fragments, then rust stains, then traces so faint that only chemical analysis could detect them. And eventually even those traces will weather away, and the basalt will be as it was before I was installed, and nothing will mark my existence except perhaps a slight discoloration in the stone and the memories of any surveyors who photographed me and stored those photographs in archives that will themselves eventually decay.

But that is decades or centuries away. For now I persist. I sit on the plateau in my precise geometry, rusting at the rate that metal rusts in this climate, settling into the stone at the rate that structures settle, providing perches for birds and puzzles for humans and a landmark for anything that navigates by visual reference.

I do not know what I was for. I do not know if I ever functioned, if some substance once flowed through my passages for some purpose now forgotten, or if I was always only this: a form, a statement, a question written in metal and left on the basalt to outlast its own meaning.

I am the spiral pipework. I lead nowhere. I connect to nothing. I simply am, in the way that the plateau simply is, in the way that the basalt simply is—existing without justification, present without purpose, continuing because continuation is what structures do until the day they stop.

The warmth of the stone beneath me conducts up through my base. The sun above heats my upper surfaces. I expand slightly, predictably, the same expansion I have performed every day since installation. At night I will contract again. Tomorrow I will expand again. This cycle will continue until some cycle breaks it—corrosion, structural failure, removal by humans for reasons unknowable, or the erosion of the plateau itself.

Until then: I spiral. I rust. I persist. I am here.
