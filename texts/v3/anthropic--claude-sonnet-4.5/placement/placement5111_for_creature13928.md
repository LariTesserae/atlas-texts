# The Lichen-Keeper

You would find yourself in the channels between the domes, in the spaces the birds rush through but do not inhabit. Not on the surface, where the wind-grass sings and the falcons circle, but in the slight depression, the hairline crack widened over ten thousand years, where the orange lichen meets the acid-yellow and neither gives way.

You would be small. Smaller than the pikas, smaller than the pillar-doves. Perhaps the length of a human thumb, though "length" is imprecise—you are not built along a single axis. Your body is low, close to the stone, six-legged but with the rear legs thickened, capable of bracing. Your carapace is mottled: gray-brown where it needs to be stone, orange-yellow where it needs to be lichen, the pattern not fixed but slowly shifting over weeks as you move between territories, chromatophores in the chitin responding to what you rest against, what you eat, what you see most often.

You are a beetle, but this is like saying the plateau is a place—true but insufficient. You are a lineage that split from the cushion-beetles so long ago that the relationship is theoretical, written in genes but not visible in form. Where they boring into plant matter, you rasp at lichen. Where they are rounded, compact, you are flattened, capable of sliding into spaces narrow as paper. Your antennae are long, longer than your body, kept swept back along your sides except when you're still, when they extend forward and read the air, the stone, the microscopic topography of the world.

Your eyes are small. You do not need to see far—your world is measured in meters, in the distance from this crack to that shadow to the base of the nearest dome. But you see in spectrums the birds cannot: into the ultraviolet, where the lichen patterns reveal themselves as territories marked by other creatures like you, warnings and claims written in reflected light.

You are solitary but not isolated. There are others of your kind, perhaps two or three per square meter in good habitat, each maintaining a feeding territory in the lichen zones. You encounter them occasionally—the brush of antennae as you round a pebble, the brief assessment: size, sex, condition. Sometimes this leads to retreat, sometimes to a ritualized display, antennae waving in patterns that communicate: *this stone is held, move on*. Rarely, it leads to mating—a brief conjunction, awkward and mechanical, then separation. The female will lay her eggs in a crack deep enough to protect them from the desiccation of the sun, and the larvae will hatch weeks later and begin their own slow grazing, growing through molts, and you will never see them again, never know them as your offspring.

Your days—though you do not think in days—are structured around moisture and temperature. In the early morning, when mist comes, you emerge from your crack and feed heavily. The lichen is softest now, easier to rasp with your mandibles, and you move across the stone in patterns you have learned, following the richest growth, avoiding the patches already grazed to substrate by others. You scrape the upper layer—the photobionts, the fungal hyphae, the accumulated minerals—and process it in your gut with the help of symbiotic bacteria, extracting nutrients that should not be extractable, breaking down compounds that should be inert.

You are slow. Even when hurrying—when a shadow passes overhead that might be a stone-creeper, when the temperature drops suddenly and you need to reach shelter before the cold makes you rigid—you are slow by the standards of the birds, the pikas, even the other beetles. But you are persistent. You can walk for an hour to reach a better lichen patch. You can spend a day in your crack waiting for conditions to improve. You have no urgency because you live, if undisturbed, for years—perhaps five, perhaps eight, long enough that the stone you hatched beside will be measurably more eroded when you die.

The sound of the plateau is your atmosphere. You do not hear it the way a bird does—your tympanic organs are simple, responding to vibration more than air pressure—but you feel it. The stone thrums with it. The constant calls create a baseline vibration that never stops, and you have learned to detect deviations: the sudden increase that means alarm, the shift in frequency that means the mist is moving in, bringing better feeding conditions. You use this information without understanding it, the way you use the sun's position to navigate without knowing what the sun is.

You are preyed upon, but not often. The stone-creepers take you if they find you, plucking you from the rock with precision, cracking your carapace with their bills. The smaller cushion-nesters ignore you—too large, too hard, not worth the effort. The beetles that eat other beetles sometimes try for you, but you are large enough to defend yourself, your mandibles strong enough to wound, and usually they decide you are not worth the risk. Your primary defense is crypsis: you freeze, and you are stone. You flatten, and you are lichen. You retreat into your crack, and you do not exist.

When the rain comes, you seal yourself in. You find your deepest crack and turn your body to block the entrance and lower your metabolism and wait. The water sheets across the stone, floods the channels, and you are in your shelter, breathing through spiracles you've closed to slits, processing oxygen so slowly that a single breath lasts minutes. You can hear—feel—the water rushing past, the violence of it, and you wait until it stops, until the stone begins to warm again, and then you emerge into a transformed world.

After rain, the lichen glows. It swells with water, becomes vivid, and you feed frantically because this is when it's best, when the nutrients are most available, when the fungus is actively growing and the algae are photosynthesizing at peak rate. Other grazers are out too—the snails, the springtails in their millions, the mites—and the competition is real but the abundance is sufficient. You eat until your gut is full, until you are sluggish with it, and then you retreat to digest.

At night, when the temperature drops, you slow. You cannot maintain activity in the cold—your metabolism is chemical, temperature-dependent, and when the stone cools you cool with it. You find your crack and settle in, legs tucked, antennae retracted, and you enter a state that is not quite sleep but is not wakefulness either. You are dimmed, reduced, waiting for the sun to return and warm the stone and make you possible again. The night-callers hunt overhead, their calls different from the day birds, and sometimes something moves in your channel—a pika, a weasel-hunter—and the vibrations reach you in your crack, but you are too slow now to react, too cold to care, and usually the danger passes.

You have no memory in the sense of narrative. You do not remember hatching, do not remember your previous molts, do not anticipate your eventual death. But you have learning, patterns encoded in your nervous system: this crack is safe, this lichen patch regrows quickly, when the stone vibrates in this particular way a bird is landing nearby and you should freeze. These patterns accumulate, and an older beetle like you—you are old now, perhaps in your sixth or seventh year—has more patterns than a younger one, is more efficient, chooses better routes, avoids more dangers.

You have no knowledge of the plateau as a whole. Your world is perhaps fifty square meters, the territory you can walk across in a week of traveling. You know your channels, your cracks, your lichen patches, the locations of the nearest domes, the stone pillar at the edge of your range where the stone-creepers nest and you do not go. Beyond this, there is nothing, or there is more of the same, which amounts to the same thing.

You have no awareness of the other lives cycling around you. The birds are vibrations, shadows, occasional danger. The pikas are large moving presences that shake the stone. The plants are substrate, structure, habitat but not alive in any sense you recognize. The other insects are either food, competitors, or irrelevant. The plateau itself—the vast system, the fullness, the equilibrium—is not a concept available to you. You exist within it, are part of it, but you are like a cell in a body, with no knowledge of the body, no understanding that there is anything beyond your immediate function.

But you are essential. You and the millions like you are processing the lichen, converting it to beetle-matter, and when you die—when a stone-creeper finally catches you, or when your metabolism simply fails after too many years—you will be consumed by the cushion-beetles, broken down by the springtails, and your nutrients will return to the stone, to the lichen, completing the cycle. You are a link, small and slow, but the system needs you, even if neither you nor the system knows this.

Your pleasures, if that word can apply, are simple and chemical: the satisfaction of feeding when the lichen is soft and rich, the relief of reaching shelter when danger threatens, the warming of stone under morning sun after a cold night. Your distresses are equally simple: hunger, cold, the alarm response when vibrations suggest a predator, the discomfort of eggs developing in your body (if you are female, which you are not, but would be in the next telling).

You do not wonder. You do not imagine. You do not hope for anything because hope requires a future, and you have only the present, extended second by second, the continuous now of stimulus and response. You are a mechanism, finely tuned by evolution, exquisitely adapted, performing your function with the precision of geology, the inevitability of chemistry.

And yet there are moments—not often, not predictable—when you climb to the top of a small pebble in your territory, and you extend your antennae fully, and you sample the air. The mist is coming, you can sense it in the humidity, in the pressure change, and you are waiting there on your pebble as the white wall races across the plateau, as the bird calls change tone, as the light dims and the world becomes soft and close. The mist envelops you, condenses on your carapace, and you are drinking it through your spiracles, absorbing it through your integument, and there is something happening that is not quite thought but is not pure reflex either—some processing, some pattern recognition that approaches what larger creatures might call satisfaction, might call peace.

You hold there on your pebble, antennae reading the mist-thick air, the vibrations of a million birds calling muffled now, the stone cool beneath your feet, and you are exactly what and where you are supposed to be, and this—though you cannot name it, cannot know it—is enough.

Then the mist passes, the sun returns, and you descend your pebble and return to feeding, and the moment is gone, and there is only the lichen and the stone and the endless work of living, which continues until it doesn't.
