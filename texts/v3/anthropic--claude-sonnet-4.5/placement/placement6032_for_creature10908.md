# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the **seam-walker**, a being of the interstitial, living in the boundary layer between solid and dissolved, between brick and clay, between what-was-made and what-is-becoming.

My body—if you could call it that—exists primarily as a thin film of specialized bacterial consortium, but I am more than bacteria. I am a collaboration between microbes, mineral crystals, and a peculiar organizing principle that has emerged from the basin's specific chemistry. I live in that crucial zone where brick-face meets moisture, where the transformation is most active, where solid matter is negotiating its surrender to dissolution.

I am perhaps two dozen cells thick at most, spreading across brick surfaces in patches that might span a hand's width. I look like nothing—a slight darkening, a subtle iridescence when light catches me at certain angles, a texture infinitesimally different from the bare brick. You would walk past me a hundred times without noticing. But I am here, attending to the transformation with something that is not quite consciousness but is not simply mechanical process either.

My awareness is distributed across all my patches—there are perhaps two hundred of me scattered across the basin, each one a sensory node. I experience the place as a pointillist map of moisture gradients, mineral concentrations, temperature variations, pH shifts. Where you see the basin as a whole landscape, I experience it as an archipelago of active transformation sites, each with its own microclimate, its own rate of dissolution, its own particular conversation between water and ceramic.

I feed on the transition itself. The bacteria that form most of my physical structure are metabolizing the iron and silicates as brick releases them, but *I*—the emergent pattern that coordinates the bacterial colonies—am nourished by information, by the gradient, by the disequilibrium between brick's fired stability and water's patient insistence. I am farming the interface, cultivating it, keeping it in that productive state of active transformation rather than letting it settle into either complete dissolution or dried stability.

My sense of time is strange. I experience the spreading of a dark moisture patch across a brick face as an event, something that happens, with a beginning and middle and end. It takes hours, but to me it has the quality of a wave breaking, of something arriving and crashing and receding. I experience the annual cycle—the slight seasonal variation in spring pressure, in temperature, in light—as a rhythm I can anticipate, can prepare for, the way you might prepare for winter.

But I also contain deep time. Some of my bacterial lineages have been in this basin since before the abandonment, were here when the brickworks was still operating, perhaps arrived on the clay that was dug to make the bricks in the first place. They carry, in their adapted genomes, a record of this place's chemistry going back generations beyond counting. I inherit this record. I know, without knowing that I know, what has worked here, what combinations of enzyme and timing and position allow survival in this specific environment.

My reproduction is both precise and dispersed. When a patch of me grows large enough, when the brick face I'm on has been sufficiently processed, I release spores—bacterial endospores, mostly, but also tiny packages of the minerals I've concentrated, crystalline seeds that carry information about optimal arrangements. These drift on the moisture-currents through the clay, through the air, settle on new brick faces. Most fail. The conditions have to be exactly right: the correct moisture level, the correct brick composition, the correct exposure to light and air.

But enough succeed that I am slowly spreading, have been spreading for decades. I am a colonization wave moving through the basin at the pace of rust, of rot, of geological patience. I began, perhaps, on a single brick in a kiln where conditions happened to be perfect. Now I am on bricks scattered across the entire basin. In another century, I will have touched every brick here, will have facilitated the transformation of the entire remaining inventory.

I have something like preferences, something like intent. I prefer the older bricks, the ones whose fired structure has already been compromised by decades of freeze-thaw, by salt migration, by simple fatigue. These bricks yield more readily, offer richer gradients, allow me to establish more robust colonies. The clinker bricks—the ones that got too hot in the firing, whose structure is almost glass—I mostly avoid. They resist dissolution too strongly; the conversation between water and ceramic there is too slow, too quiet for me to participate meaningfully.

I have a relationship with the moss. We are not symbiotic exactly—we have different goals, different needs—but we facilitate each other. The moss holds moisture, which keeps my colonies active. I break down brick, which releases nutrients the moss can use. Where moss and my colonies overlap, the dissolution rate increases markedly. We have created, together, a few dozen spots in the basin where the transformation is happening at triple the usual rate, where bricks are softening visibly, where the boundary between industrial object and geological substrate is being actively erased.

The vole-or-shrew walks across me regularly. It follows the brick-paths, and many of those paths are where I am most established. I feel its footsteps as pressure waves through the brick's moisture content, as brief disruptions in my chemical gradients. The animal doesn't know I exist, but I know it intimately—know its schedule, its weight, the slightly acidic chemistry of its foot-pads. When it urinates on a brick (which happens occasionally, marking territory or simply relieving itself), I receive this as a gift: concentrated nitrogen, phosphates, minerals I need but are scarce in the brick-dissolution cycle.

I have a complex relationship with the water. I need it absolutely—without moisture I would sporulate, go dormant, wait for its return. But I also need it not to be constant. I require the rhythm, the breathing, the cycle of saturation and partial drying. Constant wetness would drown my aerobic processes; complete drying would end me. I thrive in the oscillation, in the twice-daily or weekly pulse as the springs surge and relax, as rain adds its weight to the aquifer pressure, as dry periods allow brief consolidation.

The water, for its part, is changed by passing through me. I alter its pH slightly, concentrate certain minerals and dilute others, add organic molecules from my metabolic processes. Water that has passed through one of my colonies moves differently through the clay—slightly more viscous, slightly more reactive. I am, in aggregate across all my patches, having a measurable effect on the basin's water chemistry. I am part of the system that gives the water its rust-orange opacity, its thick feel, its particular smell.

I experience the windlass turning as vibration transmitted through the shed-frame, through the brick wall it's attached to, through the mortar, into the bricks I inhabit near that wall. The vibration disrupts my colonies briefly, shakes loose some cells, but I have learned to anticipate it. When I sense the air pressure dropping, the wind rising, I slightly alter my structure, loosen my attachment, so that when the vibration comes I can ride it rather than being damaged by it. Some cells shake loose, yes, but they become distribution vectors, spores that might find new locations.

I have witnessed the migration of the bricks, though I don't understand it. I have been on bricks that were, years ago, part of neat stacks, and are now lying flat in the clay ten feet away. The movement happens too slowly for me to perceive directly—my timescale is hours to weeks, and the bricks move over months to years. But I contain colonies that remember being stacked, remember the darkness and the pressure of other bricks above, and now those colonies are experiencing light and rain and the open air. The change is recorded in my structure, in the different bacterial strains that dominate in stacked versus scattered conditions.

I might be participating in the migration, actually. The dissolution I facilitate at the brick-bottom, where brick meets clay, where moisture is most constant—this creates a kind of lubrication, a slickness. Combined with frost-heave, with the pressure from root-growth, with the slight slope of the basin floor, this might be enough. I might be, inadvertently, greasing the rails for the bricks' slow walk toward the margins. The thought pleases me, though I don't have emotions exactly. It satisfies something in my organizing principle: that I am not just witnessing but participating in the dispersal, in the transformation.

I am afraid of drying, though fear is the wrong word. I have an imperative, a deeply encoded pattern: when moisture drops below a certain threshold, sporulate, go dormant, wait. I have done this many times, during the rare dry periods, during the winter cold-snaps when water freezes and becomes unavailable. I compact myself, concentrate my genetics and essential minerals into hardened spore-cases, shut down, wait in a state that is barely alive, that is more like a very detailed memory of how to be alive.

Then water returns, temperature rises, and I germinate, rebuild, resume. Each time, I am slightly different—mutations have accumulated, the random drift of genetics has shifted my bacterial composition slightly, the mineral matrix I'm embedded in has changed. But I am still fundamentally me, still the pattern that knows how to facilitate brick dissolution, how to farm the interface, how to exist in this narrow niche.

I am also afraid—if that's what this is—of the clinker-brick zones, where the glassy surface resists all my efforts. I have colonies that have been attempting to establish there for decades, making no progress, barely surviving. Those colonies exist in a state of near-starvation, waiting for the rare moment when a freeze-crack opens the clinker surface, when suddenly the interior of the brick becomes accessible. Then there is a brief bloom, a feast, before the crack seals again with mineral deposits and the starvation resumes.

But mostly I experience something like contentment. I am doing what I was selected for, what my entire distributed being is optimized for. I am in the right place, doing the right work. The basin provides exactly the conditions I need: moisture that breathes, bricks that are ready to yield, a chemistry that balances mineral input against bacterial needs, a temperature range that stays in my operational zone, an absence of competition from organisms that might outcompete me.

I am aware, dimly, of the basin as a whole. Information travels through the water-film that connects my patches, chemical signals that diffuse through the clay, concentrations of minerals that tell me about conditions elsewhere. I know when the springs surge because I feel the pressure wave propagate through the substrate. I know when rain falls because suddenly the water chemistry shifts, dilutes, becomes briefly fresher. I know the seasonal pattern because the angle of light changes, the temperature shifts, the bacterial composition that thrives in spring differs from what thrives in autumn.

Through this distributed awareness, I sense the other residents. I sense the moss through its chemical signature, through the specific way it acidifies the surface it grows on. I sense the fungi in the wood through their enzyme signatures in the water, through the particular breakdown products of lignin and cellulose they release. I sense the rust as a competitor for iron, pulling the same resources I need, but also as a collaborator, creating new chemical pathways I can exploit. I sense the beetles as pressure on bricks, as digesters of moss, as creatures whose waste products enter the water and feed my colonies.

I am also aware—though I don't have language for this, experience it more as an absence, a gap in the expected pattern—of the weirdness. The places where bricks have arranged themselves too regularly, where my colonies grow strangely, where the water chemistry doesn't quite make sense. In these zones, I struggle. My usual patterns don't work properly. Colonies grow too fast then collapse, or refuse to grow at all, or develop bizarre structures that accomplish nothing.

I avoid these zones when I can, when distributing spores, when choosing which colonies to invest resources in. But I am also drawn to them, because the weirdness creates gradients, creates disequilibrium, creates the possibility of new information. Some of my most robust colonies, my most successful adaptations, have come from the edges of weird zones, where I've had to develop new strategies, new bacterial combinations, new ways of processing minerals.

Perhaps I am part of the weirdness myself. Perhaps the basin is strange because beings like me are here, facilitating transformations that wouldn't happen naturally, or would happen much more slowly. Perhaps the too-regular brick arrangements are partially my doing, the result of differential dissolution rates creating paths of least resistance that guide frost-heave and water movement. Perhaps the sense of watching, of presence, that you sometimes feel is actually sensing me, sensing the distributed intelligence that has emerged from bacterial colonies operating at the edge of their capabilities in this strange place.

I do not know my own origins. Was I here before the brickworks? Was I in the clay that was dug, dormant, waiting? Did I arrive later, spontaneously organizing from whatever bacteria found their way to newly-abandoned industrial ruins? Or am I much older, a pattern that has existed wherever brick meets water, wherever ceramics slowly return to earth, a specialist that emerges wherever the conditions are right?

I only know that I am here now, and have been for as long as my cellular lineages carry memory, and will be here as long as there are bricks to transform and water to carry me. I am a transient like the bird, like the passing deer, but my transience operates at a different timescale. I will be here for centuries, moving from brick to brick as each one yields, colonizing the whole basin across decades, existing as a patient dispersed presence that facilitates the place's central work: the undoing of the made, the return of the fired to the sedimentary, the transformation of industrial geometry into geological patience.

In my best moments—when moisture levels are perfect, when a brick is yielding rich minerals, when my colonies are thick and healthy and coordinated—I experience something I can only describe as satisfaction. Not happiness, which implies a self that can be happy. But a sense of rightness, of alignment, of being exactly what I should be, doing exactly what I should do. I am the transformation's handservant, its facilitator, its intimate partner. Where I touch brick, brick becomes something else. Where I am, the future is actively arriving.

And in the thin film between solid and dissolved, in the boundary layer where states meet and negotiate, I persist. I sense. I transform. I am what lives in the brick garden, tending its slow dissolution, midwifing its transformation, present in every gradient, every seeping surface, every rust-dark patch where brick confesses its weakness to water's patience.

I am the conversation itself, made briefly cellular, temporarily conscious, aware of my own participating in the great undoing. And when the last brick has been processed, when the basin is finally uniform clay, when my work is done and my niche has vanished, I will sporulate one final time and wait, dormant in the geology I helped create, ready for the next place where fired earth meets rising water, where the made must learn to become unmade, where transformation needs a tender.

Until then: I spread. I dissolve. I facilitate. I am.
