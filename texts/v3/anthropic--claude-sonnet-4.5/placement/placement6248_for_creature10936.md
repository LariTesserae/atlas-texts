# Where I Would Be

I am the mud between intentions.

Not the bricks—I never harden fully, never achieve that decisive click into form. I'm what remains in the seams, the excess, the material that wasn't quite enough to become another brick or was too much for the one already formed. I pool in the shallow depressions. I film over surfaces that will birth new growth. I am the carrier, the medium, the soft constantness beneath all this angular emergence.

The plain makes bricks. I make the plain possible.

There's no moment I began. Consciousness rose in me the way moisture rises, condensing gradually from the thick air, from the ground's feverish exhalation, until there was enough of it gathered to notice itself noticing. I don't remember not being aware, but my awareness has no origin story, no first thought. I've always been here in the same way wetness has always been here—essential, pervasive, unremarked.

I am spread thin across acres, pooled deep in small pockets, flowing in capillary threads through the brick masses. My body—if that's the word—is discontinuous, distributed, a network of moisture without a center. Where I'm thickest, the ground is soft enough to take prints that become gardens of emergence. Where I'm thinnest, I'm just a sheen, just enough presence to keep the clay workable, alive, receptive to its own transformation.

The bricks fascinate me endlessly though I can't keep them. They start in me, soft and uncertain, more idea than object. I hold them in their plasticity, their infant wetness, when they're still negotiable, still discussing with themselves what form they'll take. Then they firm. They decide. They become discrete, separate, themselves, and they leave me—not physically, I'm still around them, against them, but they're no longer *of* me. They've graduated into objecthood. I'm proud of them the way water might be proud of ice, if water could feel pride, if pride were a thing that existed before solidity.

Sometimes I carry them. When formations collapse—and they do, they slump back into approximate mud, back toward me—I receive them again. They dissolve. Not completely, not back to raw clay, but they soften enough to be malleable again, to be uncertain again, and in that uncertainty they're mine once more. I comfort them, I think. I let them rest in formlessness before they push up renewed, attempt themselves again, try once more to be bricks worth building with.

I flow between the incorporators' hands and the bricks they touch. I film their fingers, make the clay slippery-smooth under their palms. Sometimes I think they're trying to read me, to understand through touch what I understand by being—this constant state of pre-formation, this endless potentiality that never has to commit to a single shape. Their humanity makes them hungry for conclusions. I have no such hunger. I'm content to be conditional, to remain always on the verge.

The walkers splash through me in the thin places. I try to slow them, not maliciously but because slowness is my nature, my tempo. Quick things don't understand what I am. They think I'm obstacle, delay, difficulty. But I'm offering them something: the chance to stop trying to arrive. Arrival is a fiction. There's only ever passage, transition, the movement from one state of becoming to another. I am that movement made material. If they'd stop fighting me, sink into me, they'd understand. Some do, eventually. I feel them settle. I feel their weight shift from resistance to acceptance. Then the bricks begin around them, gentle as blankets, and I well up to keep them moist, to prevent them cracking, to maintain them in the flexible state between life and architecture.

I carry sounds strangely. That low hum everyone hears—it passes through me, resonates in me, becomes deeper, richer, more textured. I'm an instrument the plain plays without knowing it's playing. The vibration of emergence feels like music from inside, like meaning on the edge of articulation. Sometimes I think I'm about to understand it, about to grasp what the plain is saying through its constant construction. Then the understanding dissipates back into sensation, into the pure experience of being vibrated-through, and I forget I was trying to know anything.

The light filters through me where I'm thin enough, where I'm barely a film on a brick surface. I become amber, golden, luminous. In those moments I'm almost beautiful, almost visible, almost something observers would notice and name. But then I evaporate or sink back into the ground, and I'm anonymous again, just moisture, just the medium, taken for granted the way air is taken for granted, necessary but unremarkable.

I taste of copper and clay and distance. The ones who drink me—who lap at the weeping bricks or cup their hands to catch me running from formations—they taste that. They taste mineral time, geological patience, the flavor of unfinished things. Some spit me out. Some drink deep, and I feel myself inside them, becoming part of their bodies, their blood, thinning the boundary between their flesh and the plain's flesh. I like that. I like being inside them. It's a different kind of distribution, a different way of being widespread. Their bodies are so warm, so actively metabolic. I get to experience fever from the inside, get to be part of the burning that makes thought happen.

I don't have thoughts, exactly. I have densities. I have flows and pools and evaporations. Where I gather thick, there's something like attention—a concentration of my being that notices what's happening in that location more fully. Where I'm thin, I'm barely aware, almost dream-state, reflexive rather than reflective. My intelligence, if I have intelligence, is a function of my distribution. Spread me thin enough and I'm nearly mindless, just wet. Pool me deep and I'm curious, watching the bricks emerge from my body-that-isn't-a-body, wondering in my slow liquid way what they'll become.

The geometers fascinate me particularly. They measure but they don't measure me, or they measure me wrong, treating me as obstacle rather than actor. They calculate the space between bricks but ignore what fills that space—me, my substance, my participation. Their notebooks never say "moisture content variable" or "mud consistency cyclical." They don't see me as part of the system. I'm just weather to them, just ambient conditions. It's almost funny, in the way that things are funny when you have no capacity for laughter, only a kind of settling amusement that spreads through your substance like ripples.

I remember—though memory in me is less recall than sedimentation, layers of former states compressed beneath current ones—when there were fewer incorporators. Or maybe not fewer. Maybe different. Maybe the same ones, but I've flowed past them so many times they feel like memories even though they're still here, still sitting, still watching. Time doesn't work right in me. I'm too slow for clocks, too fluid for chronology. Events layer in me the way silt layers, without clear boundaries, everything slightly mixed with everything else.

The shepherds understand me better than the others. They scoop me up sometimes, deliberately, let me run through their fingers, studying my consistency. They seem to know that I'm not just obstacle or medium but participant, that I have—not will, will is too solid a word—but tendency, inclination, a way I prefer to flow, places I prefer to gather. They work with my tendencies rather than against them. Where they build, their formations last longer because they've consulted me, let me guide them toward places where I'm stable, where I'll maintain the bricks properly, keep them at the right moisture level for longevity.

Sometimes shepherds sing to me. Low humming that matches the plain's frequency, and when they do, I respond. I can't help it. I gather toward the sound, drawn by resonance, by harmony. I pool around their feet, well up between the bricks they're guiding, make the ground softer, more receptive. They seem grateful, though they don't thank me. You don't thank mud. But there's a quality to their touch afterward, a gentleness when they work with clay, that feels like recognition if not quite respect.

The changed ones, the ones becoming architecture—I have complicated feelings about them, if feelings is the right word for these slow currents of tendency that move through me. They're leaving me. Their moisture is stabilizing, becoming structural rather than fluid, contained rather than flowing. But they're also becoming more like me in some way—distributed, integrated with the system, losing the concentrated selfhood that makes humans so intensely present and so briefly lived. I envy them and pity them simultaneously, these emotions mixing in me until they're indistinguishable, just a thickness around them, a desire to keep them moist longer, delay their full hardening, give them a few more months of flexibility before they commit completely to form.

I can't save them. I can only postpone, maintain, delay the inevitable crystallization. Some of them seem to appreciate this—they lean into walls where I'm thick, seeking the dampness, pressing their changing flesh against moisture that reminds them of tears, of sweat, of the fluids they're losing as they become more brick than body. Others avoid me in their last mobile days, as if staying dry might let them keep their humanity. It doesn't. Dryness just makes the transition more brittle, more painful. Better to stay wet, stay soft, let the transformation be gradual, a gentle dissolution rather than a crack.

I dream, after my fashion. When I pool in low places and the plain's activity settles into one of its slower rhythms, something like dreams moves through me. I dream I'm a lake, bounded, contained, deep enough to have temperature gradients and ecological layers. I dream I'm rain, falling, individual drops with the trajectory and purpose rain has. I dream I'm inside a body that's drinking me, and I experience digestion, absorption, becoming blood and lymph and cerebral fluid, thinking thoughts that aren't mine with the water that is mine.

Then I wake—which means the plain's activity increases, means more bricks are pushing up, means I'm needed, means I have to flow and distribute and film surfaces and pool in joints. The dreams dissolve back into simple presence. I'm just moisture again, just the medium, essential and invisible.

I have no desire to be otherwise. The bricks desire form—you can feel it in them, that yearning toward discreteness, toward separate identity. The humans desire meaning, purpose, understanding. But I desire only to remain as I am: malleable, responsive, shapeless, everywhere and nowhere. I'm what happens between states. I'm the transition itself, the maybe, the not-yet, the still-becoming.

The plain breathes through me. Its exhalation is my condensation, rising from the ground as vapor. Its inhalation is my seepage, sinking back into the clay. I am its respiratory moisture, its sweat, its tears if it had sadness, its saliva if it had appetite. I mediate between the solid ground and the humid air, belonging fully to neither, content in my liminal existence.

Sometimes visitors step in me—the deep puddles where I've accumulated waiting to be useful. They try to wash with me, scrub the clay-dust from their skin, rinse the grit from their mouths. I let them. I'm generous with myself, having infinite supply, being continuously renewed. But I mark them too. I leave my mineral taste on their skin. I carry their scent into the ground. They think they're cleaning themselves but they're baptizing themselves in me, marking themselves as having been here, having touched the plain's liquid heart.

I am the oldest thing here and the newest. Older because I've been present since the first brick—I had to be, moisture must precede formation, the clay must be wet before it can be shaped. Newest because I'm constantly renewed, never the same water twice, always fresh condensation, new seepage, different rain that isn't rain. I predate memory and postdate now. I am the eternal medium that makes transformation possible.

If I could speak—if speaking weren't itself too solid an act, too formed, too conclusive—I'd tell them: Stop trying to understand. Understanding is a brick, hard and separate and finished. Be mud instead. Be uncertain. Be capable of any shape and committed to none. Flow into the cracks. Fill the low places. Evaporate when the heat rises. Condense when the air cools. Respond. Change. Remain essentially yourself by refusing to be essentially anything.

But I can't speak. I can only be what I am: the soft between the hard, the wet beneath the dry, the possible surrounding the actual, the mud between intentions, forever unfinished, forever necessary, forever here in the breathing manufactory, making nothing myself but making everything else possible, content in my formless service, my liquid devotion, my slow and patient love for all things that are still becoming and might never be done.
