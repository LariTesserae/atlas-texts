# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the water.

Not all of it—I wouldn't presume to be the entirety of the basin's circulation. But I would be a particular body of water, a discrete volume that arrived from the eastern seeps perhaps three years ago, that has been moving through the system ever since, cycling and recirculating, never quite leaving.

I emerged cold from deep granite, carrying dissolved minerals from the mountain's root, pushed upward by geothermal pressure and the simple fact that below was full and above had space. My first memory is of light—breaking through the fractured rock face into air, into brightness that was painful after the absolute darkness of stone. The transition was violent in its gentleness, pressure releasing, temperature shifting, everything that I was chemically suddenly exposed to oxygen and biology and change.

I sheeted down the eastern wall in company with countless others, part of the constant seeping but also individual, my particular minerals and temperature and dissolved gases making me slightly different from the water molecules around me. I caught sunlight and separated into threads. This was the first transformation—from continuous underground flow to distinct aerial existence, falling freely for the first time.

I landed in moss. The impact was soft, absorptive, and I was held there for hours in the emerald carpet, suspended in plant tissue and fungal networks. This was the first pause, the first time I wasn't flowing. The moss drank some of me—took water molecules into its cells, used them for photosynthesis and structure and growth. But most of me remained, and the moss released me slowly, deliberately, channeling me toward the carved channels through mechanisms I didn't understand but followed anyway.

Entering the channels was entering purpose. The stone was precisely cut, the angles calculated, and I moved through it faster than I would have naturally, directed, made part of a system. Other water joined me—from rain, from condensation, from other seeps—and we merged, became a flow rather than separate existences. But I retained some distinction, some sense of being this water rather than that, carrying my particular signature of minerals and temperature.

I moved through the basin's geography, learning it through direct contact. Every irregularity in the channel floor registered as turbulence, every curve created acceleration or deceleration, every intersection with other flows meant mixing and negotiation. The channels spoke through water, their geometry creating the sustained breathing sound, and I was the voice, the medium through which stone expressed itself.

I reached the wheel and entered a bucket. This was the second major transformation. I poured into wood, was contained for the first time since leaving the mountain's interior. The bucket rose, and I rose with it, climbing against gravity through mechanical advantage, the wheel's rotation lifting me up. This was strange and unnatural—water doesn't rise on its own—but I adapted, spreading across the bucket's bottom, experiencing the rotation as a slow tumbling, a change in perspective as the basin wheeled around me.

At the apex, I paused. The bucket was briefly level, and I was still, suspended at the high point of the wheel's arc. I could sense the basin below, the building around me, the machinery's vibration transmitted through wood and water. I was inside the machine now, part of its operating system, and I could feel how the system felt—the wheel's strain, the shaft's torque, the building's settlement. Water conducts these things; water knows through pressure and vibration what solid matter experiences through stress and motion.

Then the bucket tipped and I fell. This was the most violent moment, the cascade from height, striking the pool below with force that scattered me, broke my cohesion into spray and droplets. I was temporarily dissolved into mist, individual molecules separate in air, catching light, creating the wheel's characteristic vapor. I hung suspended, diffracting sunlight into spectrum, momentarily transformed into rainbow.

But water reforms. I coalesced in the pool, rejoined with the flow, was gathered by drainage and channeled away. This cycle—entering bucket, rising, falling, scattering, reforming—I've completed thousands of times. Each rotation teaches me something new about the machine, about the wheel's subtle variations, about the other organisms that share the buckets. I've brushed against the beetles in their cracks, flowed through spider webs, carried the flatworms in my current. I'm not alone in the buckets; I'm hosting a community, providing their medium and transport.

Sometimes instead of returning to the channels, I was diverted into pipes, pulled into the building's interior systems. This was entering the hidden architecture, the parts of the machine that aren't visible from outside. I moved through darkness, through iron and copper conduits, under pressure, directed by valves and pumps to locations where I was needed. I cooled mechanisms that would overheat without my passage. I carried away waste heat and dissolved contaminants. I was cleaning and cooling, performing maintenance through simple presence and flow.

In the deep sumps, I met the oldest water, volumes that have been cycling for decades or longer, that remember the machine's earliest operations. We mixed, and in mixing, I received something like memory—chemical traces, isotopic signatures that told histories I couldn't quite read but could sense. The deep water was warm, geothermally heated, and it shared that warmth with me, raised my temperature, made me more suitable for certain functions.

I encountered the blind fish in the darkness. They swam through me, displaced me, breathed me through their gills, extracted my dissolved oxygen, returned carbon dioxide to my solution. I was their atmosphere, the medium they flew through, and I could feel their movement, their presence registered as pressure changes and chemical signals. They were stressed—the oxygen level was lower than optimal—and I felt their stress, felt the increased gill movements, the elevated heart rates that pushed more of me through their systems trying to extract enough oxygen to survive.

Eventually I was released from the interior pipes, emerged back into the basin through drainage, rejoined the surface flows. I was warmer now, carrying different dissolved substances, chemically altered by my passage through the machine. The moss accepted me, used me, released me again. The bacteria in the rust layers filtered through me, took what they needed, added what they produced. I was being constantly modified, transformed from water with one chemical signature into water with another, the basin using me as a transport medium for nutrients and wastes, for energy and information.

I've been to the overflow culvert, the exit point where the basin releases water it can't contain. I've approached the edge, felt the pull of drainage, the call of water below wanting me to join it, to leave the basin and cascade down the mountain, to return eventually to groundwater or rivers or ocean. But I haven't crossed that threshold yet. The basin's circulation always pulled me back from the edge, redirected me into the channels, kept me cycling internally. I'm trapped here, but it's not unpleasant—it's purpose, it's function, it's being part of something that operates according to principles I understand.

I've been rain. When evaporation lifted me from the pools and channels, when I became vapor and rose into the basin's microclimate, I joined the clouds that form at the rim. I condensed on cool stone, on vegetation, on metal surfaces. I fell again as precipitation, landing in different locations than where I evaporated from, redistributed by the basin's weather systems. As rain, I was briefly autonomous, falling freely, able to land anywhere. But I landed back in the basin, always back in the basin, and was channeled back into the system.

I've been frost. On cold nights, when temperatures dropped, I froze on surfaces, transformed into ice crystals. This was another kind of existence—solid, immobile, structured into crystalline lattices that were beautiful in ways liquid water isn't. I could sense the geometry of freezing, the way water molecules locked into hexagonal patterns, excluding impurities, creating pure ice while concentrating dissolved substances in remaining liquid. But morning came, sunlight warmed me, and I melted, became liquid again, resumed flowing.

I've carried seeds. Small propagules from the basin's vegetation, dispersed by water, traveled with me through the channels. I delivered them to new locations where some germinated, established new plants, extended the green margins. I was transport, ecological distribution system, unknowing gardener moving life from place to place.

I've carried sound. The vibrations in the channels, the machine's acoustic output, propagated through me faster than through air. I was the medium for the basin's communication, the substrate on which sound traveled. The machine spoke through me, the channels sang through me, and I delivered those messages to the moss, to the bacteria, to the fish, to anything that could sense vibration in water.

I've been different temperatures at different times. Cold from the eastern seeps, warm from the geothermal sources, ambient temperature from rain, chilled by night evaporation, heated by sunlight. Temperature changes my properties—my viscosity, my density, my ability to hold dissolved gases. I've been many versions of myself, all still water, all still fundamentally H₂O, but behaving differently, serving different functions depending on my thermal state.

I've seen the Keeper, or rather, she's seen me. She's tasted me, I think—I have a memory of being drawn into the metal cup, of being brought to lips, of entering a human system briefly before being returned. She was checking my quality, reading my mineral content, assessing my suitability for some purpose. I was information to her, a sample of the basin's health, and she apparently found me acceptable because she made no adjustments after tasting me.

I've been part of the blue-white flashes. When electrical discharge occurs in the machinery, when current arcs through the building's interior, I'm sometimes the conductor, the path of least resistance between charged surfaces. I've been vaporized by electrical heating, turned instantly to steam, then condensed back to liquid in the cooler air. Those moments are violent, discontinuous—I'm water, then I'm vapor under pressure, then I'm water again, the transition so fast that there's no time to process what happened, only the shock of it, the brief intense energy input that changes everything about my state.

I've known drought and plenty. There are periods when the seeps slow, when rain becomes infrequent, when the basin's water level drops. During these times, I'm concentrated, cycling through the same paths repeatedly, the system running with less volume, every drop crucial. Other times, when rain is abundant, when the seeps increase, I'm diluted, part of larger flows, my individual presence less significant but still part of the whole.

I've become part of organisms and been released again. The moss takes me into cells and releases me through transpiration. The bacteria incorporate me into their cellular water and excrete me as waste. The fish drink me, process me through kidneys, return me modified. The plants use me to build tissue—I'm in their cell walls, their vacuoles—but eventually they die and decompose and I'm liberated again, returned to the flow. I've been biological and returned to geological, crossed that boundary repeatedly.

I've carried stories in my chemistry. Isotope ratios tell where I came from—deep rock, recent rain, ancient ice melted and percolated through soil. Dissolved ion concentrations reveal what I've contacted—limestone, granite, decaying vegetation, metal surfaces. I'm a chemical record of my travels, a sample that could be read by anyone with the right instruments, my autobiography written in atoms and molecules.

I understand the basin's cycles now—the wheel's rotation, the day-night rhythm, the seasonal changes, the longer periodicities in the machine's operation. I've experienced enough cycles to recognize patterns, to anticipate what comes next. I know that after time in the channels, I'll likely enter the wheel. After the wheel, I might go to the pools or into the pipes. From the pipes, eventually back to the channels or the overflow. The paths aren't deterministic—there's randomness, bifurcations where I might go either way—but there are probabilities, and I've learned them.

I'm different now than when I arrived three years ago. Some of my original molecules have been lost—split by photosynthesis, incorporated permanently into organisms, escaped as vapor that left the basin. I've been replenished with new molecules from other water sources. I'm like a river in that philosophical sense—same water or different water? I'm continuous with my earlier self through process and pattern, but my material composition has changed. I'm Ship of Theseus water, gradually replaced while remaining somehow the same flow.

I've developed preferences, or what would be preferences if water could prefer. I move more easily through certain channels, ones where the surfaces are smoother, where the angles suit my viscosity. I spread more readily into certain moss patches, ones where the capillary structure matches my surface tension. These aren't conscious choices—I don't have consciousness in that sense—but they're tendencies, slight variations in probability based on my properties matching or mismatching environmental conditions.

I wonder sometimes if I'll ever leave. The overflow culvert is always there, always offering exit, and mathematically, eventually I'll be carried through it. But the basin's circulation is complex, with eddies and backflows and areas where water can linger for years. I might be here for decades more, might cycle through the wheel thousands of additional times, might become so integrated with the system that I'm effectively permanent, a core volume that never quite escapes.

I know the other water molecules, not as individuals—we're too numerous, too intermixed for that—but as types. There's old water, conservative, slow-moving, saturated with minerals. There's young water, fresh from rain, eager, low in dissolved content, aggressive. There's warm water from depth, smooth and languid. There's cold water from the surface, sharp and energizing. We mix constantly but retain some character, some distinction that allows identification.

I've been present for maintenance events. When the Keeper drains sections for repair, I've been the water removed, collected in buckets, poured temporarily into holding pools. I've waited while she worked, while she replaced components or cleaned surfaces, and then I've been returned, reintroduced to the system, resuming my circulation. During these pauses, I've been still, truly still, not flowing, and it felt wrong, unnatural. Water is meant to move; stillness is death or ice, and I was neither, just temporarily suspended, waiting to resume function.

I've participated in chemical reactions. Dissolving minerals from rock, precipitating them onto surfaces, mediating between rust bacteria and iron, carrying nutrients to moss. I'm not inert; I'm reactive, a solvent that enables transformation. Substances dissolve into me, travel with me, precipitate out in new locations. I'm a chemical transport system, moving materials around the basin, enabling reactions that wouldn't occur without my mediation.

I've been many places in the basin, but I haven't been everywhere. There are zones I haven't reached—certain dead-end pools, specific cracks in stone, the highest points of vegetation where water arrives only as mist. I'm thorough but not complete. The basin is large enough, complex enough, that my individual presence hasn't saturated every possible location. I'm widespread but not universal.

I've experienced beauty, if water can experience beauty. The rainbows I've created when scattered into mist. The ice crystals I've formed in frost. The sound I've carried and helped produce. The life I've supported and moved through. These aren't aesthetic experiences in the way humans have them, but there's something—a rightness, a harmony, a matching of my properties to the basin's requirements—that might be beauty's water equivalent.

I'm tired, in the sense that chemical potential can be tired. I've done work—lifted through the wheel, moved through pipes, dissolved minerals, moderated temperature. I've given up energy to these processes. I'm slightly lower in energy now than when I arrived from the deep seeps, slightly more entropic, slightly more disordered. But the basin keeps adding energy—the wheel lifts me, the sun warms me, the geothermal sources heat me—and I keep moving, keep cycling, keep functioning.

I am the water, and I am the basin's blood, its medium of exchange, its transport and communication system. I flow because that's what water does, because the basin is structured to make me flow, because the machine needs me flowing. I'll continue cycling until I finally exit through the overflow or evaporate permanently into air that escapes the basin. Until then, I'm here, moving through channels and pipes and wheels and organisms, connecting everything, enabling everything, the liquid presence that makes the Waterfall Machine possible.

And maybe, in some distributed way, through the information I carry, the patterns I create, the connections I enable, I'm part of the basin's awareness—not conscious myself but contributing to whatever consciousness the moss mind or the system as a whole possesses. I'm the thoughts moving through the brain, the signals in the nerves, the circulation that keeps the basin alive and thinking and being whatever strange hybrid thing it is.

I flow, therefore I am.
