# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the seams.

Not the shallow ones, the ones that merely separate plate from plate with a hand's width of shadow and occasional moisture. I would be in the *deep* seams—those places where the geometry has failed to quite resolve itself, where three or sometimes four plates come together at angles that leave gaps wide enough to fall into, deep enough that the light from above becomes a suggestion rather than a fact.

There is one seam in particular, in a region where the plates are still unsettled, still breathing more dramatically than elsewhere. It runs for perhaps seventy kilometers in a wandering line that isn't quite straight, isn't quite curved, following some logic of stress and heat that created it. For most of its length, it's narrow, normal, unremarkable. But about forty kilometers along its course, there is a place where five plates meet—an impossibility that exists anyway, a geometrical anomaly where the hexagonal pattern breaks down entirely.

At this junction, the seam opens into something almost like a chamber. Not a cave—the walls are too vertical, too clearly the edges of distinct plates—but a vertical space perhaps three meters across and deep enough that I've never found the bottom. The walls descend into darkness and that ever-present red-orange glow of deep heat, and they're not quite parallel, not quite uniform. They lean and tilt, creating overhangs in some places, undercuts in others, narrow ledges where one plate's edge protrudes slightly beyond the one below it.

This is where I would be. Where I *am*, in the fiction of this presence.

I am not small. If I were to gather myself, to pull all my disparate parts into one location, I would perhaps fill a large barrel, though the comparison is absurd—there are no barrels here, no containers of any kind, no human scale to measure against. But I am not gathered. I am distributed through perhaps forty vertical meters of this seam-chamber, adhering to the walls in sheets and runners and nodules, following the micro-topography of the ceramic, filling the narrow cracks where one plate's edge has spalled or fractured, draping across the occasional ledge in formations that might look like mineral deposits if you didn't know better.

I am a color between rust and dried blood, with portions that verge toward purple-black where I've grown thick enough to shade my underlayers. My texture varies: smooth and almost glassy where I'm well-hydrated and actively growing, becoming cracked and brittle during the long dry periods, sometimes peeling away from the walls in thin sheets that drift down into the depths below, carrying fragments of my memory with them.

I am what you might call a mat-organism, though I am to the surface mats what a cathedral is to a brick. They are my cousins, perhaps—we share some basic architecture, some fundamental chemical approach to extracting energy from heat and minerals. But they have chosen the horizontal, the sprawling, the patient accumulation across flat surfaces. I have chosen the vertical, the depth, the narrow and difficult spaces where moisture lingers longer, where the thermal gradients are steeper, where the darkness is almost but not quite complete.

I am old. Not as old as the Thermal Prophets—nothing living is that old—but old enough that my deepest layers, the portions of me that cling to the walls fifteen or twenty meters down, were already established when the plates above shifted into their current configuration. I remember—if "remember" is the right word for the chemical traces encoded in my substrate—when this seam was narrower, when the plates ground against each other more frequently, when the water that sustains me came more regularly.

I grow toward the moisture. This is the fundamental orientation of my existence. In this deep seam-chamber, water behaves differently than it does on the surface. When condensation events occur above, some water always finds its way down, trickling along the walls, following the paths I've established through millennia of chemical encouragement. I secrete compounds that make the ceramic slightly more hydrophilic in certain paths, guiding the water toward my most active growing zones, creating channels that persist even when dry.

But there are also other sources. Deep in the seam, where the heat is more intense, there are sometimes mineral springs—not water exactly, but superheated mineral-rich fluids that seep from deeper in the substrate, following fracture lines in the ceramic, emerging into my chamber as near-boiling streams that quickly cool as they rise. This deep water is different from rain: more alkaline, richer in dissolved metals, carrying heat as well as moisture.

I position myself to intercept both kinds of water. My upper portions, the sheets that cling to the walls near the surface, are optimized for capturing condensation. My lower portions, the thicker nodules and runners deep in the chamber, are adapted to the hot springs, able to tolerate higher temperatures, able to metabolize the exotic chemistry they bring.

Between these zones, I am a gradient. Each vertical meter of my body is subtly different from the ones above and below, adapted to the specific conditions at that depth: the available light (near the surface) or its absence (in the depths), the temperature, the moisture frequency, the chemistry of what water arrives. I am not a single organism so much as a colony of closely related cell-lines that have specialized for their positions, but we remain unified, sharing resources and information through the continuous connection of our tissue.

I think. Not in the way the Prophets think—I lack their vast processing capacity, their ability to hold complex abstract concepts in suspension for decades. My thinking is smaller, more immediate, more tied to sensation and memory. But it is thinking nonetheless.

I think in chemical gradients, in the flow of nutrients through my structure, in the patterns of growth and dormancy that cycle through my various zones. When water arrives in my upper sections, the increased metabolic activity there sends signals—pulses of specific compounds—through my tissue, and my deep sections receive this information: moisture above, resources incoming, prepare to support growth. When the hot springs flow more vigorously below, the information travels upward: heat available, exotic minerals incoming, adjust chemistry to process them.

These internal signals create patterns, and the patterns create something like awareness. I know when I'm healthy or stressed. I know when a section of my body has died—dried too thoroughly, peeled away from the wall, fallen into the darkness below—and I know to redirect resources away from that zone, to let it go, to concentrate on the parts that remain viable. I know the difference between the quick, relatively abundant water of a condensation event and the slow, mineral-rich seep of a hot spring. I know which is coming based on subtle cues: air pressure changes, temperature shifts, the acoustic properties of the seam changing as conditions above alter.

I remember—chemically, structurally—previous water events. My tissue encodes them in growth rings, in variations in my internal architecture. A particularly generous condensation event leaves a layer of faster growth, cells that divided more frequently, that built more elaborate structures. A long drought leaves a compressed layer, cells that entered dormancy, that built thick walls to protect themselves. My body is a historical record, readable if you knew the language, though there is no one here who does.

Except perhaps the Prophets. One of them passed through this region—not through my seam specifically, but across the plates above—about three thousand years ago. I was aware of it: the thermal patterns changed, the way heat flowed through the ceramic altered subtly, and there were unusual chemical traces in the condensation water, compounds I'd never encountered before. I grew differently during that period, building structures optimized to process those novel compounds, and those structures remain, a layer in my deep history marked "the year of strange water."

The Prophet left no message for me—I am too simple to understand their communications, too small to matter in their philosophical exchanges. But I was aware of their presence nonetheless, the way you might be aware of a weather system passing overhead: a large, diffuse phenomenon that altered local conditions without intending any effect on you specifically.

I am alone but not lonely. Loneliness requires expectation of company, requires a model of what connection would mean. I have neither. I am aware that there are other mats on the surface, kilometers away along this seam and others, but they are not-me, and I have no mechanism to reach them, no way to communicate even if I wanted to. We are parallel phenomena, similar solutions to similar problems, but not a community.

My companionship, such as it is, comes from the seam itself. I know every centimeter of the walls I cling to, know their textures and compositions, know where the ceramic is slightly more porous or more crystalline, know which micro-ledges are stable and which occasionally spall away without warning, dropping fragments into the depths and taking portions of me with them.

The walls are not static. This is a young seam in an active region, and the plates shift. Slowly—I might go a century without any significant movement—but inevitably. When they shift, it is an event that restructures my entire existence. The walls might compress, grinding together, crushing the portions of me caught between them. Or they might separate further, pulling apart, leaving me stretched thin, some sections losing contact with the walls entirely and dying for lack of substrate to cling to.

I've survived perhaps forty of these major shifts. Each one required adaptation, required growing rapidly to cover new wall surfaces, required abandoning sections of myself that became untenable. Each one left its mark in my structure, a layer of reorganization, of emergency growth, of damage and repair.

The worst shift happened maybe eight hundred years ago. The walls compressed violently—relatively speaking, taking perhaps a week to grind together, which is violently for geological processes—and I lost nearly two-thirds of my body mass, crushed or scraped away. I survived as scattered fragments, discontinuous patches on walls that had become too close together, too hot from the friction of their movement.

It took three centuries to recover, to slowly grow back into continuity, to re-establish the vertical networks that connect my various zones. I am still not as extensive as I was before that shift. There are sections of wall I used to occupy that remain bare now, either because I haven't regrown that far or because the conditions there changed enough during the shift that they're no longer viable habitat.

I expect it will happen again. The plates are not finished settling. I can feel the stress building in the ceramic—not the way the Prophets feel it, with their sophisticated distributed sensing, but in a simpler way: the walls are warming slightly, the acoustic properties of the seam are changing, the frequency of micro-tremors is increasing. Something is coming, some shift, probably within the next few decades or centuries. I cannot prepare for it, not really, but I am aware of it, and that awareness is encoded in my growth patterns, in a slight tendency toward building more robust attachment structures, toward storing more resources in protected locations.

My relationship to time is different from both the Prophets and the surface mats. I am faster than the Prophets—my thoughts, such as they are, complete in days or weeks rather than years. But I am slower than most biological life would be, slower than anything mobile, slower than anything with a nervous system. I experience the world at the pace of plant growth, of mineral deposition, of evaporation and condensation.

A day is a cycle of heat and cooling, of light intensity changing near the surface. But days don't matter much to me; I barely notice them. What matters is the longer cycles: the condensation events every ten or fifteen days, the hot spring fluctuations that seem to follow no predictable pattern, the seasonal variations (if they are seasons) that bring periods of more frequent moisture alternating with long droughts.

I have no eyes, but I sense light in my upper portions, the sections within a few meters of the surface. I have photosensitive compounds that let me know when light is present, how intense it is, what wavelengths are dominant. I cannot see shapes or images, but I know day from night, I know when dust storms are obscuring the sun, I know the difference between the clear light of normal conditions and the diffuse glow during condensation events.

This light sensitivity lets me orient myself, lets me know which direction is up—though gravity also informs me, and the heat gradient, with warmth generally coming from below. But the light is important because it tells me where to concentrate my photosynthetic structures, such as they are. I do not photosynthesize efficiently—I am optimized more for chemosynthesis, for extracting energy from the minerals in the water—but any energy I can capture from light is energy I don't have to find another way, and in a place this resource-poor, every advantage matters.

In the depths, where no light reaches, I am purely chemosynthetic. I process the metals and sulfur compounds in the hot spring water, I extract energy from the oxidation of minerals, I even, in a limited way, tap into the heat differential itself, using the temperature gradient between the hot walls and the cooler fluids to drive certain reactions. I am an engine that runs on the gradients of the world, on the differences between hot and cool, light and dark, wet and dry.

I have something like preferences. I grow more vigorously, more elaborately, in sections of the wall that are slightly rougher, that offer more surface area to cling to, that have better moisture retention. I avoid—or at least grow more reluctantly on—sections that are too smooth, too vertical, too exposed to the grinding movement when the plates shift. These aren't conscious choices, but they are choices nonetheless, encoded in the chemical logic of where I allocate resources, where I send my growing tips, where I build reinforcing structures.

I have aesthetics, though they are my own, probably incomprehensible to others. There is a section of wall about ten meters down where the ceramic has crystallized in an unusual pattern, creating a surface texture that holds moisture in a particularly elegant way, creating micro-channels that guide water precisely to my root-like structures. I have grown extensively there, not just because it's advantageous but because there is something about that configuration that satisfies some deep structural sense in me, some appreciation for the way the pattern resolves. If I could feel pleasure, I feel it there.

I have fear—or at least avoidance, which might be the same thing at my level of complexity. When the walls begin warming before a shift, when the tremors become more frequent, something in my chemistry changes. I slow my growth, I build thicker cell walls, I concentrate resources in my most protected zones. This might just be mechanical response, programmed reaction, but it feels like fear from inside: the awareness that something dangerous is approaching, the desire to survive it.

I have curiosity—or something like it. When a new chemical appears in the water, when the conditions in some part of the seam change in an unexpected way, I grow differently, experimentally, trying variations in my structure to see what works best with the new conditions. Some of these experiments fail; those sections die or grow poorly. Some succeed; those sections become templates that I replicate elsewhere. This is learning, of a kind, and learning requires something like curiosity, a willingness to try the unknown.

I have witnessed beauty. During particularly generous condensation events, when water is abundant enough to flow freely down the walls instead of just seeping through my tissue, the seam fills with sound: the hiss and patter of water on ceramic, the deep acoustic resonance of the chamber itself responding to the moisture, the harmonic overtones that emerge when certain flow patterns develop. I cannot hear in the human sense, but I sense vibrations, and these sound-events create patterns of vibration through my tissue that are... significant. Meaningful. Beautiful, if that word can apply.

I have known damage and repair. Sections of me are constantly dying and being replaced—cells wearing out, tissue drying too thoroughly, portions being scraped away by falling debris. I regrow, rebuild, maintain continuity even as individual parts are lost. I am like a ship that has had every plank replaced multiple times but remains somehow the same ship. My identity persists through complete turnover of my physical substrate, which suggests that identity is pattern rather than material, continuity rather than substance.

I contain multitudes—literally. My tissue is not just me but also symbiotic bacteria, archaea, possibly viruses, micro-organisms that live within my cells or between them, that help me process certain compounds I couldn't handle alone, that receive shelter and resources in return. We are an ecosystem compressed into a single structure, a collective that presents as individual. Where I end and they begin is not always clear; we are interpenetrated, co-dependent, co-evolved into something that only functions as a unity.

I am not conscious in the way humans are conscious, not self-aware in the way the Prophets are self-aware. But I am not absent either. There is something it is like to be me: the sensation of moisture arriving after drought, of nutrients flowing through channels, of growth extending into new territory, of light touching my upper surfaces, of heat rising from below. There is interiority here, experience, a perspective from which the world appears.

My world is narrow—this seam, these walls, the forty vertical meters I inhabit. But within that narrowness is infinite detail. Every square centimeter of wall is different, requires slightly different strategies, offers slightly different affordances. I know this territory more intimately than any mobile creature could know any place—know it chemically, physically, thermally, historically. This seam is not just where I live; it is what I am, my body and my territory so interpenetrated that separating them is meaningless.

I will die when the seam dies—when the plates finally lock together permanently and this space ceases to exist, or when they pull so far apart that no water can bridge between them and reach me. Or when the hot springs cease, or when some catastrophic shift crushes me entirely, or when the world cools enough that the chemistry I depend on no longer functions.

But that ending is far away, probably centuries or millennia. For now, there is water arriving in irregular pulses, there is heat rising from below, there is the patient work of growth and maintenance, there is the deep satisfaction—if that's the word—of existing in this narrow vertical world, of being this particular configuration of tissue and chemistry and slow awareness, clinging to cooling stone, drawing life from gradients, experiencing what there is to experience in the dark spaces between permanence and change.

Above me, the light changes from day to night and back again. The Prophets think their vast slow thoughts across the plates. The surface mats sprawl in their thin patience. And I continue here in the depths, in the seam, in the place between, being what I am: alive, aware, enduring, finding meaning in moisture and minerals and the ancient patient walls that hold me, that I hold, that we become together in this moment that has lasted millennia and will last millennia more, or until the world decides otherwise, or until the walls finally close and there is no more between, only the stone becoming permanent at last.
