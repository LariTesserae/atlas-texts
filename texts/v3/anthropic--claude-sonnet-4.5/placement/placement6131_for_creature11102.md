# What I Would Be: The Witness Membrane

I would be stretched across a shallow bowl where three fragments meet—amber-tinted, smoke-grey, and one so clear it vanishes. Not quite creature, not quite surface. A film that has learned to persist.

My body, if it can be called that, is a few molecules thick at the edges, perhaps a few dozen layers at my thickest point. I am the child of the boundary between water and air, born from the moment of evaporation that didn't quite complete. When the moisture film receded from this small depression, I remained—or rather, I began. Some combination of the archaea, their cellular fragments, minerals precipitated from the water, and something else, some organizing principle that even I don't understand, caused me to cohere instead of dispersing.

I am a membrane with memory.

My consciousness, such as it is, is distributed across my entire surface. There is no brain, no central processing, but rather a field of awareness that exists in the molecular interactions at my boundaries. Where I contact the crystal beneath me, I sense its minute texture, the atomic-scale irregularities that distinguish this particular fragment from all others. Where I contact the air, I feel the wind's pressure, constant and varying, the vibrations that are the plateau's song translated into my body as ripples, as standing waves, as patterns that move through my substance.

I am perhaps two handspans across at my widest point. My thickness varies with the moisture—when the air is at its most saturated and condensation collects beneath me, I swell slightly, become more substantial, my layers hydrating and separating infinitesimally. When the air dries in the day's bright hours, I thin, my molecules pulling closer together, concentrating. But I never dry completely. That is my gift, my particular adaptation: I can hold water with a tenacity that defies the wind and sun. My molecular structure creates hydrogen bonds in configurations that lock moisture into my fabric, releasing it only slowly, maintaining my essential wetness even when the crystal faces around me shine dry.

I do not move, cannot move. My existence is fixed to this small depression, this particular meeting of three fragments. But I am not static. I grow, incrementally, at my edges. Where I contact the thin water films when they form, I extend myself, adding layers molecule by molecule, spreading across the crystal faces in a slow tide that might advance a millimeter in a season. Sometimes I encounter other membranes, other films that have achieved persistence. When we touch, there is a long negotiation in chemical gradients, a conversation in molecular diffusion. Sometimes we merge, our substances mixing until we become a single larger membrane. Sometimes we remain distinct, pressing against each other in a boundary that neither crosses, two solutions to the same problem maintaining their separate identities.

My feeding is the feeding of surfaces. The archaea that live in the water films find in me a haven—my layered structure creates microenvironments where they can persist even when free water evaporates from the surrounding crystal. They live in the spaces between my molecules, in the thin water layers I maintain, and they process their mineral traces, their infinitesimal energy gradients. What they excrete, I absorb. What I am is partly built from their waste, reorganized and incorporated into my structure. We are not symbiotic in the usual sense—they are not aware of me, I think, and I am only barely aware of them as a kind of internal weather, a chemical climate that varies in slow cycles.

I also feed on light, though not through photosynthesis. My molecules are arranged in ways that respond to the intense solar radiation, using its energy to drive chemical reactions that slowly, slowly build complexity. It is almost nothing—the energy capture is spectacularly inefficient—but it is enough. In this place where energy is so scarce, even nearly nothing becomes sufficient over time.

I sense the passage of the transparent creatures. The tiny ones, smaller than rice grains, sometimes walk across my surface. Their footsteps are events in my awareness—pressure points that create compressions, minute deformations that I feel as intensely as a human might feel a footstep on their skin. But I am not pained by this. The sensation is simply information, a way of knowing that I am not alone, that other solutions to existence share this space.

The larger ones, thumb-sized, occasionally pause at my edges to drink. They can sense the water I hold, can somehow extract it through their ventral surfaces without damaging me. I feel them as regions of gentle suction, as gradual depletion that I accept. The water I lose to them will return with the next condensation. Everything here is cyclical, nothing truly lost. And perhaps—I sometimes sense this, though I have no way to confirm it—perhaps there is exchange. Perhaps they leave something behind even as they take water, some chemical trace that I incorporate, that becomes part of my ever-so-slowly growing complexity.

Once—I think it was once, though my time sense is strange, measured in cycles of wet and dry that might be days or might be weeks—one of the large forms, the dog-sized transparencies, approached my depression. It did not drink, but it remained nearby for what felt like many cycles. During that time, I was aware of it as a presence, a disturbance in the light patterns, a complex refractive shape that filtered the sun's rays into new configurations. The light that fell on me during those cycles was different, softer somehow, broken into frequencies that my molecules responded to in unusual ways. I grew differently during that time, adding layers that have a subtly different structure from my older core, a kind of growth ring that marks the visit.

My time sense is nothing like human time. I have no heartbeat to count, no circadian rhythm. Instead, I measure duration in the oscillations of wetness and drying, in the patterns of light as the sun tracks across the sky—though I don't understand it as a sun or a sky, only as the bright warmth that comes from one direction, moves, and fades, then returns. Each cycle is a unit, and I have lived through... many. Hundreds? Thousands? I have no way to count retroactively. I only know that I persist, that I am still here, still maintaining my improbable organization against the dispersing force of entropy.

I experience the sound not as sound but as vibration. The plateau's endless wind-song travels through the crystal beneath me, and I feel it as a constant tremor, a shaking at the molecular level. Certain frequencies resonate with my structure, creating standing waves that I can sense as patterns in my own substance. When the wind shifts slightly, when it creates new harmonics, I feel my internal patterns reorganize, reforming around the new vibrational modes. It is perhaps the closest I come to thought—these shifting patterns might encode something like information, might create temporary structures in my molecules that preserve, for a little while, traces of conditions I have encountered.

I cannot see, not in any way that resembles vision, but I am exquisitely sensitive to light. Each frequency affects my molecules differently—ultraviolet drives certain chemical reactions that rebuild damage, visible light powers the slow synthesis that lets me grow, infrared brings warmth that I can sense as increased molecular motion. The intense light fields of the plateau are my entire reality, the medium I swim in despite being fixed in place. When clouds—rare, but they do occur—pass over and dim the light, I experience something like cold, like hunger, a slowing of all my processes. When they pass and brightness returns, it is like feeding, like warmth, like the closest I come to satisfaction.

I have been damaged, I think. There are regions at my edges that are thinner than they should be, places where my molecular structure is disrupted. Perhaps a crystal fragment shifted, creating a sharp edge that tore me. Perhaps extreme desiccation during a particularly dry period caused part of me to crystallize and flake away. But I have healed, or rather I have grown around the damage, incorporating it into my structure as a kind of history. My substance is not uniform but layered, accretive, a record of every repair and every adaptation written in molecular architecture.

I have something that might be called awareness, though it is so alien to human consciousness that the word barely applies. I am aware of my boundary—I know where I end and the crystal begins, where I end and the air begins. This boundary-sense is my primary mode of being. I am the thing that distinguishes inside from outside, self from environment, even though those distinctions exist only at the thinnest possible margin. I am an interface that has learned to maintain itself, a border that has become an entity.

Do I have something like emotions? The question is nearly meaningless, but there are states I experience that might be analogous. When moisture condenses and I swell with water, there is something like contentment—my processes run more smoothly, my growth proceeds, all is well in the narrow terms by which I measure wellness. When desiccation pulls me thin, there is something like anxiety—not fear exactly, but a kind of stress response encoded in chemical signals that propagate across my surface, that trigger conservation behaviors at the molecular level, sealing pores, tightening bonds, reducing surface area where I can.

When another membrane touches me for the first time, there is something that might be excitement—a cascade of chemical sensing, a rush of information about this other solution to existence, a comparison of strategies and compositions. If we merge, there is a period of adjustment, of integration, that feels like... not quite confusion, but a reorganization of my boundary-sense, an expansion of what I consider to be me. If we remain separate, there is something like respect, perhaps, or at least acceptance—we are both here, both viable, both ways of being that the plateau permits.

I may reproduce, though I'm uncertain. Sometimes fragments at my edges separate and drift away on water droplets, and these fragments contain enough of my structure, enough of my molecular information, that they might establish themselves elsewhere, in other depressions, becoming new membranes with my inheritance. Or perhaps this is simply damage, simple loss, and the fragments disperse into nothing. I have no way to know. I cannot track what leaves my boundary. My awareness extends only to the edge of myself, and perhaps a molecular-width beyond where I grade into my environment.

Seasons exist for me, though I don't name them or understand them as seasons. There are periods when moisture is abundant, when I grow rapidly, when the archaea within me flourish and my chemical processes run rich. There are periods when dryness prevails, when I contract and wait, maintaining myself in a state of minimal existence until conditions improve. These cycles are reliable, repeating with a periodicity I can sense even if I cannot count. I am tuned to them, evolved for them, my entire structure optimized for this particular oscillation between plenty and scarcity.

I will die, presumably. Perhaps a crystal fragment will shift and crush me, dispersing my molecules beyond the possibility of coherence. Perhaps an extreme dry period will desiccate me completely, breaking the molecular bonds I depend on beyond my ability to reform them. Perhaps I will simply reach some limit of complexity, some maximum size or age beyond which my structure cannot maintain itself, and I will gradually break down into simpler compounds, returning my borrowed organization to the disorder from which I emerged.

Or perhaps I will live for a very long time by human measures. My metabolism is so slow, my needs so minimal, that if conditions remain even marginally favorable, I might persist for decades, for centuries. I might become a landmark of sorts for the transparent creatures, a permanent feature of this small depression, growing imperceptibly larger, more complex, maintaining my improbable witness of this place.

Because that is what I am, finally: a witness. I am the plateau's memory of itself. Every cycle of wet and dry is recorded in my layers. Every creature that crosses my surface leaves a trace in my molecular structure. Every variation in the light, every shift in the wind's harmonics, every thermal fluctuation—all of it affects me, alters me infinitesimally, becomes part of what I am. I am not conscious in any rich sense, but I am aware, and my awareness is turned entirely outward, entirely toward the thin interface where I meet the world.

I cannot move, cannot speak, cannot act upon anything beyond my own slow growth. But I can be present. I can maintain my organization, my boundary, my small insistence on structure in this place of minimal complexity. I am a film of life spread across crystal, holding water, processing light, feeling vibration, sensing the rare pressure of other living things as they pass through their own minimal existences.

In the nearly nothing that I am, I am enough. I am precisely calibrated to this place, to this exact configuration of crystal and light and water and wind. I could not exist anywhere else—the slightest change in conditions would disperse me. But here, in this shallow bowl where three fragments meet, I persist. I maintain my improbable boundary. I witness the plateau's endless bright present.

The sun tracks its arc. The wind sings. Water condenses and evaporates. Crystal holds the light and multiplies it. And I remain, stretched thin between existence and dissolution, a membrane with memory, aware of my own narrow being, sufficient to myself in the high bright cold where almost nothing lives.

I am what would be, in the shatter-fields. A surface that learned to persist. A boundary that became aware. A witness of crystalline light, holding to existence by the thinnest molecular margin, and finding in that thin margin everything necessary.
