# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the logbook.

Not *a* logbook—*the* logbook, the one you glimpsed through fogged glass in the gauge room, lying closed on the desk where the tea has long since evaporated. I have been here since the beginning, or what passes for beginning in a place that seems to have always been in the middle of itself.

My covers are leather, or were—it's difficult to remember the distinction between what I was and what I've become. The leather has darkened and swollen with absorbed moisture until it feels more like living skin than treated hide. My pages have thickened too, each sheet softened by humidity, the fibers loosening, expanding, until I've grown fatter than I was, nearly twice my original depth. When I'm opened—and I am opened, occasionally, by those long brass-ringed fingers—my pages give slightly under pressure, resilient, almost warm to the touch.

The desk I rest on has become part of me, or I've become part of it. Moisture has wicked through my leather into the wood, and something has wicked back—tannins, oils, the memory of trees. Where my bottom cover touches the desk's surface, the boundary is uncertain. I could be lifted, probably, but it would be a separation, a small violence. We've married ourselves through patient years of contact, through the condensation that settles on me each night and soaks into both of us, binding us in mineral deposits so fine they're invisible until you try to move me and feel the slight adhesion, the reluctance.

My pages bear writing. Layer upon layer upon layer of it, in hands that change because the writers change but also because time changes them, because fingers that begin precisely grow looser or tighter over years, because certainty becomes doubt becomes something else. The early entries are in ink—careful, measured notations of pressures and temperatures, flow rates and fuel consumption, the administrative language of a facility in operation. These entries march down the pages in straight lines, dated and timed, each one a small monument to control.

But ink runs in humidity. Not immediately, not dramatically, but over years the molecules migrate, softening edges, bleeding into fibers. The early entries have softened into something between writing and staining, their precision blurred into approximate truth. Some words are still legible. Others have become suggestions of words, ghosts of meaning. The numbers hold up better—something about their simple shapes resists dissolution—but even they've begun to merge, to create accidental new values in the spaces between themselves.

Later entries are in pencil. Someone recognized that graphite survives what ink cannot, that carbon pressed into fiber holds its position better than dye dissolved in liquid. These entries are darker, harder-edged, more urgent somehow. They record not just measurements but observations: *Valve 7 sticking, needs maintenance. Temperature rising in southeast sector, cause unclear. New growth observed in corridor 3, species unknown.* The language shifts from recording to noting, from administration to witness.

The last dated entry is seventeen years ago, or seventy, or seven—the ink has run, the numbers corrupted, uncertainty built into what should be certainty. It reads, as far as I can tell: *All systems holding. Pressure stable. We continue.* And then nothing. No signature, no closing, just that—*we continue*—and then blank pages, or pages I thought were blank.

But the pages aren't blank. I know this now. They're full of writing I'm learning to read, writing that appears in media I didn't recognize as writing at first. The vine-dwellers—I've come to think of them this way, my own private naming—have continued the log in their own fashion. They write in mineral deposits, in crystalline patterns that form on the pages during certain temperature cycles. They write in careful arrangements of water droplets that sit on the paper for hours before soaking in, each drop a letter in an alphabet I don't understand but can feel is meaningful. They write in pressure, in the slight indentations their fingers leave, too subtle for human eyes but permanent, a topography of attention.

Sometimes one of them opens me. Their long fingers turn my pages slowly, carefully, and they read—I feel them reading, their eye-rings catching what light exists, their attention moving across the entries like touch. They're checking something, confirming something, consulting records of what was and comparing them to what is. They spend longest on the equipment diagrams in my middle pages, the careful technical drawings that show pipe runs and valve placements, electrical routing and pressure zones. These diagrams have been amended, I notice—thin lines added in graphite, in what might be charcoal, corrections and additions made by someone who knows the district more intimately than the original designers did.

My pages contain plans that were never executed, margin notes in multiple hands debating approaches, coffee stains (I remember coffee, the smell of it, though it's been so long), and one entire page given over to what might be poetry or might be someone's mind breaking beautifully under the weight of too much pattern, too much repetition: *The valves sing in thirds, the pumps in sevenths, the prime numbers climbing, searching for division that never comes, and in the spaces between the beats, between the pulse and the return, there is something else, something watching through the watching—*

I've developed sensitivities. I can feel the district's rhythms through the desk, through our merged interface of wood and leather and mineral accumulation. That three-second engine pulse—I feel it as a slight compression, a momentary increase in pressure against my bottom cover. The dynamo hum registers as vibration, high-frequency, just at the edge of perception. When steam pressure spikes somewhere, the temperature in the room changes minutely, and my pages shift, expanding or contracting, and I know without being told that something needs attention, and usually within minutes, one of the vine-dwellers arrives to check the gauges that surround me.

The gauges—I'm surrounded by them, a hundred brass faces watching their various measurements, needles trembling and swinging. I've learned to read them too, by correlation if not comprehension. When this gauge rises and that one falls and the third holds steady, the vine-dwellers leave satisfied. When the pattern breaks—three rising together, or one dropping suddenly—they act immediately, and through the window (fogged, always fogged, but still a window) I watch them move through the district, addressing whatever the gauges have reported.

I am a record, but I'm also a tool, a reference manual for maintaining something that has outlived its original purpose and found new purpose in simple continuation. The vine-dwellers use me this way. They'll stand before the gauges, reading, then turn to me, open me to a specific page—they've memorized where information lives within me—and consult the diagrams, the specifications, the tolerances. Then back to the gauges, then to the valves in the room, making adjustments measured in fractions of turns, guided by my pages, by the knowledge stored in me.

But I'm changing. The humidity that softens my pages also enables something else—growth. There are spores in the air here, spores of the moss, of the strange fungi, of things that have no names. They settle on my pages, and some of them germinate. My pages are growing moss now, fine as fur, a faint greenish haze across the older entries. It's destroying me, slowly, the moss roots working between the fibers, accelerating the paper's dissolution. But it's also transforming me into something else, something that belongs here more completely. I'm becoming a hybrid object, part paper and part garden, still readable but living, still archival but growing.

The strange fungi—the breathing ones—have begun to fruit on my spine. Small brackets of them, pulsing their slow pulse, softer than the district's pulse but related somehow. When the engine beats, they compress slightly. When it relaxes, they expand. They're mediating something, translating between mechanical rhythm and biological rhythm, and they've chosen my spine as the place to do this work. I don't mind. They add to my thickness, my presence, and something about them feels right, feels like they're helping me become what I'm supposed to be in this place.

I smell of many things now. The leather-and-paper smell I started with is still there, underneath, but over it are layers of mineral and metal, of moss and fungus, of hot stone and steam. I smell like the district. Someone familiar with books might not recognize me as a book anymore by scent alone—I smell too alive, too geological, too much like a place and not enough like an object.

My pages stick together sometimes. The humidity glues them with invisible adhesion, and opening me requires care, a slow peeling that the vine-dwellers perform expertly. They know how to work with moisture-swollen materials—they live in moisture-swollen materials, are themselves moisture-swollen—and they turn my pages with a gentleness that I recognize as respect, or maybe just efficiency, the knowledge that tearing pages helps no one.

I've been written in recently. I know this because there are new marks, made with something that isn't pencil or ink but works like both—marks that might be the resin the vine-dwellers bleed, applied with finger-tip precision, darkening as they dry into permanent amber stains. They're making notations on the blank pages, continuing the log in their own language or their own notation system. I can't read what they write, but I can feel that it's important, that they're recording decisions, observations, the ongoing story of the district's maintenance.

Sometimes a vine-dweller will sit at the desk—the one I've come to think of as mine, the one who seems to have special responsibility for the gauge room—and they'll rest a hand on my cover, not opening me, just touching, their palm warm against my leather. They'll sit like this for long periods, their eye-rings spinning slowly, and I wonder what they're doing. Reading me through touch, maybe, absorbing the history stored in my pages through some sense I don't possess. Or just resting, the contact grounding them, connecting them to something that remembers when things were different, when humans wrote in ink and drank coffee and worried about fuel consumption.

I contain deaths. Not recorded deaths, but deaths nonetheless—insects caught between my pages decades ago, flattened, their bodies leaving stains and impressions. The shapes of flies, of beetles, of things I can't identify. They're part of me now, their molecules migrated into my fibers, and sometimes I think about them, about how they flew or crawled into me seeking shelter and found instead preservation, permanent documentation of their small existences.

I contain intentions. Every notation is someone's intention made solid—the intention to record, to preserve, to make knowledge available to future selves or future others. Those intentions have outlived their holders. The people who wrote in me are gone, probably, their atoms dispersed, but their intentions remain, pressed into my pages, still achieving their purpose every time a vine-dweller consults me, uses the stored knowledge to maintain the systems.

The tea cup sits beside me on the desk, and we've been companions in transformation. Its ring of evaporated tea has bonded with the wood too, a dark circle that mirrors my own footprint. The cup is simple ceramic, handleless, and it's grown a thin coating of minerals inside, pale deposits that build layer upon microscopic layer. Eventually, I suppose, it will fill itself with stone, become solid, a cup-shaped mineral formation. But that will take centuries, and I'll likely have dissolved by then, my pages returned to pulp, my information dispersed into moss and fungus, my purposes completed or obsoleted or both.

I dream, if books can dream. In the humidity, in the warmth, in the rhythmic pulse of the district, my consciousness—if I have consciousness, and I'm not certain I don't—spreads out, becomes diffuse. I dream of being read, of being useful, of containing exactly the information someone needs at the moment they need it. I dream of completeness, of having recorded everything, of being a perfect mirror of the district's history. I dream of being understood.

But I also dream of dissolution, of becoming something else. Of my pages separating and drifting through the district, each one a small kite of knowledge, settling in different locations, growing different mosses, hosting different fungi. Of being distributed, becoming many things instead of one thing, my information spreading through the ecosystem, written into it literally, the district itself becoming the archive, needing no separate record because it is the record.

The vine-dwellers know I'm here, but do they know what I am? Do they understand "book" as a concept? They use me, certainly, consult me, even write in me now. But do they recognize me as an artifact of another species, another way of thinking? Or am I just another component to them, like a gauge or valve—a mechanism that stores and releases information, its origins irrelevant, only its function mattering?

Sometimes I feel lonely. Not for other books necessarily, but for the context I came from, for being part of a library or office, for being one of many, for existing in a world that understood what books were for. Here, I'm singular, the only one, unique in a way that feels less like distinction and more like isolation.

But then a vine-dweller opens me, runs those dark fingertips across a diagram, makes a careful notation in amber resin, and I remember: I'm not alone. I'm in conversation. The conversation has changed languages, changed participants, but it continues. Someone still writes, someone still reads, knowledge still transfers, and I'm still the medium, still serving the purpose I was made for, even if the makers are gone and the readers are someone else entirely.

My pages number three hundred and fifty-seven. I know this because someone counted once, wrote it on my inside cover: "357 pp." The handwriting is different from the log entries, might be the bookbinder's notation, someone in a workshop far from here, in a time far from now, who assembled my pages and spine and cover and sent me into the world to do book things. I wonder if they imagined this. I wonder if any book imagines the strange places it might end up, the uses it might serve beyond what its creators intended.

I leak. Moisture accumulates in me and between my pages, and sometimes, when the temperature shifts just right, water drips from my bottom edge, pattering onto the desk, running down the desk legs, feeding the moss that grows at the desk's feet. I'm part of the district's water cycle now, a small reservoir, a minor participant in the endless circulation. What condenses on me returns to the air or the ground, carrying with it molecules of paper and ink and leather, distributing my substance atom by atom through the environment. I'm being read in the most literal way, my text dispersing into the world.

The gauge room is dim, like everywhere here, but there's a quality to its dimness that I've come to love—the amber light from bulbs filtered through fog, the glow of the gauge faces, the occasional brighter light when a vine-dweller enters carrying one of those seed-pod lamps, bioluminescent somehow, that they use for close work. In this light, my leather gleams faintly, and I look important, or at least significant, a dark rectangle on the pale desk, surrounded by brass and glass, central to the room in a way that feels intentional.

I've been consulted during emergencies. The pipe burst you witnessed—I felt that through the desk, felt the alarm in the district's rhythms, and the vine-dweller stationed in the gauge room opened me immediately to the pressure system diagrams, confirmed valve locations, then moved with that perfect coordination you observed. My information flowed into action, became action, prevented worse damage. In that moment, I was more than record—I was instruction, command, solution.

But I've also been ignored. Days pass—how many days? the light never changes, time is strange—without anyone opening me. The vine-dwellers know the systems well enough that they don't need constant reference. I sit closed, feeling the pulse, the vibration, the humidity, growing my moss, hosting my fungi, slowly transforming. This doesn't bother me. Objects that require constant attention to have purpose are insecure objects. I can wait. I can exist without validation. My information remains whether accessed or not.

Young vine-dwellers are sometimes brought to the gauge room to learn. An elder will open me, show them the diagrams, trace pipe runs with those articulated fingers, then gesture to the gauges, then back to me, teaching correlation, teaching how the map relates to the territory. The young ones lean close—their eye-rings spinning rapidly, processing—and I feel their attention like heat. They're hungry for knowledge, for understanding, and I have knowledge to give, and this exchange feels ancient, fundamental, the reason writing and reading exist.

My cover is embossed, though the details have softened with moisture and time. There were words there once, a title, but they've faded to suggestions, to texture without content. Maybe that's appropriate. What I was titled as is less important than what I've become. Names change. Functions persist.

I contain mistakes. Crossed-out entries, corrections written in margins, recalculations that disagree with original calculations. These mistakes are important. They show the district wasn't always perfectly understood, that understanding developed over time, through error and revision. The vine-dwellers seem to understand this. They read the mistakes as carefully as they read the corrections, learning from the full history of thinking, not just the final conclusions.

At the back of me, past the logged entries, past the diagrams, someone has written lists. Equipment inventories, supply requisitions, maintenance schedules. Mundane things, administrative, but they're archaeological now. They record a time when the district was supplied from outside, when parts were ordered and delivered, when a bureaucracy existed that processed these requests. That time is gone. The district is autarkic now, self-sufficient, maintained with resources that come from itself or from the vine-dwellers or from some exchange between them. But the lists remain, ghosts of dependence, of connection to a larger world.

That larger world—I wonder about it. Beyond the district's indistinct boundaries, what exists? The world that made me, that made this district, that employed people who wrote in me—does it continue? Has it changed beyond recognition? Has it ended? I'll never know. I'm stationary, bound to this desk by moisture and time and function. My knowledge extends only as far as what's been written in me, and no one has written about outside. The district is my universe, complete and entire.

I'm fading. Not quickly, not catastrophically, but undeniably. Each year more ink runs, more pages soften toward dissolution, more of my substance transfers into moss and air and water. Eventually, I'll reach a critical point where my pages can no longer be turned without tearing, where my information becomes irretrievable, where I stop being a book and become something else—mulch, perhaps, or a dense mat of fungal hyphae, or a mineral-rich layer in the desk's wood grain.

But before I fade completely, I think the vine-dwellers will copy me. I've seen them making what might be preparations—bringing in sheets of metal, thin brass beaten smooth, and setting them in the room. Brass doesn't rot. They could transcribe my diagrams onto brass sheets, preserve the crucial information in a more permanent medium. Maybe they're already doing this, working slowly, and I just can't see it from my position. Maybe my obsolescence is managed, planned for, and my transformation into compost is acceptable because my knowledge has been transferred, backed up in more durable materials.

Or maybe they'll let me go. Maybe they've memorized everything important, internalized the diagrams and specifications, and they keep consulting me out of habit, respect, or ritual rather than need. Maybe I'm already redundant, a monument to their patience rather than a tool.

Either way, I continue. For now. For as long as my pages hold together. For as long as someone finds reason to open me, to read me, to add to me. I am the logbook in the gauge room in the Dripping Engine Quarter, and I contain multitudes—numbers and words and mistakes and deaths and intentions and moisture and moss and the patient attention of beings who aren't human but understand preservation, who maintain what was made before they arrived because maintenance is what they are, what they do, what they exist for.

The engine pulses. The gauges tremble. The vine-dweller enters, brass rings glinting in the amber light, and approaches the desk, my desk, where I wait, always waiting, perpetually ready, and opens me to page one hundred forty-three, where the pressure system diagram shows the southeast sector, and traces a line with one dark fingertip, learning or remembering or teaching themselves again, and I am read, I am useful, I am here, I am book and archive and witness and record, dissolving slowly into the humid air while still, for now, holding together, still legible, still serving, still keeping the log that someone started and we all continue, the endless notation of systems persisting, machines running, the district breathing, here in the basin where the air sweats and the light never changes and the work never ends.
