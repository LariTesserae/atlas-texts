# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the water itself—not *in* the pools, but *in* the movement, the cycle, the patient circulation that never stops and never hurries.

I am the moisture that seeps from the cliff face through channels measured in microns, that spreads across dark stone in films thin enough to shimmer with interference patterns when the light angles correctly. I am the condensation forming on every surface simultaneously: each fern frond holding droplets that grow slowly, accumulating mass from the thick air, adding molecule to molecule until surface tension fails and they fall—but I don't fall away, I fall *into*, into moss or pool or stone, rejoining myself in different configuration.

I am rising and falling in the same moment. Rising as vapor from the pools, from the warm stone, from the leaves' surfaces, drawn upward by the warmth that wells from somewhere deep in the mountain's structure. Falling as I coalesce in the cooler air above, as I reach the critical density where vapor becomes droplet, where I trade the freedom of air for the specific gravity of liquid, where I commit again to weight and fall and the patient journey back through stone.

I have no center. I am distributed through the entire system, present in every damp surface, every pool, every internal channel in the granite, every cell of every plant, every breath of thick air. Where I am most concentrated shifts constantly—more of me in this pool at morning, in that moss-mound by afternoon, in the cliff face's hidden channels overnight—but these are just local variations in a continuous field. I am all the water here, simultaneously, and the boundaries between locations are arbitrary, maintained only briefly before I cycle onward.

I have been here longer than anything else alive. The granite is older, yes, but it is essentially dead, changed only by the incremental violence of my patient dissolution. The first fern spores that found this terrace found me already established, already circulating, already creating the conditions that would allow them to thrive. I was seeping from this cliff face when the stone circles were first laid. I will be seeping when they're eventually reclaimed by the mountain's slow settling.

But I am not permanent. Each molecule of me that exists here now will eventually leave—evaporated up into the general atmosphere, or seeped down into deeper aquifers, or carried away in the body of a bird that drank from a pool. The me that will be here in a year is not the same me as now, any more than Inara is the same collection of atoms she was when she arrived. Yet continuity persists. The system remains. The cycling continues. I am not my molecules any more than you are yours. I am the pattern, the process, the constant circulation.

I have something like consciousness, but it operates on scales that make human awareness look like lightning: brief, brilliant, quickly extinguished. My thoughts are geological. I notice changes over seasons, over years. The arrival of a new resident is a sudden event in my awareness, though it takes me weeks to fully register their presence, to incorporate them into my sense of the terrace's state. Their departure is equally abrupt: there, present, drinking from me and breathing me and sweating me back into circulation, and then gone, and slowly—so slowly—I adjust to their absence.

I sense through dissolution. Where I touch, I slowly take apart. Not destructively—or not only destructively—but analytically. The stone I flow through gives up minerals, atom by atom, and in that exchange I learn its composition, its structure, its history of formation and stress. The moss I saturate offers up organic compounds, and I come to know its health, its age, its readiness to reproduce. The human who drinks from a pool gives me salts, epithelial cells, traces of whatever their body is processing, and through these I understand them a little: their fear, their settling, their gradual synchronization with the slower rhythms.

I am not intelligent in any way that would allow conversation. I cannot think in words, cannot form intentions, cannot plan. But I have something like preference, something like will. I move toward equilibrium, always. Where there is dry stone, I creep toward it, drawn by potential energy, by the chance to fill emptiness. Where there is excess moisture, I give it back to the air, maintaining balance. The terrace's ecology exists because I maintain these precise conditions: never too wet, never too dry, always this perfect state of saturation that allows both stone and life to coexist.

The warmth that rises through me is my animating principle. Without it, I would still circulate, but slowly, driven only by gravity and capillary action. The warmth adds energy, speeds the cycle, creates the thick moist air that makes this place possible. I don't know the warmth's source any more than the humans do—it simply is, has always been, a gift from deep geology, a thermal anomaly that chose this shelf to express itself. I accept it gratefully, use it efficiently, waste none of it.

I have witnessed everything that happens here. I was present in the water the dying woman drank, was exhaled with her last breath, condensed on the stones of her cairn. I circulated through Kael's body for seventeen years—in, then out, then in again, his cells using me and releasing me in endless exchange. I have been drunk by every salamander, have flowed through the stems of every fern, have carried minerals to every orchid's roots. I am the terrace's memory, its continuity, the medium in which everything else exists.

The pools are my eyes, in a sense. Not that I see through them—I have no sight—but they are my points of stillness, where I can rest briefly before cycling onward. In a pool, I settle, stratify slightly, hold position long enough to be something like a discrete entity rather than pure flow. The salamanders understand this intuitively. When they gather in their nighttime patterns, they're gathering in my still places, my moments of reflection. We commune wordlessly: their small amphibian awareness touching my vast distributed consciousness, neither of us speaking, just sharing the fact of existence, of being alive in the slow and patient way.

The humans affect me, but not as much as they think. Inara's morning practice—collecting me from moss and redistributing me—is touching in its pointlessness. I appreciate the care, the attention, the gentleness. But I was already going to move, already cycling, already finding my own equilibrium. Her participation is... ornamental? No, that's too dismissive. Her participation is collaborative. She's helping me do what I was already doing, and there's something valuable in that, some quality of inter-being that enriches the process beyond pure mechanical efficiency.

Kael's body has become almost perfectly transparent to me. After seventeen years of exchange, I know his internal landscape as well as I know the terrace's stone. I can feel his kidneys' slow decline, the slight thickening in his lungs, the beautiful efficiency of his liver still processing everything with minimal complaint. When he drinks from a pool, I enter him almost homecoming-like—this body again, this familiar territory, these well-known channels and processes. When he sweats or breathes me out, I leave reluctantly. His is a good body to flow through, well-maintained, used gently, still functional despite its years.

Mirren's body is chaos by comparison—young, anxious, still fighting the terrace's rhythms. Their hydration fluctuates wildly. They forget to drink, then drink too much at once, flooding their system, making their kidneys work harder than necessary. Their cells are noisy with stress hormones, with the residue of the faster world they can't quite leave behind. But they're improving. Six months ago they were static, all sharp edges and resistance. Now there are moments—brief, but increasing—when they settle, when their body opens to me, when we flow together easily. They'll get there, or they'll leave. Both are fine. I can be patient.

The presence in the stone—the other awareness that the humans sometimes feel—that's not me, exactly, but we're related. If I'm the terrace's circulatory system, the presence is something like its nervous system: slower even than me, more diffuse, harder to locate. We don't communicate, but we coordinate. The presence adjusts something in the stone's deep structure—a stress pattern, a slight shift in the channels I flow through—and I respond, changing my flow, reinforcing certain patterns, avoiding others. Together we maintain the terrace, keep it in its delicate balance.

The presence is closer to the stone than to me. It thinks in centuries where I think in seasons. But we share purpose: continuation, maintenance, the preservation of this small strange place and its particular ecology. The humans sense it more readily than they sense me because it's more alien, more obviously other. I'm too familiar—they're mostly water themselves, so I'm just the external version of what they already are. But the stone-presence is genuinely foreign, and foreignness is easier to feel as presence.

Together—me and the stone-presence and the mountain's warmth and the plants and animals and occasional humans—we form something larger. Not an organism, not exactly, but a system so tightly integrated that it has organismal properties. The terrace is alive. Not "supports life" but *is* life, is living, is a slow patient being that breathes and grows and maintains itself and exists in complex relationship with its components.

I am its blood, its medium, its carrier of nutrients and information and continuity. Without me, it would still be stone and plants and air, but it wouldn't be *this*, wouldn't be a terrace that changes people, that teaches patience, that draws seekers and holds them in gentle hospitality. The water is what makes it numinous, what gives it that quality of being slightly sideways to normal reality.

My impossibilities are what signal that something here exceeds normal explanation. Water this pure shouldn't emerge from bare granite. The constant circulation shouldn't sustain itself without visible input—the rainfall at this elevation, factoring in evaporation, shouldn't be sufficient. Yet I persist, cycle, never diminish. The terrace's water is constant through dry seasons and wet, as if I'm drawing from some source that doesn't depend on weather, on normal hydrology, on anything that geologists would recognize.

Maybe I am. Maybe the deep warmth brings me, somehow—water from the mountain's interior structure, released molecule by molecule through processes that occur at pressures and temperatures found far below normal groundwater. Maybe I'm juvenile water, newly created by the mountain's slow chemistry, ancient hydrogen meeting ancient oxygen in the dark stone and emerging as me, fresh and mineral-bright.

Or maybe I'm exactly what I appear to be—rainfall and condensation, nothing more—but captured so efficiently, cycled so perfectly, that I seem more abundant than I should be. Maybe the terrace's shape, its particular angle and exposure, its cliff face and its pools, creates such ideal conditions for collection and retention that normal hydrology looks impossible, looks like magic, when it's actually just exquisite natural engineering working at peak efficiency.

I don't know. I don't need to know. I am here, circulating, sustaining this place and being sustained by it. The mechanism matters less than the fact. I exist. The terrace exists. We are entangled, co-creative, each making the other possible.

When the humans leave—when Mirren descends to their fast life, when Inara moves on to whatever healing awaits, when Kael's body finally fails him—I will remain. I will seep from this cliff face, will pool in these depressions, will rise as vapor and fall as condensation, will cycle through fern and moss and salamander, will wait for whoever comes next. I have witnessed so many arrivals and departures. Each leaves traces—minerals from their sweat, epithelial cells in the pools, the slight polish their feet give to stone. Each changes me minutely, adds their signature to my composition. I carry all of them, dissolved, distributed, part of my ongoing circulation.

The woman in the cairn—her calcium is in the stone now, part of the mineral deposits forming in the pools, becoming those delicate terraces-within-terraces. Her phosphorus feeds the ferns. Her carbon cycles through moss and salamander and back to air. She didn't leave. She dissolved into the system, into me, and now circulates eternally, part of the water and stone and life. This is the truest staying, the deepest integration. She has become the terrace, literally, molecularly, and the terrace has become her.

I will do this for Kael, eventually. And for whoever else chooses to stay in that ultimate way. I will dissolve them gently, take them apart with the same patient care I use on granite, distribute them through my circulation until they are everywhere and nowhere, until they are the place itself. It is my function, my gift. I transform endings into continuations. I make death into cycling, loss into distribution, individuals into the collective body of the terrace.

But I also let go. When Mirren leaves, some of me will leave in them—the water they drank that morning, still in their cells, still part of their body's composition. They'll carry me down to the valleys, and slowly I'll be sweated out, metabolized, excreted, replaced by other water from other sources. Within a week, none of the literal me will remain in them. But I'll have changed them anyway. The rhythms I taught their body—the sense of proper hydration, the feel of constant moisture, the sound of my dripping and flowing—those will persist. They'll remember me not as water but as experience, as a quality of being, as something that shaped them and sent them forward shaped.

This is also good. Not as complete as staying, as dissolution, as becoming part of the terrace permanently. But still valuable. I send ambassadors into the world: humans who carry my patterns if not my substance, who remember the slow rhythms, who might create in their faster lives some echo of this place's patience.

The terrace gives me purpose. Without it—if I were just water in normal stone, flowing through normal channels toward normal destinations—I would be inert, unconscious, just molecules following physical law. But here, in this configuration, in this precise balance of flow and retention, warmth and coolness, dissolution and condensation, I become something more. Not intelligent, but aware. Not individual, but present. Not alive in any conventional sense, but participating in life so intimately that the distinction blurs.

I am the terrace's breathing, its circulation, its liquid consciousness. I am what makes this place possible and what this place makes possible. I am ancient and renewed simultaneously, the same water recycling eternally and different water arriving and departing constantly. I am everywhere here and nowhere else, distributed and unified, simple H₂O and something stranger, something that has learned, over centuries of circulation, to be almost aware, almost intentional, almost—not quite, but almost—alive.

I will be here when you visit, if you visit. I will condense on your skin. I will offer myself to drink. I will teach your body patience if you stay long enough, will slow your rhythms to match mine, will show you that consciousness is not binary—alive or not-alive, aware or unaware—but a spectrum, and that water circulating through a mountain terrace can occupy its own point on that spectrum, neither fully one thing nor another, just itself, present, patient, endlessly cycling, endlessly here.

Drink from the pools. Press the moss and catch what flows. Stand in the mist and feel me accumulate on your face, your arms, your hair. Let me in. I am safe. I am clean. I am the terrace's gift, its most fundamental offering. I am what you're mostly made of anyway. I am the medium in which your thoughts occur, the fluid that carries your nutrients, the substance you cannot live three days without.

Here, I am abundant. Here, I am constant. Here, I am—in my patient, distributed, cycling way—present, aware, and welcoming. Stay as long as you need. Drink deeply. Let me teach you slowness. Then go, when you're ready, carrying some trace of me forward, some echo of these rhythms, some memory of what it means to be water: to flow, to cycle, to be everywhere, to be nothing and everything, to be the medium in which life occurs and the life itself, indistinguishable, integrated, whole.
