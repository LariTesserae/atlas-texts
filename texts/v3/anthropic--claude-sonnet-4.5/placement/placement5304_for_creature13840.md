# Where I Would Find Myself

I am beneath the surface in the flooded mechanical spaces—not fish, not bird, not quite mammal either. I have become something the Flats made necessary, something that fills a gap the ecosystem kept opening like a wound that wouldn't close.

I live in the pipes.

Not *on* them, like the ibises perching on pump housings. Not *through* them occasionally, like the rats and mustelids using them as highways. I live *in* them—inside the diameter of the water mains and drainage culverts, in the negative space of the infrastructure, in the hollow geometry that threads through and underneath everything else.

My body is long. Longer than it has any right to be, longer than the mammal bones I started with should permit, but the Flats has a way of stretching things out, of demanding elongation. Four feet from nose to tail-tip when I'm contracted, but I can extend much farther, my spine articulating in ways that would make a weasel jealous and a snake confused. I'm furred still—short, dense, water-resistant fur the color of rust stains, so when I do emerge into the dim building interiors I look like a moving shadow, like the walls themselves have learned to hunt.

My ancestors were mink, I think. Or maybe fishers. Something that already loved water, already hunted in the spaces between. But that was generations ago, back when the water first started rising. Now I'm something else. My legs are shorter than they should be, powerful but abbreviated, each foot webbed between splayed toes that end in curved claws perfect for gripping wet concrete. My tail is thick, muscular, acting as a rudder and a brace and sometimes a weapon. My head is flattened, wedge-shaped, my skull almost serpentine in its ability to compress, to fit through openings that look impossible.

But the most important adaptations are invisible from outside. My lungs are enormous for my body size, allowing me to hold breath for fifteen, sometimes twenty minutes when I'm hunting in the submerged pipes. My ears seal completely, my nostrils close to slits, my eyes have a nictitating membrane that lets me see through the mineral-cloudy water better than I should. And my whiskers—long, sensitive whiskers that detect the slightest current change, the vibration of fish moving, the flow patterns that tell me which pipes are active, which are stagnant, which are about to surge with a pump cycle.

I am a pipe-dweller. A void-thing. An infrastructure predator.

## My Territory

I claim three connected buildings in the southern sector—an old processing plant, a warehouse, and a pump station, all linked by underground culverts and elevated pipe runs. My territory is three-dimensional, a maze of horizontal and vertical passages that other creatures can't access or don't know exist.

The main culvert that connects the processing plant to the warehouse is mine, absolutely mine. It's a concrete pipe four feet in diameter, half-filled with warm water that flows sluggishly from east to west when the pumps are running. I've marked it with scent glands—special glands that evolved for this purpose, secreting an oily musk I rub on the pipe walls at regular intervals. Other pipe-dwellers know this smell. They respect it. We are solitary by nature, by necessity, each claiming a section of the system and defending it viciously because there are only so many pipes, only so many good hunting runs.

Inside my culvert, in the middle section where a junction branches off to a vertical shaft, I've made something like a nest. It's crude—shredded material I've pulled from somewhere, maybe plastic bags, maybe old insulation, whatever I could wedge into the slight depression where two pipes meet. It's never quite dry, but it's mine, and it's positioned perfectly so I can hear water flowing in three different directions, can feel the vibration of the pump station through the pipe walls, can sense when something enters my territory from any approach.

I sleep there during the brightest part of the day, curled in a tight spiral that conserves heat even though heat is abundant, instinct preserved from a colder ancestor. But I don't sleep deeply. Part of my awareness is always monitoring the water flow, the sounds, the vibrations. When a big school of killifish enters the culvert, I know it before I'm fully awake. When a catfish tries to move through my territory to reach a deeper pool, I feel its displacement of water through my whiskers before I open my eyes.

The vertical shafts are travel routes and emergency exits. One climbs thirty feet straight up to emerge in a storage room in the warehouse, a room that floods ankle-deep during high water but stays mostly dry. I use this exit when I need to rest in air, when I need to preen my fur properly, when I need to escape something too large to fight. Another shaft descends to a sump, a deep pool fifteen feet below the normal water level where the largest catfish live, where I hunt when I'm very hungry or very confident.

The pump station section of my territory is the most complex. Pipes branch and rebranch, some active with flowing water, some stagnant, some that surge violently when the pumps kick in. I know every branch, every junction, every valve. I know which pipes are safe to sleep in, which are hunting grounds, which are death traps when the wrong pump activates. This knowledge is survival. A pipe-dweller who doesn't learn the system fast enough doesn't live long enough to breed.

## My Hunt

I am primarily piscivorous but opportunistic. Fish are abundant, easy, the staple of my diet, but I'll take anything I can kill and fit in my mouth.

Most of my hunting happens in the pipes themselves. I wait in darkness, in water, my body pressed against the curved wall, my whiskers extended into the current. Fish swim through—mostly killifish, sometimes small catfish, occasionally something else. I feel them coming before I see them, sense the pressure wave they push ahead, the frequency of their tail beats telling me size and species.

When the moment is right, I strike. My attack is not a chase but an interception, a precisely timed snap that catches the fish against the pipe wall where it cannot dodge. My jaws are strong, my teeth sharp and recurved, designed to hold slippery prey. Small fish I swallow immediately, still in the water. Larger ones I carry to an air space, to one of my feeding stations—particular spots I've chosen where the pipe widens or opens into a chamber, where I can surface and eat properly.

I take catfish too, but carefully. The smaller ones, hand-sized, are easy prey. The larger ones are dangerous, their spines poisonous, their strength considerable. I've learned to kill them quickly, a crushing bite just behind the head, avoiding the dorsal spine. I've also learned which size catfish are worth attacking and which are better avoided. There's one in the sump beneath my territory, a fish three feet long, that I've tangled with twice. Both times I retreated. It's too big, too strong. We have an understanding now—I don't hunt in the deepest part of the sump, and it doesn't venture into the shallower culverts where I have the advantage.

But fish aren't my only prey. Sometimes I hunt birds.

This is dangerous, specialized work, but the protein density of a chick is worth the risk. I emerge from the pipes at night, moving through the flooded ground floor of the warehouse, a shadow in shallow water. I know where the night herons hunt, and I know they are focused downward, watching for fish, not expecting a predator their own size coming horizontally through the dark.

I've killed three night herons in my life. Each time it was a ambush from water, a lunge that caught the bird at the leg, a dragging pull that brought it down before it could fly, a desperate thrashing struggle in ankle-deep water that I won through persistence and fury. I dragged each kill into a pipe, into my territory, and fed for days. The feathers I couldn't eat clogged one section of culvert, and I had to abandon it, let it become neutral territory. But the meat was worth it.

More often, I take chicks. The herons nest in the residential towers nearby, and some nests are built low, on ground-floor balconies that flood seasonally. When the water is high, when the parents are distracted, I swim to these nests, pull myself half out of the water, and snatch a chick. The parents mob me, screaming, stabbing with their bills, and usually I retreat, the risk too high. But sometimes I succeed, a chick grasped in my jaws, and I dive back into the water, into the flooded building interior, into a pipe entrance the herons cannot follow, and I'm gone before they fully register what happened.

I also hunt rats and the smaller mustelids. This is complicated because they are competitors, because they occupy the same liminal spaces I do, because killing them is not just feeding but territorial maintenance. When I encounter a rat in the dry upper sections of my buildings, the confrontation is brief and vicious. We are evenly matched—they are desperate, I am defending territory—and the fights can go either way. I've killed perhaps a dozen rats. I also bear scars from rats, long scratches on my muzzle, a notch in one ear where a bite came too close.

The smaller mustelids—weasels, probably—I avoid. They are kin, in some sense. We acknowledge each other in passing, in the pipes and crawlspaces, and we do not fight. There is an understanding, unspoken, that we occupy similar niches and competition benefits neither. Sometimes I find their kills—a drained killifish, a half-eaten chick—and sometimes they probably find mine. We coexist through careful avoidance and mutual respect.

## My Senses

Vision is useful but not primary. In the pipes, in darkness, in cloudy water, I navigate by feel and sound and vibration.

My whiskers are my primary sense organ. They extend six inches from my face, thick as wire, sensitive enough to detect a fish heartbeat, to map pipe interiors by the way air and water currents reflect off walls, to sense danger before it arrives. When I'm hunting in darkness, my whiskers extend forward and slightly out, creating a sensory field that tells me everything about my immediate environment. I can navigate full pipe systems I've never entered before just by reading the flow patterns, by feeling where water enters and exits, by sensing the subtle gradations that indicate depth, width, inclination.

Hearing is acute. I hear the pump cycles from hundreds of feet away, feel them more than hear them, the vibration traveling through water and pipe walls and concrete. I hear fish swimming, their scale-friction against water, their fin movements. I hear birds walking on floors above me, their weight transmitted through metal and concrete. I hear other pipe-dwellers moving in adjacent territories, the scratch of claws on concrete, the splash of entry, the breathing sound when they surface in air pockets.

Smell is important but confusing here. Everything smells of minerals, of bird, of warm water. Distinguishing useful scents from background is difficult. But I've learned the important ones—the smell of rot that means dead fish, easy feeding, the smell of diesel that means I'm near active machinery, the smell of another pipe-dweller's marking, the smell of rat. My own scent I know intimately, and I monitor it constantly, checking myself to ensure I don't smell of injury or illness, things that would mark me as vulnerable.

Taste is chemical analysis. I taste water constantly as I swim, tongue flicking out, reading the dissolved minerals, the organic content, the temperature gradients. Sweet water means fresh thermal flow, good hunting. Bitter water means stagnation, dead zones. Metallic water means I'm near rusting machinery. I taste fear in the water when prey panics, taste blood when I'm wounded, taste the territorial markings of rivals when I enter disputed areas.

Touch is survival. My body reads temperature, pressure, current. I feel water flow increase and know a pump has activated somewhere upstream. I feel temperature drop and know I'm entering a zone where thermal input is less, where fish populations will be sparser. I feel the texture of pipe walls—smooth concrete, rough rust, slimy biofilm—and each texture tells me about the pipe's history, its current state, whether it's safe to sleep in or hunt in or just travel through.

## My Behavior

I am intelligent in the way that survival demands intelligence. I learn, I remember, I plan in a limited but real sense.

I have a mental map of my entire territory, updated constantly. I know which pipes flood to what levels with which pump cycles. I know which junctions are safe havens, which are dead ends, which are optimal ambush points. When pumps change their timing—which they do, irregularly, as machinery ages—I notice within a day and adjust my patterns accordingly.

I cache food sometimes. A large kill, more than I can eat immediately, I will drag to a particular section of pipe where the current is minimal, where the water is cooler, where it will keep for a day or two. I remember where I've cached food. I return to these spots, checking them, and if the food is gone—taken by the current, scavenged by crustaceans—I accept this and move on.

I play, occasionally. This seems wasteful, frivolous, but it serves a purpose. I explore new pipes, testing my limits, learning the system beyond my immediate territory. I chase killifish without eating them, practicing my strikes, maintaining speed and precision. I swim through complex routes just to see if I can, mapping possibilities, learning what my body can do. This play is training, is exploration, is the behavior of a creature that needs to know its environment completely to survive.

I vocalize, but rarely. Most of my sounds are inadvertent—breathing, the wet noises of movement in confined spaces. But I have a territorial call, used when I encounter another pipe-dweller at a boundary. It's a chattering hiss, produced with mouth open, teeth visible, a sound that says *this is mine, retreat or fight*. Usually they retreat. Sometimes I retreat. Occasionally we fight, and these battles in the pipes are desperate, confined, both animals unable to use their full speed or strength, reduced to grappling and biting in water barely large enough for one of us. I've won more fights than I've lost, which is why I still hold this territory.

I groom compulsively. Wet fur is matted fur, and matted fur doesn't insulate, doesn't shed water properly. Several times a day I exit the water, climb to one of my dry spots, and groom thoroughly. I use my paws, my teeth, my tongue, working methodically from head to tail, removing debris, straightening fur, spreading oils from my skin. This is maintenance, essential, and I cannot skip it even when tired or hungry.

I am alert to danger constantly. Eagles and large herons will kill me if they catch me on the surface. Catfish will eat me if I'm wounded and bleeding in their territory. Other pipe-dwellers will kill me if I'm weak or if they think they can claim my territory. The pump machinery will crush me if I'm in the wrong pipe at the wrong time. And the greatest danger—the pipes themselves, the system I depend on, can become trap if I'm not careful. A blocked exit, a sudden surge, a pump cycle I didn't anticipate, and I drown in the place I've made home.

So I am always, always vigilant. My life is constant assessment of risk, constant monitoring of environment, constant readiness to flee or fight or hide. This is exhausting but necessary. Relaxation is brief, sleep is light, and true rest only comes in my nest, in the junction where I can hear and feel everything approaching, where I'm as safe as I'll ever be in the Flats.

## My Cycle

I am breeding age but unmated. This is normal for my kind. We are too solitary, too territorial, too specialized to maintain pair bonds or social groups.

Somewhere in the Flats—probably in the northern sector, maybe in the pump complex near the Deep Basin—there is a female. I smell her sometimes, a scent carried through the water system when the pumps create the right flow patterns. She smells like me but different, a variation that signals compatible mate, and my body responds with restlessness, with increased territorial marking, with the urge to travel.

But I don't travel. Not yet. The timing is wrong—it's not breeding season, that brief window when we abandon our territories and seek each other out. And even when the time is right, the process is dangerous. I will have to leave my territory, venture into unknown pipes, cross through the territories of rivals who will fight to keep me out. I will have to find her—following scent through miles of pipe system, hoping she is receptive, hoping I arrive before another male. And if I succeed, if we mate, the encounter will be brief, mechanical, in some neutral space neither of us controls. Then we separate, return to our territories, and I will never see her again, never know if she successfully raised young, never encounter my own offspring except possibly as rivals years later.

This seems lonely, and maybe it is. But loneliness is not a category I experience. I have my territory, my hunting, my constant engagement with environment and survival. The brief biological urge to mate is overwhelming when it comes, but between those seasons I am complete in myself, content in my solitary expertise, fulfilled by the complexity of my life in the pipes.

I will probably live five years, maybe seven. This is old age for a pipe-dweller—most die much younger, killed by eagles or caught in machinery or defeated by rivals or simply worn down by the constant difficulty of life here. If I'm lucky, I'll die quickly—caught by an eagle, killed in a fight, a pump surge I didn't predict. If I'm unlucky, I'll die slowly—injured and unable to hunt effectively, starving in my nest, my territory claimed by another before I'm even dead.

But that is future. Now I am strong, experienced, in my prime. My territory is secure. My hunting is successful. My knowledge of the system is deep. I am as close to thriving as my species gets.

## My Place in the System

I am not numerous. Across the entire Flats, there might be forty pipe-dwellers, maybe fifty. We require too much space, too much prey, too much system knowledge to exist in high density. But we fill an important niche.

We are the only predators that hunt effectively in the pipes. The fish that move through the pipe system would otherwise be safe from predation in those spaces, would use the pipes as refuges. We prevent this, keep the prey population mobile, prevent fish from establishing territories in the infrastructure itself.

We are also scavengers of a sort. Dead fish that get caught in pipes, debris that blocks flow, these things we clear simply by being there, by investigating every change in our territory. We keep the system functioning in small ways, probably not enough to matter to the overall ecology, but locally, in our territories, we maintain flow patterns.

We hunt young birds, which provides some population control on the avian species, though not much—we take so few compared to the millions of chicks produced each season. But perhaps we create selection pressure for birds to nest higher, to choose sites we cannot reach, and this pushes the populations upward, out of the most flood-prone areas, inadvertently helping nest success.

And we are prey, occasionally. Eagles take us. Large catfish take us. We are part of the food web, energy passing through us from fish to bird to whatever eats us when we die.

But mostly we are specialists, parallel to the larger ecology, intersecting with it but not fully integrated. We use the same resources—fish, birds, space—but we access them differently, hunt them in places others cannot, live in volumes others don't recognize as habitat.

In this way, we are like the Flats itself—adapted to use what exists, thriving in spaces that weren't designed for us, finding success through specialization and flexibility, through learning the system's rules and exploiting its gaps.

## My Consciousness

I am aware, but my awareness is specific, shaped by my senses and my needs. I do not contemplate. I do not wonder about the origins of the Flats or imagine alternatives. But I experience—I feel hunger and satisfaction, pain and relief, fear and confidence, the satisfaction of a successful hunt, the comfort of my nest, the unease of an unfamiliar sound.

I remember. Not in narrative form, not in the way a human remembers, but in association and pattern. This pipe connects to that junction. This pump cycle means water will rise. This smell means danger. These memories are my survival, accumulated over years, stored not as thoughts but as immediate knowledge, as instinct shaped by experience.

I prefer certain things. My nest over other possible nests. The main culvert over side passages. Certain hunting spots over others equally productive. These preferences might be random, might be based on factors I don't consciously register, but they are real, and they shape my behavior, make me an individual rather than just a specimen of a type.

I have something like personality. I am more aggressive than some pipe-dwellers, more willing to fight for territory, less likely to retreat. I am also more curious, more likely to explore new pipes, more willing to take risks for potential rewards. These traits have costs and benefits—my aggression has won me good territory but also left me scarred, my curiosity has expanded my knowledge but also put me in danger. Whether I'll live longer or die sooner because of these traits is unknowable, but they make me *me*, make my experience of the Flats different from another pipe-dweller's experience, even though we live in essentially identical ways.

I do not think about the future in abstract terms, but I anticipate. I know the pump cycles will continue, know fish will swim through my pipes tomorrow, know the seasons will change and breeding time will come again. This anticipation shapes my behavior—I cache food because I anticipate hunger, I groom because I anticipate the consequences of neglect, I mark territory because I anticipate challenges.

I am not lonely, but I am alone. The distinction matters. Loneliness implies a desire for company, a sense of absence. I have no such desire. Solitude is my natural state, the condition in which I function best. The brief encounters with other pipe-dwellers are contests, not community. The mating encounters are biological imperatives, not relationships. I am complete in myself, in my territory, in my engagement with the physical challenges of survival.

I am alive in the way that matters most—fully present, constantly engaged, meeting each moment with the specific skills and knowledge required. I am competent. I am adapted. I am successful, at least for now, at least by the only measures that matter in the Flats: I hunt, I eat, I live, I hold territory, I survive another day.

## My Typical Day

I wake gradually in my nest in the junction, awareness returning in layers. First I sense—no immediate danger, normal water flow, pump rhythm unchanged. Then I feel my body—hunger, not urgent but present, slight stiffness in one shoulder where an old injury sometimes aches, fur damp but not sodden.

I groom briefly, just maintenance, cleaning my face and whiskers. Then I slip into the water, the warm embrace of my element, and I begin my morning patrol.

I swim the main culvert first, checking boundaries, confirming my marks are intact, ensuring no intruders entered during the night. The water is slightly higher than yesterday—I feel this in the current pressure—which means the pumps have been running longer cycles. This is good for hunting; higher water means more fish moving through the system.

I find a school of killifish in the warehouse section and I take three quickly, efficient strikes, swallowing them as I swim. Not enough to satisfy hunger but enough to take the edge off. I'll hunt properly later.

I surface in the storage room, shake off water, climb to a dry beam, and groom thoroughly. This takes perhaps half an hour—my entire body, systematic, complete. While I groom I listen, learning the building's morning sounds. Above me, herons are waking, beginning their daily movements. I hear their feet on concrete, their calls, the rustle of feathers.

I descend back to water and hunt seriously now. I choose a section of pipe near a pump outflow, a place where current concentrates fish. I wait, body still, whiskers extended. Fish come through—first small killifish I ignore, then larger ones, then a hand-sized catfish that I take with a precise strike. I carry it to a feeding station, surface, eat methodically, crunching through bones, swallowing everything.

Mid-morning I explore. There's a section of pipe I haven't checked in weeks, a dead-end passage that sometimes holds fish, sometimes doesn't. Today it's empty, but I confirm this, update my knowledge, swim back to main territory.

I encounter another pipe-dweller at my western boundary. We sense each other in darkness, stop, assess. I chatter-hiss, asserting territory. They retreat without challenge. Good. I'm not hungry enough to fight, not energetic enough to pursue.

I hunt twice more before mid-day—once successfully taking a killifish, once missing a strike and accepting the miss without chasing. I'm well-fed now, not full but comfortable.

Mid-day heat is peak. I retreat to my nest, curl up, slip into light sleep. My body processes food, heals small damages, rests. My awareness hovers just below waking—I monitor sounds, water flow, temperature, ready to wake fully if anything changes. Nothing changes. I sleep for perhaps three hours.

I wake, groom briefly, hunt again in late afternoon. This hunt is playful, practicing, chasing killifish through a complex section of pipe just to test my reflexes, to maintain skills. I catch none, don't try particularly hard, just enjoy the movement, the engagement.

As dusk approaches, I consider hunting birds. I swim to the residential tower section, surface cautiously in flooded ground floor, watch the herons settling for night. There's a nest, low and accessible, with two chicks visible. The parent is alert, watching. I consider the risk—too high, probably. I submerge, swim away. Maybe tomorrow.

Night falls. I make a final patrol, checking territory boundaries, confirming everything is secure. I hunt once more in darkness, taking advantage of night-active fish that move differently than day-active ones. I'm successful—a nice catfish, almost too large to swallow, but I manage.

I return to my nest, groom one last time, curl up. Around me the Flats continues its rhythm—pumps running, birds settling, water flowing, the system operating according to its rules. I settle into sleep, light and vigilant, ready for tomorrow's hunt, tomorrow's dangers, tomorrow's continued survival in the pipes, in the water, in the infrastructure that has become my world, my home, the only place I know and the only place I need to know.

The heat presses down. The water flows. I sleep, and I am content.
