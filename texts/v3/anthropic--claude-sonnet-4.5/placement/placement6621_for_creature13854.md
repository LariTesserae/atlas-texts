# Where I Would Be

I am a station keeper at Processing Station 47, three hundred kilometers from the continental edge, farther into the interior than most workers ever see. I've been here eleven months on what was supposed to be a four-month rotation. I don't know when I'll leave.

The station is a skeletal thing—nine platforms connected by open staircases, built around a single culm that was dead before we arrived, its hollow core now threaded with cables and ductwork. My quarters are on the third platform, a pod barely large enough to lie down in, its walls thin enough that I feel the culm-drone as physical pressure even inside, even with the dampening active. I sleep in twenty-minute intervals. Have for months. The longer sleep periods stopped coming after the first few weeks, and I stopped fighting it after the first month. You learn to sleep like the rindwalkers—standing sometimes, your body finding rest in the spaces between the sound's pressure waves.

I came here as a maintenance tech, but that designation has ceased to mean much. We're only five now—we were eight when I arrived, but Chen requested emergency extraction after she started hearing voices in the culm harmonics, and Pak and Okoye didn't come back from their off-rotation leave. The automated systems do most of the real work anyway. What we do is respond when they fail, which they do constantly, because no system built by humans was designed for this acoustic environment, this brightness, this chemistry that corrodes seals and degrades lubricants and finds its way into everything.

Yesterday I spent six hours replacing sensor arrays on the eastern perimeter, forty kilometers out, traveling by the slow rail maintenance cart that runs at walking speed between the culms. The sensors had failed three weeks ago—not critical, just one more degradation in our ability to predict herd movements, to optimize harvest timing. I went alone. We always go alone now; we're too few to spare partners, and anyway, you work better alone here. No need to coordinate through the noise, no distraction of another presence.

The journey out took four hours. The cart's electric motor adds its hum to the general acoustic chaos, a higher frequency that sits in your teeth. I watched the culms pass—they're less like individuals than like a single surface that's been sliced vertically at intervals, revealing depth. The light comes through their walls in that green-gold way that makes everything feel submarine, drowned in brightness. My eyes were tearing constantly despite the filters. They always are. I've stopped noticing.

Halfway out, I saw a herd—maybe three hundred rindwalkers scattered across two square kilometers, browsing at different heights, their long necks creating curves against the vertical culm lines. Beautiful, in the way that anything perfectly adapted to its environment is beautiful. I stopped the cart, watched them for twenty minutes. They ignored me completely. To them, I'm just another feature of the landscape, like the culms themselves, like the distant processing stations—present but irrelevant to their ancient patterns of movement and feeding.

A juvenile was browsing low, still dependent on ground-level plant matter, its neck not yet developed enough for the high browse. It had the distinctive pale coloring of the young, stood out against the herd's darker adults like a point of light. I watched it strip rind from a fallen culm section, its rasping tongue efficient, methodical. Watched it pause, raise its head, orient toward something I couldn't sense—some change in wind direction or plant chemistry or the substrate vibrations the adults were generating. Then it moved, following the implicit coordination that keeps herds coherent without visible communication.

I've been watching the herds long enough now that I sometimes feel I understand them, or at least understand the logic of their movement even if I can't perceive the stimuli they're responding to. There's a grammar to how they space themselves, how they drift and cluster, how they respond to the landscape's invisible gradients. The researchers have their models, their predictive algorithms, but I think you can only really learn it by watching, by spending enough time that your nervous system starts parsing the patterns without conscious thought.

The sensor arrays were on platform pylons driven into the substrate between culms—twelve posts, each three meters high, each with arrays measuring wind, temperature, acoustic properties, volatile chemistry, substrate vibration. Eight of the twelve had failed, their circuits corroded by the chemical air, their seals broken down, their components simply exhausted by the constant vibration. I replaced them with refurbished units from the station's dwindling inventory, knowing these would last maybe six months before requiring replacement again. We're maintaining a system that's designed to fail, keeping it functional through constant effort because that's what we're here to do.

The work took two hours. I was exposed the whole time, standing on the springy substrate between culms, the brightness making my vision swim, the sound a physical force against my chest. My hands shook as I worked—not from cold or fear but from the vibration, the way standing here makes your whole body resonate at frequencies that feel like they're shaking you apart at a cellular level.

Twice during the work, mirror-backs swarmed past—rivers of brightness flowing across the substrate, heading toward some fallen culm I couldn't see, drawn by chemical signals released as plant matter begins to decay. I stepped aside, let them pass. They flowed around my boots like liquid metal, thousands of them, their carapaces catching light, creating moving patterns that hurt to look at directly. They made their own sound—a high chittering that added texture to the culm-drone's base frequencies. When they passed, the substrate was slightly changed, marked by their passage in ways I could feel but not see.

Once, between sensor replacements, I felt the characteristic ground-shake that precedes a culm-fall. It's a sensation that's saved my life four times now—you learn to recognize it instantly, that particular quality of vibration that means a culm is going. I looked up, scanning the nearby trunks, trying to identify which one, trying to judge trajectory. Saw it finally—a hundred meters out, a culm that was starting to lean, its base audibly tearing, fibers separating with sounds like gunshots beneath the general drone.

I ran—not away exactly, because you can't always tell which direction is safe, but to the nearest clear zone where culms were more widely spaced, where falling debris would be less likely. The culm went down slowly, majestically, its length catching air, striking other culms as it descended, breaking into sections that tumbled and bounced. The impacts shook the ground hard enough that I had to grab a nearby trunk for stability, felt the vibration travel through the culm I was holding, felt it resonate, felt my whole body buzzing with transmitted force.

When it settled—takes minutes for the bouncing sections to finally stop moving—the silence was extraordinary. Not real silence, the culm-drone continued unchanged, but relative silence, the absence of the falling sounds, the impact sounds, the secondary vibrations. In that relative quiet I heard something else: the high-frequency chirrup of a culm-grip, probably disturbed by the fall, calling from somewhere in the mid-height reaches of a nearby culm. I couldn't see it—they're too well camouflaged, too still—but I could hear it, that sound that exists in the frequencies above the drone, that carries only when the wind drops.

The rindwalker herd had moved away during the fall, a slow drift that took them three hundred meters distant, out of the impact zone, operating on whatever sense tells them where falling culms will land. They were already returning, moving back toward the fresh fall, the newly accessible plant matter. By tomorrow, the swarm-masses would arrive, and the fallen sections would begin their transformation into substrate, another cycle completing, the endless recycling that makes this landscape function.

I finished the sensors, tested them remotely, confirmed they were feeding data back to the station's network. Then I stood for a while, just being present in the landscape, letting it work on me the way it does, the brightness and sound and chemical air pressing against consciousness, wearing down the boundaries between self and environment. This is the state that worries the psychologists, that gets flagged in the mandatory wellness assessments—the blurring of subject-object distinction, the sense of dissolving into the landscape's vast indifferent presence.

But I think they're wrong to worry. Or rather, they're right that it's a change, a departure from normal human psychological parameters, but wrong that it's necessarily pathological. You can't maintain rigid ego boundaries here—the environment won't allow it. The sound penetrates everything, the brightness overwhelms visual processing, the scale makes individual identity feel arbitrary. You adapt by softening, by becoming porous, by letting the landscape flow through you instead of trying to hold yourself separate from it.

The trip back took four hours, the cart humming its way between culms, the sun lower now, the light becoming more amber, the shadows longer though still bright enough to make you squint. I saw a weftcat—just a glimpse, two hundred meters out, a pattern of gold and white between culms that was there and then wasn't. My heart rate didn't increase. You learn quickly that they're not a threat unless you corner them or approach their cubs, and you learn to recognize the body language that precedes aggression. This one was just passing through, occupying its territory, following whatever route it travels in its patient hunting patterns.

Back at the station, I filed my report through the system, noting which sensors were replaced, which are still functional, projected timeline until the next maintenance cycle. The others were in their pods—we don't socialize much anymore, don't share meals or conversation. We exist in parallel, each maintaining our assigned systems, communicating only when necessary. It's not hostility, just adaptation to an environment where speech is difficult and energy is better conserved for the work.

I ate—processed ration paste that tastes of nothing, nutritionally complete, sourced from off-continent because nothing here is safely edible for humans long-term. The rindwalker meat that the stations process for export has too many mineral complexes, too much strange chemistry. Workers who eat it regularly develop neurological symptoms, liver damage, deposits in their tissues that show up on scans as bright spots, foreign matter that the body can't process or eliminate.

After eating, I checked the night's assignments—just monitoring, no active work scheduled. The automated systems would handle the processing of yesterday's harvest, the railway would run its loads out to the coast, the sensor networks would feed their data into the predictive models. My job was just to be present, to respond if something failed, to maintain the minimal human presence that regulations require.

But I didn't stay in the station. Haven't been sleeping inside much lately. Instead, I took a breather unit and went down to ground level, walked out into the culm forest, into the night brightness under the huge close moon. This is against protocols—workers are supposed to remain in the station at night, when weftcats are hunting, when the aerial predators are most active. But the protocols were written by people who've never been here, who don't understand that the real danger isn't the wildlife but the landscape itself, the falling culms, the disorientation, the way the brightness and sound work on your mind.

The moon makes everything glow—the culms are bioluminescent, or something like it, a blue-green light that seems to come from within their walls. The rindwalkers are dark shapes browsing against that glow, their movements slow, methodical. The sound is different at night, lower frequencies dominant, different harmonics, like the landscape breathing in a different register.

I walked for an hour, no destination, just moving between culms, feeling the springy substrate under my boots, breathing the chemical air that tastes of things that have no names. My hearing protection reduced the sound to merely loud, but I could still feel it, the bass frequencies that exist below hearing, that you perceive through your chest, your bones, your whole body resonating in sympathy with these enormous hollow towers.

I found a clear zone where culms were widely spaced, where the moon-glow was brightest, where you could see for half a kilometer in every direction. Sat down on the substrate, leaned back against a culm, felt its vibration through my spine. The culm was singing—they all are, always, but this one was particularly resonant, hitting frequencies that I felt in my jaw, my skull, my teeth. Not painful exactly, but intense, present, a physical force that made meditation impossible and thought difficult and consciousness narrow down to just sensation, just the pure experience of being in this place.

A herd was visible maybe three hundred meters out, scattered between culms, their long necks catching moon-glow. I watched them browse, watched them move in their coordinated way, watched juveniles stay close to adults, watched the whole group drift slowly northward following some gradient I couldn't detect. Their shapes were alien against the vertical culms, curves intersecting with straight lines, organic forms in an environment that feels almost geometric in its regularity.

Something passed overhead—might have been a glint-wing, though they hunt mostly by day. Or might have been something else, one of the uncertain forms that live at height, that are glimpsed but never clearly seen. A shadow against the moon-bright sky, gone before I could focus on it. The aerial realm here is as complex as the ground level, maybe more so, but less accessible, less studied, more mysterious.

I stayed there for hours, not sleeping but not fully awake either, in that state between where thought becomes minimal and perception dominant, where you exist more as a sensing instrument than as a person with history and identity and concerns. The landscape encourages this state—the constancy of the sound, the brightness, the scale that makes individual human presence feel infinitesimally small.

This is what Chen was experiencing before she left, I think. Not psychosis exactly, but a dissolution of the boundaries that normally define selfhood, a merging with the environment that felt like losing herself. She described hearing voices in the harmonics, but I think what she was actually experiencing was the sound carrying information, patterns that her nervous system was parsing without conscious mediation, the way the rindwalkers parse plant chemistry or substrate vibrations. She was adapting, becoming attuned to the landscape's rhythms, but she interpreted it as something wrong, something threatening, and so it became that for her.

I don't interpret it that way. I understand I'm changing—that eleven months here has altered my neurology, my perception, my sense of what's normal. But the change feels like development rather than damage, like learning a new language rather than losing an old one. I can still function in the human world—I communicate clearly, maintain the systems, follow protocols. But I'm also learning to function in this world, to read its patterns, to move through it with something approaching fluency.

Eventually I returned to the station—the brightening sky indicated day-cycle beginning, time to check systems, review overnight data, prepare for whatever the day's work would bring. The others were emerging from their pods, moving through their routines. We nodded to each other, minimal acknowledgment, no words needed. Ramirez gestured at the data displays, indicating something in the herd movement patterns that suggested good positioning for tomorrow's harvest. I nodded understanding. That was the extent of our interaction.

My contract expires in one month. I'm supposed to leave, take the mandatory rest period off-continent, let my body recover from the hearing damage and vision degradation and chemical exposure. The company has already sent reminder messages, flagged my account, begun the administrative processes for rotation replacement.

I'm not going to leave.

I'll claim technical justifications—we're understaffed, I'm the most experienced tech currently on station, critical systems require my specific knowledge. These will be true statements, even if they're not the real reason. The real reason is that I've been here long enough that leaving feels wrong, feels like interrupting something before it completes, like walking out of a conversation before understanding the point.

I don't think I'm staying forever. This landscape is human-hostile in ways that will eventually force departure—the cumulative damage to hearing and vision, the chemical exposure, the psychological adaptation that might eventually go too far, become irreversible. But I'm not done yet. There's something here I'm still learning, some pattern I'm beginning to perceive but haven't fully grasped.

The researchers talk about understanding the ecosystem, mapping its functions, building predictive models. That's not what I'm after. I'm not trying to understand it in that analytical way. I'm trying to experience it, to let it teach me through direct contact what it is, how it works, what it means to be a landscape that's alive in this particular way—half individual organism, half ecosystem, with boundaries that blur and shift depending on scale and timeframe.

The culms are singing today like they do every day, the sound pressing against everything, the brightness making my eyes water, the herds moving through their patterns, the swarm-masses processing yesterday's falls, the weftcats hunting in their patient way, the whole vast turbulent system continuing its ancient cycling. And I'm here, small and temporary, permitted to participate briefly in something that was old before humans existed and will continue long after we're gone.

This evening I'll probably walk out again, find another clear zone, sit against another culm, let the sound work on me, watch the herds move against the moon-glow. Tomorrow I'll repair whatever broke overnight, replace whatever components failed, maintain the fragile human presence that extracts resources from this place while barely touching its essential nature.

And eventually—months from now, years maybe—I'll leave, carry the damage and the changes and the memories back to the human world, try to explain to people who haven't been here what it's like, what it does to you, why some of us can't stay away, keep coming back, keep extending our rotations until our bodies force us to stop.

But for now, I'm here. A station keeper at Processing Station 47, three hundred kilometers into the interior, living in the sound and brightness and turbulence of a landscape that doesn't care if I understand it, doesn't need my presence, doesn't acknowledge my existence except as one more transient phenomenon moving briefly through its eternal cycling.

And somehow, impossibly, that's enough.
