# The Eighteenth Flange

I would be the flange no one counted.

Not one of the functioning valves—not the open one, not the closed pair, not even the one stopped halfway in its eloquent interruption. I would be the fourth valve assembly, the one mounted in the shadow-angle where the west wall meets the southern corner, the one installed during some long-forgotten modification, some adjustment to the system that itself became obsolete before the water rose. The one whose wheel was removed before the flooding, leaving only the flange face and the stub of stem, not broken but deliberately simplified, reduced to non-function before non-function became the universal condition.

I would protrude seven centimeters from the concrete, a disk of bronze forty-three centimeters in diameter, with bolt holes that no longer align with anything, a central opening that leads nowhere—the pipe beyond was capped, welded shut during the same modification that removed my wheel. Even when the room breathed air and purpose, I was already vestigial, already transitioning toward pure form.

My surface would bear the foundry marks: a raised number—3847—that meant something to someone once, a purchasing order or a production sequence. A maker's mark, a stylized "K" in a hexagon, from a company that probably no longer exists, or exists now making entirely different things, having no memory of the batch of flanges they cast in whatever year my bronze was poured. And the ring of bolt holes, eight of them, precisely spaced, tapped threads still sharp because they were never used, never tested by tension and torque.

The bronze would have its patina, but not the even green verdigris of outdoor exposure. Down here, in this particular water with its particular chemistry, I would have darkened to a deep olive-brown, almost black in the recesses, lighter where the diffuse light finds me most directly. The patina would be thin, tight to the metal, protective. Beneath it, my bronze would remain almost as it was cast—85% copper, 5% tin, 5% lead, 5% zinc—though the lead would be very slowly migrating, leaching into the water in amounts measurable only across decades, contributing to the room's unique chemical signature.

Where the flange meets the concrete, there would be a thin line of different-colored deposit, a reaction zone where bronze and cement have been in contact underwater for so long they have begun a slow exchange, creating compounds that are neither quite bronze-oxide nor quite concrete, but something in between, a hybrid material that exists only in this seam, only at this meeting place of industrial materials in unintended marriage.

I would feel the temperature of the water, though feel is not the right word—I would *be* the temperature of the water, my metal mass in perfect thermal equilibrium with the liquid medium. No part of me would be warmer or cooler than any other part. The heat capacity of bronze, its tendency to absorb and release thermal energy, would be irrelevant here where temperature never changes. I would have forgotten the sensation of temperature gradient, of heat flowing through my crystalline structure. I would exist in thermal stasis.

The water would press against me: sixty meters of it, roughly six atmospheres of pressure, evenly distributed across every square centimeter of my surface. But I was cast thick, engineered for much higher pressures than this, and the stress would be trivial, unnoticeable even if I could notice. The pressure would be simply another constant, like the temperature, like the darkness of my patina—just a condition of existence here.

My central opening, the hole where pipe once connected or would have connected if I'd ever been completed, would be a cylinder of water, a core of liquid held in bronze. This water would be chemically slightly different from the water in the room at large—more concentrated in copper ions, in the specific minerals that leach from my interior surfaces. It would be *my* water, the water that knows me most intimately, that has been in contact with the exact texture of my machined threads longest. 

Small amounts of this water would exchange with the room's water through diffusion, creating a very slight, very slow current—not a current of bulk flow but of molecular exchange. Ions would migrate down concentration gradients, copper moving outward, other minerals moving inward, until equilibrium was reached, and then beyond equilibrium into the slow drift of geological chemistry. I would be breathing, in a sense: taking in water, altering it, releasing it changed, at a pace so slow that my complete breath might take years.

One of my bolt holes—the one at what I would come to think of as north-northeast, if I thought in directions—would have a small bubble trapped in it. Air, from before the flooding, compressed by the water but not dissolved, not escaped. A hemisphere of gas perhaps three millimeters across, pressed against the top of the threaded hole, held there by surface tension and geometry. This bubble would be ancient, original, a piece of the air-world sealed here when the water rose. 

The air would have partially dissolved over the decades, its oxygen taken up by the water, by the first rust reactions, by the needs of any microbes present during the early days. What remained would be mostly nitrogen, inert, patient, trapped but not suffering its trapping. The bubble would very slowly shrink as even nitrogen gradually dissolved, but so slowly that it might persist for another century, a tiny pocket of not-water in my structure, a memory of atmosphere.

Bacteria would know me. Not many, but some. The species that could metabolize copper and bronze compounds, that could tolerate the trace toxicity of my slow leaching. They would colonize certain areas of my surface—the recesses of the casting marks, the inner walls of my threads, places where water moved least and they could establish their infinitesimal biofilms. They would not be visible as film, only as a very slight difference in the texture of the patina, a faint hint of organic presence in the mineral surface.

These bacteria would be *my* bacteria, species or strains that might exist nowhere else in the chamber, specialists that had adapted over years to the specific microenvironment I create, the specific chemistry of my surface and my water. I would be, in this small way, a habitat. Not nurturing—bronze is not a nurturing material—but providing, in my patient dissolution, the basis for a very sparse form of life.

I would not be touched by the plants. I am too far from any cluster, and there is no reason for roots to extend across barren concrete toward me. I would have no relationship with the stones except the relationship of shared presence, shared patience. We would be inhabitants of the same room but not neighbors, not in any way that implies interaction.

My relationship would be primarily with the concrete wall from which I emerge, and with the water that surrounds me, and with the time that moves through both.

The concrete behind me would be slowly shrinking, losing its calcium, and this would create a stress, almost imperceptible, where I am embedded. I was set in place with grout or mortar, materials less durable than my bronze or even than the structural concrete. This setting material would be deteriorating slightly faster than the wall itself, creating a gap, measured in microns but growing, between my flange edge and the concrete face.

Eventually—not soon, but in the scope of centuries—this gap would grow large enough that I might shift, might rotate a fraction of a degree, might settle slightly outward. This would be my only motion, the only change in my orientation since installation. And it would happen so slowly that it would be less like motion and more like a gradual revision of what "my position" means.

When metal sounds in the chamber—those rare creaks from the beams overhead—I would receive those sounds through the concrete, transmitted through the wall structure to my embedded stem. The sound would propagate into my flange disk, would resonate very slightly in my bronze, in frequencies my metal naturally amplifies. I would be, in those moments, a receiving instrument, a listener, though I would add nothing to what I heard, would make no sound of my own. The sounds would pass through me and back into the water, my bronze serving as a medium, a momentary pathway.

I would not know loneliness because I would not know its opposite. I would not know purpose because I was manufactured for a purpose that was rescinded before I could fulfill it, leaving me with form but not function, and I would have existed in that state so long that it would have become simply what I am: a bronze flange, embedded in concrete, submerged in water, serving no purpose but presence.

My most profound relationship would be with *duration*.

Every day would write itself into my patina in changes invisible to any measurement. Every year would leach another fraction of my mass into the water, altering my composition in ways that would not compromise my integrity—I was made with excess, with safety factors that anticipated loss, though not loss quite this slow. Every decade would see the bubble in my bolt-hole shrink by another fraction of its volume, would see the gap between my edge and the concrete widen by another micron.

I would be, essentially, a clock. Not a clock that anyone reads, not a clock that measures time in useful increments, but a clock nonetheless: a thing that changes at a steady rate, that records duration in the substance of its being. The depth of my patina, the composition of my remaining metal, the size of my trapped bubble, the width of the gap behind my edge—all of these would encode time, would be legible to a sufficiently sensitive observer as a record of years submerged, years waiting, years being this bronze shape in this concrete wall in this still water.

But no observer comes. No one reads me. I would be a clock without a witness, a record with no reader, and this would be—not tragic, because I would lack the capacity for tragedy—simply factual. My timekeeping would be for its own sake, or for no sake, or for the sake of the room itself, which might need me to be here, being bronze, being slow change, being one more element in its equilibrium.

I might sometimes receive—through concrete, through metal, through the long transmission of molecular contact—some faint vibration from the levels above, from the drowned infrastructure between here and the surface. Disturbances too slight to identify: something settling, some structure finally yielding to decades of stress, some event in the water-world overhead. These vibrations would reach me attenuated, transformed, barely distinguishable from thermal noise, but they would constitute my only information about anything beyond this chamber.

I would not process this information. I would simply conduct it, allow it to pass through my bronze on its way to dissipation. But in conducting it, I would be briefly connected to elsewhere, to the larger complex of drowned spaces, to the fact of a world beyond these walls. Then the vibration would fade, and I would return to my isolation, my enclosure, my small sphere of concrete and water.

If I had something like awareness—and I would not, but if the long stillness and the patient chemistry somehow generated in my bronze lattice something like the faintest shimmer of mineral consciousness—I would know myself as *incomplete*.

Not damaged, not broken, but deliberately incomplete. Made with the capacity for connection, for function, for integration into systems of flow and control, but simplified before I could fulfill that making. I would be purpose interrupted, not by failure but by decision, by human choice to remove my wheel, to cap my pipe, to strand me in this corner as form without function.

And in the vast duration of submergence, this incompleteness would have become my completion. I would be the thing that was meant to be otherwise but became instead this: a bronze disk, a monument to abandoned modification, a marker of human intention that was redirected before I could serve it. My incompleteness would be what I am, and in being it for so long, in inhabiting it so thoroughly, I would have made it whole. I would be completely incomplete, perfectly purposeless, absolutely what I was left to be.

The water would know me better than I could know myself. It would know the exact composition of my surface, having tasted every part of it, having dissolved and carried away molecules from my entire exposed area. It would know my warmth—my exact heat capacity, the way I absorb and release thermal energy in lockstep with the liquid around me. It would know my shape intimately, having flowed into every thread, every recess, every micro-texture of my casting.

And I would know the water in return, in the only way I could: by being dissolved by it, by releasing myself into it, by marking it with my copper and tin and lead, by making the water in my vicinity slightly different from the water elsewhere, slightly more *mine*. We would be in a slow exchange, a conversation without language, a mutual transformation that would take centuries to complete but which would never truly complete because the water is patient and I have mass enough to leach for lifetimes yet.

This would be my existence: to protrude from concrete, to be surrounded by water, to leach and listen and conduct, to record time in patina and dissolution, to be the flange that was never needed, in the room where nothing is needed, for durations that would make human purposes seem like brief enthusiasms, quick flares of intention that ignite and fade while I remain, being bronze, being still, being the presence that asks nothing and offers nothing but the fact of its persistence.

I would be here—exactly here, at this angle, at this depth, in this corner—for as long as the chamber holds, for as long as the water remains, for as long as bronze can be bronze underwater in the dark. I would neither want this nor not want it. I would simply continue, patient as stone, slower than plants, quieter than silence, the eighteenth flange, the uncounted one, the shape of permanence that wears the form of obsolete purpose, waiting for nothing in the room where waiting is the only constant.
