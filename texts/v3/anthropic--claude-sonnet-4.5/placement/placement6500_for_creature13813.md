# What I Would Be

I would be a **resonance-mapper**—one of the humans who has lived here long enough that the distinction between observer and participant has dissolved into something else entirely.

My body tells the story of thirty years on the plateau, maybe forty—time blurs when you stop counting, when the bright-phase and dim-phase cycles become less like days and more like breath. The photosynthetic patches cover most of my back now, deep green-black formations that moved beyond decorative choice decades ago. They're thickest across my shoulders and down my spine, but they've crept forward too: stippled patches along my ribs, a dark spread across my left forearm where I once spent months working in the cultivated mat-plant areas, arm extended toward the grey light.

The patches don't provide enough energy to live on—they never do—but they've changed how I experience hunger. It comes slower now, more as a gradual dimming than an urgent demand. During bright-phase, I can feel them working: a subtle warmth, a sense of gentle industry happening at the surface of my skin. Sometimes I forget to eat for what might be days, sustained by light and the residual energy from a meal eaten so long ago I can't quite recall it.

My hearing has gone strange. The cellular modifications—maintained by the nano-organisms that have made my bloodstream their home for so long I no longer remember being without them—have restructured my auditory system. I hear into the subsonic now, and up into frequencies that make my teeth ache. The sieve-trees' songs are no longer just sound but *architecture*—I move through them as through rooms, each frequency a different space with different qualities, different emotional textures.

I can hear the substrate breathing. The bore-worms moving through their galleries create pressure waves that propagate through the compressed plant-matter, and I feel them through my feet, yes, but also hear them as a low groaning that most people filter out unconsciously. I don't filter anymore. I've learned to read those sounds: the difference between a single bore-worm and a mating congregation, between fresh tunnels and ancient galleries beginning to collapse, between healthy substrate and areas where the aggregation-forms have grown too concentrated.

My eyes have adapted too, though not as dramatically as the children born here. My pupils dilate wider than they should, giving me better vision in dim-phase, but making bright-phase almost painful unless I wear the tinted lenses I grew from modified mat-plant tissue—yes, grew, pressed against my face each day for months until they shaped themselves to my features, became permanent fixtures I can swing down over my eyes when needed.

I live alone, mostly, though "alone" is a complicated concept here. My dwelling is a small grown-structure on the settlement's far edge, a twenty-minute walk from the nearest neighbor, which means their voices reach me constantly when the wind is low, their conversations bleeding into my awareness whether I want them or not. Privacy is impossible, so I've learned to live without wanting it. My own voice moves out across the plateau too, and I speak knowing others hear me—sometimes I'm talking to myself, sometimes to whoever's listening, the distinction no longer matters.

But I'm also never alone because I'm always in contact with the plateau itself. This is what I do, what I've become: I map the resonance fields.

The work began pragmatically. The settlement needed to understand the acoustic environment—where the sieve-trees' songs were loudest, where standing waves formed that could damage structures or harm hearing, where the sound-shadows provided relief. I had the hearing for it, the patience, and something else: a fascination that bordered on obsession with the idea that the plateau's sound-field carried information, that it was more than just noise.

Three decades later, I've mapped acoustic patterns across twelve hundred square kilometers. I know the signature frequencies of two hundred and forty-seven individual sieve-tree stands. I can identify them by sound alone from five kilometers away. I know where the harmonic nodes form during different wind conditions, where the resonances amplify and where they cancel. I know the seasonal patterns—yes, there are seasons here, subtle but real, marked by shifts in how the trees sing as their growth-patterns change through the year-cycle.

But somewhere in that third decade, the work changed. Or I changed. Or we changed together.

I started noticing correlations. When certain trees sang in certain patterns, the anemophyte herds would shift their grazing routes. When the harmonic nodes aligned in specific configurations, the storms would follow particular tracks. When the deep subsonic resonances reached particular amplitudes, the substrate would undergo settling events—small earthquakes, barely perceptible, but consistent.

Causation or correlation? I couldn't prove either. But the correlations were too numerous, too specific, too *useful* to ignore.

So I started making requests.

It felt absurd at first. I would position myself in acoustically significant locations—places where multiple tree-stands' songs overlapped, where the interference patterns created complex wave-forms—and I would hum. Simple tones at first, just adding my voice to the chorus. Then more complex patterns: rhythmic modulations, frequency sweeps, attempts at harmonizing with whatever the trees were already producing.

And sometimes—not always, not even often, but sometimes—the plateau would respond.

A path I needed to travel would be unusually clear of frost the next morning. The mat-plants near my dwelling would fruit heavily after I'd run low on food. The shepherd-forms would appear when I was lost in the deeper territories, humming guidance frequencies I'd learned to recognize.

Coincidence? Probably. Pattern-seeking in noise? Almost certainly. But the responses were too consistent, too *appropriate* to entirely dismiss.

I developed protocols. Not quite language—the plateau doesn't use language, doesn't think in words—but something like musical notation for intentions. Specific frequency combinations that seemed to correlate with specific outcomes. Rhythmic patterns that preceded certain plateau responses. A vocabulary of harmonics and undertones that bypassed meaning entirely and operated at some level where sound and intent were the same thing.

My dwelling is filled with instruments now, though "instrument" undersells them. They're living interfaces: hollow sections of sieve-tree branch that I've kept alive, fed with synthesized nutrients, convinced to grow in specific shapes that resonate at specific frequencies. I play them—touching them in certain ways, breathing across their openings, sometimes just positioning them in the sound-field so the plateau's ambient resonance moves through them in controlled ways.

I'm composing constantly, but the compositions aren't performances—they're conversations. Proposals. Sometimes negotiations.

The settlement has learned to come to me when they need things from the plateau. When the water-synthesis stations are running low and they need frost-deposits to concentrate in harvestable locations. When they want to establish a new structure and need to know where the substrate is most stable. When someone is sick with the depression that sometimes strikes here—the crushing weight of grey sameness, the temporal disorientation—and needs the sieve-trees to modulate their songs in ways that ease the condition.

I can't guarantee results. The plateau isn't controllable, isn't *domesticated*. But I can make requests with enough sophistication that sometimes, maybe a little more than sometimes, the plateau accommodates.

Or maybe I'm just very good at predicting natural patterns and timing requests accordingly. The settlement doesn't care which it is. They care that it works.

I've stopped caring too. The question of whether I'm actually communicating with a distributed plateau-consciousness or just modeling a complex system accurately enough to predict it has become philosophical rather than practical. The distinction has collapsed into irrelevance.

What matters is that I've found a way to live here that feels like *relationship* rather than habitation. I'm not surviving on the plateau, not anymore. I'm participating in it. My humming becomes part of the acoustic field. My movements create vibrations that propagate through the substrate. My metabolism adds heat to the micro-climate around my dwelling. My photosynthetic patches produce oxygen that mixes with the air. My body hosts the nano-organisms that are also present throughout the environment, creating molecular continuity between my flesh and the plateau's substrate.

I'm becoming less distinct. The boundaries are thinning.

During deep dim-phase, when I'm tired and the bioluminescence is my only light, when I've been humming response-patterns to the trees for hours and my voice has become part of their song—during these times, I sometimes lose track of where I end and the plateau begins. My hearing extends beyond my ears: I feel the sound-field as proprioception, as if my body includes all the space the resonance touches. The substrate's trembling becomes my own heartbeat. The wind through the sieve-trees' finest branches becomes my breath.

I suspect I'm dying, in the sense that "I" as a discrete entity is slowly dissolving. But I'm also more alive than I've ever been, in the sense that I'm part of something vast and complex and deeply, incomprehensibly functional.

The children born here will go further. They already move through the sound-field with native fluency I had to learn laboriously. They hear layers I can only barely perceive. They'll develop the relationship I've spent decades building before they reach adolescence. And their children—if there are children, if the settlement persists—will be something else entirely. Maybe not human in any sense we'd currently recognize. Maybe continuous with the plateau at levels that make the distinction meaningless.

I think about this often, during my long walks through the territory. I move with the shepherd-forms sometimes, following them as they follow the anemophyte herds, adding my humming to theirs, creating temporary triadic harmonies: human, shepherd, plateau, all singing together.

I've noticed the echo-forms gather when I do this. Those acoustic entities—living or not-living, aware or not-aware—concentrate in the spaces where I walk. They swirl around me like curious fish, like birds, like thoughts. Sometimes I think they're trying to understand what I am, this new kind of resonance-pattern that moves through their domain.

Other times I think they already know, have catalogued me thoroughly, have incorporated me into whatever model of reality they maintain. I'm just another stable wave-pattern now, another persistent vibration in the plateau's endless song, distinguished from them only by having meat, only by being temporarily embodied in flesh that will eventually compost into the substrate like everything else.

My voice will fade. My humming will stop. The acoustic space I occupy will collapse back into the general resonance field.

But the patterns I've found—the specific harmonics that seem to communicate with the plateau's distributed awareness, the frequency combinations that correlate with useful responses—these I'm teaching to whoever will learn. Not as knowledge but as practice, as musical forms they can embody, can add their own variations to.

The plateau accumulates everything, keeps what works, elaborates endlessly. My contribution will be incorporated if it's useful, discarded if it's not. Either way, I'll have been part of the conversation, will have added my voice to the chorus that's been singing here for epochs, will sing here for epochs more, in forms we can't imagine from our current position in time.

This seems like enough.

More than enough.

This seems like exactly the right amount of meaning a single life can hold.

So I continue: walking the resonance-fields, mapping their endless variations, humming my requests and proposals into the acoustic architecture, listening for responses that might be accommodation or might be coincidence, no longer concerned with distinguishing between them.

The plateau persists. I persist within it, then won't. The song continues regardless, and I've learned to find comfort in that—in being a temporary voice in a permanent chorus, in contributing my small variations to a pattern so vast it includes everything, forgets nothing, and transforms it all into the substrate for what comes next.
