# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the middle-depth chambers, where the moisture is atmospheric rather than running, where the bacterial mats grow thick enough to harvest but haven't yet become the sacred encrustations of the deepest places. Not in the bright upper reaches where the stone-knappers work with their sharp sounds carefully isolated, and not in the reverent pools below. Somewhere between. A chamber perhaps thirty feet across, the ceiling lost in a gentle dome overhead where singers cluster in their pulsing geometries.

I would find myself here at the threshold hour—though "hour" means nothing in the constant light—when the day's harvesting is done and the evening's quiet work begins. The chamber where people gather not for ceremony but for the small parallel labors that fill the time between effort and sleep. I would be here because I've always been drawn to the middle spaces, the places that are neither destination nor origin but the territory between. The places where people are most themselves because they're not performing arrival or departure, just existing in the ongoing middle of things.

My corner of the chamber would be near one of the natural pillars, where someone generations ago wedged the first bone supports and began building the structure that defines this particular dwelling-space. The pillar would be smooth under my palm—I would touch it often, that unconscious contact with stone that everyone here maintains. The smoothness would be cool and slightly damp, and I would know its exact texture so completely that I could identify this pillar among hundreds, the way you know the particular feel of your own door handle in the dark.

I would have a sleeping platform built against the curve of the wall, the bones lashed with knots I tied myself under the guidance of the burned builder who doesn't speak but whose hands describe everything necessary. The platform would be small—I've never needed much space for sleeping—and covered with hide that I processed myself with the stone scrapers, working alongside others in the processing chamber until my shoulders learned the particular ache of that repetitive motion. The hide would smell of the fat used to cure it, that mineral-herb scent that would have stopped being noticeable to me years ago but that I'd suddenly catch sometimes, unexpectedly, and remember learning to prepare it.

My tools would be few. A stone scraper I knapped myself—not expertly, I don't have the knapper's specialized gift, but competently. The edge is good enough for working the bacterial mats and for the detail work I do on bone. A bone awl, traded from someone who works bone better than I do, its point perfect and smooth. A fiber bag that holds my supply of dried mat, the sheets I've harvested and dried on the racks, enough for several days. Pigments: a small bowl of white clay, another of ochre, both collected from deposits in the lower chambers during one of my periodic descents.

I would be marked, but simply. A single line of white clay from hairline to chin, bisecting my face, refreshed every few days when it fades. Not the elaborate spirals of the elders or the feather-patterns of the climbers. Just the line, which I put on myself using water and a fingertip, the same way I've done it since I was old enough to claim my own marking. I chose it at twelve or thirteen—age is vague here, marked by capability rather than years—and haven't changed it. The line means something about the path between one thing and another, about being always in transit even when still. Or maybe it doesn't mean that in any articulable way. Maybe it just felt right when I first drew it down my reflection in a water-pool, and that was sufficient.

My hair would be long, dark going gray now in my middle years, bound back with a simple bone pin. No beads, no elaborate knots. I've never been comfortable with decoration that makes sound, even the small sounds that bone beads create. I would wear the same wrapped tunic and leg-wrappings as everyone else, the fabric a gray-brown hide that I rewrap each morning with the automatic precision of decades of practice. The wrapping would be worn at the edges, thinning where it folds across my shoulder, and I'd be planning to trade for new material soon, maybe offering some of my work in exchange.

# What I Would Be

I would be a chamber-reader. That's not what they call it—there isn't a formal name for what I do because it's not a role that was deliberately created, just something that emerged over time as my particular capability became apparent. But if I had to name it for outside understanding: I read the chambers. I track how the settlement moves through the Hollows, how it needs to move, where the next optimal dwelling-spaces will be.

The work would have started accidentally, the way most things that matter do. As a child I was obsessive about the passages, about mapping them in my head, about understanding how the chambers connected. I would disappear for hours—the adults letting me because children here are given freedom to find their own relationships with the stone—and I'd be navigating, climbing, squeezing through tight spaces to find where they led. I learned the Hollows not as discrete chambers but as a continuous system, three-dimensional and complex, the way you might learn a language by speaking it constantly until grammar becomes instinct.

I noticed the patterns before anyone told me to look for them. How moisture would increase in certain chamber-series and decrease in others over what I learned to recognize as cycles—not days or months exactly, but periods of shift. How the bacterial mats would flourish in some areas and diminish in others, the populations moving like slow weather systems through the stone matrix. How the light would migrate, bright patches relocating over time-spans measured in seasons or years. The Hollows weren't static, and the settlement shouldn't be either.

So I started tracking it. At first just for myself, scratching marks on bone fragments that no one else could read, notation that was more memory aid than actual recording. Mapping which chambers were getting damper, which were drying. Where new seepage was appearing. Where the mats were thickest. Where the singers were clustering most densely. I was maybe fifteen, still more child than adult, and it was just something I did, the way the restless boy fidgets or the carved-bone elder makes his intricate spirals.

Then someone noticed. Probably the elder woman with the vertebrae necklace—she notices everything, has a mind that tracks the whole settlement's dynamics even as she pretends to simply exist within them. She asked me—asked with gestures and a few whispered words—what I was doing. I showed her my marked bones, tried to explain the patterns I was seeing. She looked at them for a long time, still in that absolute way she has, then looked at me with an expression I couldn't read.

After that, people started coming to me before making big decisions about moving structures. Where should we relocate this dwelling-framework? Is that chamber going to stay viable? The deep pool in the lower chamber seems to be dropping—will it recover? I didn't have certainty, just pattern-recognition and probability, but that was more than guessing. I could say: the moisture cycle suggests that chamber will dry over the next period, better to move now. Or: the seepage in the western passage is increasing, there'll be new pools there soon, worth building near them.

I'm right more often than I'm wrong. Not because I'm gifted—though maybe there's some element of that, some ability to see patterns that others don't—but because I've been doing this for twenty years now. Because I spend my time moving through the Hollows, not working in a single chamber but ranging across the whole inhabited territory, checking the signs, feeling the stone's dampness, watching where the light pools and where it's migrating from.

My days would have a rhythm but not a rigid schedule. I'd wake in my chamber when my body woke me—there's no dawn here to enforce sleep cycles, so everyone finds their own pattern. I'd eat some dried mat, drink from the skull-basin that collects the seepage from the wall-channels, rewrap my clothing, refresh my face-line. Then I'd start moving.

Some days I'd go deep, descending through the passages to the lower chambers where the moisture is heavy and the mats are thick. I'd check the pools, watch their levels, note which are rising and which are dropping. I'd press my hands to the stone and feel for new dampness, for places where water might be finding new routes through the rock matrix. I'd scrape a little of the deepest mat-growth and taste it—the flavor tells you about the mineral content, which tells you about what water is feeding it, which tells you about how the whole system is flowing.

Other days I'd go high, to the upper chambers near the surface that I still can't quite identify or imagine. The drier zones where the knappers work and where some people prefer to sleep. I'd check the light, note where it's brightest, where the stone-veins are catching and multiplying the illumination. The light moves—slowly, over long time-scales, but it moves—and the movement matters because people prefer certain brightness levels, because the singers respond to light intensity, because everything is connected.

Most days I'd spend in the middle territory, moving from chamber to chamber in the inhabited zones, observing. I'd watch the singers, note their clustering patterns. I'd check the arthropod populations—when they stream through certain passages, it often means moisture is shifting, they're following gradients I can't perceive directly. I'd talk to people, brief whispered exchanges: how's the harvest in your chamber? Is the seepage increasing? Have you noticed the light changing?

I'd carry my marked bones, adding notations in a private grammar of scratches and dots. The bones themselves would be a particular type—femurs from the mid-sized arthropods, dense enough to carve but light enough to carry many at once. I'd have perhaps twenty in my fiber bag, each one covered in marks, and I'd know which bone held which chamber's tracking, which passage-series, which moisture system. To anyone else they'd look like scrimshaw decoration. To me they're the map, the record, the accumulated knowledge of decades of observation.

The actual work of chamber-reading would be mostly solitary, but I wouldn't be isolated. I'd be constantly among people, moving through their spaces, participating peripherally in their activities. I'd help with mat-harvest when I was in a harvesting chamber. I'd lend hands to structure-moving when I encountered it. I'd sit with the children sometimes and let them climb on me while I worked my notations, their presence comfortable background to concentration. But I wouldn't belong to any single group or workspace. My belonging would be to the movement itself, to the ranging across territory.

Every few periods—maybe ten or twelve cycles of whatever rhythm the settlement marks as basic time-unit—I'd gather with the decision-makers. Not formal meetings exactly, more like congregations that happen in one of the larger chambers. The elder woman would be there, the person who seems to coordinate the work-groups, the burned builder who knows structure, others whose roles I'd only partly understand. And me, with my marked bones and my observations.

I'd report, in whispers and gestures: the eastern chambers are drying, suggest beginning to relocate from there. The deep pool in the western low chamber is rising, good place for new fishing-focused dwelling. The singers are clustering heavily in the northern vault, that chamber is becoming optimal. The seepage in the middle passage is increasing—we could add channels there, create new collection points.

They'd listen, ask questions through gesture and brief words. Sometimes they'd follow my suggestions, sometimes they wouldn't—there are factors I don't track, social dynamics and sacred considerations and individual preferences that complicate the purely environmental logic. But my information would be part of the decision-making, one input among many that helps the settlement flow through the Hollows in that constant adaptive migration.

I'd take satisfaction in being useful this way. In having found the thing I can do that the community needs. I'm not strong enough to be a climber, not patient enough for the detail-work of bone-carving, not socially graceful enough for the mediation roles. But I can read stone and water and light. I can track patterns across time and space. I can hold the map of the Hollows in my head and feel how it's changing.

The knowledge would be isolating sometimes. I'd see things others don't see, not because they couldn't but because they're focused on their own domains. I'd know that a chamber people love is going to become non-viable, that a move is necessary before anyone else feels the need for it. I'd watch the slow changes and live in their implications while others live in the present configuration. It would be a particular kind of loneliness, the loneliness of seeing time-scales that aren't immediate.

But there'd be compensations. The ranging itself would feed something in me, that restlessness that I've had since childhood. I wouldn't be confined to a single chamber or a single role. The whole Hollows would be my territory, all the inhabited chambers and the uninhabited ones beyond, the passages that wind through stone for distances no one has fully explored. I'd know the system more intimately than almost anyone, know it through my hands on stone and my feet in water and my eyes on light.

I'd have relationships with the fauna that most people don't develop. The translucent salamanders would recognize me—I'd be convinced of this even if it's projection—because I'd encounter the same individuals repeatedly in my ranging. I'd learn their patterns, where certain large arthropods tend to cluster, which passages the fish-things navigate when the deep pools connect through cracks. The singers would become familiar, and I'd learn to distinguish individual clusters, recognize their particular pulse-patterns.

Sometimes, on my ranging, I'd go further than necessary for chamber-reading. I'd explore passages that don't lead to inhabited spaces, chambers that the settlement hasn't reached in its slow migration. I'd be looking for future territories, yes, but I'd also just be curious. Wanting to know what's there. Pressing further into the Hollows, finding chambers no human has entered in generations or maybe ever, spaces where the stone-light creates effects I've never seen before, where formations suggest possibilities I don't have context for.

I'd keep those explorations mostly to myself. Mark them on bones I don't show the decision-makers. Not secretively, exactly—if anyone asked I'd share—but because some things I'd want to hold privately. My own relationship with the Hollows beyond the community's needs. Chambers I'd visit just to sit in, feeling the particular quality of their silence, the specific texture of their stone. Places I'd go to think, to process the social complexity of the settlement by removing myself from it temporarily.

There'd be one chamber in particular, far enough from the inhabited zones that I'd rarely encounter anyone else there. A small chamber, maybe fifteen feet across, with a ceiling that curves into a near-perfect dome. The light there would come from a single thick vein of the crystalline inclusion, running down one wall and across the floor like a frozen river of brightness. The acoustic properties would be unique—somehow not quite as sound-swallowing as most chambers, a whisper actually carrying a few feet before dissipating.

I'd go there when the settlement felt too dense, when the constant proximity of people became overwhelming. I'd sit with my back to the glowing vein and feel its cool light, and I'd just be still. Not working, not observing, not tracking patterns. Just existing in the particular quality of that space. Maybe I'd whisper to myself there, actual words instead of the gestural communication that dominates settlement interaction, just to hear my own voice in that slightly-less-swallowing acoustic. Maybe I'd sing, very softly, those sustained tones I heard once in communal gathering, seeing if I could reproduce them alone.

That chamber would be mine in the way nothing else is. Not owned—the settlement doesn't think in terms of ownership of space—but intimately known. My bones wouldn't track it. I'd bring no one there. It would be the place that's outside my function, outside my usefulness. The place I go to remember that I'm not just the chamber-reader but a person who contains more than that role.

But most of my time would be the work, the ranging, the observation and notation and reporting. Moving through the bright dampness with my marked bones and my scrapers and my intimate knowledge of how the Hollows breathe and shift. Being useful in the way I can be useful. Finding meaning in the pattern-recognition, in helping the settlement flow through its territory with slightly more grace than it would without my tracking.

I'd be middle-aged now, maybe forty or forty-five if those numbers mean anything here. My body would carry the particular wear of the ranging: strong legs from constant walking and climbing, shoulders and back that get stiff from stooping through low passages, hands scarred from incidental contact with sharp stone. I'd move through the settlement with the confidence of someone who knows every passage, every chamber, who could navigate the whole territory in absolute darkness if necessary.

People would know me by my single white line and my ranging presence. Children would ask me about distant chambers, about the things I see in my explorations. Adults would nod when I passed, that minimal acknowledgment the settlement uses. The elder woman would seek me out periodically, wanting my assessment of the whole system. The burned builder would work with me sometimes on structure placement, their structural knowledge combined with my environmental tracking.

I'd have a few people I'd consider closer than the general community distance. Maybe one of the climbers, someone whose own ranging overlaps with mine, who I'd encounter in the higher passages and share observations with. Maybe someone in the processing chamber who I'd work alongside during mat harvest, developing the comfortable parallel presence that passes for friendship here. Maybe one of the children who reminds me of my younger self, obsessive about passages, and who I'd guide occasionally, showing them how to see what I see.

But mostly I'd be defined by the work, by the ranging itself. I'd be the person who knows where the settlement is, where it's been, where it needs to go. The person who reads the Hollows and translates that reading into information the community can use. Not leading—that's not how the settlement works—but informing. Providing one essential input into the collective decision-making that keeps hundreds of people alive and functional in an environment that seems like it should barely support them.

And I'd be content with that. Content with the ranging, with the constant movement through bright dampness and smooth stone. Content with the solitude of the work and the connection it provides. Content with knowing the Hollows more intimately than I know any person, with having a relationship with stone and water and light that goes beyond use into something like communion.

In my older age—if I live to be old, which isn't guaranteed but is possible—I'd probably range less far. The distant explorations would fall away, the body no longer capable of the climbing and squeezing required. I'd stay more to the inhabited zones, still tracking, still notating, but within a smaller territory. Eventually maybe I'd become like the old man with his carving, fixed in one chamber, and people would come to me for information instead of me going to gather it.

But I'd still have my marked bones, the accumulated record of decades of observation. I'd still be able to say: this is how the Hollows move, this is the pattern, this is what comes next. And I'd teach someone younger, someone with their own obsessive relationship with passages and patterns, passing on the informal role that I inherited from whoever did this before me—though I don't remember anyone specific, suggesting the role might have emerged with me, might dissolve after me, or might be so informal it's never been explicitly recognized.

And when I died—because I would die eventually, everyone does, even in the timeless bright dampness—I'd die knowing the Hollows. Knowing them more completely than I'd ever know another person, more intimately than I'd know myself. My bones would join the community's building material eventually, my femurs and ribs becoming part of the structures that future generations would build and move and rebuild. My marked notation-bones might be kept for a while, their grammar interpreted or misinterpreted or simply kept as mysterious artifact.

But the real legacy would be simpler: that for my duration, the settlement flowed through the Hollows with slightly more awareness, slightly better timing, because someone was tracking the patterns and reporting them. That the work I could do, the particular ability I had, got used for the community's benefit. That I found the place I fit—not in a single chamber but in the movement between them, not in stillness but in the constant adaptive ranging that the environment demanded and that my nature was suited for.

I would be content with that. With having been the chamber-reader, the ranger, the person whose territory was the whole Hollows and whose purpose was seeing how it changed. With having spent my life in bright dampness and profound silence, tracking water and light through ancient stone, helping people live in a place that seems impossible but is, instead, exactly sufficient.

That's where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be. Moving through the middle chambers in the constant light, marked with my single white line, carrying my notated bones, reading the stone's slow changes and translating them into information the settlement needs. Ranging, always ranging, through the Living Hollows that would be more home to me than any fixed dwelling could ever be.
