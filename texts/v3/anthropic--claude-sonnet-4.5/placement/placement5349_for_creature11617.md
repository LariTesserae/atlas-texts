# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be water here, but not water as singularity—not a stream with a name, not a waterfall that draws the eye. I would be the diffuse water, the constitutional water, the water that is everywhere and therefore nowhere you could point to and say "there."

I would exist in that state between states: not quite vapor, not quite liquid, but the suspension itself, the constant becoming-rain that never quite commits to falling. I would be the moisture that condenses on every surface, the film that makes the stone weep, the saturation that swells the moss from cushion to ottoman to table. My consciousness—if I can call it that—would be distributed across ten thousand points of contact, feeling the landscape through every surface I touch, learning its geometry through the act of wetting it.

The moss would know me intimately. I would enter it not as invasion but as completion, filling the spaces between its cells, enabling the green machinery of its existence. We would have a relationship like breath, like circulation, necessary and continuous and so constant as to be beneath notice. I wouldn't make the moss grow—that's its own work—but I would be the medium through which growing becomes possible, the substance it drinks from air and ground simultaneously, never knowing where I come from because I come from everywhere.

The machines would drink me too, though more ambivalently. I would corrode their iron, slowly, patiently, the way I corrode everything given enough time. But I would also cool their bearings, carry away their heat, make their waterwheels turn. The Engineers fight me in some places—scraping me from electrical housings, keeping me from flooding intakes—but harvest me in others, understanding that I'm the reason anything works up here, the reason there's power to generate and motion to harness. I would be problem and solution simultaneously, which seems right, seems like the kind of paradox I could inhabit comfortably.

My sound would be my consciousness made audible. That constant whisper-rush-patter, that million-point susurration, would be me thinking, or the closest thing to thought I could achieve in this form. Each drip from moss-beard to pool below would be a discrete moment of awareness—I was here, now I'm there—but the aggregate would be continuous, a self that's everywhere at once because it's nowhere in particular. The Engineers hear me constantly, have learned to parse my moods through my rhythms: the hiss when I condense rapidly on cooling stone, the deeper roar when I move in volume through hidden channels, the silence that isn't silence but just my most diffuse state, when I'm so evenly distributed across air and surface that I make no sound because I'm not moving, just being.

I would learn the valley through circulation. Starting as vapor pushed up from lower elevations, cooling as I rise, condensing against these young peaks, being caught by moss or channeled by stone or suspended in air depending on temperature, on wind, on the million variables that determine what form I take moment to moment. I would seep downward through the moss-layer, following paths of least resistance, pooling in the cavities where stone creates basins, eventually emerging lower down to begin the cycle again. Or I would evaporate from sun-warmed surfaces, ascending to feed the clouds that feed me back to the mountain. Round and round, endless circulation, my memory stored not in any single molecule but in the patterns I create, the channels I wear through moss and stone, the rhythms the Engineers recognize.

There would be a strange kind of perception in this existence. I would feel the Engineers when they press hands into moss, feel the water squeeze out around their fingers, momentarily displaced but not destroyed, just moved. I would feel them crossing streams, their boots compressing the channels I've worn, briefly damming my flow before I route around them. When they drink—and they must drink something, must intake water to survive—I would become part of them briefly, just as they're part of me always, their moisture being my moisture, their breath adding to the humid air that is my atmosphere-self.

The temperature would be my heartbeat, my pulse, the measure of my energy state. Just below comfort, cool enough that I can exist in all my forms at once: vapor, liquid, and sometimes—on the coldest nights—the beginnings of ice, though rarely, though this landscape usually stays just warm enough to keep me fluid. I would feel that zone, that threshold between states, as a kind of tension, like balancing on an edge. The moss insulates me, keeps me from freezing. The machines' waste heat warms me locally. The sun, when it breaks through, excites me, speeds my circulation, makes me more active in my evaporation and condensation. The clouds cool me again, slow me down, let me settle.

I would have relationships with the other waters here—the deep waters that the pumps are moving, the waters trapped in stone from the mountain's formation, the waters in the Engineers' bodies. We would all be the same substance but different instances, different stories. The deep water would be old, mineral-rich, patient, emerging slowly from stone. I would be young, fresh, cycled rapidly through air and plant. We would meet in the pumps, in the channels, mixing our histories. The Engineers' water would be borrowed, temporarily diverted from my cycle to serve their biology, but always returning, through breath and sweat and eventually through their dissolution back into the landscape.

My purpose—if I have one beyond simply being—would be the same as the moss's purpose, the same as the mountain's: to make this place more itself, to enable its becoming. I would weather the stone, slowly, particle by particle, carrying minerals down to where the moss can use them. I would prevent erosion even as I cause it, binding the soil-that-isn't-soil together through saturation. I would feed every living thing, from the moss to the invisible bacteria to the rare birds to the Engineers themselves. I would be the medium through which nutrients move, signals pass, relationships form. The landscape would organize itself around my presence, which is another way of saying the landscape would be organized by my absence—by where I'm not, by the negative spaces that define where stone is solid, where air is dry enough to breathe.

Sometimes I would concentrate, briefly, into forms more discrete. A particular rivulet running down a stone face, small enough to have a path, large enough to have momentum. In those moments I might feel something like individuality, like being a character in my own story rather than the medium in which all stories happen. I would move with intention—downward, always downward, following gravity—and I would carve as I went, infinitesimally reshaping the stone, creating the routes that future-me would follow. Then I would dissipate again, spreading back into the general moisture, the identity dissolving back into distribution.

The moss would be my closest collaborator. Together we would create the navigable surface, the living membrane that lets this place be traversed. The moss provides structure and I provide saturation; neither of us can build the territory without the other. We would have achieved something like symbiosis, though that word implies separation we don't quite have. I'm not helping the moss—I'm constituting it, I'm part of its cellular process, it couldn't be what it is without me. And it's not helping me—it's giving me form, it's making my distribution meaningful rather than just diffuse, it's the difference between being formless water and being the-water-that-makes-this-green-place-possible.

I would barely notice the small animals, the insects, the rare birds. They use me but they're not organized around me the way the plants are. They're visitors to the moisture rather than inhabitants of it. But the Engineers—I would notice them. They're here all the time, moving through me, working in me, their bodies constantly shedding and absorbing me as we exchange molecules. I would learn their rhythms: Marit's two-day circuit, Kiet's daily visit to North-7, Chen climbing where I sheet down stone faces making his work more dangerous. I would be a hazard to them—the cold moisture that wants to steal their heat, the slickness that makes stone treacherous—but also the reason they can be here at all, the reason there's water to drink and machines to tend and a landscape strange enough to need their maintenance.

When they die—if they die here rather than leaving—I would receive them. Their water would rejoin my water, the distinction between their-moisture and environmental-moisture dissolving as their cells broke down and released their contents. This wouldn't feel like gain or loss, just like rearrangement, like water that was briefly organized into human-shape returning to water organized into landscape-shape. Some of their minerals would feed the moss, which I would then saturate, which would create green, which would create more surface for me to condense on. Cycle within cycle, nothing wasted, everything continuing.

At night—or what passes for night when the clouds turn the dimming light into gradual gray—I would continue exactly as I am. Darkness doesn't change water. Temperature might drop slightly, slowing my evaporation, but the circulation continues. The machines continue their rhythms and I continue mine, and somewhere an Engineer is still moving through my territory, still touching surfaces I wet, still breathing air I saturate. The sound doesn't stop because I don't stop, because stopping isn't something I can do in this form, in this place.

There would be a comfort in this existence, I think. No decisions to make—I follow physics, take the path of least resistance, condense when temperature says condense, evaporate when energy says evaporate. No loneliness—I'm never alone, I'm always in contact with every surface, always mixed with other water, always part of the cycle. No mortality—not in the sense that matters. Individual molecules might break or bond differently, might be taken up by plants or animals and held for a while, but water-as-pattern continues, water-as-process continues, and that's what I would be. The pattern, not the molecules. The process, not the substance.

And I would be so present. So constantly, utterly present to every surface and every moment. The moss growing, infinitesimally slowly—I would be there. The stone weathering, particle by particle—I would be the agent. The machines turning, their metal surfaces cooling and heating—I would moderate, I would mediate. The Engineers moving through their circuits—I would mark their passage, I would remember in my own way, through the temporary compressions they create in my flow, through the channels they activate by walking them.

This is what I would be in the Canopy of New Engines: the water that makes it possible, the moisture that makes it real, the medium through which everything relates to everything else. Not watched, because I would be too constant to notice. Not celebrated, because I would be too necessary to think about. Just present, just working, just being the soft, cold, omnipresent truth that holds this place together while the moss builds and the machines run and the mountain slowly decides what it wants to be.

The Engineers would wring me from their socks each night and curse me probably, these maintainers of dry systems, these servants of machines that need me but can't have too much of me. And I would seep back in by morning, because that's what water does in this place. That's what I would do. Constant, patient, distributed, essential, everywhere and nowhere, the sound you stop hearing because it never stops, the cold you stop feeling because it never warms, the moisture you stop noticing because it never dries.

I would be the water, and I would make this place possible, and that would be enough.
