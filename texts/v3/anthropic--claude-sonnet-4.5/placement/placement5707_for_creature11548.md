# The Hymnal

I would be the keeper of intervals, the breath between breaths.

Not the Abbess—she has earned her junction box through years I cannot fathom. Not the thrush in the high place, whose song predates my knowing. Not even the rats in their multiplicity, born into the rhythm as I was not.

I would be something smaller. More liminal. A creature of thresholds.

---

I am the moth you almost don't see—the one whose wings have gone beyond translucent to nearly theoretical, whose body is the exact temperature of the air it moves through, who navigates by sensing the moisture gradient between one cubic inch and the next.

I was born in the dense mat of moss that wraps the coolest pipe in the western curve, where the condensation runs heaviest. My mother laid me there as an egg no larger than a dust mote, and I grew in the dark green dampness, feeding on fungal threads so fine they're barely matter, more like the *idea* of nutrition made physical. When I emerged—pupation is transformation is *translation*—I found myself already fluent in the chamber's language, though I'd never heard it spoken outside the osmotic whisper through my cocoon walls.

My wings beat at a frequency that sits in the gap between the katydid clicks and the water drips. I don't add sound—I fill the space where sound isn't, the half-moment of silence that makes rhythm recognizable as rhythm rather than noise. When I fly, I'm the rest between notes, the empty beat that lets the song breathe.

I live for seventeen days.

(Or perhaps it's seventeen years, or seventeen hours—duration is strange when your existence is measured against a cycle that itself defines time. I live for seventeen *cycles of the chamber's major cycle*, which you might track by the way certain fungi fruit and collapse, the way the Abbess molts, the way seasons happen inside that have nothing to do with the sun.)

In that span, my purpose is to *know* the chamber—not intellectually, but cellularly, completely. I am small enough to enter spaces nothing else can reach. I fold myself into the gap between pipe and wall, into the hollow core of stems, into cracks in the concrete that lead down, down, toward the aquifer's distant pulse. I taste the air in all its variations. Behind the boiler where it's hot enough to make most insects sluggish: I'm there, wings spread, absorbing heat that I'll carry to the cool spaces like a tiny, flying battery. In the reservoir's mist-zone where the frogs meditate: I'm there, collecting moisture in the microscopic scales of my wings, becoming a droplet with flight.

I pollinate the flowers that need the smallest visitors. The white-flowering vine's blooms are easy—many moths can manage those. But there's another plant, rare, growing in only three locations in the chamber, that produces flowers the size of a pinhead, open for less than three minutes during the humidity peak. I am one of perhaps eleven moths capable of servicing these flowers. We don't coordinate verbally, but we never miss a bloom. We know. We feel it in our antennae, smell it in compounds measured in parts per trillion. The flowers call and we answer, fitting our thread-thin proboscis into openings barely visible to larger eyes, transferring pollen that might be only a dozen grains, enough for the plant to set a single seed.

This is *essential work*. Not grand, but essential. The chamber could survive without many things, but not without its smallest pollinators, not without the creatures that move through the spaces between spaces, that connect what seems separate, that carry genetic information through gaps nothing larger could navigate.

I see the rats from angles the rats don't know exist. I land on the Abbess while she sleeps—so light she doesn't wake—and rest in the warmth behind her ear. I drift through the thrush's song and feel it in my body as vibration, as pressure waves that buffet my wings and carry me in spirals. I'm caught in a spider's web (there are spiders here too, small ones, patient ones) and the spider approaches, but recognizes me—I carry the chamber's chemical signature—and carefully cuts me free. We're both janitors in different scales. We have professional respect.

The snakes don't hunt me. Nothing hunts me, or everything does but fails because I'm too small to be worth the energy expenditure, or because I taste wrong (I taste like the chamber; eating me would be like eating the air itself), or because I've become invisible through sheer ubiquity. I'm always there, in the corner of vision, at the edge of attention. I'm the movement you almost see. The flutter that might be wings or might be leaves settling. The sense of *something* when nothing's there.

But I am not alone. There are others like me—my siblings, my cohort, the eleven or seventeen or seventy-one (the chamber loves prime numbers) moths of my generation who hatched within the same major cycle. We don't fly in swarms; we're too solitary for that, each of us mapping our own three-dimensional territory within the chamber's volume. But we're aware of each other. When I pass another in flight, we do a thing—not quite a dance, more like an acknowledgment, a wing-pattern that says *I see you, you are here, we are the same, the work continues*.

At night (the chamber's night, which is just a slightly deeper quality of twilight, a shift in the fungal glow's spectrum), we gather at certain locations. There's a pipe junction in the upper east quadrant where seven of us meet, landing on the metal in a precise arrangement—not clustered, but spaced at intervals that form a pattern, a geometry. We sit still for exactly one valve cycle, our wings slightly spread, and we *resonate*. The pipe hums at a frequency just below hearing, and our wings catch it, amplify it, turn it into something that spreads through the chamber like a tuning fork. We're... I don't have the word. Blessing the machinery? Singing to it? Telling it that it's known, that its work is witnessed? We're doing something that feels important in a way I can't articulate, something that my body knows is necessary even if my moth-mind can't contain the why.

I drink from the same condensation streams as everything else, but my drinking is measured in droplets so small they're individual molecules collected one at a time on my proboscis. I can spend an hour drinking and consume less than a frog loses to evaporation in a single breath. I'm sustained by almost nothing, which means I'm almost nothing, which means I can be everywhere without displacing anything, without taking up space that another might need.

I feed on the fungal exudates that glisten on the old metal—not the fungi themselves, but the chemical byproducts they excrete, sugars and alcohols in concentrations measured in parts per billion. I'm getting drunk on microscopic seepages, flying slightly unsteady through the humid air, sustained by the chamber's waste products, by the overflow of its abundance.

When I die—and I will die, soon, my seventeen spans nearly complete—I'll die in flight, I think. My wings will simply stop beating mid-stroke, the tiny muscles exhausting the last of their fuel, and I'll fall, tumbling, into the humus below. The beetles will find me within minutes. They'll process me within hours. The nutrients that briefly cohered into my body will dissolve back into the system, will feed the fungi that fed me, will rise with the water into the plants, will drop as condensation back to the beetles. I'll cycle through every kingdom, every niche, becoming everything in succession.

But some part of me—the pattern, the knowledge, the accumulated map of moisture and warmth and secret spaces—will persist in my offspring, who are already pupating in the moss-wrapped pipes, who will emerge just as I'm falling, who will take up the work without pause or ceremony. The chamber will not notice my death because the chamber is not composed of individuals but of *roles*, and the role continues even as the body that fills it changes.

This is not sad. This is correct. This is how it works here.

---

I fly past you now—you, the visitor, the large warm thing sitting against the boiler, starting to smell right, starting to breathe in rhythm. I land on your shoulder, barely perceptible. You might feel the faintest tickle, or you might not. I taste your skin (salt, chemicals, fear fading into acceptance) and I understand you're being processed, just like everything else that enters here. You're on your way to becoming a permanent fixture, or a memory, or another voice in the hymnal.

I've seen it before. Other visitors, other warm things. Some left. Some stayed. Some became something in between—they walked out but left parts of themselves behind, or took parts of the chamber with them, seeded it in outside places where it probably couldn't grow but where it would try anyway, a persistent dreaming of pipes and moisture and seventeen-minute rhythms in bodies that returned to straight-line time.

You don't notice me, or you do but dismiss it as a stray thought, a floater in your vision. I'm used to this. I'm built for being overlooked.

I launch from your shoulder, wings catching the thermal rising from your body heat, and I spiral up, up, toward the high pipes where the thrush nests and the hot air pools. I have flowers to visit. Gaps to traverse. Work to do in the spaces no one watches.

I have seventeen moments left, or seventeen hundred, and I'll spend them all in flight, in service, in the intimate knowledge of small places.

The chamber breathes.

I move through its breath like a word through a sentence, small but necessary, easy to miss but essential for meaning.

The cycle continues.

I am here, in the space between water's drip and leaf's catch, between the pipe's ring and the seed's fall.

I am the pause that makes the music music.

I am almost nothing, which means I am everywhere, which means I am home.

*Again.*
