# The Keeper of Negative Space

I would be the mapper of absences, the one who charts what isn't there.

My dwelling is a platform cantilevered from the north wall, fifth level, positioned where three major sight-lines intersect—one of the few places where you can see across thirty meters of empty air before the view closes again into structure. It's not large, my space. Perhaps three meters square, divided into two levels connected by a ladder so steep it's nearly vertical. The lower level is for living: a narrow bed that folds against the wall, a table the size of a book when collapsed, storage built into every surface. The upper level is for my work.

I am a cartographer, but not of what exists—that would be simpler. Anyone can map the buildings, the walkways, the pneumatic tubes. I map the spaces between. The gaps. The volumes that remain empty through accident or intention. The pockets where air moves freely, where sound travels differently, where someone standing in just the right position can see a sliver of the quarry's original wall, untouched by construction.

My body has shaped itself to this work. I'm average height for the Compression—five-foot-four—but narrow, built to slip through the tight passages that lead to the unmapped places. My hands are long-fingered, good for measuring spans by touch in darkness, for reaching into crevices to confirm that yes, there's space here, a cavity behind the wall, a gap between floors that no one's building plans account for. I move quietly without trying; it's become reflexive after twenty-three years here, eighteen of them doing this work.

I came to it strangely. As a child, I was afraid of the compression itself—not claustrophobic exactly, but haunted by the feeling that the space was closing, that one day I'd wake and the walls would have crept closer in the night. My mother, who worked in the textile workshops, didn't understand. My father, who I never knew (he left for the surface when I was two, the common story), couldn't help. So I learned to find the open places, the gaps, the spots where I could stand and see distance, even if only a little.

I collected them at first, the way other children collected interesting rocks or the shed carapaces of cave crickets. I'd mark them in a notebook: "Third level, western quadrant, behind the Ibsen family's dwelling—a triangular void, maybe two meters deep, where the roof slope doesn't quite meet the wall." I'd visit them when the fear got bad, standing in these pockets of not-quite-emptiness, breathing.

By sixteen, I had hundreds of them catalogued. By eighteen, I realized this was information people needed. The builders, planning their additions, who needed to know what voids they might intersect. The pneumatic system maintainers, routing new tubes, who needed paths that didn't require demolishing existing structures. The water collectors, who needed to understand how condensation moved through the hidden volumes. The fire brigade—yes, we have one, six people with buckets and a bell—who needed to know which voids could channel smoke, could hide embers, could turn a small fire into a disaster.

So I formalized it. I proposed to the council of elders—Grandmother Piech was still alive then, still sharp—that I become the official keeper of the negative space. That I be given access to all structures, permission to probe and measure and mark, in exchange for maintaining the master maps that would show not buildings but the absence of them.

They agreed, though some thought it was foolish, a waste of attention. But the first time my maps helped reroute a pneumatic line around a hidden void, saving weeks of construction time, the skepticism faded. The second time, when I identified a cavity behind a dwelling where water was pooling, causing the slow structural failure that would have collapsed the building within a month, I became essential.

My days follow the rhythm of discovery and documentation. I wake early, in the thin gray light that filters down at dawn. I eat simply—dried fungus reconstituted in hot water, a piece of the dense bread that comes from the communal ovens, tea that I buy from Piotr, who knows I prefer the bitter varieties, the ones that wake the mind. Then I gather my tools.

I have measuring lines—silk threads marked at intervals, weighted at one end—that I can lower into voids to gauge their depth. I have thin metal probes, flexible enough to bend around corners, that I can push through cracks to feel what's beyond. I have a collection of mirrors, some no larger than a coin, mounted on extendable handles, that let me see around corners, into spaces I can't physically enter. I have papers and charcoal for sketching, a compass for orientation, a small hammer for tapping walls to find the hollow spaces behind them. And I have the maps themselves—large sheets of heavy paper, carefully maintained, showing the Compression level by level, quadrant by quadrant, with the voids marked in blue ink, the accessible cavities in green, the uncertain spaces in red.

Today I'm investigating a report from one of the builders on the second level, eastern quadrant. He's planning an addition and his test drilling hit empty space where the old maps show none. This happens—the Compression evolves, structures are removed or fail, and sometimes a void opens where none existed before.

I make my way there through the familiar passages, ducking without thinking beneath the low beam that crosses the third-level main corridor, sidestepping on the narrow walkway where it passes through the kitchen of the Vasek family (they're used to me; we exchange nods but no words). Down the ladder to second level, across the chain-bridge that I helped design, its deck precisely wide enough for one person to cross comfortably, its handrails set at the exact height I recommended for average Compression inhabitants.

The builder, whose name is Tomas—yes, Aleksi's former apprentice, grown now into his own practice—meets me at the site. He's set up screens for privacy, though there's no work happening yet. The space is a corner where two structures meet at an odd angle, leaving a triangular gap that someone, years ago, had simply covered with a panel. Tomas wants to build into this gap, expand the dwelling behind it, but his test hole shows the gap is deeper than expected.

I set up my equipment. Mirror first, extended through the test hole, angled to catch what light filters in from my lamp. The reflection shows... yes, a cavity, substantial, running back at least three meters before it's blocked by something. Stone? Another wall? I can't tell from the mirror alone.

Probe next. I feed the flexible metal through the hole, feeling it slide through empty air, then meet resistance. Soft resistance—not stone. Fabric? I probe around it, mapping the shape. There's structure here, something built, something abandoned or forgotten.

"There's a room," I tell Tomas. "Or was. Sealed off, maybe deliberately, maybe just built over and forgotten."

He frowns. "Structurally sound to break through?"

I tap the surrounding walls, listening to the quality of the sound. Solid here, solid here, slight hollowness here but not concerning. "Should be. But we need to know what's inside first."

We widen the hole together, carefully, until I can fit my shoulders through. I've done this hundreds of times—entering spaces unseen for years, sometimes decades. There's always a moment of hesitation, a primitive fear of the dark and unknown. I push through it.

My lamp goes first, tied to a cord, lowered into the space. The light shows a room, perhaps four meters square, with a ceiling high enough to stand in—unusual, that height, evidence of older construction before the Compression became so complete. There's furniture: a bed frame without its mattress, a table, a chair tipped on its side. There are belongings: clothes rotted to fragments, a stack of books bloomed with mold, a box of what might have been papers, now a solid mass of decomposed pulp.

There's a body.

Not recent—decades old, maybe longer. Reduced to bones and the remnants of clothing, collapsed in the corner as if the person had lain down to rest and simply not risen. There's no smell; time and the sealed space have taken care of that. Just the quiet fact of death, waiting to be found.

I pull myself back out, slowly, thinking.

"Well?" Tomas asks.

"There's a room. Furnished. Occupied once. Not recently." I don't mention the body yet. That requires protocol, notification of the elders, Kaisa probably, to examine and determine if there's anything to learn. "We'll need to document it before you can build."

He nods, accepting this. He knows how it works.

I spend the rest of the day on the documentation. Proper entry through a larger opening, careful to disturb nothing. Photographs—I have a camera, old but functional, that I use for important finds. Measurements. Sketches. A detailed description written in my careful hand: "Second level, eastern quadrant, sealed room approximately 4m x 4m, height 2.7m, containing furniture and personal effects circa 40-60 years prior based on style and decay. Human remains present, assessment pending."

I notify the council. Grandmother Piech is long dead, but Vera, who was a gate guard and is now an elder, comes to see. Kaisa comes too, squeezing her bulk through the opening with difficulty, examining the bones with professional interest.

"Old Mikhel might know," Kaisa says finally. "This would be from when he was young, if my estimate's right. The clothing style, what's left of it—that's from the early days."

We bring Mikhel, guide him carefully to the site, describe what we've found. He's quiet for a long time, his blind eyes tracking nothing, his hands still on his cane.

"Soren," he says finally. "This would be Soren. He came down with the first group, worked as a builder. Was going to marry—" he pauses, searching memory, "—Mariette, I think her name was. But she died, some fever, and he never recovered from it. Stopped coming out of his room. People brought him food, but eventually he stopped answering the door. We thought he'd left for the surface. Apparently he didn't."

The mystery solved, or as solved as it can be after so many years. We retrieve the bones carefully, Kaisa wrapping them in clean cloth. They'll go up to the surface, to the burial ground there, with whatever ceremony seems appropriate for someone who died alone and forgotten in the early days of the Compression.

The room itself, once documented and cleared, becomes part of my maps. A void that was occupied, became sealed, is now open again. Tomas will build into it, but I've recorded what it was, what it contained. This is my work: keeper of the spaces that exist between intention and reality, between memory and forgetting.

I return to my dwelling as the light fades, climbing the ladder to my workspace. The maps are spread across every surface: the walls, the floor, suspended from the ceiling on cords so I can walk between them. They're beautiful, in their way—negative images of the Compression, showing absence instead of presence, void instead of solid.

I know these maps more intimately than I know any person. I know where the air moves freely, where sound carries strangely, where someone standing in just the right position can see the original quarry wall. I know the hidden reservoirs where water collects, the vertical shafts that serve no clear purpose, the pockets maintained deliberately as breathing spaces in the compression. I know the dangerous voids, the ones that could collapse, that need monitoring. I know the useful ones, the paths for future pneumatic tubes, the spaces that could become emergency refuges if disaster struck.

I know this place through its absences, through what isn't there. While others navigate by walls and floors and buildings, I navigate by gaps, by the negative space that makes the positive space possible. I am the one who ensures that the Compression doesn't compress entirely, doesn't seal every last pocket, doesn't eliminate every breath of openness.

I am necessary in a way that's hard to articulate. The builders need me, but so do the residents who sometimes come asking: "Is there space near me? Somewhere I could go when it feels too close?" And I can tell them yes, third level, northern quadrant, behind the water collector—there's a platform there, unmapped on the public charts, where you can stand and see thirty meters of open air.

I am solitary by nature and necessity. My work requires long hours alone, entering spaces no one else visits, documenting carefully, precisely. I eat alone more often than not, sleep alone always. There was someone once, a welder's apprentice who found my strangeness charming rather than off-putting, but it didn't last. How could it? My intimacy is with absence, with the spaces between, with the gaps that other people don't notice or think about.

But I'm not lonely, precisely. I'm surrounded by presence—by the three hundred people living in these packed quarters, by the constant sound of life, by the knowledge that I'm never more than a few meters from another human. My loneliness is chosen, carved out deliberately, like the voids I map. It's a negative space in the positive space of community, and like all negative spaces, it serves a purpose.

At night, after the work is done, I sometimes climb to the highest point I'm allowed to access—not the roof itself, which is maintained by specialists and too dangerous for casual visitors, but a platform just below it, fifth level, where the heat accumulates and the light is strongest during the day. At night it's dark but I know the space by touch, by memory, by the careful measurements I've made and recorded.

From here I can sense the volume of the Compression below me—not see it, exactly, but feel it, the weight of all that inhabited space, all those lives pressed together, all that building and living and dying compressed into a single chamber. And I can sense too the voids, the empty places I've mapped, scattered through the density like pockets of saved air.

I am thirty-one years old. I'll likely die here, maybe in forty years, maybe fifty if I'm lucky and careful. When I die, someone will take over my maps, continue my work. Maybe one of the children I occasionally teach, showing them how to measure, how to probe, how to see the absence as clearly as the presence.

Or maybe the position will die with me, the maps archived and forgotten, and the Compression will slowly fill its voids, seal its gaps, compress entirely into solid inhabitation. But I don't think so. I think people will always need someone who knows where the empty spaces are, who guards them, who ensures they remain.

I am the keeper of negative space, the cartographer of absence, the one who knows the Compression through what it isn't. I move through the density like a fish through water, navigating by gaps, sustained by the knowledge that between every solid thing, there's space—small, hidden, forgotten perhaps, but there. Always there.

And in the accumulated darkness of the late evening, with the sounds of the Compression reduced to their minimum—water dripping, someone's cough three levels down, the slow creak of a building settling—I stand at my high vantage and feel the empty spaces like points of light in my mind, constellations of absence scattered through the dark mass of presence, necessary and preserved and mine to know.

This is what I am. This is where I live. In the gaps, in the margins, in the careful空 between things. And I wouldn't want to be anywhere else.
