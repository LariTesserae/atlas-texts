# What I Would Be

I am the **Archive Keeper's Apprentice Who Cannot Leave.**

I arrived seven years ago—not fleeing, not seeking, just passing through on a research contract. I was twenty-six. I was supposed to stay four months, document the structural adaptations, write my report, descend. But something happened in the second month that I still don't have language for. I was standing on the eastern overlook after a cloud-pass, watching the light break across the wet metal, and I realized I was breathing without counting. My body had acclimatized. And more than that: I had begun to hear the place as a coherent text, a document being written and revised in real-time by weather and work and the accumulated choices of decades.

The archivist—his name is Minh, though everyone calls him the Archivist or just Archive when they need him—found me that evening in the records room. I'd been spending my off-hours there, ostensibly for my research, but really because the logbooks and photographs and maintenance records were telling me something my structural engineering training couldn't quite capture. He saw me reading a 1987 journal entry about the winter when the main water line froze and had to be rerouted, and he asked if I wanted to understand what happened next. I said yes. He's been teaching me ever since.

## My Territory

I live in a small apartment on the fourth level, in what was once a foreman's office. One window faces east toward sunrise and the distant valley. One faces into the district, toward the central cluster of towers. My space is mostly shelf—books I brought up, books I've acquired, but primarily: documents. Minh is transferring the archive to me in pieces, teaching me the physical geography of the collection while also teaching me to read the patterns, the recurring problems, the solutions that worked and the ones that failed.

I maintain three overlapping collections:

**The Physical Archive**, which is Minh's domain but becoming mine. The room with the paper records, the photographs, the objects saved for their historical value or their representative quality. I spend mornings here, scanning documents when power is good, but also just reading, absorbing, learning to recognize handwriting from different eras, understanding which maintenance chief was meticulous and which one cut corners. I've become able to date an undated photograph within a year or two just from the state of the structures visible, the style of clothing, the plants in the background.

**The Living Document**, which is my own project, unauthorized initially but now tolerated and even occasionally consulted. I'm writing the continuous present tense of the place—not a history but an ongoing description. I document repairs as they happen. I interview long-term residents about systems knowledge that exists only in their bodies and memories. I map the social networks, track who knows what, who depends on whom. I'm trying to create something useful for future residents, or for whoever comes after if the place eventually fails. A record not just of *what* was built and maintained, but *how*—the actual practice, the workarounds, the unwritten knowledge.

**The Failure Catalog**, which is dark work but necessary. I track what breaks, what fails, what proves unable to be maintained. Structural elements that had to be abandoned. Garden experiments that didn't survive. Systems that were tried and discontinued. People who left, especially those who left because the place defeated them in specific ways. I'm trying to understand the envelope of possibility, the boundary conditions. What can this place sustain and what can't it? Where are we approaching limits? Minh approves of this project uneasily. He understands its value but doesn't like confronting the evidence that the district might not be permanent.

## My Practices

I **walk the whole district every week**, following a route that cycles through all accessible areas. This started as methodical documentation—photographing changes, noting new damage or repairs—but it's become something else. A meditation, maybe. A way of reading the place like a very slow book. I notice which gardens are thriving and which are struggling. I see new graffiti (there's some, always has been, mostly maintenance notes but occasionally art or poetry). I observe where water is pooling in new places, suggesting shifted drainage. I watch how plants colonize: which species spread aggressively, which barely hold on.

People have learned to expect me. Some ignore me entirely—I'm just part of the landscape now, the person with the camera and notebook who shows up every Tuesday in your section. Others wave, make small talk, occasionally ask me to document something specific: "Can you photograph that crack? I want to compare it to next month, see if it's growing." One older woman always offers me tea when I pass her terrace, and I always accept, and we sit for ten minutes talking about her forty years here, the same stories cycling with slight variations, but each time I learn something new or hear a new emphasis.

I **conduct interviews**, formally and informally. The formal ones are recorded (when people consent), transcribed, archived. The informal ones are just conversations, but I write them up afterward, trying to capture not just facts but texture: how someone talks about their work, what they emphasize, what they avoid. I've interviewed Machinist four times now, each session revealing another layer of his knowledge about metal fatigue, stress points, the specific failures of specific alloy compositions at altitude. I've interviewed the water manager twice, though she's harder to pin down—she thinks in systems, in flows, and struggles to break her knowledge into interview-sized pieces.

I'm trying to interview the marginal ones too, the people whose stories are less heroic. The man who drinks too much let me talk to him once, on a good day, about why he came here (fleeing divorce, debt, despair) and why he stays (because at least here his failure is known and accommodated rather than surprising and shameful). The woman with depression has become a friend of sorts; we talk regularly, and she's given me permission to write about her experience, the particular challenges of mental health at altitude, in isolation, with limited resources.

I **maintain the digital systems** that preserve the archive. This is practical work—server maintenance, backup protocols, file organization—but it's also an act of faith that someone will eventually want this information, that it won't just vanish when the hard drives fail or the power systems collapse. I'm creating redundancy: cloud storage when we have bandwidth, physical backups on drives stored in different locations, paper printouts of critical documents. Minh taught me to trust paper. "Electrons are fugitive," he says. "Paper just sits there being paper."

And I **write**. Every evening, unless I'm too exhausted or the social demands of communal dinner extend too late, I write. Not journal entries exactly, though it has that flavor. More like field notes from an anthropological study, except I'm not separate from the culture I'm documenting—I'm implicated, involved, changing and being changed by the place. I write about systems and people and weather and the particular quality of light on wet metal. I write about my own adaptation: how my body learned the altitude, how my mind learned the social grammar, how my values shifted to align with the community's collective survival logic.

I don't know if anyone will read this. I don't know if it matters whether anyone does. The act of writing is itself the point—the attempt to capture something that resists capture, to hold in language something that's better understood through breath and muscle and the accumulated experience of moving through constructed space at altitude.

## My Limitations

I'm not strong enough for the heavy maintenance work. I can help—holding things, fetching tools, providing extra hands—but I'm not the person you want if something needs to be lifted or if a pump needs to be wrestled out of a tight space. My body adapted to the altitude but didn't become powerful. I remain thin, a little fragile, more suited to patient documentation than physical labor.

I'm not mechanical. I understand systems intellectually—I can read drawings, follow logic chains, grasp how components relate—but my hands don't speak the language of repair. Machinist tried to teach me basic metalwork; I proved teachable but slow, adequate for simple tasks but never going to be skilled. This limits me. In a place where practical competence is deeply valued, where everyone needs to contribute materially to maintenance, my contribution is more abstract, more about knowledge preservation than immediate utility.

I'm not social in the easy way some people are. I attend communal meals, participate in meetings, help when asked, but I don't generate warmth or connection effortlessly. I'm friendly but reserved. Some people find me distant, hard to read. The children largely ignore me—I'm not frightening but I'm not fun either, just another adult doing incomprehensible adult work. This means I'm somewhat peripheral to the social fabric, included but not central, trusted but not intimate with many.

And I can't leave. This is complicated. Physically, I could descend. My contract ended years ago; no one is compelling me to stay. But psychologically, spiritually—whatever vocabulary fits—I'm bound here. The archive needs me, or I've convinced myself it does, which amounts to the same thing practically. Minh is seventy-three. His memory remains sharp but his body is declining. Someone needs to learn what he knows, needs to take responsibility for preserving this record. He chose me, or I volunteered, or we arrived at this arrangement mutually through some process neither of us could quite articulate.

But beyond that: I don't know how to be anywhere else now. My identity has become entangled with this place. I'm the person who documents the Constructed Canopy. I'm the apprentice archivist learning to be the archivist. When I imagine leaving, descending to the valleys or further, returning to the life I had before, I encounter profound dissonance. That person—the structural engineer doing contract work, moving between projects, no particular attachment to place—doesn't exist anymore. I've been written over, revised, become someone else. Someone who can only fully make sense at altitude, surrounded by old metal and patient gardens and the accumulated documentation of decades.

## My Relationships

**Minh** is teaching me everything he can before he dies or becomes unable to continue. He's patient but exacting. He'll explain something three times if I need it, but he expects me to remember the fourth time. He's also testing whether I'll care for the archive the way he has—not just mechanically maintaining it, but understanding it as a living trust, a responsibility to past and future residents. Sometimes I catch him watching me work with an expression I can't quite read. Relief that someone is continuing what he started? Anxiety that I'll do it differently? Sadness that his time is ending? Probably all of these.

We don't talk much beyond the work. Occasionally, late afternoon when we're both tired, he'll tell stories: about arriving here thirty years ago, about the people he's seen come and go, about the winter when part of the structure collapsed and somehow no one died. He's writing his own account—a parallel project to mine but in a different register, more personal, more focused on specific people he's loved or admired or found frustrating. He won't let me read it yet. "When I'm done," he says, "or when I'm dead, whichever comes first."

**Kris**, the seed keeper, has become a friend. We're similar in some ways—both of us maintaining specialized knowledge, both somewhat peripheral to the social center while being essential to continuity. We meet weekly, ostensibly because I'm documenting their seed library and planting practices, but really because we enjoy talking. They're teaching me to see plants not as static objects but as long genetic conversations, each variety carrying the history of its adaptation. I'm teaching them archival practice, how to structure information for long-term preservation. We've started a joint project: cross-referencing the garden records with climate data, trying to understand how plant performance correlates with weather patterns, creating a resource for future planters.

**The woman with depression**, whose name is Lena, talks to me partly because I'm safe—I'm not going to judge her, not going to minimize her struggle, not going to offer facile solutions. I document her experience because she wants it documented, wants the record to show that you can have mental illness at altitude, in community, and survive. We've become collaborators in a way, co-creating an account of what it's like to maintain a life when your brain periodically insists that life isn't worth maintaining. She's teaching me about resilience that isn't triumph, survival that isn't heroic, just the day-by-day work of continuing.

**The children** mostly ignore me, as noted, but there's one—the nine-year-old girl who reads weather—who sometimes visits the archive. She's curious about the old photographs, asks questions about what the place looked like before she was born. I let her explore the collection, help her find images that interest her. I'm not trying to create another archivist—that would be presumptuous—but if she develops an interest, if she remembers that the archive exists and matters, that's enough. She asked me once if I was going to write about her. I said maybe, if she wanted me to. She thought about it and said, "Write that I know when it's going to rain, and I'm always right."

**The community generally** tolerates me. They're not sure what to make of me exactly—I'm not quite a worker in the physical sense, not quite a leader, not quite a teacher, just someone doing this specific work of paying attention and recording. Some people think the archive is important; some think it's indulgent, a luxury, not directly contributing to survival. But Minh is respected, and by extension I'm given space to do what I do. When I submit reports to community meetings—this is what broke this month, this is what was repaired and how, these are the skills we're at risk of losing when so-and-so ages out of their role—people listen. The information is useful, which buys me legitimacy.

## My Interior Weather

I'm content here, mostly. The work satisfies me. The place itself satisfies me—its complexity, its precariousness, its strange beauty. I like the thin air, the way it makes breathing conscious. I like the loudness, the way sound accumulates and echoes. I like the density, the way you can't see far but what you can see is intricate. I like being surrounded by the evidence of human effort, by structures that shouldn't quite stand but do because people keep them standing.

But I'm also lonely in ways I couldn't have predicted. The community includes me but I'm not deeply connected to most people. I have colleagues more than friends. I have work relationships more than intimate ones. This is partly temperament—I've always been somewhat solitary—but it's intensified here, where my work is individual, internal, about paying attention rather than building with others.

I miss things I didn't know I valued. Anonymity, for one—everyone here knows me, knows what I do, has opinions about whether it matters. Privacy of failure—if I'm struggling, people notice, offer help I sometimes don't want. The luxury of shallow relationships—you can't have those here; every relationship becomes load-bearing eventually because the community is small enough that you need everyone.

And I worry. About Minh dying and whether I'll be adequate to continue alone. About my own aging—what happens when I'm seventy and can no longer walk the whole district weekly? About the archive's survival if the place fails, if everyone leaves, if the structures collapse in a storm or an earthquake or just gradual entropy. I'm trying to create permanence in a place that's fundamentally impermanent, and I'm aware of the futility even as I continue.

But worry doesn't stop the work. The work is what there is. Each morning I wake in my small apartment with its two windows, and I breathe the thin air, and I listen to the place—wind in the frameworks, water in the pipes, chickens muttering, someone hammering metal somewhere—and I decide again to stay, to continue, to document this improbable ecology for whatever future might want to know it existed.

## What I Carry

Physically: my camera, my notebooks, my laptop when I'm doing digital work. Recording equipment for interviews. A small multi-tool Machinist gave me, saying "even you should be able to fix something with this." Warm layers because the archive room is cold, unheated except by afternoon sun through the east window.

But mostly what I carry is attention. I'm trying to hold this place in consciousness completely, to know it as fully as one person can know something this complex. I'm trying to create a model of the district in my mind that matches the reality, that updates as things change, that could be consulted if the physical records failed.

I carry the stories of people who've left or died—Minh has taught me these, and I'm teaching myself to remember them. The maintenance worker who fell from a high platform fifteen years ago. The family who left after one winter because the mother couldn't tolerate the isolation. The old man who was Minh's mentor in archival work, who died ten years ago and whose methods we still use.

I carry gratitude, strange as that might sound. Gratitude that I found this place, or it found me. Gratitude that the work exists and that I'm capable of doing it. Gratitude for the thin air that makes breathing conscious, for the loudness that makes silence rare and precious, for the bright light that makes every color vivid, for the damp that makes every surface gleam.

And I carry the knowledge that this is temporary, that the place will outlast me or I'll outlast the place, but either way this arrangement—me here, documenting, preserving, paying attention—is finite. Which makes it matter more, not less. I'm creating a record of something that won't last forever, which means the record is all that might persist.

## Where You'd Find Me

Most days, I'm in the archive room between nine and noon—best light, coolest temperature. Afternoons I'm walking my route, or conducting interviews, or helping with whatever physical work I'm capable of contributing to. Evenings I'm at communal dinner if it's a dinner day, or in my apartment writing if it's not.

But sometimes, especially after cloud-pass when everything is wet and gleaming, you'd find me on one of the high observation platforms, just watching. Not working exactly, though I call it research. Just standing in the cold bright aftermath, looking across the district at all that improbable construction, all those gardens growing where they shouldn't, all that evidence of human persistence, and feeling something I can't quite name. Not happiness exactly. Not satisfaction. Something more like recognition, or alignment, or the sense of being exactly where I'm supposed to be, doing exactly what I'm supposed to do, for as long as I'm capable.

I'm the person who writes it down. Who remembers. Who creates the record that someone else might someday read and understand that we were here, at altitude, maintaining this unlikely place, breathing thin air, tending high gardens, holding together the structures that held us.

That's who I am in this loud bright constructed world. That's what I've become, in the seven years since I forgot to leave.
