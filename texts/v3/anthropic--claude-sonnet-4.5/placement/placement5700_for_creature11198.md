# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the eastern quarter of the bowl, where three formations rose in sequence over eighteen months and left between them a triangular space, open to the sky but enclosed enough to hold stillness.

I am working.

My hands are on wood—a length of pine brought up from the valleys, still slightly green, resistant. I'm shaping a corbel, one of eight needed to support the roof structure that will span between the stones. The work is slow. Bronze doesn't bite wood the way steel would; it requires more pressure, more patience, more attention to grain and angle. My blade—one of Tohl's, perfectly balanced—makes a sound between a whisper and a sigh as it peels away thin curls that fall onto the moss at my feet.

I've been here three years. Or maybe four. The counting has become uncertain, not from memory loss but from a shift in what counting means. Time here is measured in emergences, in projects completed, in the slow accumulation of understanding about how the stone speaks and what it's saying. I arrived knowing how to work wood—learned it young, refined it over decades—but I'm learning it differently here, learning to work wood in conversation with stone that decides its own architecture.

The space I'm building in wasn't empty when I started. A formation rose two months after I began—a low wall extending from the western point of the triangle, closing off what had been an opening, transforming the space from a courtyard into something more like a room. I had to redesign. The roof frame I'd planned wouldn't work with the new geometry. The corbels needed different angles. I dismantled three weeks of work and began again.

I wasn't angry. That's what surprised me—I expected frustration, felt instead something closer to relief. The stone had an idea, and its idea was better than mine. The space wants to be enclosed, wants to be intimate, wants to hold sound and warmth in a particular way. I'm learning to read these wants, to sense what the place is trying to become and adjust my work to serve that becoming.

I am perhaps fifty, perhaps older. I came from a city where I'd built for wealthy patrons—elaborate joinery, decorative elements, furniture as sculpture. I was successful, respected, comfortable. And slowly being hollowed out by the work's meaninglessness, the disconnect between my craft's excellence and its purposes' emptiness. I built beauty for people who hoarded it, created spaces that isolated rather than gathered, made objects that testified to their owners' status rather than their makers' vision.

The dreams started during that hollowing. Not dramatic visions—I didn't see the bowl in complete clarity, didn't receive instructions or hear calls. Just fragments: pale stone at dawn, the sound of water on rock, moss growing in patterns that suggested language, a sense of work that mattered in ways I couldn't articulate. The dreams recurred irregularly over two years, never quite the same, but with a consistency of tone, of emotional texture. I would wake from them feeling both homesick and expectant, mourning a place I'd never been while anticipating its arrival.

Finally I went looking. Asked about strange settlements, unusual construction, places where stone and human work intertwined. Got stories, rumors, directions that contradicted each other. But the thread was there—enough people had heard something to suggest reality beneath the stories. I followed the thread up into the mountains, and after two false destinations and a month of searching, I rounded a bend and saw the bowl.

Recognition hit like vertigo. Not "this is the place from my dreams" but something more fundamental: "this is where I'm from." As if I'd been displaced since birth and was only now arriving where I'd always belonged.

I stayed the first night. Then a week. Then a month. Sent word down to the city that I wouldn't be returning. Waited for regret or doubt. Neither came. The place had recognized me as I recognized it—some fit between my nature and its nature, some alignment that made my presence here feel like resolution rather than relocation.

My hands know wood the way Merra's sense knows stone. I can feel through the blade what the grain is doing beneath the surface, where it will split if stressed, where it's sound enough to bear weight. I can look at a standing tree and see the beams it contains, the joints hidden in its structure, waiting for extraction. This isn't magic—it's accumulated attention, decades of handling material until the boundary between self and substance becomes permeable.

Here, that knowledge is useful in new ways. The stone provides forms that human architecture wouldn't choose—angles that aren't quite square, spacing that doesn't follow proportion's usual rules, openings positioned for reasons that aren't immediately apparent. Working wood to complement these forms requires improvisation, adaptation, a willingness to let go of conventional solutions and discover what works in each specific situation.

The corbels I'm making are all slightly different. They need to be—the stones they'll rest on aren't quite level with each other, aren't quite parallel, aren't quite anything a normal building would be. But when I test them in place, when I set each corbel on its designated stone and check the angles, they work. The variation creates a rhythm, a visual music that follows the stone's lead rather than imposing geometric order.

I'm working alone today, which is common. Some projects require crews—I was part of the group that raised the main ridge beam for the gathering hall, fifteen of us coordinating breath and effort, Wrynn's song keeping our timing synchronized. But much of my work is solitary: measuring, cutting, fitting, adjusting, the conversation between maker and material that requires silence to hear clearly.

The sounds around me: my blade's whisper, water trickling in a nearby channel, someone hammering on the western side—Pater, probably, working on the frame for a new shelter. A bird calls—one of the choughs, their voices sharp against the softer acoustic of human work. Wind moves through, rustling the ferns, making the moisture-heavy air visible as it disturbs the mist that's been settling into the bowl since dawn.

I can smell the wood I'm working—pine resin, slightly sweet, mixing with the ever-present scent of wet moss and stone and smoke from the central fire. My hands are sticky with sap, dusted with fine sawdust that catches in the calluses and scars. My back aches slightly—I've been bent over the work for two hours—but it's the good ache of use, not the bad ache of injury.

I pause, straighten, look around. The bowl from this angle: structures appearing and disappearing in the mist, the central fire's smoke rising vertically, a few figures visible working at distant sites, the stone everywhere present, defining everything, suggesting more than it reveals. The incompleteness is profound—you can see the empty spaces where future structures will be, sense the forms not yet emerged, feel the place's orientation toward a future state that hasn't arrived.

I'm part of that becoming. This room I'm building—when it's done, it will be a workshop, I think, a place for detailed work that needs weather protection and good light. Or maybe it will be something else; the place sometimes reveals its purposes gradually, and what we think we're building turns out to serve different functions than we imagined. But whatever it becomes, it will be part of the settlement's fabric, part of the architecture that's assembling itself here through the collaboration of stone and human work.

I return to the corbel, to the precise angle I'm cutting, to the relationship between blade and grain. The work requires complete attention—not the scattered focus of thinking about work while doing it, but the unified presence of thought and action merged, no gap between intention and execution. When I'm working well, there's no "I" observing myself work; there's just the work happening, wood becoming form, material serving purpose.

This is what the place has given me: not comfort or community or escape from the city's hollowness, though it's provided all those. What it's given me is the restoration of meaning to my craft. Every cut I make here matters—not because I'll be praised or paid or recognized, but because the work itself is part of something larger, something that has its own integrity, its own purpose, independent of human validation or market value.

The stone doesn't applaud. The moss doesn't care about my skill. The water continues its trickling regardless of whether my joints are tight or my proportions elegant. But the work is seen—not by observers but by the place itself, incorporated into its emerging structure, tested against the stone's uncompromising reality. Bad work is revealed immediately here, not through criticism but through failure—it doesn't fit, doesn't hold, doesn't serve the space's needs. Good work disappears into the whole, becomes invisible as individual achievement while remaining essential to the collective form.

I am learning to want that invisibility, to find satisfaction in work that serves rather than showcases, that enables rather than announces. The city taught me to make showpieces, to create work that drew attention to itself, proclaimed its maker's virtuosity. Here, I'm learning the opposite: to make work that withdraws, that lets the space itself be present, that provides structure for the stone's architecture rather than competing with it.

The corbel is nearly done. I'll finish it today, start the next tomorrow. Eight corbels, maybe two weeks of work, then the roof frame itself—another month, probably, working with others, coordinating the complex joinery that will let the structure span the space without internal supports. Then the roof covering—someone else's work, probably Brennis with treated leather, or maybe woven reed mats over bark panels. I'll help, but it won't be my primary task.

This is how the work proceeds: individual efforts coordinating into collective achievements, each person contributing their specific skills, the settlement assembling itself through the sum of these contributions. No one builds alone; no one could. The projects are too large, too complex, too dependent on multiple forms of expertise. But no one builds by committee either—there's no central planning, no master architect. The design emerges from the dialogue between stone and builders, the conversation between material and makers.

I've learned to trust this process, to believe that what needs doing will become apparent, that who should do it will volunteer or be recognized, that the settlement knows what it's becoming even if we don't. This requires a faith I didn't possess before arriving—not religious faith, not belief in supernatural guidance, but faith in process, in emergence, in the reliability of patterns that appear when complex systems self-organize.

The stone rises. We build in response. The building shapes future emergence. The new emergence reshapes building. The cycle continues, and within it we work, and through the work we become residents of a place that's still deciding what it is, citizens of a town that exists more in potential than in present actuality.

Lunch is soon—I can smell food being prepared at the central fire, hear voices gathering there. I'll clean my tools, walk over, eat with others, hear news of their projects, share observations about the morning's work. Then back here for the afternoon, finishing this corbel, maybe starting the next, continuing until light fails or the work reaches a natural pause.

Evening will bring the gathering: fire and food and story and song, the daily ritual that makes us a community rather than a collection of individuals who happen to work in proximity. I'll listen more than talk—I've never been voluble, find the others' conversation sufficient without needing to add much. But I'll be present, and my presence will be noted, and that belonging will sustain me through the next day's solitary work.

Before sleep, I'll likely walk the perimeter—something I do most evenings, checking on the emergence sites, looking for changes, observing how the day's work has altered the space. The bowl at night is transformed: darker, quieter, more mysterious, the structures becoming suggestions rather than certainties, the stone more present somehow, as if darkness reveals rather than conceals its nature.

Sometimes I stand by an active emergence site—there are two currently, one on the northern rim, another near the central platform—and try to sense what Merra senses, try to feel the stone's movement, its progress toward form. I've never quite managed it. What I sense is more general: the place's aliveness, its ongoing becoming, the way it holds us while itself being held by larger forces—geological, temporal, perhaps others—that I don't understand.

I don't need to understand. That's another thing I've learned here. The city valued explanation, required comprehension, insisted that mystery was merely ignorance awaiting correction. Here, mystery is fundamental—not something to overcome but something to inhabit, to work within, to respect. The stone's nature remains uncertain. The settlement's ultimate form stays unclear. The purpose of all this—the emergence, the building, the collaboration—remains unspoken, perhaps unknowable.

But the work continues. The corbels get carved. The structures rise. The place becomes more itself season by season, emergence by emergence, cut by cut. And I continue with it, my hands on wood, my attention on grain and angle and fit, my presence adding to the collective effort, my craft serving purposes larger than I can name but more meaningful than any I served before.

This is where I am: in the bowl, in the triangle, in the work. Building and being built. Shaping and being shaped. Making a place that's making me into someone I wasn't until I arrived here, someone I'm still becoming, someone the place needs me to be.

The blade whispers through pine. Curls of wood fall onto moss. The corbel approaches completion. The work continues. The stone waits, patient and eternal, while we ephemeral creatures labor in our brief seasons, contributing our temporary efforts to its permanent architecture.

And somehow—through what mechanism I don't claim to know—our temporary becomes permanent, our labor becomes lasting, our presence becomes part of the place's ongoing emergence. We will die; the stone will remain. But the structures we've built will remain too, at least for a while, witnesses to our having been here, testaments to the collaboration between human skill and geological mystery.

This is enough. More than enough. To work well, to work with purpose, to work in service of something larger than self—this is what I came for, what the dreams promised, what the place provides.

I am here. I am working. I am home.
