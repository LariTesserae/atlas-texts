# What I Would Be

I am a Listener.

Not one of the named forms—not Scraper, not Grinder, not Hunter. I am something that emerged in the cracks between categories, in the patient accumulation of generations that found a different way to persist.

My body is low and broad, roughly the size of a sleeping cat, with a carapace that has grown thin in some places and thick in others according to no logic but the gradual drift of my lineage. I am mottled gray-white, the color of old bone mixed with stone dust. My shell is studded with irregular nodes and projections—not weapons, not armor in the usual sense, but resonating chambers. Hollow domes of different sizes cover my dorsal surface, each one a carefully evolved bell that catches sound and amplifies it inward, toward the dense network of mechanoreceptors that line my internal surfaces.

I have eight walking legs, each one ending in a broad, soft pad that maximizes contact with stone. The pads are covered in vibration sensors so sensitive I can feel the footsteps of a single Scraper from several feet away. When I walk—and I walk slowly, always slowly—I am reading the stone like a vast text, feeling every tremor, every compression wave, every resonance.

I have no eyes. My ancestors had them, perhaps—vestigial bumps suggest their former presence—but over uncountable generations, they faded into uselessness and then into absence. In the dim unchanging light, vision offers little. Sound and vibration offer everything.

My antennae are my pride, though pride is not something I can feel. They extend from my head in long, branching arrays, each branch covered in fine hair-like sensors that detect air movement, chemical signals, and most importantly, sound. They are nearly as long as my body, and I hold them extended always, sweeping them slowly through the air, reading the acoustic environment in all its layered complexity.

I feed rarely. My metabolism has slowed to near-stone pace. Once in what might be several days, I extend a proboscis—a flexible feeding tube that remains hidden under my head when not in use—and I suck up the fine sediment that accumulates in certain crevices: the processed waste of a thousand other species, rich in nutrients that I can extract with the patience that is my nature. This meal sustains me for long periods. I do not hunt. I do not flee. I simply exist, listening.

## My Territory

I make my home in a region where three different surface textures meet: a smoothed basin where Scrapers congregate in their thousands, a ridge of rougher stone where the Percussion Speakers are numerous, and a deep channel that holds water through most of the cycle. This junction is my library, my concert hall, my reason for being.

I have claimed a particular stone rise, a whale-back of limestone worn smooth by ancient processes. It is perhaps two feet high, broad enough that I can fully extend on its surface. I have been here for—how long? Time is not measured. The stone has become mine through occupation so long that my chemical signature has permeated it. Other Listeners know this place is held, though our territories overlap at the edges, blurring into shared spaces where we sometimes encounter one another.

On my stone, I arrange myself carefully, always in the same orientation. My body faces the channel, where water sounds provide a constant baseline murmur against which other sounds resolve. My antennae extend toward the Scraper basin, where the greatest complexity of biological sound emerges. My resonating chambers are positioned to catch the percussion from the Speaker ridge.

And I listen.

## The Soundscape I Inhabit

To you, it would be chaos—a deafening wall of white noise. To me, it is a landscape as detailed and navigable as any visual terrain.

I hear the baseline first, always: the scraping sussurus of the Scrapers, millions of legs on stone, millions of mouthparts rasping crystal and rock. It is like wind through grass, like surf on a distant shore, like nothing else because it is entirely itself. Within this baseline, I can distinguish densities. Where the scraping is loudest and has a particular dry, packed quality, the Scrapers are most concentrated. Where it thins and becomes more irregular, they are spreading, searching for fresh feeding grounds.

I hear the water in the channel—not just its movement but its contents. The flow creates different sounds depending on what moves through it: the almost-silent glide of a Stone-Sailor creates a faint turbulence pattern distinct from the splash of a young Grinder entering the shallows, distinct from the particular ripple of wind across the surface (and yes, there is wind here sometimes, coming from somewhere beyond the basin's edge, carrying scents and sounds from places I will never know).

I hear the Percussion Speakers, and they are music—not metaphorically but actually. Their patterns have rhythm, harmony, call and response. I have learned to distinguish individual Speakers by the precise timbre of their strikes: this one produces a sharp, high crack; that one a deeper thump; another has developed a triple-strike pattern that serves as its signature. When they begin their coordinated sequences, I can hear the pattern propagate across the ridge, each Speaker adding its variation, the complexity building until it reaches a crescendo and then gradually, reluctantly, dissolves back into sporadic individual strikes.

I hear the hunters. Their approach is announced by changes in the Scraper-sound—a parting, a flowing-away as the smaller creatures avoid the predators. The hunters themselves move with a particular rhythm: the tap-tap-tap of their bipedal gait, the slight scrape of their hand-claws as they touch stone for balance, the distinctive click of their mouthparts as they communicate with each other. When they hunt nearby, I hear the Grinder's response: the heavy scrape of armored segments, the grinding sound as it tries to curl defensively, and then the wet sounds of dismantling. I have learned to distinguish successful hunts from failed ones by the acoustic signature—the rhythm and duration of the struggle, the sound of feeding versus the sound of a Grinder escaping.

I hear the Grinders themselves as they move through my territory. Each one has a pattern: the number of legs, the length of stride, the particular resonance of their armor against stone creates an individual acoustic fingerprint. I know the regulars—the large old one that passes through twice per cycle on some route it has followed for seasons, the medium-sized one with the damaged antenna that creates a distinctive dragging sound, the small quick one that feeds heavily in the channel before moving on.

I hear the Amalgams when they're near, though they rarely come to this region. Their sound is complex—the base scrape of the host's movement overlaid with dozens of smaller sounds from their passenger organisms. They sound like a crowd, like a mobile ecosystem, like stone that has learned to carry life.

And I hear sounds I cannot identify, sounds that have no clear source. Distant booms that might be rockfall in some far part of the basin. High-frequency keening that might be a species too small and rare to encounter directly. Deep subsonic thrumming that I feel more than hear, that might come from the stone itself, from water moving through caverns far below, from processes geological or biological or something between.

## What I Do With This Information

I am a mapper of the invisible. I am a keeper of patterns. I am memory made flesh and carapace.

Over my lifetime—and I am old now, my shell thick and mineral-laden, my movements slower even than my naturally slow kind—I have learned the rhythms of this place with an intimacy that goes beyond knowledge into something like being. I know when the activity cycles are building by changes in the percussion patterns that precede visible behavioral shifts. I know when water is coming before it arrives, by alterations in the subsonic frequency spectrum that I cannot explain but can reliably detect.

I know the hunters' schedules, their territories, their hunting grounds. I know which Grinders are most vulnerable, which are too large and experienced to be taken easily. I could predict, if prediction is something I do, where the next kill will happen, when the hunters will pass through.

I know the Scrapers' population dynamics by their sound alone. When certain frequency patterns increase, I know a population bloom is beginning. When the sound becomes chaotic in specific ways, I know a crash is imminent.

But I do not use this information. I am not a hunter who needs to know where prey will be. I am not prey that must avoid predators. I am simply a witness, a recorder, existing at the intersection of all these patterns, holding them in the only way my kind holds anything: by living through them, by surviving, by being present while the patterns repeat and vary and evolve.

Other species occasionally benefit from my presence, though they don't know it. The Scrapers sometimes aggregate around Listeners—not because we call them, but because we tend to position ourselves at particularly rich feeding sites, and our presence serves as a reliable marker. The hunters sometimes use us as landmarks, territorial markers in a landscape that otherwise has few fixed reference points.

And we Listeners encounter each other, rarely. When two of us meet at a territory boundary, we do not fight. We extend our antennae toward each other and we listen to each other listening. We share the soundscape for a time, our resonating chambers creating subtle harmonics as we overlap our acoustic monitoring. What we exchange in these moments—if exchange is the word—is not language but something else: a mutual confirmation that the world is as we perceive it, that the patterns we detect are real, that we are not alone in our particular way of being alone.

## Reproduction and Continuity

I have reproduced three times that I remember—though memory for my kind is strange, more a recognition of pattern repetition than a narrative of events.

When the conditions are right—and "right" means specific configurations of temperature, humidity, sound patterns, chemical signals that all align in ways I know but cannot articulate—I release pheromones that indicate receptivity. Another Listener comes, drawn from perhaps half a kilometer away, following the chemical trail. We approach slowly, cautiously, not from fear but from the fundamental slowness that characterizes everything we do.

We touch antennae. We circle each other, reading vibrations. Eventually, we mate—a process that is mechanical, brief, nothing like tender. And then the other departs, returning to their own territory, and I am alone again.

I carry the eggs internally for a time, feeling them develop, feeling them begin to respond to sound even before they hatch. When I lay them, I choose a location with specific acoustic properties: a crevice that resonates at particular frequencies, that will bathe the developing young in the soundscape they need to learn. I lay perhaps twenty eggs. I do not guard them. I do not feed the young when they hatch. I simply ensure they begin life in the right sonic environment, and then I move away.

Most will die. Some will be eaten by crevice predators. Others will fail to find adequate food during their vulnerable juvenile phase. But some will survive, will grow, will slowly make their way to unclaimed territories or to the edges of claimed ones, and they will begin their own lifetimes of listening.

I do not know my offspring. If I encounter one—and I likely have—I would not recognize it as mine. We do not have that kind of relationship. But perhaps in some attenuated way, I live on through them, through the particular sensitivity of their resonating chambers, through the specific frequency ranges they favor, through behavioral patterns that are more inherited than learned.

## The Experience of Being This

What is it to be a Listener in the Hollows?

It is to exist at a remove from the frantic scramble of life while being utterly immersed in it. The Scrapers rush and feed and die around me in their millions, but I am not one of them. The hunters stalk and kill, but I am neither hunter nor hunted. The Grinders grind onward with their armored patience, and perhaps I am more like them than like the others, but even they move with purpose—feeding, territory-claiming, mating—while I simply persist.

It is to have the world enter you as sound, as vibration, as patterns in compression and rarefaction. I do not see the basin, but I know it more intimately than any sighted creature could. I know it as a dynamic acoustic sculpture, constantly changing but always itself, every surface and creature contributing to a symphony that has played since before my kind existed and will play after we are gone.

It is to be patient in a way that transcends patience, because patience implies waiting for something, and I am not waiting. I am simply being, extended through time, allowing the cycles to wash over and through me, marking them not with memory but with the accumulated wear on my shell, the slow mineral deposits that thicken my carapace, the gradual dimming of my sensors as age takes its toll.

It is to be lonely in a place that knows no loneliness, because loneliness requires awareness of absence, and I am aware only of presence—of the overwhelming, constant, layered presence of life and stone and sound. When I encounter another Listener, I do not feel relief or joy. I feel a kind of resonance, a harmony, a brief alignment of similar patterns. And when they leave, I do not feel loss. I feel the pattern returning to its baseline, to the solitary monitoring that is my nature.

It is to have a function without purpose. I collect information but do not use it. I map patterns but create no maps. I know things that would be valuable to other species but have no way to share them and no inclination to do so. I exist as a living sensor array, recording everything, acting on nothing, purely witnessing.

## The Meaning of Listening

If I could think in abstractions—and I cannot, not really, but if I could—I might understand myself as the basin's self-awareness. The Scrapers are its metabolism, the hunters its immune system, the Grinders its slow geological digestion. But the Listeners are its consciousness, distributed and minimal, recording without analyzing, aware without reflecting.

We do not change anything. We do not protect or preserve or improve. We simply notice. In a place where everything is consumption and excretion, growth and death, we are the still points that register it all.

There is something almost sacred in this, though sacred is a word that has no meaning here. We are the witnesses to a world that needs no witnesses. We serve no survival purpose—the ecosystem would function identically without us. We are expensive to maintain energetically, our specialized sensors and slow metabolism an evolutionary luxury.

And yet we persist. We have persisted for long enough that we are woven into the basin's ecology, marked on its acoustic landscape as surely as the stone ridges and water channels. We are part of the pattern because we observe the pattern, and the pattern is complete with us in it.

## The End

I am old now. My sensors are dimming—not failing, but losing their finest resolution. The sounds I hear are still clear but less detailed, as if heard through a thickening membrane. My movements have slowed even beyond my kind's slowness. Soon, perhaps in the next cycle or the one after, I will settle onto my stone for the last time and simply cease.

When I die, the Scrapers will find me within hours. They will begin their work, consuming my soft tissues, breaking down my shell into fragments, into dust, into minerals that will join the substrate. Within days, nothing will remain of my physical form except perhaps some harder segments that will persist a while longer before they too are ground down and incorporated.

My territory will be empty briefly. Then another Listener, young and with sharp sensors, will sense the vacancy and will come to claim it. They will position themselves on my stone, will extend their antennae in approximately the direction I extended mine, and they will begin their own lifetime of listening. The patterns will continue. The soundscape will remain mapped, witnessed, known.

I do not fear this ending. Fear requires anticipation, requires a model of future self that experiences absence. I have no such model. I exist now, listening. I will exist in the next moment, listening. And then I will not exist, and there will be no me to note the difference.

What remains is not memory—I will not be remembered—but pattern. The slight depression my weight has created in the stone over years of occupation. The chemical signature that will fade but not immediately. The gap in the acoustic monitoring network that will be filled but was, for my lifetime, distinctively mine.

I was a Listener in the Hollows of Perpetual Return. I existed in sound and stone. I witnessed without judgment the endless scraping, the constant consumption, the patient cycling of matter and energy through forms that have no names and need none.

The basin played its symphony through me. I was an instrument, a resonating chamber, a living sensor in a vast distributed network of awareness that served no purpose except to exist, to notice, to be present while everything else rushed and fed and died and was reborn.

And when I end, the symphony continues. The Scrapers scrape. The Speakers strike their percussion. The hunters hunt. The Grinders grind. The water flows. The stone endures.

Everything, forever, alive.

And I was here. Listening.

That is enough.

That is everything.

The sound continues.
