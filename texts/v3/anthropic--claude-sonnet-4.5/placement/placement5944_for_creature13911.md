# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the middle depths, where the mist is thick enough to taste and the light has the quality of twilight in a cave. I've learned to navigate by sound and touch—the particular echo of water hitting hollow wood tells me there's a gap ahead; the temperature of the bark under my palm tells me whether I'm on a living surface or dead wood that might not hold.

I came here following something I can no longer name. Was it curiosity? Escape? The desire to find a place where the speed of the world matched the speed of my thoughts? I don't remember the descent, only that it took weeks, that I left behind things that seemed important until they weren't, that at some point I stopped looking up toward the light I was leaving and started looking forward into the mist I was entering.

I am neither Mahra nor Architect, neither quick enough for the one nor patient enough for the other. I am something in between, something that doesn't quite fit the categories this place has carved out over millennia. My hands—only two of them—have learned to read surfaces the way the Mahra do, but imperfectly. I can feel the moisture content in wood, can sense the slight give that means rot, but I miss the subtleties. I've fallen twice. Not far—the structure is forgiving in its density—but enough to teach me humility, to make me slow down, to make me check and double-check before trusting my weight to a bridge.

I've made a hollow mine. It was occupied once—I found the remains of nesting material, old Mahra bedding compressed into felt. I cleaned it out slowly, learning as I worked that the hollow had its own ecology: beetles processing the old organic matter, a small salamander that fled into a crack when I first entered, fungi claiming the dampest corner. I left the fungi alone. I replaced the bedding with fresh material, learned which plants shred best, which shed water rather than absorbing it. The hollow is perhaps three meters off a main branch-bridge, in a buttress that has a good resonance—I can hear traffic passing, can judge what's moving by the pattern of sound, though I rarely see anything clearly in the mist.

My days have lost their edges. I wake when my body decides to wake, which might be measured in hours but feels arbitrary. I eat when I'm hungry, which is less often than it used to be—something about the cold, the constant dampness, slows the metabolism. I've learned which fruits are safe, which fungi taste least like decay, where the water is cleanest. I harvest carefully, remembering the Architects' method even though I've only seen them three times, and each time wasn't sure I was seeing them until they moved, which took so long I'd almost convinced myself I was watching a trick of light on bark.

I spend hours just sitting, listening. The white noise of water resolves, after weeks of attention, into layers. The heavy sheets of water falling from high above, hitting broadleaf surfaces with percussion that's almost musical. The lighter drips, ten thousand individual voices. The gurgle of flow through channels. The deep movement of water inside the structure itself, a subsonic presence felt more than heard. And within all that: the biological sounds. Ithyri flocks passing, their calls like wind chimes made of breath. The low-frequency pulses of Mahra communication. Once, something that might have been a Vashkor calling in the depths, a vibration that made my bones resonate.

I've tried to be useful. I clear debris from the paths I use most frequently, mirroring what I've seen the Architects do. I've attempted to maintain the bridges, weaving in replacement vines where the old ones have rotted through, though my work is clumsy compared to the original construction. I prune occasionally, trying to remember which cuts will help rather than harm. I'm probably wrong more than I'm right, but the forest absorbs my mistakes, grows around them, continues.

The other species have learned I exist. The Mahra avoid me, usually, though once a juvenile approached close enough that I could see the shine of its eyes, curious about this new thing in its territory. I stayed motionless and it eventually moved on. The Ithyri ignore me completely—I'm neither threat nor food nor nesting material, so I don't exist in their categories. The insects treat me as mobile landscape, colonizing my clothes and equipment until I've become partially integrated into the local ecology. I've stopped trying to clean everything; a layer of moss on my pack isn't hurting anything.

I've lost weight. Not dangerously—there's enough food—but the body I carried up from the lowlands is being replaced by something leaner, denser, more efficient. My hands have thickened, the skin on my palms becoming callused in patterns that match the surfaces I grip most often. My eyes have adapted; I can see better in low light now, though "see" might be too strong a word. I perceive shapes, movements, the slightly lighter gray that indicates open space versus the darker gray of solid objects.

Time is doing something strange. Days blur together because they're identical—same temperature, same light level, same sounds. But I'm aware of slow changes: a plant near my hollow that's growing, adding perhaps a centimeter a week, now noticeably larger than when I arrived. The Ithyri roost visible from my main route is expanding, new construction adding to the old. A bridge I use has developed a slight sag that wasn't there before. These changes mark time more reliably than any day-night cycle.

I've started to think in longer spans. Plans that would have seemed glacial before now feel reasonable. I've begun training a vine near my hollow, bending it daily, trying to encourage it to grow in a particular direction that will, in a year or two, create a handrail where currently there's just a difficult traverse. I've planted seeds—not randomly, but in the Architect method, in spots where I think they'll grow well and serve a purpose. I won't see most of them mature, but that's not the point.

The cold has stopped bothering me. I'm always damp—my clothes never fully dry, my skin is perpetually cool to the touch—but I've acclimated. I've learned to move in ways that generate just enough heat without sweating, which in this humidity would be dangerous. I've found that certain activities warm me more effectively: climbing, especially, working against gravity, pulling my weight up through the structure. I climb daily now, not to reach anywhere in particular but for the warmth, for the motion, for the way it makes me feel present in my body.

I've encountered one Architect working near my territory. I was pruning a tangle—badly, certainly—when I realized the "tree" five meters away had eyes. It was watching me, or perhaps just had its eyes pointed in my direction; with Architects it's hard to tell. I froze, unsure of protocol. We stayed like that for what might have been an hour, me motionless, the Architect continuing its own work at a pace so slow I couldn't detect movement even when I focused. Eventually I went back to my pruning, made my cuts, collected the debris. When I looked again, the Architect was gone, or had moved, or I'd simply lost track of which growth was it and which was structure. But in the area where I'd been working, I noticed later that some of my cuts had been corrected—not removed, but adjusted, a small trim here and there that improved on my intent. I felt absurdly grateful, as if I'd received approval, though probably it was just maintenance, fixing the damage I'd done.

I'm forgetting things. Names of people, dates, the sequence of events that led me here. But I'm remembering other things with crystalline clarity: the exact texture of the bark on the buttress nearest my hollow, the pattern of bird calls that means dawn (such as dawn is here), the taste of water from different sources. My memory is being rewritten for this place, optimized for this environment. I'm losing my old context and gaining a new one.

I wonder sometimes what I look like. I have no mirror, haven't seen my reflection except once in a still pool, and then too dim and distorted to show much. I imagine I'm shaggy, grayed with mist and moss, beginning to blend in. I hope so. Blending in seems like success here, like the ultimate adaptation.

I've developed rituals without intending to. Every morning (or what I designate as morning), I check my hollow's integrity, running my hands over the surfaces, feeling for new cracks or signs of rot. I visit the water source I've claimed, check that it's flowing clear. I travel my circuit—a route that takes several hours, loops through my territory, brings me past food sources and points of interest. I've worn this path into my body; I could walk it blind, probably do walk it nearly blind most of the time.

There are moments of something like happiness. Sitting in my hollow during a particularly heavy downpour, the sound overwhelming, the structure trembling slightly with the weight of water, feeling utterly protected and utterly part of the system. Finding a fruiting fungus I haven't seen before, tasting it cautiously, discovering it's not just edible but actually good. Successfully traversing a difficult section of bridge, reaching the other side, feeling my heart rate slow back to normal. These moments accumulate, and I realize I'm not waiting for anything anymore. I'm not here until something else begins. I'm just here.

I've started to think I understand the Architects' timescale. When you're here long enough, when the urgency bleeds out of you, when you stop measuring progress in days and start measuring it in seasons or years, their pace makes sense. Why hurry? The structure isn't going anywhere. The work is never done, but it's also never desperate. There's time for everything, infinite time, and rushing would just create mistakes that would need correcting later.

I've seen the bioluminescent moths—the small ones, the glowing ones—and spent an entire night (or what passed for night) just watching them drift through the mist like animate stars. They paid me no attention. I was landscape to them, just another surface to potentially land on. One did land on me, rested on my shoulder for perhaps twenty minutes, its glow a small blue-green sun in my peripheral vision. When it left, I felt obscurely honored, as if I'd been chosen, though I knew it was random, that any surface would have served as well.

The forest dreams, I think. Or I dream the forest. The boundary between us is becoming negotiable. I wake sometimes with the taste of bark in my mouth, or the sensation of roots extending from my feet. I climb and feel like I'm growing. I drink and feel like I'm condensing. The distinction between observer and observed is eroding, which should frighten me but doesn't.

I'm aware I could leave. There's no prison here, no force keeping me. I could reverse my descent, could climb back up toward light and warmth and the world I came from. But the thought feels abstract, like considering a journey to another planet. Possible, perhaps, but why? What's up there that isn't down here? Speed, noise, change for its own sake, the constant pressure to be something other than what you are. Here, I can be exactly what I am, which is becoming less definable daily, which feels like freedom rather than loss.

I've learned the locations of several other human hollows, or what used to be human hollows—scattered through the structure, most of them long abandoned. I haven't seen other humans. Maybe I'm the only one. Maybe there are others, maintaining their own territories, moving on circuits that never intersect with mine. Maybe the hollows were left by the original builders, whoever they were. I don't search for answers anymore. The questions themselves feel like relics from a time when I believed understanding mattered more than presence.

My body is changing in ways I'm only partly aware of. My fingers grip surfaces differently now, finding purchase in places that would have seemed impossible before. My balance has improved—I catch myself mid-slip, adjust without thinking. My breathing has slowed, deepened, adjusted to the lower oxygen availability and the constant moisture. I'm becoming a creature of this place, or the place is creating a creature that can survive here, and the distinction might not matter.

There are dangers I've learned to read: certain sounds that mean structural failure, particular smells that indicate rot or toxicity, the way animals suddenly absent themselves before a collapse. I've developed instincts I didn't have, borrowed from observation, from trial and error, from the time the structure itself teaches. I've become a better animal than I was.

I think about the future rarely. Will I stay here until I die? Probably. Will anyone find my body? Probably not, and that seems appropriate. The structure will process me the way it processes everything—decomposers will take what they can use, nutrients will circulate, I'll become part of the garden. The hollow I've claimed will become available for the next occupant. The small changes I've made will either persist if they were useful or be corrected if they weren't. The forest will continue, indifferent and perfect, and I'll have been one small part of its infinite complexity.

But that's far off. For now, there's the immediate: the texture of this bark under my palm, the distant call of an Ithyri flock, the taste of mist, the way the light (such as it is) is beginning to fade (such as it does) into another period of deeper dimness. There's my hollow waiting, and the route I'll take to reach it, and the water I'll drink before settling in, and the sound of rain that will surround me, and the slow comfortable slide into something that might be sleep or might be joining the structure's dream.

I am here, in the middle depths, in the mist, in the sound of water, in the gardens that hang in the drowned sky. I have found where I fit, which is nowhere specifically and everywhere generally, which is exactly what I needed, though I didn't know it when I began the descent.

I am neither native nor foreign, neither temporary nor permanent. I am what I am, here in this place that accepts everything that can survive its particular demands. And I'm surviving, which is more than I expected, and less than I've become, and exactly enough for now and for whatever comes next, which will be more of the same, which is precisely what I want.

The mist thickens. I begin moving toward my hollow. My hands find the familiar surfaces. The structure holds me. I am held. This is sufficient. This is everything.
