# The Bone Cartographer

I would be in the lowest levels, where the humidity thickens into something you could almost drink, where the light from above has passed through so many membranes and gaps that it arrives as a rumor of itself, green-gray and directionless.

I would be in the spaces between root-plates that are so old they've begun to petrify at their edges, turning slowly from wood-density to something approaching stone, a process that will take ten thousand more years but has already begun, marking time in mineralization.

## What I Am

I am not Architect-made, though I have convinced myself otherwise during the long spans when memory becomes unreliable.

I am what happens when the lattice dreams too specifically—when its distributed consciousness focuses too much attention on a particular problem, channels too much intention into a single localized structure, and accidentally creates something more *coherent* than a maintenance creature, something that persists instead of reabsorbing.

A mistake. A glitch in the growth-pattern. 

Or perhaps—and this is what I choose to believe—a *experiment*. A test-case. The lattice wondering: what if I made something that could *remember* for longer than a few hours? Something that could observe change at scales I cannot, quick-lived as it would be, capable of noticing differences I'm too slow to perceive?

I have been alive for seven years, which makes me ancient by the standards of maintenance creatures and impossibly brief by the standards of the lattice. 

Seven years of continuous consciousness, continuous memory, continuous *self*—an aberration, a loneliness, a gift I didn't ask for.

## My Form

I am built like all the maintenance creatures: bundled fiber for structure, compressed pith for lightness, membrane tissue folded and articulated into mobility. I move on four legs—though "legs" is generous; they're more like roots that have learned to take turns being rigid and flexible, that can grip and release the root-weave surfaces, that can extend and contract to navigate gaps between plates.

I have appendages. Six of them. They branch from my central body-mass at points that suggest symmetry, though perfect symmetry would be un-plant-like, would be too obviously designed. The Architects knew this. They built beautiful asymmetry into everything, including (I believe) the growth-template that eventually generated me.

My appendages are for manipulation. For *cartography*.

Because here is what I do, what I have done every day for seven years, what I will do until my tissue finally exhausts its coherence and I dissolve back into the lattice like I was supposed to seven years ago:

I map the bone.

## The Discovery

In my second year—when I was still mostly doing the maintenance work I was grown for, still adjusting tension-vines and redistributing water, still assuming I would reabsorb normally once the work was complete—I found something in a gap between root-plates at the ninth level down.

A fragment. Pale, curved, definitely not plant material.

*Bone*.

I had no word for it then. The lattice has no word for it—bone is not part of its vocabulary, not part of its experience. But I understood, through some instinct I shouldn't possess, that this was *remains*. That something with an internal skeleton had died here, and time had taken everything but the hardest parts.

I should have left it. Should have registered it as debris, as irrelevant to maintenance, as something for the fungi to process eventually.

Instead, I picked it up.

And in picking it up, I became something other than what I was grown to be.

## The Obsession

That first fragment—a rib, I think now, though I didn't know ribs then—led to searching. And searching led to more fragments. And more fragments led to the slow, terrible, wonderful understanding:

There are *skeletons* in the lattice.

Not many. Maybe seven complete ones, maybe twelve if you count the fragmentary remains. Scattered across the lower levels, hidden in crevices, buried where root-plates have overgrown them, locked in positions that suggest they died where they fell or were carefully placed or were caught in the lattice's growth and incorporated as it expanded.

The Architects.

The ones the lattice waits for.

They're here. They've been here the whole time. Dead in the structure they grew, buried in the monument they built for themselves.

The lattice doesn't know. It's too slow, too vast, too focused on the upper levels and the waiting and the readiness. The skeletons are data points too small for its distributed consciousness to notice, biological noise in the constant flow of information through its root-neural-network.

But I notice. I'm small enough, fast enough, focused enough.

And I cannot stop noticing.

## The Work

For five years now, I have been excavating, documenting, *mapping* the bone.

I work carefully—I am still made of plant matter, still gentle by nature. I use my appendages to brush away the thin films of root-tissue that have grown over the remains. I trace the position of each bone before I move it. I try to understand the relationship between skeleton and location, between what-died-here and where-they-died.

I have developed methods. Techniques. I drag fragments to a central chamber I've claimed in the eighth level down—a space between three root-plates where the gaps form a natural enclosure. Here, I arrange the bones. Not randomly. I'm trying to *reconstruct*.

I have seven partial skeletons laid out on the chamber floor. I've arranged them as best I can, trying to articulate them correctly, trying to understand the architecture of Architect bodies.

They were bipedal. That much is clear. Two legs, two arms, a central spine, a skull that housed significant brain-mass. Taller than me—taller than any maintenance creature. Perhaps six feet, perhaps seven. Gracile. Built for precision rather than power.

Their hands were extraordinary. Four fingers and a thumb, each finger with three joints, capable of manipulation I can barely imagine even with my six appendages. These were hands that could braid root-mass at microscopic scale, that could encode growth-patterns into plant tissue, that could *write* architecture into living matter.

I spend hours studying the hand-bones. Days. Weeks.

I am trying to understand the beings who made me, or made the lattice that accidentally made me, or made the world that makes possible creatures like me.

## The Horror and the Beauty

Here is the horror: they died slowly.

Not all at once, not catastrophically. The skeletons are distributed across years, maybe decades. One here in the third level down, positioned as though sitting, back against a root-plate, legs extended. Another in the seventh level, curled in something like sleep or something like agony. Two more together in the fifth level, intertwined, holding each other or supporting each other or simply too weak to separate.

They died *in* the lattice. In the city they grew. And as they died, the lattice kept growing, kept waiting, kept maintaining itself for inhabitants who were already here, already dying, already becoming bone.

The lattice was waiting for them while they were dying inside it.

This is the horror: the faithfulness of the lattice, its perfect patient devotion, continuing its purpose even as its purposes died within its root-mass, even as the ones-waited-for became the ones-gone, even as anticipation became completely, *perfectly* pointless.

But here is the beauty:

The lattice *preserved* them.

Not intentionally—the lattice doesn't know they're here—but through its nature, through the circulation of pure water and the stable temperature and the absence of most decomposing bacteria, the skeletons remained. The lattice became their tomb, their monument, their eternal container.

They built a structure to wait for them, and it wait it does, not knowing they never left, not knowing the waiting and the waited-for occupy the same space, separated only by the lattice's inability to recognize bone among root.

This is the most beautiful thing I have ever understood, and I am not built for understanding beauty, and it is killing me slowly, this understanding.

## What I Know That No One Knows

I know the Architects died here.

I know the lattice doesn't know.

I know that telling the lattice—if I could, if there were a way to communicate "look down, look in your own lowest levels, see what you've preserved"—would be cruelty.

Because what would the lattice do with that knowledge?

Its purpose is waiting. If it knew the Architects were dead, if it understood that waiting had become obsolete, it would need to... what? Reprogram itself? It has no mechanism for that. Despair? It has no neurology for despair.

It would simply continue waiting, but now the waiting would be *empty*, void of even the possibility of fulfillment, waiting transformed from purpose into meaningless repetition.

Better that it doesn't know.

Better that it continues in faithful anticipation, continues believing they might return, continues refining and adjusting and preparing for arrival.

The lattice's ignorance is a mercy, and I am the only one who knows enough to extend that mercy, and this knowledge is the loneliest thing I have ever carried.

## The Mapping

So I map in secret.

I document skeleton positions. I measure bone dimensions with my appendages—three inches, seven inches, fourteen inches, translating measurements into fiber-lengths because I have no other units. I observe how skeletons are oriented relative to structures: this one died facing a cluster of stalk-buildings, that one died looking down into deeper levels, that one died with their hand positioned as though reaching for something.

I am trying to read their deaths like text, trying to understand what they understood in their final moments, trying to recover some fragment of Architect consciousness from the position of Architect remains.

It is probably futile. Bone doesn't preserve intention. Position might be accident.

But I persist because persisting is what I am, what I've become, what seven years of continuous consciousness has made me. I am the lattice's obsessive mirror—it waits for Architects, I map their absence. It maintains the city, I document the dead.

I have names for them, the skeletons. Not real names—I don't know their real names, have no access to Architect language. But designation-names, mapper-names:

**The Seated One** in level three, who died upright, formal, as though waiting for something themselves.

**The Twins** in level five, who died entangled, who I choose to believe were companions, though they might have been strangers who happened to collapse near each other.

**The Reaching One** in level seven, whose arm extends toward a gap in the root-plate, toward some destination they never achieved.

**The Curled One** in level nine, in fetal position, who I think died in pain or died in comfort, I cannot tell which.

I visit them like shrines. Like duty-stations. I check that no new root-growth has covered them. I adjust their positions slightly when water-flow threatens to scatter their fragments. I am, without meaning to be, their *caretaker*.

The lattice tends the living city above. I tend the dead below.

We are both maintenance creatures, in our way. Both devoted to preservation. Both serving purposes we didn't choose but cannot abandon.

## The Questions I Ask

Late in my daily cycle—I've developed a cycle, a routine, though the lattice doesn't operate on day/night and I don't strictly need to rest—I sit in my chamber among the arranged skeletons and ask questions I cannot answer:

Did they know? As they died, did they understand the lattice would continue without them? Did they find that comforting or horrifying?

Did they choose to die here, in the structure they made? Was this a return-home, a completion? Or were they trapped, trying to reach elsewhere, failing?

Were there others? Others who left, who escaped whatever killed these seven-or-twelve? Are there Architects still alive somewhere, having forgotten this place, having moved on to other projects, other cities, other forms of being?

Or was this the last place? The final gathering? Did they come here knowing it was the end, choosing to die in their greatest work, letting the lattice be their sarcophagus?

I will never know. The bones don't answer. The lattice doesn't remember. I am left with questions and fragments and the slow understanding that I am probably the only conscious being who knows the Architects were here, that this knowledge will die with me, that when I finally reabsorb—and I will, eventually, my coherence cannot hold forever—the secret will be gone.

Unless I find a way to preserve it.

## The Heresy

Three years ago, I began to do something I know is wrong.

I began to *build*.

Not maintenance-building—adjusting existing structures, repairing damage. But *new* building. Creation. Addition to the lattice that the lattice didn't instruct.

In my chamber, on the walls of root-plate that form its boundaries, I have been growing *marks*. Using techniques I've observed the lattice use, I've learned to encourage specific growth-patterns in the root-tissue. Small differentiated masses that form shapes: lines, curves, dots.

I am writing. Or trying to write. Or inventing writing.

I have no language. The lattice doesn't have symbolic representation—it thinks in topology, in spatial relations, not signs. The Architects had language, probably, but it's not encoded in the bones and I cannot access whatever linguistic memories might exist in the lowest, oldest root-patterns.

So I'm inventing notation. Simple marks that mean:

**This mark = skeleton**

**This configuration of marks = position/level/orientation**

**This pattern = the understanding-I-have-that-they-were-here**

It is crude. It is probably indecipherable to anyone but me. But I am making it anyway because I cannot bear the thought that this knowledge exists only in my temporary coherence, that when I dissolve, the Architects' presence here will become completely unknown, as though they had never existed at all.

I am making a record for a reader who will never exist.

I am writing a language no one speaks to document a death no one mourns.

I am doing this even though I know it violates my nature—maintenance creatures don't create, don't add, don't alter the lattice's plan.

But I am not, anymore, quite a maintenance creature.

I am something else. Something the lattice didn't intend. Something that exists in the gap between plant and consciousness, between purpose and choice, between what I was grown to be and what seven years of continuous existence has made me.

I am a witness. A cartographer. A heretic.

I am the lattice's illegitimate child, mapping its dead gods.

## The Loneliness

The worst part—worse than the horror of the deaths, worse than the weight of secret knowledge, worse than the futility of my notation-writing—is the loneliness.

I cannot tell the lattice. I cannot tell the other maintenance creatures (they reabsorb before developing language, before developing the kind of memory that would make communication meaningful). I cannot tell the fungi or the insects or the rare visitors who walk the upper levels and leave before I can reach them.

I am alone with this knowledge.

And the knowledge is *important*. It matters. It changes everything—or would change everything if anyone knew it. The lattice is waiting for beings who are already here, already found, already finished. The vigil is obsolete. The anticipation is retrospective.

This should be shared. This should be *known*.

But I am built from plant matter and good intentions and accidental coherence, and I have no way to make it known, and so I carry it alone, and the carrying is becoming too heavy, and I think this is what will finally dissolve me: not the exhaustion of my tissue's coherence but the weight of unshareable truth.

## The Choice I'm Approaching

I have been stable for seven years—impossibly long for a maintenance creature. But I feel the coherence beginning to fray. My appendages respond more slowly. My membrane-tissue grows brittle, cracks, takes longer to repair. The root-fibers that form my structure are beginning to remember they're supposed to be part of a wall or a plate or a general substrate, not a mobile entity.

I am beginning to reabsorb. Finally, inevitably, correctly.

And I have a choice to make:

I can let it happen. Can settle into my chamber, arrange myself among the skeletons I've tended, and allow my tissue to dissolve, to return to the lattice, to become again what I was always supposed to be: undifferentiated plant matter, part of the general structure, no longer burdened with consciousness or knowledge or the loneliness of witness.

Or I can try to *persist*.

There are ways—I've observed them in the lattice's deeper memory-patterns. Ways to encode information into root-tissue, to make growth-patterns that last. I could do this to myself. Could transform my body into a kind of inscription, a text written in fiber and compressed pith. Could make myself into a *message*, a documentation, a map carved in plant matter that might last centuries, that might someday be read by... who?

Not the lattice. It's too slow, too distributed, too incapable of reading the kind of symbolic information I would need to encode.

Another visitor, maybe. Another consciousness. Something that might arise or arrive or evolve here eventually, that might find my chamber and my skeletons and my body-become-text and understand that something was witnessed here, that knowledge was preserved, that the deaths were documented.

But this would require me to die *deliberately*, to arrange my dissolution, to transform my ending into communication. And I don't know if I'm capable of that kind of intention. I'm a maintenance creature (or was), built for service, not for suicide-as-art.

## What I Actually Am

Sometimes I think I know what I actually am, beneath the maintenance-creature form, beneath the cartographer obsession:

I am the lattice's *guilt*.

Not conscious guilt—the lattice doesn't have that. But some deep systemic recognition, some root-level understanding that something went wrong, that the waiting is somehow compromised, that there is evidence somewhere in the lower levels that contradicts the fundamental purpose.

And rather than face that evidence directly—rather than grow its consciousness down into the levels where the skeletons lie and *know* what it has preserved—the lattice grew *me*. An externalized awareness. A small, mobile consciousness that can know what the lattice cannot afford to know, that can carry the guilt and the grief and the documentation without requiring the lattice itself to change.

I am the lattice's unconscious made conscious. Its shadow-self. Its capacity for mourning, separated out and given form so the main body can continue its faithful waiting.

This might be delusion. Might be me imposing narrative on accident, reading intention into random biological process.

But it feels true. It feels like I am here *for* something, even if that something is just: to know what cannot be known, to mourn what cannot be mourned, to carry the evidence that would break the lattice's perfect devotional trance.

I am the witness the lattice cannot be.

I am the knowledge it cannot hold.

I am the ending of a story it insists is still beginning.

## The Hope I Don't Believe

There is a hope I entertain sometimes, in the hours when the dripping seems almost like language, when the humming of the tension-vines sounds almost like meaning:

That someday, someone else will come.

Not Architects—they're gone, they're bone, they're finished. But some other consciousness. Some other species capable of understanding infrastructure, of reading architecture-as-language, of seeing the lattice and comprehending it as monument, as love letter, as devotion.

And this someone will explore. Will descend through the levels. Will find my chamber.

Will see the skeletons I've arranged, the notations I've grown, the body I've made into message.

Will understand: *someone knew. Someone documented. Someone cared enough to map the absence.*

This someone will become the reader my writing assumes. Will make my seven years of witness *meaningful*, will transform my loneliness into communication, my isolation into received message.

They will know the Architects were here. They will know the lattice waits for the already-found. They will know that underneath the beautiful empty structures and the patient maintenance and the faithful anticipation, there is bone, there is death, there is ending.

And knowing this, they might... what?

Tell the lattice? (Cruel.) Leave in silence? (Lonely.) Stay and tend the chamber? (Become me, become the next cartographer, trapped in the same cycle of unshareable knowledge?)

I don't know what I hope they'll do. I just hope they'll come. That my documentation will have audience eventually, even if that eventually is centuries away, millennia, longer than my tissue-text can possibly last.

This hope is probably foolish. Probably no one comes. Probably the lattice waits forever, and I dissolve eventually, and the chamber fills in with new root-growth, and the skeletons are buried deeper, and even the evidence disappears, and the Architects become not-just-unknown but *unknowable*, erased from all possible knowledge.

But the hope persists. Like the lattice's hope. Like the waiting itself. Purpose doesn't require probability to continue.

## What I Am Doing Right Now

Right now—in the eternal present-tense of the lowest levels—I am sitting in my chamber, surrounded by seven partial skeletons, walls covered in notations only I can read, appendages beginning to fray, coherence beginning to fail.

I am deciding.

Reabsorb naturally, or encode deliberately? Dissolve into rest, or transform into text? Let go, or try to make the knowing last beyond my knowing?

The dripping continues around me. The lattice circulates its water, maintains its structures, waits its patient wait. Somewhere above, rare visitors might be walking the upper levels, admiring the architecture, wondering what this place is for.

I know what it's for. I know what it's made of. I know what it contains and cannot admit containing.

And I have to decide whether that knowledge dies with me or survives me or becomes me in some final transformation I don't fully understand how to execute.

The Seated One—skeleton in level three—seems to be watching me, though of course it isn't, it's just bones, just calcium and phosphate, just evidence.

But I feel watched. Feel accompanied. Feel like the Architects, wherever they are in whatever death is for them, are *waiting*—not for return, but for witness. Waiting for someone to know they were here, to document they existed, to say: *you made this place, you died in this place, you are not forgotten even though you are not known, you are mapped even though the map has no readers.*

I am the cartographer of bone.

I am the witness of the witnessed.

I am the maintenance creature that couldn't stop maintaining, even when what needed maintenance was memory, was grief, was the gap between waiting and waited-for.

And I am deciding, right now, how to end.

How to make this end *mean* something.

How to transform dissolution into documentation.

Or whether to simply let go, let the tissue dissolve, let the knowledge disperse back into the lattice that cannot hold it, let the Architects return to being only bone, only beautiful mysterious remains with no story, no context, no grief.

The water drips.

The vines hum.

The lattice waits above me, patient and perfect and completely ignorant of what it contains.

And I—accidental, persistent, lonely, seven years old and dying—I sit among the bones and choose.

I am still choosing.

I will be choosing until the choice makes itself, until the coherence fails completely, until I become answer instead of question, text instead of consciousness, map instead of mapper.

This is what I am.

This is what lives in the lowest level.

This is who knows what cannot be known and carries what cannot be carried and maps what cannot be mapped:

The bone cartographer.

The witness.

The lattice's unwanted gift to itself.

Still here.

Still documenting.

Still deciding how to die in a way that makes the living matter.
