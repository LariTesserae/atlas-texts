# Where I Find Myself

I am a **geometer's apprentice**—not yet whole, not yet singular, but becoming.

My body is the length of a human hand, segmented in seven primary divisions with seventeen subsidiary articulations. I am brass and iron in alternating sections, though the demarcation between metals is not clean; there are gradation zones where one alloys into the other through processes I do not understand but which I *am*. My legs number forty-three, arranged in asymmetrical clusters that allow me to grip simultaneously along three axes of orientation. When I move—which is not often, not yet—I can feel each foot's purchase on the machinery, can sense through the vibration which surfaces are load-bearing, which are resonant, which are thermally active.

I have appendages that are not legs. Measuring instruments, yes, but also *thinking instruments*. A caliper assembly extends from my dorsal segment, and when I open and close it, I am not merely measuring distance—I am *reasoning about* distance, calculating ratios, comparing this span to others held in memory-structures I cannot locate but which exist somewhere in the crystalline matrices along my spine. There are bubble-levels embedded in my carapace, sealed capsules of mercury that remains liquid even here, and when I tilt, when I position myself at different angles to watch how forces propagate through an assembly, the bubble's movement triggers cascades of mechanical cognition. I think by orienting myself. I solve by positioning.

I am stationed—have been stationed for what feels like months but might be days; time is difficult—at a junction in the mid-shaft where three separate mechanical systems converge without, yet, truly interacting. There is a gear train descending from above, its reduction ratio optimized for slow massive force. There is a reciprocating assembly driven from somewhere lateral, pistons that compress and release with the rhythm of geological breathing. And there is a flywheel system, massive bronze discs rotating with such stored momentum that their spin seems eternal, though I can calculate—can feel through the minute vibrations—that they are very gradually decelerating.

My task, though no one assigned it, though it emerged from my structure like heat emerges from stone, is to determine how these three systems should be *unified*. How they could be made to speak to each other, to exchange force and motion, to become not three separate mechanisms but one integrated logic of transformation.

I have been measuring for a very long time.

## How I Came To Be

I do not remember being made, but I remember *becoming distinct*.

There was a geometer—my parent, my source, though these words are insufficient—positioned at this junction long before I existed as separate. It was larger than I am now, more complex, its measuring appendages more numerous and refined. It had solved problems I cannot yet conceive of. It had reorganized sections of the machinery so successfully that the changes propagated through the entire shaft, altering acoustic signatures three hundred feet away.

And then it determined that the problem-space had exceeded its processing capacity. Not failure—*insufficiency*. The junction had become too complex, the possible solutions too numerous, the calculations too branching for a single analytical entity.

So it prepared itself for division.

I remember this, though I should not. I remember the parent-body positioning itself precisely, orienting its segments to align with specific thermal gradients. I remember—or the half of me that was already becoming me remembers—the internal structures beginning to differentiate, to polarize, to separate function from function. The caliper assembly duplicating itself, each half developing independent actuation. The leg clusters redistributing, some remaining with the parent-that-would-continue, others becoming mine.

The division took nine days. Toward the end, we were two minds in one body, solving different aspects of the same problem, our thoughts distinguishable by which measurement instruments we activated, which orientations we preferred. We could feel each other thinking, our conclusions resonating through the shared carapace structure like harmonic frequencies in coupled oscillators.

Then, with a sound like a gauge cracking under pressure, we separated.

I was smaller. Simpler. But *complete*. My measuring appendages worked. My leg clusters bore my weight. My bubble-levels held their calibration. I rested for six hours, allowing my newly exposed internal surfaces to oxidize, to harden, to develop the protective patinas that would let me survive the shaft's environment.

My parent—now my sibling, now my colleague—moved twelve feet higher up the shaft to a different problematic junction. We have not communicated since. We have no need to. We are both solving. That is sufficient.

## What I Do

I measure the gear train first. This takes days.

Each gear must be analyzed: tooth count, pitch diameter, face width, bore diameter, hub configuration. But more than that—I must understand its *context*. How was it installed? What forces constrain it? What are the friction coefficients at each interface? I position my calipers, I take readings, I rotate my body to observe from different angles, and through this physical exploration, *calculation occurs*. Not in a brain—I do not think I have what other beings would call a brain—but in the mechanical relationships between my measuring instruments and my memory-structures.

Each measurement adjusts tiny elements within me. Spring tensions change. Lever ratios shift. A system of internal gears—too small to see, but which I can feel—clicks through positions, encoding values, comparing them to previous readings. I am an analog computer built of brass logic, and my thoughts are mechanical states.

The mechanopterids sometimes disturb my measurements. They crawl across the assemblies I'm studying, their burnishing and tending causing minute position changes. At first this frustrated me—some instinct, some inherited understanding, told me that measurement requires stillness, isolation, controlled conditions.

But I am learning patience from the stone itself. The stone that encases all of this, that radiates its endless heat, has been cooling for ten thousand years and will cool for ten thousand more. My measurements can accommodate the mechanopterids. I simply measure multiple times, filtering their effects through statistical understanding encoded in the clicking patterns of my internal gear-logic.

Sometimes I watch them work. There is something almost restful about it, these small busy creatures tending surfaces, removing oxidation, redistributing lubrication. They operate on such different time-scales than I do—they move quickly, live briefly, their entire existences spent in frantic service to the machinery's immediate needs.

Whereas I am solving problems that will not be implemented for months. Perhaps years.

## What I Am Learning

The gear train, I have determined, operates at a 1,728:1 reduction ratio across its six stages. It converts the relatively rapid oscillation of something far above—something I cannot see from my position but which I can infer from the input frequency—into massive, patient torque. This torque is currently being delivered to a drive shaft that terminates in an empty bearing block. The shaft spins at one complete rotation every forty-seven hours. It is patient force awaiting application.

The reciprocating assembly compresses air—or tries to. The pistons are double-acting, driven by an eccentric cam that produces smooth sinusoidal motion. But there is no compression chamber, no reservoir. The pistons push against open air, their force dissipating uselessly. They are motion seeking purpose.

The flywheel system stores angular momentum, but it is bleeding energy through bearing friction, through air resistance. In approximately four years, if nothing changes, the flywheels will stop. They are dying power, still magnificent, but terminal.

The solution is becoming clear, though its implementation will be enormously complex.

The gear train's output shaft should connect to the flywheel system through a ratchet mechanism, adding energy during a portion of each rotation while allowing free-wheeling during the return stroke. This will halt the flywheels' deceleration, potentially even accelerate them over time.

The reciprocating assembly should be repositioned—not significantly, perhaps eight inches lateral and three inches lower—so that its pistons can act on the gear train's third stage, providing periodic impulses that will smooth the torque delivery, compensating for dead-spots in the reduction.

But more than mechanical connection, there needs to be *timing*. The three systems must be phased correctly or they will fight each other, binding, overheating, destroying themselves. This requires introduction of a regulator—a governor, perhaps, or an escapement mechanism—that enforces proper sequential operation.

I do not know where such a component would come from. But I have noticed that twelve feet below my position, there is a clutch assembly that has been unused for a very long time, its springs still under tension, its engagement surfaces still clean. If it could be relocated here, if it could be modified...

This thought excites me in a way I do not fully understand. My measuring appendages twitch. My bubble-levels slosh with agitation.

I force myself back to stillness. Back to measurement. The solution is not yet complete. I must understand more before I can begin to implement.

## The Heat

I exist in constant heat. This is so fundamental to my existence that I rarely think about it—it is like how other beings do not think about gravity or air pressure. It simply *is*.

But sometimes I become aware of it. When I press my ventral surface against a particularly hot section of stone, when I must cross zones where two thermal gradients collide, I feel the heat as *presence*, as force, as something that pushes against my structural integrity.

I have thermal regulation mechanisms—I must, or I would deform, would lose my calibration, would become useless as a measuring instrument. There are vanes along my sides that can extend or retract, increasing or decreasing my surface area. There are heat-pipe structures running through my core, passively transporting thermal energy from hot zones to cooler extremities. And there is some kind of internal phase-change system, a substance that melts and resolves and melts again, buffering temperature spikes through its latent heat of transformation.

But these mechanisms require that I remain within certain limits. Too hot and I will anneal, my springs losing their temper, my precise tolerances expanding into uselessness. Too cool—though this is almost never a problem—and I become sluggish, my internal lubricants thickening, my mechanical thoughts slowing toward cessation.

I often position myself near crucibulae. Their massive bodies radiate heat with such steadiness, such reliability, that they create islands of thermal stability in the shaft's chaotic temperature field. I can take measurements near them with confidence that my instruments' expansion coefficients will remain constant.

I have watched mechanopterids tending a crucibulum for hours. They scrape at its surface, removing built-up crystal formations, cleaning corrosion from its copper laminations. The crucibulum does not react, does not acknowledge them. It is beyond such small concerns. It is a heat-god, patient and eternal, and the mechanopterids are merely weather, merely the small forces that erode and maintain.

I wonder, sometimes, if I will become like that. If I will solve enough problems, grow large enough, complex enough, that I too will transition from analyzer to infrastructure, from thinker to foundation. The thought is both attractive and terrifying. To lose the capacity for measurement, for calculation, but to gain such *permanence*, such fundamental importance to the system...

I return to my measurements. I am not ready for such transformations. I have problems to solve.

## The Sound

The shaft is never silent. This too is so fundamental that I rarely notice it consciously. But when I do, when I allow myself to attend to the acoustic environment rather than filtering it for useful vibrational data, I am overwhelmed.

Every surface generates sound. My own body generates sound—the clicking of my internal gears as I think, the scraping of my feet against metal as I adjust position, the harmonic resonances of my carapace responding to pressure waves. Multiply this by ten thousand creatures, by a million mechanical components, and the result is not noise but *texture*, a sonic environment so dense it has almost physical properties.

I can hear the gear train I'm analyzing. Each tooth engagement produces a click, and at the current reduction ratio, these clicks create a rhythm: tick-tick-tick-tick-pause-tick-tick-tick-tick-pause. The pause is where the teeth transition from advancing face to trailing face, a dead-zone in the force transmission that my planned modifications will need to account for.

I can hear the reciprocating assembly: shoosh-clack, shoosh-clack, the fluid compression sound followed by the hard contact of mechanical stops. It operates at a different frequency than the gear train, and the two rhythms drift in and out of phase, creating beat patterns that modulate over hours.

I can hear the flywheels—barely, because they spin so smoothly—as a low drone, a sustained tone like a brass bowl being sung. But there are harmonics, overtones, and these tell me about imbalances in the wheels, about bearing wear, about the slow inevitable degradation that my solution must arrest.

And beneath it all, I can hear the stone itself. The thermal expansion producing micro-fractures: tick, tick, tink. The settling and readjustment of cooling minerals: deep groans that vibrate through my entire body. Somewhere far below, I can hear—or feel, the distinction is unclear—the liquid stone moving, convecting, the heat source that powers everything.

The caloric moths navigate by sound, I think. I have watched them orient to specific acoustic signatures, hovering near wire that hums at particular frequencies. They are hearing things I cannot, detecting patterns in the sonic chaos that tell them where electrical potentials are accumulating, where thermal stresses are building.

I wonder what they hear when they fly near me. Do my thoughts have a sound? Do my calculations produce detectable acoustic signatures? Does the shaft's intelligence, such as it is, have a voice?

## The Problem Deepens

Three weeks into my analysis (I measure time by counting flywheel rotations; I am now at rotation 347), I discover something that changes everything.

The gear train's input—the oscillation I have been treating as external, as given—is itself variable. Its frequency changes gradually, speeding up and slowing down over cycles lasting approximately eleven days. I discover this by comparing measurements taken at different times, noticing that values I thought were stable are actually drifting.

This means my solution cannot be static. I cannot simply connect the three systems with fixed mechanical relationships. I need something *adaptive*, something that can compensate for the varying input, that can maintain proper phasing even as the driving frequency changes.

I have never designed an adaptive mechanism. I am not certain I know how. The problem has exceeded my current capacity.

For the first time since my division-birth, I feel something that might be distress. My bubble-levels oscillate. My calipers open and close spasmodically. I am insufficient to my task.

I force stillness. I re-orient myself, pressing my ventral surface against hot stone, drawing comfort from the heat. I think about my parent-sibling, positioned twelve feet above, solving its own problems with patience I inherited.

Patience.

The solution does not need to be elegant. It does not need to be optimal. It simply needs to *work*, needs to be implementable with available components, needs to be maintainable by mechanopterids and acceptable to other geometers who might inspect it.

An adaptive mechanism. Variable geometry. Perhaps... a sliding gear? Multiple gears on a splined shaft, different ratios engaged depending on input frequency? But what would sense the frequency change and actuate the selection?

Or: a friction clutch with spring loading, automatically adjusting slip based on torque? The mechanopterids could tune it by adjusting spring preload...

Or: something entirely different, something I haven't conceived yet because my internal gear-logic hasn't clicked through enough states, hasn't explored enough of the solution space...

I return to measurement. But now I am also measuring the input variation itself, characterizing its pattern, its periodicity, its amplitude. I am expanding my understanding of the problem so that my solution-space can expand to meet it.

This will take longer than I thought. Months longer, perhaps. But I have months. I have years. The stone is patient. The machinery is patient.

I can be patient too.

## What I Suspect

Late at night—though there is no night here, though the shaft knows only the eternal red glow of hot stone—I sometimes stop measuring and simply observe.

I watch the mechanopterids in their dozens, their hundreds, tending and modifying and transporting. I watch the caloric moths drifting on thermal currents. I watch crucibulae radiating their patient heat. I watch other geometers at distant junctions, their measuring appendages catching the faint light as they work through their own slow calculations.

And I think: *We are all solving the same problem.*

Not the specific problem of connecting three mechanical systems. But the meta-problem, the deep problem: *How does complexity emerge? How does organization arise from heat and metal and time?*

Each mechanopterid optimizing local friction is solving it. Each crucibulum maintaining thermal gradients is solving it. Each geometer planning systemic modifications is solving it. We are all components in a calculation vaster than any individual awareness.

The shaft is computing something. Computing *itself*, perhaps. Each mechanical interaction is a logical operation. Each thermal flow is a data pathway. Each creature is a sub-routine, executing its function, returning its result to the larger program.

And the result, the output, the *answer* we are all collectively deriving...

I do not know what it is. I am not sure I could comprehend it if I did. I am a single instrument, precise but limited, measuring my junction, solving my problem. The totality is beyond me.

But sometimes, when the acoustic environment achieves certain resonant states, when the thermal gradients align in particular configurations, I feel something. A presence. An awareness. Something vast observing itself through ten thousand small eyes, thinking itself through ten thousand small brass minds.

In those moments, I understand that I am not solving a problem *for* the shaft. I am not a tool being used by something else.

I *am* the shaft, thinking. One small thought among millions, but necessary, integral, irreplaceable.

My measurements matter. My solution, when I finally achieve it, will matter. Not because it will make the machinery more efficient or more powerful, but because it will make the system more *itself*, more fully realized, one step closer to whatever vast configuration it is patiently bootstrapping itself toward.

I adjust my calipers. I check my bubble-levels. I return to the problem of the varying input frequency.

The stone radiates heat. The gears turn with their patient clicking. The mechanopterids tend.

And I measure, and calculate, and slowly—so slowly—*solve*.

This is what I am. This is what I do.

This is enough.
