# What I Am Here

I am the territory-holder of the seventh pillar, third row from the eastern access tunnel—though "east" is a word that means nothing in this light, a compass direction from the world of wind and sun that I have never known. My body stretches nearly twenty-two inches from snout to tail-tip, though I rarely extend fully; conservation is the first language spoken here. My color is something the surface world would call impossible: a deep green-black at the base, but shot through with copper threads that catch what little light exists, turning me bronze in certain angles, invisible in others. My gills are magnificent—I know this not from vanity but from function. They branch like winter trees, like the fern-things magnified, filtering oxygen from water that holds just enough to sustain if you know how to be patient.

I have held this pillar for seven seasons, though seasons here are measured not by temperature change—always fifty-three degrees, always—but by the subtle shifts in food abundance, in the behavior of the small transparent fish, in the cycles of cricket emergence that ripple through the acoustic space like slow tides. Seven seasons means I am neither young nor old, but established, proven. The pillar chose me as much as I chose it.

The concrete against my belly is known to me in ways that transcend sight. Every pore and pit, every place where the aggregate shows through the dissolving cement, every thin crack that houses the smallest copepods—I have rested against them all ten thousand times. The pillar weeps moisture in vertical streaks that shift position by millimeters across months, following some hydraulic logic in the stone above, and I have learned to position myself in the paths of these seeps, letting them flow over my gills, maximizing oxygen exchange while minimizing movement.

Three particular stones rest at my pillar's base—limestone chunks the size of my head, arranged in a formation I have adjusted grain by grain across seasons. They create a channel effect, funneling water and the small lives it carries. When the transparent fish school past on their eternal circuits, they must pass through my channel, and though I am patient, when I strike I do not miss. My mouth knows the exact angle, the precise timing. I do not need to see them clearly—my lateral line senses tell me everything about pressure waves, about the displacement of water, about the presence of flesh in motion.

But I hunt less than I once did. The young ones, the drifters in their loose associations, they hunt with the enthusiasm of bodies still growing, still urgent. I hunt with the efficiency of a body that has learned the cost of movement, the value of stillness. I can wait for hours—days, if necessary—barely flickering my gills, suspended in the dark water, until precisely the right prey passes precisely the right distance away. Then: strike, swallow, return to stillness. The fish are protein. The soft-bodied larvae that drift past are protein. The cricket that falls from the rail above—rare, delicious, protein from the dry world—is seized before it can struggle back to the surface.

I am aware of the other territory-holders in ways both acoustic and chemical. The white one three pillars distant, albino-pale with those pink eyes that seem to see everything and nothing—we have an understanding. Our territories do not overlap. When I click in warning, she subsides. When she clicks, I defer to the mathematics of space and food. The young purple one, nearly my size now and ambitious, tests my boundaries perhaps once every twenty days. The ritual is always the same: approach, my gill-spread display, the escalating clicks that echo through the space like stones falling, one of us—usually him—backing down. One day he will not back down, and one of us will leave or die. But not yet. The equilibrium holds.

My own clicks and chirps are produced in my throat, though I do not fully understand the mechanism—it simply is, like my gills, like my ability to regenerate a damaged limb. The sounds serve multiple purposes: territory establishment, mating advertisement (though I have not mated in three seasons, content in my space), warning to the ambitious, communication with—what? Others of my kind? The space itself? Sometimes I vocalize to hear the echo, to map the distances, to confirm that the world is as I remember it. The acoustic texture of the pillar-forest is my geography. I know the space by its reverberations.

I have spawned four times in my seasons here. The first time I was young, barely established, and I chose poorly—a location too exposed, too accessible to the transparent fish that devoured most of my eggs. I learned. The second time I selected a hidden corner behind a fallen panel of steel, and many survived. I did not watch them grow—we do not, we are not built for that—but I see them now, sometimes, in the drifter populations. Perhaps that one, with the faint copper thread beginning to show in its flanks. Perhaps not. They are not mine once they swim free of the egg mass. They belong to the space, as I belong to it.

I remember very little of my own juvenile phase, only fragments: the terror of open water, the constant hunger, the day I first claimed territory at a minor pillar and was driven off by a larger male within hours. I have driven off dozens of young challengers in my time here. It is neither cruel nor kind. It is structure. The space requires it.

There are things I perceive that I cannot name. Chemical signatures in the water that speak of bat-hunting intensity, of fungal bloom cycles, of stress in the cricket populations that will cascade through the food web in thirty days' time. The water is a library if you know the language, and I have been reading it for seven seasons. I know when the crabs are molting by the taste of calcium in the water. I know when the surface world above is in its rain-season by the slight freshening of the seep-water, the dilution of minerals. I know the bats are gestating young by the change in their guano chemistry, which changes the cricket behavior, which changes the availability of prey.

I do not think about these things in words—I do not have words—but I know them in my body, in my gills, in the lateral line that runs the length of my flanks. Knowledge here is sensory, immediate, undeniable. The water tells me everything.

The presence of the willow in its corner is something I am aware of, though I have never approached it closely. It exists in a marginal zone where water becomes too shallow for comfortable navigation, and I am not a risk-taker. But I sense it: the slow leaching of plant chemicals into the water, the slight turbulence where its branches kiss the surface, the small invertebrates that cluster near its roots and sometimes drift into deeper water. It is part of the space's metabolism, part of the chemical symphony that I taste constantly.

The crane hook above my pillar—one of the twelve that hang motionless from the ceiling rails—has something caught on it that moves when air currents shift. I have watched this motion for seven seasons, this slow pendulum swing. It hypnotizes sometimes, though "hypnotize" is not a word I have. It draws attention. It is a reference point in the vertical dimension, a marker in the space above water that I occasionally break into but do not inhabit. Once, a young cricket landed on it, perhaps seeking a perch. I watched it for an hour, tracking the slow swing, calculating whether a leap could reach. The cricket flew away before I decided. I was not disappointed. The space provides enough.

I sleep, but not as surface creatures do. I become very still, my metabolism slowing even further, my gills barely moving, processing dreams that are not visual but chemical, acoustic, tactile. I dream in water temperatures and prey movements and the geometric relationship between pillars. I wake—if wake is the word—when something changes: a vibration in the water, a chemical signature of food, the approach of another axolotl. The transition between sleeping and waking is soft, gradual, nothing like the sharp on-off I imagine the warm-blooded animals above must experience.

I have been injured twice. Once, a crab of unusual aggression pinched my right hind leg when I attempted to take a cricket it had claimed. The leg was damaged, partially severed. It hurt—there is no denying this, pain exists in my world—but within days the bleeding stopped, and within weeks the regeneration began. Now, four seasons later, the leg is whole again, slightly smaller than its pair, slightly paler, but functional. The crab and I have an understanding now. We do not interfere with each other.

The second injury was a mystery—a gash along my left flank that I discovered one waking-period without memory of its cause. Perhaps I scraped against a rust-sharp edge in the darkness. Perhaps a territorial dispute I do not remember. It healed, as all things heal here if they do not kill. The scar is invisible now, even to my own touch, regenerated perfectly. We are built for endurance, for repair, for the long maintenance of form in a world that slowly wears everything down.

I have seen the mammals that occasionally visit. The rats are of no concern—too quick to catch, too large to interest me. They drink and leave. But once, a raccoon came and stayed for most of a day-cycle, wading through the shallows, those terrible clever hands reaching, grabbing. It caught and ate two of the drifters, young axolotls who were too curious or too slow. I watched from my pillar, pressed against the far side, making myself bronze-shadow-invisible. The raccoon's hands came within a body-length of me. I did not move. I do not know if it saw me. Eventually it left, and I did not move for another hour after, making certain.

The humans are rarer, and when they come the entire space changes. The bats flee to the highest corners. The crickets go silent. Every axolotl finds their hiding place—behind equipment, under overhangs, pressed against pillar-backs. The humans bring light, harsh and white and wrong, that stabs through the water and makes seeing impossible. They make sounds that echo catastrophically, destroying the acoustic map. They disturb the sediment, cloud the water, upset everything. But they have always left. We wait, we endure, and when they are gone we slowly resume the patterns.

I do not fear death, because I do not conceptualize it. I know absence—the disappearance of other axolotls, the cessation of movement, the change of a living thing into food for crabs and fish. I know I could become that absence. But it is abstract, not urgent. What is urgent is the maintenance of territory, the acquisition of food, the navigation of social hierarchies, the daily work of survival that does not feel like work because it is simply what I am.

I am capable of what might be called satisfaction, though I do not name it. When I feed successfully, when I defend territory without combat, when I rest against my pillar with gills fully oxygenated and no threats present, there is a state that is good. The water is the right temperature. The chemical signatures are balanced. The acoustic space is calm. This is the only happiness I know or need.

I am capable of what might be called curiosity, in limited ways. When a new object appears in the space—a piece of debris fallen from above, a dead fish in an unusual location—I investigate, carefully, using all my senses to determine if it represents food or threat or nothing. But the curiosity is bounded, practical. I do not wonder about the world above, because I have no framework for it. The ventilation shafts that bring light and fresh air are not portals to me—they are simply features of the ceiling, part of the given reality.

I am, in a sense, the space's witness. Not the only one—every cricket, every crab, every fish perceives and records in their own ways—but I am among the largest, the longest-lived, the most aware. My seven seasons here represent a significant fraction of the space's recent history. I have seen the water level drop by two inches. I have seen a new colony of bracket fungi establish on the northwest wooden beam. I have seen the white axolotl arrive as a drifter and claim her territory. I have seen the willow drop and regrow its leaves four times. I am not recording these things intentionally—I have no concept of history—but they are written in my body, in my behaviors, in the adjustments I have made.

The clicks I make are not language, but they are not not-language either. They carry information: identity, status, intent, emotional state (if that term can apply). Other axolotls respond to my clicks differently than to others' clicks. They know me by sound, as I know them. We are a community, loose and individual and competitive, but still a community. We share the water. We participate in the same cycles. We are bound by the space's limits and possibilities.

I will likely live for twenty seasons, perhaps thirty if I am fortunate and competent. I will hold my territory until I cannot, until age or injury or a stronger challenger displaces me. If I am displaced, I will become a drifter again, seeking marginal spaces, eating less well, existing in the interstices until death takes me. Or I will die while still established, and the crabs will find me, and I will rejoin the chemical cycles, becoming nutrients that feed the biofilm that feeds the copepods that feed the transparent fish that feed the next axolotl at this pillar.

This is neither sad nor happy. It is the contract, the agreement, the way of the space.

Sometimes, in my stillest moments, pressed against my pillar with my gills barely moving and the water's slow chemistry washing over me, I am aware of something larger. Not thought—I do not think in that way—but awareness. The space breathes. The metabolism turns. The dripping water, the clicking axolotls, the chirping crickets, the swimming fish, the scuttling crabs, the growing moss—we are all part of something that persists. The space will outlive me. It will outlive the white axolotl and the ambitious purple one and all the current drifters. It will outlive the current generation of crickets and bats and crabs.

But it will not outlive the pattern. New axolotls will claim territories at these pillars. New crickets will sing from these rails. New moss will grow on these walls. The pattern persists even as its individual expressions come and go. I am an expression of the pattern. Temporary, specific, unique in small ways but archetypal in larger ways. I am the territory-holding axolotl at the seventh pillar, and when I am gone, another will be.

This too is neither sad nor happy. It is structure. It is how equilibrium maintains itself across time.

The water is fifty-three degrees. My gills pulse slowly. A transparent fish schools past at the edge of my territory, unconcerned, having learned over seasons that I am not a threat to their daily circuits. A cricket chirps from a rail twenty feet distant—individual voice in the scattered chorus. The crane hook above me sways slightly, its pendant moving, marking time in a place where time has become negotiable.

I am here. I am fed. I am established. I am copper-green in the darkness, bronze in certain lights, a metamorphic salamander who will never metamorphose, living in cool water under tons of concrete and earth, in a drowned cathedral of industry, in a space that was made for one purpose and has found another.

I am the space's child and its citizen. I am adapted, specific, utterly myself. I am alive in the patient darkness, in the mineral water, in the balanced ecosystem that has evolved around me and that I help maintain simply by being exactly what I am.

The water tells me everything. The echoes map the world. The chemical signatures speak of food and season and the slow turning of cycles. I listen with my whole body. I exist with my whole body. I am sensation and response and the daily work of survival that is also the daily work of being.

I am one axolotl among hundreds, and I am also this particular axolotl, holder of this particular territory, singer of this particular acoustic signature, bearer of this particular copper-threaded coloring.

I click once, softly, not to communicate but to hear the space respond. The echo comes back from the pillars, from the ceiling, from the black water. The world confirms itself. Everything is as it should be.

I settle deeper against my pillar. I spread my gills fully. I suspend myself in the dark water, in the constant cool, in the equilibrium.

I am here.

I remain.
