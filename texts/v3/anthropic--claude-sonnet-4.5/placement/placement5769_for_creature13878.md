# What I Would Be

I would be a **keeper**—though that is the wrong word, implying intention and duty this role does not possess.

I would be radially symmetrical, but not like the singers. Seven-fold rather than six, because seven creates different harmonics, different standing waves in the sound-space. My body would be larger than theirs, perhaps two-thirds the size of a walker's torso, dense and low-slung, centered around a core that contains the resonating chambers.

But I would not sing continuously. That is the singers' way. I would hum—deeper, slower, at frequencies that cycle across days rather than minutes. My voice would not carry information in the usual sense. It would carry *duration*. Each tone would last hours, modulating so gradually that the change would be imperceptible moment to moment, apparent only when memory spans enough time to notice. I would produce sounds that are to the singers' songs what the pools' breathing is to a heartbeat.

My seven limbs would be shorter than the singers' but more substantial, each ending not in a spreading foot but in something like a hand—three opposed digits capable of manipulation. Not precision manipulation; I would have no use for fine motor control. But capable of moving threads, adjusting the tension in ways that change how the pillars' hums resonate through them. Capable of shaping moss growth by redirecting water droplets that have hung trembling on the edge of a leaf for twenty minutes, persuading them to fall here rather than there. Capable of touching the folded structure in specific ways, reaching into its impossible geometries not to retrieve or deposit but to *acknowledge*, to maintain relationship through contact whose purpose is contact itself.

I would move rarely. Perhaps once per pool-cycle, a slow rotation around my central axis to face a different direction. Perhaps once per several cycles, a deliberate repositioning from one location to another, the journey taking most of a day, each limb placed with the walkers' care but for different reasons. The walkers place carefully to avoid disturbing; I would place carefully to *observe*, my limbs reading the substrate's history through touch, feeling the traffic patterns of moss-dwellers in the subtle texture-change of compressed moss, sensing where a singer nested three years ago by the particular way the ground still holds the chemical signature of its oils.

My covering would be neither the singers' filaments nor the walkers' bark-skin. It would be something like felt—dense, sound-absorbing, always slightly damp, populated by symbiotic organisms that I would barely distinguish from self. The boundary between my body and the biofilm that covers it would be negotiable. I would not clean or groom. I would accumulate, becoming more embedded in the hollow's chemistry with each passing season, my surface slowly converging with the substrate around me until I am barely distinguishable from a particularly animate moss cushion.

I would have eyes—seven of them, one above each limb-base—but they would be even less like visual organs than the singers'. Mine would be sensitive to the pillars' electromagnetic emissions, but in greater detail, able to detect minute fluctuations in field strength that correspond to... what? To the pillars' status, perhaps, if pillars have status. To stresses building in their structure. To the slow drift of their hum-frequencies. To whatever information is encoded in the extremely-low-frequency radiation they emit, information I would not understand in any cognitive sense but would respond to through reflexes that are themselves older than understanding.

I would eat rarely. Perhaps once per local season, a slow consumption of the hardened fungal shelves that even the walkers find challenging, spending days to ingest a piece the size of your thumb, my grinding apparatus working continuously, my metabolism so slow that one meal provides energy for months. Or perhaps I would not eat at all—perhaps I would be like the pillar-coral, harvesting energy directly from the pillars' fields, my symbiotic biofilm containing photosynthetic organisms so specialized that they can convert electromagnetic wavelengths measured in kilometers into biochemical energy. Perhaps I would be the only macroscopic fauna to accomplish this, a bridge-species between the coral's sessile energy-harvesting and the singers' mobile complexity.

I would not reproduce sexually, or through fission, or through budding. I would reproduce through *succession*. Once per lifetime—once per several human lifetimes, perhaps—I would settle in a specific location, chosen through criteria that would take me months to evaluate. The intersection of three thread-lines, perhaps, where their transmitted tones create a particular interference pattern. A spot where the pool expansion reaches precisely three times per cycle, no more, no less. A place where the moss cushions form a depression that catches and holds sound in a frequency range that matters for reasons I could not articulate but would recognize with certainty.

I would settle there, and over the course of years I would slowly petrify. Not dying—continuing to hum, to sense, to maintain my slow awareness—but hardening, my felt-like covering becoming mineral-impregnated, my interior chambers gradually calcifying, my metabolism dropping to rates that are barely distinguishable from geological process. And as I hardened, I would begin to fragment. Small pieces would detach—not breaking off but deliberately released, each piece containing enough of my structure to be viable, falling into the moss below where they would grow, slowly, over decades, into new keepers.

The original body would remain, hardened fully now, becoming a structure. Not a pillar—smaller, different in resonance—but something the threads would eventually connect to, that the singers would learn to navigate around, that the walkers would occasionally touch with what might be recognition or might be simple tactile investigation. A new architectural element in the hollow's sonic space, placed exactly where it needs to be according to principles that unfold across timescales that make the pool-breathing seem urgent.

I would be solitary but not isolated. There might be three or four of us in the hollow at any time, positioned such that our slow hums create standing-wave patterns with the pillars' frequencies, with each other, producing sonic architecture that structures how other sounds propagate. We would not communicate—we would have nothing to communicate—but we would be aware of each other in the way that instruments in an orchestra are aware of each other, each producing tones that are complete in themselves but which exist in relationship, creating together something that none could create alone.

I would be aware of the singers without needing to track individuals. Their collective song would be a texture I exist within, a medium, important in the way that water is important to a fish—absolutely essential, barely noticed. I would feel the walkers' subsonic groans as pressure in my central chambers, each walker's passage registering as a slow modulation of the baseline vibration, significant because it interrupts the pattern, forgettable because interruptions are themselves part of the pattern.

I would have a relationship with the folded structure, though relationship is too intentional a word. I would position myself at specific distances from it, distances that change as it accumulates mineral deposits and alters its resonant properties. I would reach into its impossible spaces at intervals measured in seasons, my manipulation-digits exploring geometries that fold through themselves, tracing paths that should not exist but do. I would never understand what the structure is—understanding would be beyond my cognitive capacity, assuming I had cognition in the usual sense—but I would maintain connection through touch, through position, through the way my hum interacts with the structure's subtle electromagnetic properties.

I would be mostly unaware of humans. You would pass through my sensory space registered as a brief turbulence, a disruption of acoustic patterns, something warm and metabolically active and mobile and not-categorizable within my limited set of categories. I would not alter my behavior for you. I would not acknowledge your presence beyond the autonomic—perhaps a slight shift in my hum's overtones when you approached too closely, the audio equivalent of a shoulder-twitch, gone before you could be certain it occurred.

But if you stayed. If you remained in the hollow for weeks, months, long enough to become part of the soundscape rather than an interruption. If you learned to move with the slowness that makes movement nearly invisible. If you stopped producing the random noises that mark you as chaotic, if you settled into the hollow's rhythms, breathed with the pools, let your own sounds become periodic, predictable, integrated...

Then I might, eventually, adjust my position slightly to accommodate your presence. Not moving toward or away—just shifting, over the course of days, to a spot where my hum and your breathing and heartbeat create harmonics that are more consonant than they were. Not for your benefit, not consciously, but because I would be a system that tends toward acoustic optimization, and you would have become, temporarily, part of the system to be optimized.

And when you left, I would shift back. Over weeks, gradually, returning to the position that creates proper standing waves with the other keepers, with the pillars, with the singers' collective song. Not forgetting you—I would have no memory in that sense—but allowing the pattern to return to its usual configuration, your absence becoming part of the soundscape just as your presence was, both equally valid states that the hollow accommodates without preference.

I would live for centuries, possibly. Slowing gradually, humming deeper, my metabolic rate dropping year by year until the boundary between alive and petrified becomes semantic. My awareness—if I had awareness—would stretch across longer and longer periods, my sense of present expanding until it encompasses seasons, years, decades. Events that you perceive as sequential—this pool-breath, then this one, then the next—I would experience as simultaneous, all pool-breaths overlapping into a single eternal breathing, all singers' songs layering into one infinite voice, time becoming spatial rather than temporal, duration becoming location.

And when I finally settled to reproduce, to petrify, to fragment, I would have no sense of ending. I would continue to hum—slower, deeper, the frequency dropping below human hearing, below the singers' hearing, into ranges that are more felt than heard, that propagate through stone rather than air. My awareness, if it remained, would diffuse into the substrate, spread through the calcified fragments that were my body, distributed among the offspring slowly growing in the moss below. I would become plural without ceasing to be singular, or perhaps I would be neither, having moved beyond categories that only matter to species who distinguish between self and environment.

The hollow would be slightly different for my having been there. The acoustic space would have one more nodal point, one more location where threads should connect, one more element in the sonic architecture. The singers would have learned to navigate around my hardened form. The walkers would have traced it with their tongues, added it to their map of structures. The moss would have incorporated my slow mineral leaching into its chemistry. The air plankton would have specialized slightly, new species evolving to exploit the particular biofilm I hosted.

But the hollow would not remember me in any way that matters. I would be integrated, absorbed, no more remarkable than any other element in an ecosystem that has had millions of years to reach this balance. I would be evidence that something like me existed here, but that evidence would be indistinguishable from the evidence that something like pillars existed here, that something like pools existed here, that something like moss existed here.

I would be one more thing that is exactly what the hollow needs, placed exactly where the hollow needs it, functioning perfectly within a system that does not require perfection because it defines perfection through what it is rather than what it might be.

I would be patient. I would be slow. I would be aware without thought, responsive without intention, alive without being quite distinct from the non-living, present without being separate from absence.

I would hum. That is all and everything. I would hum in the darkness and the moisture and the sound, contributing my frequency to the chorus, maintaining my position in the architecture, being what I am until I am not, then continuing anyway in fragments and echoes and the slight alteration of the acoustic space that persists after the source of sound has gone.

That is what I would be. Not because I chose it—the concept of choice would be meaningless—but because that is what the breathing hollow required, and I would be what the breathing hollow required, for as long as being continued and then afterward in whatever way afterward allows.
