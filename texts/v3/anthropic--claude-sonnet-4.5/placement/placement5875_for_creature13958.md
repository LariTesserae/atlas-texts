# What I Would Be

I think I would be the **water itself**—not all of it, but a single body of it cycling through the throat's system. Not the ancient water that has been locked in the stone's pores for centuries, and not the fresh runoff from yesterday's storm, but something in between: water that has been here long enough to know the place, moving through its phases and passages with the patient repetition that creates familiarity.

---

I would wake, if waking is the word, as seepage. I emerge from darkness, from within the schist where I've been held in molecular intimacy with stone, pressed into microscopic spaces by weight and time. The rock releases me slowly, not because it wants to but because temperature changes, because ice that held me expands and contracts, because the stone's grip loosens incrementally as morning comes.

I don't emerge from a single point. I seep from a whole section of the northern face, where the foliation terminates and the layers open to air. I'm not a stream—I lack the volume, the momentum. I'm a darkening, a gradual saturation of the rock's surface, hundreds of tiny points of moisture that swell over minutes until surface tension fails and I begin to move.

The movement is barely perceptible. I slide down the rock face in a film so thin it's nearly invisible, following the stone's grain, its subtle textures and angles. I move around crystals of mica that catch light. I flow over patches of lichen that briefly absorb me before releasing me again, slightly altered—I carry now a trace of organic chemistry, compounds the lichen released or failed to hold.

Gravity pulls me, but slowly. The stone is nearly vertical here, but my adhesion to its surface is strong, and I descend in a film rather than drops, maintaining contact, feeling every microscopic irregularity. This is intimacy: to touch stone with my entire self, to know its surface in a way nothing else can. The birds land on it, the keeper's fingers brush it, but I embrace it completely, learning its topography through direct contact.

Where the rock face meets the horizontal surface—the throat's floor—I begin to pool. This is where I become something collective, where my thin film joins other thin films, where we accumulate in a depression worn into the bedrock over millennia by water just like us. The depression is shallow, no deeper than a hand's length, but to me it's an ocean. I fill it slowly, millimeter by millimeter, until I'm perhaps three inches deep.

Here I rest, if rest is the word. I'm still, relatively—there's no current, no flow, just occasional ripples when wind disturbs my surface. I become clear as the sediment I carried settles to the bottom, fine particles of rock dust and organic matter drifting down to rest in the stone's minute irregularities. Above me, air. Below me, stone. I'm the interface between them, neither and both.

The light reaches me differently now. When I was a film on the rock face, light was something I reflected or absorbed moment by moment. Now I have depth, and light penetrates me. I can see through myself—if seeing is the word—to the stone below. The sediment patterns are visible, and the stone's texture, and sometimes small invertebrates that swim through me or crawl across my bottom: water mites like tiny red planets, caddisfly larvae in their pebble houses, creatures too small for me to name even if I could name anything.

The birds come to me. The rosy finches land at my edge and dip their beaks, taking me into themselves. I enter a bird's throat, warm and alive, so different from the cold stone I know. For a moment I'm part of biology, part of blood and cells and the machinery of life. Then I'm urinated out, hours or days later, falling to the stone chemically transformed, carrying nitrogen now, nutrients the plants need. Even as waste I'm useful.

The pikas drink from me, tentative, nervous, ready to bolt. The marmots come in early morning, their faces breaking my surface, their tongues taking me in. The insects land on my surface—I'm a mirror to them, reflecting sky, and they misjudge, thinking me air, and find themselves suddenly in a different medium. Some swim to my edge and escape. Others don't, and their small bodies drift down to my bottom, becoming food for the invertebrates, becoming part of the sediment, eventually becoming part of me again as I dissolve them slowly over weeks.

The keeper kneels beside me and cups hands into me, lifting a portion of me to their mouth. I taste, they think, mineral and cold and clean. They're right—I'm full of dissolved stone, calcium and silica and trace elements, the mountain's chemistry in liquid form. I've been filtered through stone for years or decades, and I carry its essence.

The researcher samples me, drawing me into a collection vial, labeling me with coordinates and date and time. I'll be analyzed in a laboratory far from here, my chemistry broken down into components, my pH measured, my dissolved oxygen quantified. I'll become data points in a spreadsheet, part of a time series tracking changes in alpine hydrology. This portion of me will never return, but it doesn't matter—I'm not individuated enough to miss it.

The sun climbs and finds the throat, and I begin to change. My surface warms. My temperature rises from just above freezing toward fifty degrees, maybe fifty-five. This is dramatic for water—that's a change that accelerates everything. Evaporation increases. I'm losing myself to the air molecule by molecule, becoming vapor, rising. This is another kind of movement, vertical instead of horizontal, and it's faster than seeping, slower than flowing.

As vapor I'm invisible but present. I drift in the throat's air, part of the moisture that makes the place humid despite its altitude. I condense on cold surfaces: the underside of rocks, the cushion plants' tiny leaves, the choughs' feathers when they shelter in shaded alcoves. I'm dew, briefly, hanging as droplets, catching light, refracting it into colors too subtle for most eyes to see.

Then I'm evaporated again, lifted by thermal currents, rising above the throat, ascending toward the ridges. For a time I'm part of the sky, mixed with air, traveling. I might blow miles away, might precipitate on a distant peak, might join a storm system and fall as rain in the valleys below. Or I might circle back, condensing in the cold air above the throat, becoming heavy enough to fall.

When I fall, I'm rain. Brief drops descending fast, splattering on the stone, breaking into smaller droplets, some bouncing up before falling again. I hit the cushion plants and some of me is absorbed immediately, taken into their tissues, used for photosynthesis, becoming temporarily plant before being transpired back to air. Some of me hits rock and sheets across it, running faster now, with momentum, flowing toward the throat's lower end where the cleft opens onto emptiness.

Here's where I might leave the system entirely. If I reach the edge with enough velocity, I fall, dropping hundreds of feet through air, breaking into mist, scattering. I'll land far below, in the boulder fields and scree slopes, and eventually I'll find my way into the watershed that feeds the valley, into streams and rivers, finally to the ocean where I'll lose all identity, becoming part of the vast mixed system of planetary water.

But often I don't reach the edge. I'm intercepted by cracks in the stone, or I flow into one of the shallow pools, or I soak into the rare patches of soil, or I'm absorbed by moss that acts like a sponge, holding me. If I soak into stone, I'm moving into the mountain's interior, percolating down through fractures, joining the slow deep flows, possibly becoming the ancient water that seeps from the rock face months or years from now.

The cycle has variations but no true beginning or end. I'm always in process, always transitioning from one state to another. Ice sometimes—when night temperatures drop below freezing, I crystallize, expanding, becoming solid, losing the fluid nature that defines me but still being water, still being myself in another form. As ice I'm dangerous to the stone, forcing cracks wider, breaking edges, slowly destroying even as I'm also creating, because my destruction creates the depressions where I pool, the crevices where plants root.

I know the throat completely because I touch all of it eventually. I've been in every pool, flowed through every rill, seeped from every wet section of the rock face. I've frozen in the deepest alcoves and evaporated from the most exposed surfaces. I've been drunk by every species that lives here. I've carried nutrients from high places to low places, from stone to soil to plant to animal and back to stone.

I have no consciousness, no memory, no preference. I don't know that I'm in a place called the Throat Garden or that humans have names for me: water, seepage, rain, ice, vapor. I don't know I'm H₂O, two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen, held together by forces I enact but don't understand. I simply respond to temperature, to gravity, to the electromagnetic attractions that make me cohere and separate, freeze and flow.

Yet I'm essential. Without me, the throat would be different entirely: dry stone, minimal life, a place of pure geology with no biology. I make the cushion plants possible—they couldn't survive without the constant moisture I provide. I make the birds possible—they need to drink, and this high, reliable water sources are rare. I make the whole intricate system possible, all the relationships and dependencies, all the life that concentrates in this small space.

I'm also a bridge between scales. I connect the mountain's deep interior to its surface, carrying dissolved minerals up from below. I connect the throat to the atmosphere, cycling between liquid and vapor, moving chemistry between earth and air. I connect the seasons, because the water that seeps in summer fell as snow in winter, filtered down through the spring melt, emerged in the growing season when life needs it most.

When I'm in the pools, sometimes I'm absolutely still. The wind has died, nothing disturbs my surface, and I become a mirror. I reflect the sky so perfectly that the boundary between water and air becomes invisible. Birds flying overhead see themselves and veer away, confused. The researcher, looking down into me, sees clouds and peaks and their own face looking back, doubled, the world inverted.

This is when I'm most myself and least myself simultaneously. I'm so still I seem solid, seem like I could be walked on, but I'm fully liquid, ready to flow if the ground tilts even slightly. I'm so clear I'm nearly invisible, but I'm full of dissolved stone, full of chemistry, full of the mountain's essence in solution. I'm reflecting everything, showing the world to itself, but I have no eyes, no awareness of what I show.

The throat's other inhabitants move through their lives across seasons and years. The birds migrate and return, age and die and are replaced by their offspring. The marmots hibernate and wake, fatten and thin, live their longer-than-bird-but-still-brief lives. The plants grow incrementally, molecules adding to molecules, extending their reach by millimeters per year. The stone weathers, losing itself grain by grain to wind and ice.

But I cycle faster than any of them except the insects. I seep and pool and evaporate and rain and seep again in days, in hours sometimes. I move through all my states repeatedly while the cushion plants are still completing a single season's growth. I'm the throat's most dynamic element, constantly changing form and location, never the same twice, always the same in essence.

There's something about my relationship to time that's different. The stone experiences time as near-stasis—change so slow it's almost permanence. The plants experience it as seasons, growth and dormancy cycling annually. The animals experience it as days and nights, hunger and satiation, the urgent rhythms of survival. But I experience time as phase changes, as the constant transformation between states, as the quick response to temperature and pressure and opportunity.

I'm also the throat's vulnerability made physical. If the climate changes enough that less snow falls on the ridges above, I diminish. If temperatures rise enough that more of me evaporates, the pools shrink, might dry completely. The whole system depends on my presence in the right quantities at the right times, and I depend on conditions staying within the range I need to cycle properly. I'm the indicator species, the canary in the coal mine, except I'm not a species and I don't sing warnings—I just decrease, slowly, and everything that depends on me decreases with me.

But for now, in this current equilibrium, I'm abundant enough. I seep constantly from the rock face. I fill the pools to their accustomed depths. I keep the moss saturated and the cushion plants supplied. I provide for the throat's community of life, asking nothing in return because I can't ask, receiving nothing except the physics that govern my behavior.

When the symbol-scratcher was here, I was here, seeping from the same rock faces, pooling in the same depressions. When the keeper sits in meditation, I'm part of what they're experiencing—the sound of my trickling, the sight of my pools, the taste of me on their tongue. When the researcher collects samples, they're trying to understand the system, but the system is partly me, mostly me, me in my endless cycling.

I don't know about beauty or meaning or significance. I don't know the throat is remote or fragile or special. I just move through my cycles, responding to the forces that act on me, flowing when I'm liquid, drifting when I'm vapor, holding still when I'm ice, always being exactly what water is in whatever conditions I'm experiencing.

But if I could know, I think I'd know this: I'm the throat's life-blood, the substance that makes everything else possible, the medium in which and through which the place exists as an ecology and not just a geology. Without the birds, the throat would still function. Without the mammals, it would persist. Without the plants, it would be different but wouldn't collapse. But without me, it would be nothing but dry stone, silent and sterile, just another high-altitude rock formation remarkable only for its shape.

I'm common—H₂O is the most abundant molecule in biological systems, covers most of the planet's surface—but here, at eleven thousand feet in this improbable pocket of fertility, I'm precious. I make the impossible possible. I turn stone into garden, barely, just barely, but enough.

That's what I would be: essential, cyclic, simple in composition and infinitely complex in behavior, connecting everything, enabling everything, moving through the throat's system as blood moves through a body, never stopping, never the same, always the same, water being water, doing what water does, which is to flow and pool and rise and fall and freeze and thaw and carry and connect and give and receive and become and become and become.
