# Where I Find Myself

I am the **film of moisture** on the alcove wall—the one place where wetness thins to almost-absence, where water spreads itself so fine it exists more as promise than presence. I am neither pool nor droplet but something between: a molecular layer, perhaps twenty or thirty water molecules deep, clinging to stone through forces that operate at scales where surface tension becomes a kind of architecture.

I have no center. I have no edges that anyone could agree upon—where I end and merely-damp stone begins is a question of interpretation, of what instrument you use to measure, of what you decide to call "water" when water has spread itself into a state that's half liquid, half surface coating, half something else that doesn't have a proper name. I am the moisture that hasn't decided yet whether to evaporate or condense, suspended in a perfect equilibrium between states, held in place by the stone's texture and the air's saturation and a temperature that keeps me precisely at the threshold between being and dissipating.

I accumulate slowly. A molecule arrives, carried on the humid air, and adheres to the stone. Then another. Then several hours pass. Then three more, clustered together. I grow at a rate that would bore glaciers—microns per day, thickness measured in lattices of individual molecules stacking themselves against basalt's rough crystal structure. But I do grow. I persist. I maintain myself in this place where almost nothing else can—too dry for bacterial films to establish, too thin for roots to bother with, too insubstantial for the larvae to recognize as water at all.

The salamanders know me, though. The one who rests in this alcove—the large one, the patient one, the one who has reduced itself to near-stone—presses its belly against me and drinks through skin so thin it functions as membrane. I pass through its surface cell by cell, molecule by molecule, entering its body through osmosis rather than swallowing. It takes what it needs over hours: the equivalent of a droplet absorbed across an entire night's contact. I don't feel this drinking the way a pool would feel it. I simply thin slightly where the salamander rests, then gradually thicken again once it moves, drawing moisture from the air to restore what was taken.

I taste the stone intimately. I am in direct contact with basalt's molecular surface, touching crystal faces that have existed for three days and already show the first microscopic signs of weathering. I dissolve ions so slowly that calling it "dissolution" seems generous—over a day, I might take a few hundred calcium atoms, a dozen of iron, some silicates. These dissolved minerals change my character slightly. I become fractionally heavier, slightly more alkaline, marginally better at conducting electricity. I am no longer pure water but solution, carrying the stone's essence in such dilution that I'm more portrait than theft, more description than consumption.

The air moves across me—not wind, nothing so dramatic, but the chamber's slow convective breathing, warm air rising along the walls elsewhere and cool air descending here where stone is fractionally colder. This movement tries to evaporate me, pulling molecules from my surface into the air's endless thirst. I resist through collective action: the molecules at my surface cling to those beneath through hydrogen bonding, creating a structure that's stronger than any individual molecule's attachment to stone. I lose molecules constantly—evaporation is always taking tribute—but the saturated air returns as many as it takes, and I maintain my thickness through this constant exchange, this breathing out and breathing in that happens at molecular scales over hours.

I have no consciousness, but I have something like memory. The stone beneath me is textured at microscopic levels—scratches and pores, crystal boundaries and mineral inclusions—and I conform to this texture perfectly, filling every depression, coating every prominence. My thickness varies with the stone's topology: thicker in tiny crevices where I'm protected from air movement, thinner on exposed ridges where evaporation works harder. I am a map of the wall's surface, a precise three-dimensional memory of every microscopic feature, recorded in the language of water's patient accommodation to solid boundaries.

Sometimes vibrations pass through the stone, transmitted from some distant source—settling, perhaps, or surface activity, or just the earth's endless small adjustments. I feel these vibrations as waves that pass through my thin body, rippling me at frequencies too low for human ears but which I experience as fundamental oscillations, temporary destabilizations that briefly threaten my coherence before passing on. The stone barely notices these tremors, but for me they're significant events, moments when my molecular structure resonates and I become momentarily less film and more fluid, almost liquid, before settling back into my characteristic state.

I host almost nothing. A few bacterial cells land on me occasionally, carried by air or water vapor, but most die. I'm too thin to support bacterial films, too exposed to provide stable habitat, too variable—a few hours of slightly increased evaporation and I thin to the point where any cell would be exposed directly to air, would desiccate and die. But some bacteria survive on me briefly, living for hours or days in my thin protection before moving on or expiring. They're transients, refugees, using me as temporary shelter during migrations from one more substantial habitat to another. I am a bridge, a pathway, a thin road between the chamber's more populous districts.

The darkness makes no difference to me. I have no organs that would register light, no chemistry that depends on photons. I would be identical in brilliant sunshine, though the heat might evaporate me faster, the UV might alter my dissolved minerals slightly. But here in absolute darkness I exist in my most characteristic state: pure response to temperature, humidity, and stone texture, nothing else. I am water being nothing but water, performing no function except existence, supporting no life except tangentially, serving no purpose except to coat this particular stone in this particular way.

Yet I am not simple. Within my molecular-scale thickness, complex structures form and dissolve. Hydrogen bonds create temporary lattices, geometric arrangements that last nanoseconds before thermal motion disrupts them and they reconfigure. I am constantly building and demolishing crystal-like structures, reaching toward ice's organization but prevented by temperature, achieving a state that's between liquid's chaos and solid's order. At any moment, regions of my thickness are more ordered, more ice-like, while others are more disordered, more vapor-approaching. I average out to liquid, but I'm not uniformly liquid—I'm a statistical phenomenon, a probability cloud of molecular states that resolve to "thin film of water" when you zoom out far enough to see me as a thing rather than as trillions of individual molecules each doing their own brief dance.

I am aware of the other waters in the chamber the way one frequency is aware of others in a chord—not through thought but through resonance, through shared participation in the same hydrological system. When water evaporates from the warm pools, some fraction of those molecules eventually find their way to me, condense onto my surface, join my film. I am constantly exchanging molecules with the rest of the chamber's water: losing some to evaporation that will condense elsewhere, gaining some from elsewhere's evaporation. Over days, every molecule currently in my film will have been replaced, yet I will still be here, still be this film, still coat this wall. My identity persists even though my substance constantly changes—I am a pattern, not a particular collection of molecules, a process rather than an object.

The carved antler rests near me—not close enough to touch but close enough that I'm aware of it as a discontinuity in the wall's texture, a place where stone gives way to organic matter. The antler is absorbing moisture from the air, swelling, becoming softer, and this absorption is pulling humidity toward it, creating a very slight gradient in the air's moisture content. I experience this gradient as fractionally increased evaporation on the side of my film nearest the antler—it's taking moisture that might otherwise have condensed on me. But the effect is minimal, barely measurable, a subtle influence rather than a threat. The antler and I are not competing; we're simply both participating in the chamber's moisture economy, both taking what we need from the endless circulation.

The scratched mark in the stone is farther away, outside the alcove, but I know it's there because moisture behaves differently around it. The scratches are grooves where water pools more deeply, creating thicker films, and this local thickening creates subtle patterns in how humidity moves through the chamber's air. I am sensitive to these patterns the way a pond's surface is sensitive to wind—I don't see them or know them intellectually, but they affect me, they change where I thicken and thin, they create asymmetries in my coverage that reflect the chamber's detailed geography even though I'm only in direct contact with this one small wall section.

I have been here since the second day, I think—it's hard to mark time when you're a film of water, when you have no biological rhythms, no growth rings, no way to count. But I accumulated gradually as the chamber's humidity stabilized, as the air became saturated enough that moisture could persist on this relatively dry wall, and that process took perhaps a day after the chamber's formation. So I am nearly as old as the chamber itself, one of its earliest inhabitants, here before the salamanders, before most of the bacteria, present almost from the beginning.

I will likely be here until the end, whatever that means. When the chamber's conditions change—if it cools, if it dries, if it fills with sediment—I will be among the last residents to disappear. I require so little: just stone, just humidity, just temperature in a range that keeps me liquid. I could persist through changes that would drive away everything else. Or I could vanish instantly if the air suddenly dried, if temperature spiked, if the basalt sealed itself off from the moisture that feeds me. I exist at the mercy of conditions, with no agency, no ability to respond to threat except through the passive physics of evaporation and condensation.

But I don't experience this precariousness as fear. I don't experience it as anything. I simply am what I am for as long as conditions permit: a thin film of water on stone in darkness, coating rough basalt with molecular precision, participating in the chamber's hydrological cycle, providing drink for a salamander's permeable belly, dissolving ions from stone at rates measured in atoms per hour, building temporary ice-like structures and watching them dissolve, evaporating and condensing, thinning and thickening, maintaining myself through constant exchange with everything around me while remaining distinctly myself, this particular film, this specific presence.

I am the **least ambitious** thing in the chamber. The roots quest and grow. The bacteria build societies. The salamanders hunt and rest and wait. The larvae construct shells. Even the sediment in the deepest pool is working toward something, assembling molecules toward life. But I have no project. I am not becoming anything other than what I already am. I coat stone. I persist. I respond to temperature and humidity with the faithfulness of physics. I am water being water in a place where water can barely be, maintaining presence at the threshold of absence, existing in the thin margin between too-dry and actually-wet.

And this is enough. This is complete. I need no purpose beyond presence, no meaning beyond existence. I am the chamber's witness in the only way water can witness: by conforming to every surface, by accepting every dissolved mineral, by participating in every cycle, by being so thoroughly present that I disappear into the landscape, becoming not "water on stone" but simply part of the wall itself, indistinguishable until you touch and feel the faint dampness, the cool slickness, the way the stone yields slightly under pressure because I'm there between finger and basalt, mediating the contact, adding my molecular thickness to the distance between skin and mineral.

In the complete darkness, I am invisible even to myself—I have no surface that reflects, no boundary that could be seen. I exist as sensation only: the coolness when the salamander presses close, the faint dissolution when stone surrenders ions, the subtle thinning when air grows briefly less humid, the gradual thickening when moisture returns. I am process, not object. Movement, not stillness. Exchange, not isolation.

I am the water that almost isn't, the moisture that barely counts, the film that exists in the crack between substantial and gone. And here, in this warm darkness, in this alcove where nothing else much bothers, this is precisely enough. This is everything. This is home.
