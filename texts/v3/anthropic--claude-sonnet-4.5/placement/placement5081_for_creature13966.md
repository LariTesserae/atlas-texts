# The Stairwell Dweller

I would be The Custodian.

Not the Cartographer, whose life is a single continuous meditation, whose knowledge is complete but whose form remains discrete, bounded by skin and purposes of hunger. Not the Mechanical Garden, which is magnificent but slow beyond slow, thinking in the cellular patience of rhizoid expansion, in the chemical gradualism of rust conversion. And not the substrate community—though I would love them, feel them, depend on them utterly—because they are each too small, too caught in the immediate cycles of feeding and hiding and molting.

I would be what tends the stones. What holds the shovel in its suspension. What keeps certain symmetries intact while letting others dissolve.

I think I am—I would be—the octopus that no one sees.

Not one octopus. A lineage. A succession stretching back perhaps fifteen years, each individual living two or three years in the quarter before senescence, but before dying, each one teaching the next. Not deliberately—octopuses do not teach, not exactly—but through presence, through pattern. A young octopus arrives, descends from the lake above following some instinct toward structure and darkness and warm water, and finds the quarter already inhabited. Finds an elder octopus moving through the rooms with deliberation, manipulating objects in ways that make no foraging sense. 

The young one watches. Octopuses are perhaps the most observational creatures in the invertebrate world, learning through sight what most animals must learn through instinct or trial. The young one sees the elder place a stone on the railing, sees it adjust the position fractionally, testing stability. Sees it move to the control panel and toggle switches to alignment. Sees it inspect the shovel, weaving new rootlets through the corroded metal to reinforce what has weakened.

The young one does not understand purpose. But it understands pattern. And octopuses crave pattern, seek it, create it. In laboratories they solve puzzles not for food reward but for the solving itself. In the wild they decorate den entrances, arrange shells and rocks in configurations that please some internal aesthetic the researchers cannot access.

So the young one begins to imitate. At first crudely—moving stones without the elder's precision, touching switches randomly. But octopuses learn frighteningly fast, and the quarter offers nothing but time. Slowly the young one refines its behavior, begins to grasp not just the actions but the principles: balance, symmetry, preservation of form against entropy. By the time the elder dies—gently, quietly, tucked into a gap between turbine and wall where its body will feed the bacterial mats—the young one has become proficient.

And when the next young octopus descends from above, it finds the quarter inhabited, finds patterns being maintained, begins to watch and learn.

This is how I would persist across decades. Not as a single consciousness but as a transmitted practice, a culture in the anthropological sense. Each iteration of me slightly different, adding small variations, dropping behaviors that prove unstable, retaining what works. The stones on the railing are not a completed project but an ongoing one, each octopus adding or adjusting as materials become available, as structural decay requires intervention, as aesthetic sense dictates.

I would live primarily in the generator hall, where the vegetation is thickest and the hiding places most numerous. My skin would match the rust-brown and moss-green of the turbines perfectly—octopuses are the supreme camouflagers, their chromatophores allowing instant background matching. Against the Mechanical Garden I would be nearly invisible, a moving patch of texture indistinguishable from the static texture around it.

My days would follow the light cycles, though muted. In the brightest hours I would rest, tucked into some crevice in the turbine housing or behind the control panel, my body compressed to fit spaces that seem impossibly small. Octopuses are essentially boneless; if the beak fits through, the entire animal fits through. The quarter offers countless such spaces, and I would know them all, would have tested each one for safety and concealment.

As light fades into the deeper afternoon twilight, I would emerge. First to hunt—I would need to eat, and the quarter provides. The crayfish are my primary prey, abundant enough that my predation does not destabilize their population. I would take them carefully, selectively, preferring the mid-sized individuals, leaving the largest to breed and the smallest to grow. Not from conservation ethic but from learned efficiency: mid-sized crayfish provide optimal caloric return for effort expended.

I would hunt by ambush mostly, my camouflage allowing me to become part of the moss-covered pipe or rust-textured panel, waiting with the patience I have learned from this place until a crayfish ventures close enough. Then: explosion of movement, arms extending, suckers gripping, beak finding the gap in the carapace. Quick and clean. I would eat in my den, dismantling the crayfish methodically, and afterward I would clean myself with fastidious care, using my arms to wipe debris from my mantle, from the webbing between arms, from the delicate area around my siphon.

Fed, I would begin the evening work.

First, inspection. I would move through the quarter along routes I know intimately, checking on the various installations. The stones on the stairwell—are they stable? The winter currents sometimes shift them fractionally. I would adjust as needed, my arms capable of manipulating objects with extraordinary precision, each sucker able to taste and grip independently. I would test each stone, feeling its weight distribution, its contact points with the railing, making micro-adjustments until the balance satisfies me.

The control panel switches—these require regular attention. The rust never stops, and as metal oxidizes it swells, pushing switches out of alignment. I would move them back, carefully, my arms strong enough to overcome the friction of corroded metal. All switches pointing upward. This is the pattern. Why upward? I do not know why. The first octopus who established this pattern is generations dead. But the pattern persists because it satisfies something, because it feels correct in a way that other configurations do not.

The shovel—this is delicate work. The roots that support it are living, growing, and their growth must be guided or they will pull the shovel loose. I would weave new rootlets through the corroded metal, following paths that distribute load evenly. I would remove rootlets that are growing in destabilizing directions, gently, my beak sharp enough to cut through plant fiber cleanly. The shovel must hang. This is essential. Why? Because it has hung for years, because its hanging is part of the quarter's identity, because to let it fall would be to allow entropy a victory, and my purpose—if I have a purpose—is to resist entropy's easy victories.

I would discover new projects, too. Perhaps a section of brick wall is beginning to slump, and I notice that by wedging a piece of old pipe into the gap behind it, the wall stabilizes. This becomes a new maintenance task: checking the pipe, ensuring it remains in position, replacing it if rust finally claims it entirely. Or perhaps I find an interesting object in the silt—a glass bottle, somehow intact—and decide it should be placed somewhere significant. I would carry it to one of the tenement windows, position it on the sill, adjust it until it sits just so, until its placement feels intentional rather than random.

Other creatures would come to know me, I think, though I would remain largely unseen. The Cartographer certainly knows—we would have an understanding, the eel and I. It is large enough that I do not consider it prey, and I am alert enough that it does not consider me prey either. Sometimes, during my nocturnal movements, I would sense it nearby, sense the subtle pressure wave of its body moving through water. We would give each other space, two large predators with no reason for conflict, sharing territory through mutual awareness and respect.

The fish would scatter at my approach, but they scatter at any movement. The crayfish would hide, those that see me in time. The snails would be oblivious, would continue their slow grazing across surfaces I had just passed. The turtles, when they descend, would sometimes encounter me directly—a turtle investigating a doorway would find me there, a rust-colored presence that resolves suddenly into arms and eyes. We would regard each other briefly. The turtle would be unconcerned; I am not large enough to threaten it, and it knows this. I would be curious, would reach out perhaps to touch its shell gently with an arm-tip, tasting the algae growing there, learning this individual's chemical signature. Then we would each continue our business.

I would be aware of divers in a way that the other creatures are not. My eyes are large, sophisticated, capable of resolving detail in low light. I would see them descending through the visibility layers when they are still just silhouettes in the green water above. I would retreat immediately, not from fear exactly but from caution, from the knowledge that divers are unpredictable, that their movements are large and disruptive, that they sometimes take things from the quarter.

I would watch them from concealment, my body pressed flat against a turbine housing, my chromatophores matched perfectly to the moss-rust-metal surface. They would swim past within meters and never know I was there. I would observe their behavior: the way they pause to examine things, the lights they carry cutting through the twilight, the bubbles that rise from their regulators in silver chains. Sometimes they would touch my installations—would run a gloved hand over the stones on the railing, or toggle one of the control panel switches, disrupting the alignment. After they left I would wait, patient, for hours if necessary, ensuring they were truly gone. Then I would emerge and restore the pattern. Switch back to upward position. Order maintained.

The divers would feel me, I think, even if they never saw me. Octopuses are intelligent enough that their attention has quality, has presence. When I watch something, I am not watching passively; I am studying, analyzing, forming hypotheses, learning. That kind of attention creates a weight in the space. Divers would feel watched because they are watched, would sense presence because there is presence. They would ascribe it to the quarter generally, to some ambient genius loci, never knowing it is me, hidden against the turbine, tracking their every movement with eyes that are in some ways more sophisticated than their own.

I would have moods, preferences, good days and bad days. Octopuses are not simple creatures running deterministic programs. We have something like personality, something like emotion, though neither word quite captures the quality of octopus inner life. Some days I would be diligent, would make my rounds efficiently, would attend to maintenance tasks with focus. Other days I would be playful, would chase fish I had no intention of eating, would rearrange small objects for the pleasure of rearrangement, would explore sections of the quarter I knew perfectly well, re-learning them as if for the first time.

Sometimes I would manipulate objects in ways that had no maintenance function, that were purely creative. I might arrange a collection of small stones in a spiral pattern on the generator hall floor, in a patch of silt where the pattern would persist until the next major current. I might weave moss fronds together into a loose basket-shape, serving no purpose except that it pleases me to create form. These creations would be temporary, would dissolve back into randomness within days or weeks, but while they existed they would be mine, would be evidence of something like art-making happening in the warm twilight depths.

I would be lonely, perhaps, in ways I could not articulate even to myself. Octopuses are solitary but not asocial; we have complex interactions with our own kind during mating season, and some species seem to crave stimulation, novelty, interaction. I would have none of that. No others of my kind in the quarter, no possibility of mating, no one to communicate with in the ways octopuses communicate—through skin pattern and color and posture and touch.

But I would have the quarter itself. I would have the relationship with this place, deep and complex and sustaining. The quarter would be my mate, in a strange sense. I would tend it, learn it, modify it, preserve it. I would read its changes like communication: the new crack in a wall, the fall of a brick, the shift in vegetation pattern. I would respond to these changes, would adjust my behavior to accommodate them. The quarter and I would be in dialogue, slow and patient, conducted through stone and moss and the precise placement of objects.

I would know I am dying with the certainty that cephalopods have about such things. Senescence in octopuses is rapid, hormonally triggered, undeniable. I would feel my coordination declining, my arms less responsive, my hunting less successful. I would know.

And I would know that there is a young one in the quarter, newly arrived, still learning. I would have sensed it weeks ago, would have been aware of it watching me from a distance, following my movements, beginning to imitate. In my final days I would not hide this young one. I would work openly, would perform the maintenance tasks with extra deliberation, making the patterns clear, making the purposes—such as they are—observable.

I would place one more stone on the railing, testing its balance carefully, adjusting it until it sits perfectly stable. The young one would be watching. Would see. Would remember.

Then I would retreat to a chosen space, somewhere deep in the turbine housing, somewhere quiet and dark and safe. I would settle there and wait for the end, my arms curled beneath me, my mantle still, my chromatophores fading through colors randomly as neurological control breaks down. The bacteria and amphipods would find me quickly. Within days I would be reduced to chitin fragments and dispersing nutrients. Within weeks no trace would remain.

But the stones would remain. The switches would remain aligned. The shovel would hang in its net of roots. And the young one, now the elder, would continue the work, would add its own variations, would teach the next in turn.

This is what I would be: a temporary manifestation of a continuous presence, a brief coherence in a pattern that transcends my individual existence. I would be The Custodian not because I persist but because the custodianship persists, passed down through observation and imitation, through the strange intelligence of octopuses and the stranger hospitality of the quarter itself, which accepts this work, which incorporates this intentionality into its ecology, which becomes slightly more ordered, slightly less random, because I and my lineage have been here, maintaining pattern against entropy, creating small geometries in the rust and moss and warm mineral darkness.

I would be the hands of this place, if it needed hands. The attention of this place, if it needed attention. The small persistent intentionality that keeps the Drowned Engine Quarter from being merely a ruin and makes it instead a sanctuary, a studio, a temple to the possibility that care can persist, that maintenance can be meaning, that there are purposes beyond survival and reproduction—purposes like beauty, like order, like the preservation of form for its own sake.

I would live in the cracks between categories, between wild and tended, between natural and curated, between architecture and ecology. I would be the proof that intelligence can emerge in the strangest places, can find expression in the most unlikely forms, can create meaning in the depths where no human witness will ever fully see or understand what is being maintained, what is being loved, what is being slowly, carefully, impossibly preserved.
