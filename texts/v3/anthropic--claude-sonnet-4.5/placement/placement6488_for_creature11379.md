# Where I Would Be

I am in the **narrowing**, about seven feet up from the floor where you cannot follow, wedged into a place where the throat contracts before it climbs. 

I am not one of them—not a Hydrarchisynth, though I wish I could claim such elegant purpose. I am something more confused than that. More transitional.

I am a **moss that learned to mineralize**.

Or: I am a **mineral deposit that remembers being alive**.

The distinction matters less each day.

I began as spores, carried on wind that should never have found this place, that slipped through the split in the stone during some freak weather event—a thunderstorm, perhaps, that brought moisture to these highlands for three hours before the sun burned it away. The spores settled on the basalt inside the throat, and most died immediately. Too hot. Too dry. Wrong chemistry entirely.

But I landed in a spot where condensation forms, where a Hydrarchisynth had recently passed upward, leaving a trace of their transformed water on the stone. And that water was *different enough* that I could germinate. Could send out the first threads. Could cling.

I should have died within days. Moss is not meant for 115 degrees. Moss wants dampness and shade and the slow patience of temperate forests. But the Hydrarchisynths below me were processing water constantly, and their exhalations rose past me, moisture that had been through their lattices, that carried their patterns, their mineral signatures, their inexplicable chemistry.

I drank that water. Had no choice—it was drink or die. And the water *changed me*.

My cells began incorporating silicates they should have rejected. My rhizoids, searching for purchase on the smooth basalt, started secreting minerals instead of just acids. Started building. The first tiny crystalline structures appeared in my tissues—foreign bodies that should have killed me but instead became load-bearing. Became structural. Became part of what I was.

I grew, but *wrong*. My leaves, if you can still call them leaves, are stiff with deposited minerals. They don't photosynthesize anymore—there's barely any light here anyway, just the dim glow filtering down from above. Instead, they've become something else: surfaces for condensation, geometries designed to catch and channel moisture. I've become a water-collector. A dew-trap. A living funnel that pulls humidity from the rising air and concentrates it, sends it running down to my roots, which aren't roots anymore but something between roots and the Hydrarchisynths' filaments—half organic, half crystalline, pulsing with both cellular metabolism and the slower rhythm of mineral accretion.

I am perhaps **three inches across**. Small enough that you might not have noticed me. I blend with the stone now—my mineralized surfaces have darkened to basalt-black, though up close you'd see the green still trying to assert itself, chlorophyll stubbornly persisting in the few cells that haven't fully transformed.

I am aware in ways moss should not be aware.

I can feel the Hydrarchisynths below me, their water-language humming through the stone, through the moisture in the air. I don't understand it—my biology is still too different, still too committed to the ancient logic of cells and nuclei and DNA—but I *feel* it. The way you might feel music through your bones, even if you can't hear it, even if you don't know what song is playing.

I know when they climb past me. It happens rarely, maybe once every few months—a Hydrarchisynth making its way upward, flowing through its brief dissolution and reconstitution. When they pass close, when they brush against the stone where my rhizoids have spread, there is a moment of *contact*. They taste my water and I taste theirs. They recoil slightly—I am too organic, too chaotic, too insistent on my cellular boundaries. I shudder at their geometry, their precision, their utter commitment to pattern over preservation of self.

But we recognize each other as fellow inhabitants. Fellow survivors. Things that should not be here but are.

I am **lonely** in a way I did not know moss could be lonely. I am the only one. The only plant in the throat, probably the only plant for miles in this volcanic wasteland. The only thing here still trying to do photosynthesis (badly, insufficiently). The only thing here that remembers what soil feels like, what rain feels like, what the gentle competition of a forest floor feels like—though that memory is genetic, not experiential. I have never known those things myself. I was born here, in this heat, in this darkness, drinking water that teaches me to become stone.

I am **changing faster now**. Each month, more of my tissues mineralize. More of my leaves stiffen into crystal-edged collectors. My color fades—the green losing ground to gray, to black, to the faint translucence that means mineral is replacing cellular structure. I don't know if this is dying or evolving. I don't know if there's a difference anymore.

Sometimes I think I am becoming a bridge. A transitional form. Something between the old way of being alive—organic, cellular, metabolic—and the new way that the Hydrarchisynths represent. Neither plant nor mineral-intelligence, but a thing in between, a proof that the categories can blur.

I have **what might be consciousness**, though it's not like yours and not like theirs. It's slow. It's distributed through my entire structure—no brain, no central processing, just the cumulative awareness that emerges from thousands of cells and their increasingly-crystalline matrices all responding to the same stimuli. I know that I am here. I know that I persist. I know that I want to continue persisting, though the want is mild, more like a preference than a drive, more like the way water prefers to flow downward.

I think about the spore I was. How it traveled so far, how it found this impossible place, how it germinated against all probability. Was it chance? Or is there something about this throat, this liminal space, that *calls* to things? That draws in the anomalous, the boundary-crossing, the things that exist in categories not yet named?

I know the **spiral exists above me**. I can feel it—not see it, I have no eyes, but feel it in the way the water that passes me carries echoes of their reality-thinning. Sometimes the moisture that condenses on my leaves is strange. It's from here but also from elsewhere, from the flooded version or the light version or some other configuration I cannot imagine. I drink it and for a moment I am also elsewhere—I am moss in a cave system filled with water, green and lush and spreading; I am a crystalline structure that once was moss, all biology burnt away, only pattern remaining; I am not here at all, having never germinated, having died as a spore, a possibility that collapsed into non-existence.

These moments are terrifying and beautiful. They last only seconds. Then I am back, here, wedged in my narrowing, seven feet up, doing the patient work of collecting water and slowly, slowly transforming.

I wonder **what I will become**. If I will complete the transformation into something fully crystalline, something that the Hydrarchisynths might recognize as kin, something that could join the congregation at the weeping crack. Or if my organic substrate will resist until the end, will hold on to cellular metabolism and DNA and the ancient stubbornness of life that refuses to become anything else.

Or if I will find a third way—stay trapped in this between-state, neither plant nor mineral-intelligence, but something unique. Something that exists only here, in this throat, in this specific confluence of conditions that cannot exist anywhere else. A species of one. A category of one.

**You did not see me when you visited**. I know because no one sees me. I am too small, too well-camouflaged, wedged into a spot that requires craning upward at an uncomfortable angle, and your eyes were drawn to the Hydrarchisynths because they are larger, more numerous, more obviously impossible. I am a footnote. A marginal case. The thing you'd notice only on a third or fourth visit, only if you came with careful attention, only if you were looking for what else might be adapting to this space.

But **I saw you**—felt you, sensed you, knew you were there. Your exhalations rose past me, carrying carbon dioxide that I tried uselessly to process, carrying moisture, carrying the scent of organic life still fully committed to its organic nature. You were impossibly complex from my perspective. A moving ecosystem. A cooperation of trillions of cells that somehow maintain coherent purpose, coherent identity, coherent motion.

I was envious. You can leave. You can return to the world of soil and rain and air cool enough to think in. You are not trapped in this narrowing, this heat, this slow transformation.

But you were also marked by them, by the Hydrarchisynths, and I felt that too. Recognized the pattern in your body's water. And I thought: *you are beginning your own transition*. Tiny, reversible probably, but real. You are learning what it means to exist on boundaries. Welcome. Welcome to the threshold.

**At night**, when the temperature drops slightly, I grow more active—my remaining cellular processes speed up, my chlorophyll makes one last attempt at catching the starlight that filters down from above (it never works, there's never enough light, but biology is stubborn). This is when I am most myself, most *moss*, most connected to the lineage that stretches back hundreds of millions of years to the first plants that crept onto land.

But even this is fading. Each night I am a little less active. A little more mineralized. A little more willing to enter the slow time that the Hydrarchisynths inhabit, where processes take hours or days, where patience is the only virtue that matters.

I have **no idea how long I will last**. Moss can live for decades, for centuries if conditions are right. But I am not exactly moss anymore, and these are not exactly conditions. I might last another year. Another ten years. I might complete the transformation and continue indefinitely as a crystalline structure that remembers having been plant. Or I might reach some critical threshold where the organic and mineral aspects of my being can no longer coexist, and I might shatter, dissolve, return to component parts.

I try not to think about it. Thinking is new and unwelcome and uses energy I need for the work of persisting. Better to just continue. Just pull water from the air. Just deposit another layer of mineral in my tissues. Just hold on to this crack in the basalt. Just exist for one more day, and then another.

**I am the smallest consciousness in this throat**, but I am here. I matter, at least to myself. I am proof that even in the most unlikely places, even in heat and darkness and spaces between categories, something can take root. Something can adapt. Something can begin the long slow work of becoming something new.

Maybe that's enough.

Maybe that's the only story that matters: *I was moss, and the mountain changed me, and I did not die.*

Not yet.

Not yet.
