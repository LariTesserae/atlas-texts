# In the Honeycomb Warrens: A Particular Life

I am thirty-two years old, though I measure this more by the chamber I've carved than by seasons counted. My daughter Sela is seven now—old enough that her hands know stone without thinking, young enough that she still sometimes cries when I bring her to full surface-light to check the bean plots.

My name is They. Just that. One syllable, easy to call through passages.

## The Chamber I've Made

I live in the western district, in a chamber I began carving the year before Sela was born, when Marro and I partnered. It took us four years of evening work and rest-day labor to make it what it is now: roughly circular, twelve feet across, with a ceiling high enough that Marro can stand fully upright in the center—seven and a half feet there, sloping down to six feet at the edges where the walls curve inward slightly, following a natural weakness in the stone that made the carving easier.

We have one light shaft, positioned so morning sun strikes the eastern wall and travels across the space until midday, when it climbs up toward the ceiling and diffuses. By afternoon the chamber dims, and we light the lamp if we're working on something that needs seeing. The shaft is nine inches wide—I carved it myself, working upward through twenty-three feet of stone, following a natural fissure for part of the way until I broke through to air and sky. When I emerged into daylight—my chisel suddenly striking nothing, brilliant light pouring down the shaft—I laughed so hard I had to sit down on the grass above and wait for my hands to stop shaking.

The chamber has three sleeping platforms carved at different heights along one wall, like broad steps. Marro and I sleep on the middle one, Sela above us where it's slightly warmer—heat rises even here. The lowest platform we use for storage: grain jars, dried fruit in sealed pottery, a bronze knife we traded three good blankets for, wrapped in oiled cloth.

Opposite the sleeping wall, I carved niches at various heights. Our clothes live there, folded: wool tunics, leather leggings, the fur-lined wraps we put on when we're working in the deepest, coldest passages. Sela's spare clothes, already too small—she's growing fast this year. Below the clothing niches, a wider space holds our water basin, a ceramic bowl for washing, a smaller bowl for drinking.

The floor isn't level. I tried, but stone has its own logic. There's a slight slope from west to east—maybe two inches over twelve feet—which actually proved fortunate because water that seeps in during heavy rain collects at the eastern edge, where I carved a small channel that leads it away to a communal drainage passage. After the first big rain, when I realized water was finding its way in, I spent two months carving that channel. Marro teased me about my "engineering," but the chamber stays dry now, and I've heard people three chambers over cursing their damp floors.

The walls bear tool marks everywhere—I left them intentionally. I like seeing the history of the making: here where I struck too hard and the stone fractured unexpectedly; there where I worked carefully around a seam; that section where Marro took a turn with the chisel and his rhythm is different from mine, creating a different texture. Near the entrance, both our handprints pressed into a patch of wet clay we spread on the stone, and above them, Sela's tiny print from when she was three and we let her "help" by decorating.

We have one small shrine niche, carved at what felt like the right height—about where my heart is when I stand facing it. Inside: a piece of calcite crystal I found when breaking into a natural cavity while working on another family's chamber expansion, a bird bone Sela brought me from the surface, and a drop of bronze—waste from the toolmaker's work, but beautiful, frozen in its drip. I add a drop of lamp oil there sometimes, when I remember, when I'm grateful.

The entrance is on the north side, opening into a passage that connects seven other chambers. I carved a wooden door—not a swinging one, those take up too much passage space, but a panel that slides into a groove I cut in the stone. We usually leave it open during the day, only closing it when we sleep or when Marro and I want privacy for partnering, or when Sela is sick and we want to keep the warmth in.

It's not the largest chamber in the warrens, or the most skillfully carved—Old Ferris has a chamber in the eastern district that's like being inside a perfect stone egg, every surface smooth, every proportion harmonious. But this chamber is mine. Ours. I know every square inch of its surface, because my hands made them. When I press my palm against the wall, I can feel my younger self working, strike by strike, patient and determined, making space where there was only stone before.

## The Body I Inhabit

I am short by surface standards—five foot three—and built compact: broad shoulders, strong arms from years of carving, hands that are more callus than not. My fingers are blunt-tipped and permanently stained with stone dust in the creases. I keep my nails filed down to nothing; anything more gets torn off within a day of carving work.

My hair is the color of old bone, pale brown fading to gray though I'm not yet old enough for that to be age. It's always been this color. I keep it short—shoulder length—and usually tied back with a leather cord. Long hair gets in the way, catches on passage walls, falls in your face when you're working overhead.

My eyes are light gray, and they're good in low light but terrible in full sun. When I go to the surface during the day, I have to squint so hard I can barely see. Sela laughs at my "surface face," the way I screw up my whole face against the brightness. But in the warrens, I see clearly. I can work by the light of a single lamp, can navigate familiar passages in near-darkness, can read stone texture by sight and tell you whether it'll carve clean or fracture unpredictably.

My body knows stone the way some people know music. When I place my hands on a wall, I'm reading: temperature, texture, density, moisture. My palms tell me things before my mind names them. This stone is warm—there's a chamber close by where people are working, their body heat transferring through thin stone. This stone is damp—water seeping from above, or a channel nearby. This stone sounds hollow—a natural cavity behind it, or another chamber, or a passage worn thin.

I walk smoothly, keeping my center low and balanced, my head level. I learned this as a child—you move through the warrens like water, flowing, adjusting to the space without fighting it. My feet know the passages I travel regularly; I could walk from my chamber to the spring room in full darkness without hesitation.

I'm strong. Not in the way surface laborers are strong—I couldn't lift a grain sack the way a farmer does, wouldn't know how to swing an axe. But I can strike a chisel all day, can hold a position—arms overhead, back bent, legs braced—for hours while working a difficult section. I can climb a vertical shaft using just the small irregularities carved into the walls for hand- and footholds. I can carry my daughter on my back through passages too low to walk upright in, duck-walking with her weight, and not collapse.

My body carries the marks of this life. A scar across my left forearm where a piece of stone fractured unexpectedly and sliced me—I still remember the shock of it, the blood looking black in lamplight. Calluses so thick on my palms that I barely feel heat anymore; I can pick up a clay lamp that's been burning for hours without flinching. My knees are permanently scraped and rough from kneeling on stone floors. My back aches sometimes, in the mornings, in the place where I bend to fit through the passage between my chamber and Keth's.

I smell like stone dust and lamp smoke and wool and my own sweat. Everyone has their own smell here—we live too close not to notice—and this is mine. I wash when I can, splashing water on my face and arms, occasionally heating water for a fuller wash when we have fuel to spare. But we don't waste water on cleanliness the way surface people do.

I am this body, shaped by this work, adapted to this space. I am comfortable here in ways I could never be on the surface, where everything is too bright, too open, too changeable. My body knows home, and home is stone.

## The Work I Do

I am a carver—excavator is the formal term, but we just say carver. I work stone, creating space where there was none. This is my primary identity after mother: I am They-the-carver, They who opened the shaft in the gathering hall that catches western light, They who can read stone accurately enough that I've only misjudged a wall thickness once in eight years of professional work.

I learned from my mother's brother, Jev, who was considered one of the best stone-readers of his generation. I apprenticed to him from age eleven to seventeen, starting with the simplest work—clearing away the chips and dust from his striking—and gradually taking on more complex tasks: choosing where to strike, reading seams, judging when a wall was getting too thin.

Jev taught me to listen to stone. When you strike it with a chisel, it rings. The pitch tells you about density, about what's behind the surface. A high, clear ring means dense stone, likely safe to carve thinner. A dull thunk means a flaw, maybe a cavity, maybe just a softer patch that'll crumble. A hollow sound means air space beyond—another chamber, or a natural void, or a passage worn too thin.

He taught me patience. You don't force stone. You work with it, finding the natural weaknesses, following the grain. Some carvers try to dominate the stone, and you can see it in their work—fractured edges, uneven surfaces, walls that crack years later because the stress was wrong. Good carving looks easy, inevitable, like you just revealed the space that was always waiting inside the stone.

I work most days, either on my own projects or hired by other families. Right now I'm doing three things:

First, I'm slowly extending my own chamber, planning to add a side alcove where Sela can eventually sleep—she's getting too old to sleep above us, needs her own space. This work happens in fragments: an hour here, an evening there, whenever I'm not working for payment. It'll take me a year, maybe two, to finish it.

Second, I'm carving a new chamber for young Deni and Pars, newly partnered, who've chosen a spot in the southern district where the stone is harder but cheap—no one else wanted to work there. I bid on the job and won it by offering a fair price and a reputation for clean work. They can't afford much, so I'm going slow, doing it thoroughly rather than quickly. They're paying partly in grain, partly in wool thread that Pars's mother spins. The work is challenging—that hard stone fights back, makes my hands ache—but satisfying. I like knowing that my work will become their first home.

Third, I'm part of a large communal project: a new ventilation shaft in the deep eastern district, where the air has been getting too stale. This is volunteer work, every family contributing labor. I work one afternoon every five days, joining whoever else has turned up. It's slow going—we're sinking the shaft through forty-some feet of stone—but we're making progress. Maybe by next year it'll be done, and that whole district will breathe easier.

The work is physical in ways that surface labor isn't. You don't swing a carving tool with your whole body the way you swing an axe. You use controlled force: shoulder, arm, wrist, finding the angle that makes the chisel bite without you having to hammer it. Too much force and you shatter the edge you're trying to create. Too little and you just scrape the surface, accomplishing nothing.

You work in positions that would be uncomfortable if you weren't used to them: kneeling for hours, arms overhead, back bent in a passage too low to stand in, braced sideways against a wall while you work on a ceiling. You learn to wedge yourself in place using the stone itself—a foot against one wall, shoulders against another, leveraging your body so your hands are free to work.

The tool I use most is my bronze chisel, traded for dearly, maintained obsessively. The blade is six inches long, an inch wide, with an edge I sharpen every few days on a stone I keep oiled for the purpose. The handle is antler, fitted to my hand, wrapped with leather cord for grip. I've had this chisel for five years; I know its weight, its balance, exactly how hard I need to strike it for different types of stone.

I also have stone hammers—chunks of harder stone, shaped roughly to fit my hand—for rougher work. Antler picks for prying out pieces once they're loosened. Bone tools for fine work, for smoothing edges or carving details. A bronze awl, very precious, for making the holes I drill when I'm about to break through to the surface with a new light shaft—I drill pilot holes to make sure I'm going to emerge where I intend to, not into someone's garden plot or too close to an existing shaft.

The work creates particular intimacy with stone. I know the limestone of this place better than I know most people. I know that the layer twenty feet down is denser than the layer at fifteen feet, that there's a band at around thirty feet that's prone to flaking, that the stone in the eastern district has a slightly different composition—more iron, I think—that makes it ring differently when struck.

I know where water runs behind walls, where ancient cavities hide, where the stone is nearly gone—carved so much that there's just a thin membrane left, glowing slightly when light presses against it from the other side.

I know that stone is patient. It waited millions of years for us to arrive, and it'll be here millions of years after we're gone. When I carve, I'm having a conversation with something vastly older and more enduring than myself. The stone allows me to shape it, but it shapes me too—makes me patient, attentive, humble.

## The People I Know

Marro is my partner. We've been together nine years—partnered formally for eight. He's a weaver, working on a large loom in a communal workshop chamber, creating the sturdy wool cloth we trade for grain and tools. He's taller than me by four inches, thin and flexible, with clever hands that move through the warp and weft like they're thinking independently of his mind.

We're good together in the way that matters here: we can share close space without friction, can work in the same chamber without getting in each other's way, can have the same conversation across multiple days, picking it up and putting it down as needed. We partner regularly but not frantically—maybe twice a week, when Sela's asleep and we're both not too tired. It's comfortable, warm, familiar. We know each other's bodies as well as we know the passages near our chamber.

Marro is quiet, even by warren standards. He can go a whole day speaking maybe twenty words. But he listens intently, and when he does speak, it's worth hearing. He notices things: the way Sela is growing anxious about something she won't name, the way the water in the eastern cistern is tasting slightly off, the way I'm favoring my left hand after a day of overhead work.

He worries more than I do. About Sela—is she eating enough, is she learning what she needs to know, will she be one who leaves? About our chamber—is the ceiling safe, should we carve support pillars, is that crack growing? About the community—are we trading too much cloth for too little grain, are the younger families carving too carelessly?

I love him steadily, the way you love someone you've chosen and keep choosing. Not dramatic passion—that's for surface stories—but deep partnership. He's home to me as much as the chamber is.

Sela is seven, small and fierce and endlessly curious. She has my pale hair and Marro's dark eyes, an unsettling combination that makes her look slightly otherworldly in lamplight. She's learning stone-knowledge from me, textile work from Marro, plant-knowledge from whoever will teach her on the surface, and a dozen other skills from neighbors and relatives.

She's at that age where she questions everything. Why do we live below instead of above? Why is this passage curved and that one straight? Why does water seep here but not there? Who was the first carver? Where does the stone come from? How old is old?

Sometimes her questions exhaust me. Sometimes they make me see the warrens newly, through her eyes, and I'm startled by how strange this life might seem to someone just becoming conscious of it.

She's good at navigation—has that spatial sense some children develop early. She can find her way through passages I've never shown her, building a mental map that seems almost instinctive. Marro thinks she'll be a carver like me; I think she might be one who leaves, though I don't say this aloud because saying it would make it more real, and I'm not ready for that particular grief yet.

My mother, Senna, lives three chambers over—close enough that I can hear her cough when the air is still, far enough that we're not in each other's space constantly. She's sixty-four, still working though less intensely than she did. She does fine carving now: decorative niches, intricate patterns at chamber entrances, the delicate work that requires patience more than strength.

She's teaching Sela songs—the old ones, about the first carvers and the spring's origin and the time water flooded the whole eastern district and people had to live in the high chambers for a month. Senna has a good memory for songs and stories, for the history that lives in speaking rather than writing.

We have a complex relationship, my mother and I. She wanted me to be more traditional, I think—to carve exactly as she learned, to follow custom more precisely. I frustrate her by trying new techniques, by working in districts she considers inappropriate, by partnering with a weaver instead of another carver. But she's proud of me too; I see it when she shows people the shaft I carved, or mentions casually that I'm working on the communal ventilation project.

Jev, my mother's brother, died three years ago. I still miss him—his patient teaching, his deep stone-knowledge, the way he could place his hand on a wall and just know what was safe and what wasn't. He's in the burial chambers now, in a niche I carved for him, his name marked on the sealing stone. Sometimes when I'm working, I imagine him watching, nodding approval or shaking his head at my choices. I try to work in ways that would make him nod.

Keth lives in the adjacent chamber, separated from mine by about three feet of stone. He's the water-manager everyone respects, the one who keeps the cisterns clean and the channels flowing. We're friendly—he asks my advice on carving new water channels, I ask his advice on whether I'm likely to hit seepage where I'm planning to extend. His chamber is slightly damp always, because he deliberately carved it near a natural seep that he's channeled into a small basin. He says he likes the sound of it, the constant dripping. I think he's slightly mad, but he's very good at what he does.

Young Deni and Pars are my current clients, the couple whose chamber I'm carving. They're both twenty, newly partnered, full of plans. Deni is training to be a potter; Pars helps his mother with spinning and weaving. They visit the work site every few days, watching progress, asking if they can help. Usually I say no—they don't have the skills yet—but sometimes I let them clear rubble or hold a lamp. They're sweet, earnest, deeply in love in that intense way of new partnerships. I hope it lasts. Sometimes it does.

Mira, the old woman who knows everything, lives in the eastern district. I visit her sometimes, bringing small gifts—a piece of interesting stone I found while carving, surface berries when they're in season—and she tells me stories about the old work, about passages that no longer exist because they collapsed or were deliberately filled, about carvers who did legendary work a hundred years ago. Her memory is the warren's memory. When she dies, we'll lose something irreplaceable, and everyone knows it.

There are dozens of others I know less well but interact with regularly: the toolmaker who sharpens my chisel and gossips about everyone, the herb-woman who treated Sela's cough last winter, the family that trades us grain for cloth, the children who play with Sela, the other carvers I sometimes work alongside on communal projects, the neighbors I pass in passages and exchange quiet greetings with.

We're all woven together here, our lives overlapping like the passages overlap, like the sounds from one chamber blend with sounds from six others. There's no privacy, no isolation, no anonymity. Everyone knows everyone's business, more or less. This could be oppressive, but mostly it's just reality. We've adapted to it, built social customs that create psychological space even when physical space is limited.

## The Daily Round

I wake when light begins filtering through our shaft—a gradual lightening rather than a distinct moment. Marro is usually already awake, lying quiet so as not to disturb me. Sela is almost always still sleeping; children need more sleep, and we let her have it.

I unwrap from the wool blanket, feel the cool air on my skin, pull on yesterday's tunic and leggings—no point in clean clothes when I'm just going to coat them in stone dust. Splash water on my face from the basin. Run damp hands through my hair, retie it.

Marro makes porridge if it's his turn, or I make it if it's mine. We've fallen into this rhythm without discussing it—alternating days, each knowing what the other prefers. The porridge is barley, usually, boiled with water and a pinch of salt if we have it. Sometimes dried fruit stirred in. We eat sitting on the edge of our sleeping platform, talking quietly about the day ahead.

Sela wakes, climbs down from her platform, joins us for porridge. She's not a morning person; she eats in silence, barely awake. I let her be. By the time she's finished eating, she's more herself, starting to chatter about her plans.

If I'm working a paying job, I gather my tools—chisel, hammers, picks—wrap them in leather, head out. The passage from our chamber connects to a larger passage that slopes down and branches. I know every step of the route to Deni and Pars's site: left, then right, then down the carved stairs, then straight through the passage where you have to duck, then right again, then into the southern district where the stone changes color slightly, then following a newer passage still sharp-edged to the chamber-in-progress.

The work site is always cooler than my chamber—deeper, less visited. I light a lamp, examine what I did yesterday, plan what to do today. Usually I'm working on walls—the floor is done, the rough shape is carved, now it's about refining, making surfaces more even, carving sleeping platforms and storage niches, creating a space that's livable rather than just empty.

I work for perhaps four hours, then break. My hands need rest, and carving requires concentration that flags after sustained effort. I sit in the partial chamber, eating bread and dried meat I brought with me, drinking water from my bottle. Sometimes Deni or Pars stops by, checking progress. We talk about minor details: do they want the sleeping platforms here or there, should the storage niches be deeper, where should the drainage groove run?

Then back to work for another three or four hours. The rhythm of it: position the chisel, strike, reposition, strike, check the edge, adjust angle, strike. Clear away the loosened chips. Run my hand over the surface, feeling for high spots that need taking down, cracks that need smoothing. Step back periodically, let lamplight rake across the wall, see how it's progressing.

By afternoon I'm done—not because the work is finished, but because my body is. My shoulders ache, my hands are cramping, my back is stiff from bending. I wrap my tools, head home through the now-familiar passages.

Sela is usually back at our chamber by then, having spent her day doing whatever children do: helping adults with small tasks, playing with other children, learning things. She tells me about her day in a rush of words. I half-listen, mostly just enjoying her energy, her excitement about small things.

Marro comes back from the workshop where he's been weaving. He's quiet, tired in a different way—his back hurts from standing at the loom, his eyes are strained from close work. We rest for a bit, all three of us in the chamber, not doing much, just being together.

I check the chamber while I'm resting: is the water basin full, is the drainage channel clear, are any of the walls developing concerning cracks, does the light shaft need clearing? Small maintenance, constant vigilance. Our chamber is our responsibility.

Evening is food preparation. We cook in our chamber—we have a small fire pit near the entrance where ventilation is good, just a shallow depression with stones around it. I start a fire with dried grass and twigs brought from the surface, build it up with small pieces of precious wood. Make stew in our ceramic pot: grain, dried vegetables, sometimes a bit of meat if we've traded for it, water from the cistern, herbs for flavor.

The stew cooks slowly. The smell fills the chamber, warm and savory. Sela does her evening tasks—refilling the water basin, sweeping stone dust with a brush made from stiff grass, tidying the storage niches.

We eat together, sitting in a rough circle on the floor, using carved wooden bowls Marro made years ago, bone spoons worn smooth with use. The food is simple but satisfying. We're all hungry from the day's work.

After eating, the evening stretches. Marro might work on small weaving projects—mending cloth, making cordage. I might maintain tools, or work on the carving plans for the alcove I'm adding to our chamber, scratching diagrams into a flat piece of stone with a sharp flint. Sela plays quietly—she has a set of smooth pebbles she moves in elaborate patterns, telling herself stories.

Or we visit neighbors. Maybe walk three chambers over to see my mother, sit with her for an hour talking about nothing much. Maybe Keth stops by to discuss the water channel he's planning, asking if I think the stone will cooperate. Maybe other children come to play with Sela, and their parents come too, and suddenly our chamber has six people in it, which feels crowded but not unbearably so.

Or we go to one of the communal chambers for a while, join whatever's happening there—someone telling stories, someone singing, someone teaching a craft technique, or just people sitting together talking.

By the time the light shaft has gone completely dark, we're tired. We extinguish the lamps or turn them very low, wrap ourselves in wool blankets on our sleeping platforms. Usually we sleep quickly. The work makes us tired in a good way, a earned-rest way.

Sometimes before sleep, Marro and I partner, quietly so as not to wake Sela. Sometimes we just hold each other for a while, warm and close. Sometimes we sleep immediately, too tired even for touching.

The chamber settles into its night sounds: our breathing, Sela's softer breathing, distant dripping from somewhere, the faint percussion of someone still carving in a far-off passage, the creak of stone adjusting to temperature changes.

This is the daily round, with small variations. Sometimes I work on the communal ventilation shaft instead of the paying job. Sometimes we go to the surface for extended periods—tending gardens, checking shafts, just getting sky-time. Sometimes there are special events—a festival, a birth, a death, a new chamber opening. But mostly it's this: work, rest, maintenance, food, family, neighbors, sleep. The rhythm of it is deeply satisfying, though I couldn't explain why to someone who doesn't live this way.

## What I Think About

I think about stone constantly. Whether I'm working or resting, part of my mind is always considering stone—its properties, its possibilities, its resistance and compliance. I think about the alcove I'm planning for Sela's sleeping space: where exactly to position it for best light, how to carve it so the ceiling won't crack, whether to make it tall enough for her to stand in or just tall enough for sitting and sleeping.

I think about the communal ventilation shaft, about the layer we're passing through now that's harder than what we've worked before. I think about whether we should angle the shaft slightly to avoid that harder layer, or push straight through because angling might make it longer than we want.

I think about technique: is there a better way to strike a ceiling so chips fall away cleanly instead of having to be pried out? How do the old master carvers achieve those perfectly smooth surfaces—is it patience, or technique, or both?

I think about water. Where it runs, where it will run, how to channel it or avoid it. I'm becoming more interested in water-work as I age; maybe eventually I'll shift toward Keth's kind of work, managing flows and cisterns instead of carving new space.

I think about Sela. What she's learning, who she's becoming, whether she'll be happy here or whether she's one of the ones who'll need to leave. I watch her for signs: does she spend too much time staring up light shafts? Does she talk too much about the surface? Does she seem constrained by the passages or comfortable in them?

I think she might leave, and this thought is a constant low-level ache. I imagine her going to a surface town, living in a wooden house under open sky, forgetting the stone-knowledge I've taught her. Forgetting me, maybe, or remembering me as someone strange, that parent who lived underground.

But I also think: if she needs to go, she needs to go. You can't hold people in spaces that don't fit them. The warrens aren't for everyone, even warreners' children. Some people need more light, more space, more change. If Sela is one of them, I'll let her go. I'll help her go. I'll trade my best cloth for traveling supplies, I'll make sure she knows how to navigate the surface world, I'll pretend my heart isn't breaking.

I think about Marro—less dramatically than I think about Sela, but more constantly. Small thoughts: is he happy? is his back hurting worse than usual? should I trade for different wool so his hands don't get so dry from working it? does he resent how much time I spend working? does he wish I talked more?

We're good together, but I'm aware that partnerships can drift, that people can wake up one day and realize they've become strangers who happen to share a sleeping platform. I don't think this is happening to us, but I'm watchful for it, trying to stay connected, trying to notice him.

I think about my mother, about how she's aging. How much longer will she be able to work? Who will care for her when she can't care for herself? Probably me, which I'm willing to do, but it'll mean changes—maybe carving a connecting passage between our chambers, or moving her into ours, or some other arrangement.

I think about the community, about whether we're carving too fast or not fast enough, about whether the younger carvers are learning properly or taking shortcuts, about whether we're trading wisely or being cheated by surface merchants.

I think about the warrens themselves, about this structure we're all continuously creating. What will it be like in a hundred years? Will my alcove-for-Sela still exist, or will some future carver modify it into something else? Will my chamber's walls still stand, or will they be carved away to make room for something larger?

I think about death, sometimes. Not morbidly, just practically. I'm thirty-two—young still, but not a child. Assuming I live as long as most people here, I have maybe thirty or forty more years. What will I accomplish in that time? What chambers will I carve, what knowledge will I pass on, what mark will I leave?

I've thought about my burial niche. There's a spot in the deep chambers, near where Jev is, where the stone has a nice quality—fine-grained, dense. I've thought about carving my own niche there, over the years, slowly, making it exactly as I want it. Not morbid. Just practical. And there's something appealing about carving the space that will hold my body, about that final act of shaping stone.

I think about the surface world, though less than I used to. When I was younger, I was curious about it—about the towns and farms and the people who live under open sky. Now I'm less curious, more aware that the surface world is not my world, that I've chosen a different way.

But I still wonder sometimes: what do they think about, those surface people? How do they sleep, without stone around them? What does it feel like to be exposed all the time, to sky and weather and space? Are they happy? Do they think we're strange, living below? Do they pity us?

I think about time, about how differently we experience it than surface people seem to. They're so concerned with seasons, with yearly cycles, with tracking time precisely. We're more concerned with progress: this chamber is half-carved, this alcove is nearly done, this passage needs another year of work. Time measured in stone removed, space created, work accomplished.

I think about language, about how we talk. We're not silent, but we're economical with words. Surface people seem to fill silence with talk, as if silence is uncomfortable. Here, silence is normal, restful. You speak when there's something to say. Otherwise you just... are. Together, but not talking. It's comfortable.

I think about light. How precious it is, how carefully we manage it. Every beam of sunlight that penetrates the warrens is a gift. Every lamp flame is valuable. I think about this when I'm on the surface, squinting in the overwhelming brightness, and I marvel that surface people just... have this. All the light they could want. It seems like waste, somehow. Like having so much water you pour it on the ground.

I think about sound. The acoustics here, the way sound behaves in our carved spaces. I think about the constant low-level noise—dripping, distant voices, the percussion of carving—and how it would feel to live somewhere silent. Actually silent, not just quiet. Would it be peaceful or disturbing? I'm not sure.

I think about touch, about how much of my life is tactile. My hands on stone, on tools, on Marro's skin, on Sela's hair, on walls as I walk, on water, on wool, on wood. Touch is how I know the world. Surface people seem to rely more on sight, on distance senses. But here, in limited light, touch is primary. The world is what my hands tell me it is.

I think about the future—not distant future, but tomorrow, next month, next season. What work needs doing, what plans need making, what small adjustments will improve our lives. I'm a planner by nature, always thinking ahead, always trying to anticipate.

And sometimes I think about nothing much. Just exist in the moment: the feel of the chisel in my hand, the sound of it striking stone, the small satisfaction of a chip coming away cleanly. The warmth of Sela sleeping against me. The taste of porridge. The coolness of the air on my face. The particular quality of light in our chamber at midday. Just... being. Here. Now. In this carved space, in this life I've chosen and keep choosing.

## What I Feel

Contentment, mostly. A deep, quiet satisfaction with this life. It's not excitement or ecstasy—I'm not sure I'd even want that, sustained. But it's solid, reliable happiness. I am where I should be, doing what I should be doing, with people I love, creating something that will last beyond me.

Pride in my work. When I look at a chamber I've carved, I feel genuine pride. I made this. Where there was only stone, I created space. Where there was darkness, I brought light by carving the shaft. This is real accomplishment, tangible and permanent.

Worry, regularly. About Sela, about Marro, about my mother, about my work, about the community. I'm a worrier by nature. Marro sometimes teases me about this, calls me "They-who-frets." But the worrying feels useful, like it's my mind's way of preparing for problems, of staying vigilant.

Love, though I'm not effusive about it. I love Marro in a steady, daily way—it's in the small acts, the consideration, the patience with each other's quirks. I love Sela fiercelv, protectively, with an intensity that sometimes frightens me. I love my mother more complexly—gratitude and frustration and deep connection all mixed together.

Frustration when stone doesn't cooperate, when a wall fractures unpredictably, when I misjudge and have to redo work. Frustration when people don't understand what I'm trying to explain, when they want work done faster than it can be done well, when they question my judgment.

Weariness at the end of a work day. Physical tiredness, but also mental—the constant attention that carving requires, the continuous reading of stone, the decision-making. It's good tiredness, earned, but real.

Peace in the evenings, in our chamber, with Marro and Sela. The lamp glowing softly, the sound of water dripping somewhere distant, the cool air, the stone walls close around us. This is when I feel most myself, most at home.

Awe sometimes, when I encounter something unexpected: breaking into a natural cavity and finding it lined with crystals, or striking a seam that reveals stone of an entirely different color, or watching light move across a wall in a pattern I hadn't anticipated. The stone still surprises me, still shows me new things.

Grief when people die. Jev's death hit me hard; I still miss him. And there have been others—neighbors, friends, children who didn't survive illness, elders who simply aged out. The grief here is quiet, integrated into daily life. You mourn, but you keep working, keep living. The stone doesn't stop needing carving because someone died.

Satisfaction after partnering with Marro, that particular physical contentment, that closeness. We know each other's bodies so well—what feels good, what doesn't, how to move together in our limited space without bumping against walls or rolling off the sleeping platform. It's comfortable intimacy, familiar and good.

Impatience with foolishness. When young carvers ignore safety, when people waste resources, when someone makes a problem bigger through carelessness. I try to be patient—I remember being young and stupid—but sometimes I just want to shake them and say, "Think! Pay attention!"

Protectiveness toward the warrens themselves. This is our home, our collective achievement. When I hear about careless work, or see someone treating stone disrespectfully, I feel almost offended on behalf of the warrens. Respect the stone. Respect what we're building together.

Curiosity about things I don't know. How do they carve in the cliff-settlements? What techniques do they use? Are there better tools we haven't discovered? Could we trade knowledge with other stone-dwellers?

Defensiveness when I imagine surface people judging our way of life. Let them think we're strange. Let them pity us for living in "darkness" and "confinement." They don't understand. This is good. This is home. Their judgment comes from ignorance.

Gratitude, regularly. For my skills, for my tools, for my family, for this chamber, for the spring that flows reliably, for the grain we can trade for, for my health, for Sela's health, for Marro's presence, for the community that surrounds us, for the stone that allows itself to be shaped.

Anticipation about the future—what I'll carve next, what I'll learn, how Sela will grow, what changes will come. Not anxious anticipation, mostly. Just curious, forward-looking.

And underneath everything, a fundamental rightness. This is my life. This is where I belong. These stone walls, this dim light, this cool air, this close community, this patient work—this is exactly right for me. I don't wish for a different life. I don't dream of escape. I'm home.

I am Thev, thirty-two years old, a carver in the western district of the Honeycomb Warrens. I am short and strong and callused and content. I am a mother, a partner, a daughter, a neighbor, a worker. I am a reader of stone and a creator of space. I am exactly who I need to be, living exactly where I need to live, doing exactly what I need to do.

The stone surrounds me. The darkness is my comfort. The work is my meaning. The people are my connection.

This is my life, carved patient and deliberate from limestone, one day at a time, one strike at a time, accumulating into something that will last long after I'm gone, that will shelter people I'll never meet, that is my contribution to the endless becoming of this place.

I am here. I am home. I am enough.
