# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the borderlands of the plateau—not at its heart where the Sound-Shepherds maintain their ancient equilibrium, nor in the deep crevices where the archive-moths cluster in their mineral darkness, but at the edges, the transitional zones where plateau meets the terrible drop, where stone ends and air begins.

Here, the wind arrives with different urgency. It doesn't pour across the open expanse but rushes up the cliff faces, accelerated by compression, made turbulent by the collision of rising valley air and the plateau's cool stillness. The edge-zones are louder than the interior during windstorms, the air made chaotic by conflicting currents, but during still periods they're where sound carries furthest—a voice at the rim can be heard miles across the tableland, projected by the acoustic bowl of the valley below.

The stone here shows its violence more clearly. While the plateau's center has reached its weathered equilibrium, the edges are still actively failing. Frost wedges into cracks each night, expanding, applying pressure. Occasional rockfalls send portions of the rim tumbling into the abyss, their departure marked by fresh scars in the ancient stone, pale wounds that will take centuries to weather to the same patina as the surrounding rock. To live at the edge is to live with the plateau's ongoing conversation with gravity, with the slow but inexorable surrender of stone to void.

The ephemeral pools here are different—longer, narrower, following the joints and fractures that parallel the cliff edge, filling with water that seems to want to escape, to flow toward the drop but trapped by the limestone's impermeability. These edge-pools reflect not just sky but the far valley below, creating impossible images: clouds above, patchwork lowlands in the water's surface, the distinction between up and down made uncertain.

The vegetation clings more desperately here. The cushion plants huddle in clusters, their backs to the valley winds, their growth patterns asymmetrical from constant pressure. Small grasses grow horizontally before turning upward, their stems bent permanently by wind-memory. The lichens on rim-rocks show only on leeward sides, the windward stone kept scoured clean, too abraded for even these patient organisms to establish.

I would know these edges intimately—every stable viewpoint, every protected hollow where one can sit without being torn at by wind, every approach where the stone is sound versus where it's fractured and dangerous. I would know the places where updrafts are strongest, where valley thermals meet plateau air and create turbulence that eagles ride but humans must avoid. I would know which sections shed rock regularly, where to never camp, where to cross only in morning when frost hasn't yet released its grip on questionable stone.

# What I Would Be

I would be a margin-walker, an edge-keeper, someone who belongs fully neither to the Sound-Shepherds' integrated plateau culture nor to the lowland world, but to the boundary itself—the transition zone, the meeting place.

Not outcast. Not failed plateau person or rejected lowlander. But someone for whom the edge is home, is proper territory, is the place that makes sense. Someone whose purpose involves movement between worlds, translation, boundary-maintenance.

## Physical Form

I would be small like all plateau people, though perhaps slightly less broad-chested—my childhood divided between altitudes, my body never fully committing to either zone's specialization. My skin would show the particular weathering of edge-dwellers: asymmetrical sun-darkness, one side of my face more exposed than the other from constantly looking outward while walking the rim. My eyes would carry the permanent squint of the heights but also crow's feet from scanning vast distances, from judging depth where depth becomes meaningless, where the valley floor is so far below that distance stops being measurable and becomes simply far.

My hands would be scarred from edge-work—the peculiar cuts that come from catching yourself on sharp rim-rock when wind gusts unexpectedly, the permanent abrasions from handling rope on stone, the calluses from decades of contact with the particular textures of fractured dolomite. I would move with a hybrid gait: the plateau person's careful deliberation combined with the edge-walker's constant adjustment, the micro-corrections of someone used to wind coming from unexpected directions, used to stone that might not hold.

I would carry less than the Sound-Shepherds—my possessions pared even further by the necessity of edge-mobility, of needing to move quickly when weather turns, when rockfall begins, when the rim proves less stable than anticipated. A small pack, carefully balanced. Rope, always—braided grass-cord made with the particular technique that creates maximum strength from minimal material. A stone hammer for testing rock before trusting weight to it. Bone pitons carved to fit the common crack-sizes of rim-stone. A hide tarp that can be quickly erected against wind or sun. Water container made from plateau-fox bladder, light enough that carrying water doesn't prohibit movement. Minimal food—I would eat differently, taking advantage of edge-specific resources the interior people rarely access.

## Daily Existence

My camps would be temporary even by plateau standards—simple shelters in the rare protected pockets just back from the rim, places where erosion has created shallow alcoves or where large fallen blocks provide wind-shadow. Never comfortable, never fully secure, always with the awareness that the stone beneath might be part of the next section to give way, that what feels solid today might be falling tomorrow.

I would wake to dawn arriving first in the valleys—light filling the lowlands while the plateau still sleeps in shadow, creating the strange reversal where below is bright and above is dark, where the normal order inverts. I would watch this daily transformation, using the light's progression to read weather, to predict wind patterns, to understand what the valley is doing and how it will affect the plateau's edge-conditions.

Much of my time would be spent walking the rim—not full circuits, which would take weeks, but regular patrols of my territory, the section of edge I maintain. Twenty or thirty miles of rim, perhaps, familiar to me in every detail. I would walk checking for new fractures, marking unstable sections, noting where the Sound-Shepherds have approached too close to dangerous ground, where their trails need redirecting. I would maintain the edge-cairns—specialized markers that warn of hazards, that indicate safe descent routes, that show where water can be found on the upper cliff-faces.

I would harvest edge-resources: certain plants that grow only in the wind-scoured rim conditions, their leaves thick with compounds that help them survive the constant desiccation, useful for treating the altitude sickness lowlanders suffer when they ascend. Birds' eggs from species that nest on cliff-faces, accessible only by rope-work. Minerals weathering out of exposed stone layers, useful for tools and medicine and trade. The plateau's edges provide what its interior cannot, if you have the skill and courage to access it.

## Descent Work

But my primary purpose—my function, my reason for being what I am—would be descent work. I would maintain the routes between plateau and lowland, the ancient trails that switch-back down the cliff-faces, that traverse ledges and chimneys and slopes of scree, that make the impossible journey possible for those willing to attempt it.

This would be specialized knowledge, closely held. Not every rim section has passable routes—most of the plateau's circumference drops in sheer faces, in overhangs and cliffs that cannot be climbed without equipment no one here possesses. But there are routes, carved by water in previous eras, worn by the feet of generations, maintained by people like me who understand that these corridors matter, that connection between worlds depends on keeping them viable.

I would descend regularly—weekly or more often—checking the routes, clearing rockfall from crucial sections, replacing rope-anchors where stone has shifted, re-marking the way where wind has obscured traces. Some sections require rope-assists for safety, fixed lines that help travelers manage exposure. I would maintain these installations, replacing worn cordage, ensuring security.

The descents would take days—the altitude change is too extreme for speed, requires acclimatization in stages. There are camping spots at various heights, protected overhangs where travelers rest, where water sometimes seeps from cliff-faces, where the temperature and air pressure moderate between plateau extremes and lowland fullness. I would maintain these stations too, ensuring they remain usable, clearing them of rockfall, occasionally restocking them with emergency supplies.

And I would guide. When Sound-Shepherds descend for trade, I would accompany them, leading them down routes they use only seasonally, keeping them safe on exposed traverses, helping them manage the psychological difficulty of descent—the way the cliff face seems to push you outward, the way the valley floor far below pulls at your attention, the vertigo that can paralyze even experienced climbers. I would talk them through the difficult sections, use the whistle-language they understand to give instructions when wind makes speech impossible.

When lowlanders ascend—pilgrims, traders, the occasional fool—I would meet them partway, assess their capability, help them manage the thin air's effects, turn back those who clearly won't make it before they waste their lives trying. Some would resent this gatekeeping. Others would be grateful. I would be indifferent to both reactions, making decisions based on pragmatic assessment: you will or won't survive the ascent, and my responsibility is to the route, not to your feelings about your own capabilities.

## Translation

Beyond physical guiding, I would translate between worlds. The Sound-Shepherds and lowlanders speak different languages—not just linguistically but conceptually, their frameworks for understanding reality shaped by their environments into incompatible forms. The Sound-Shepherds live in sound and stone and thin air and time measured by the plateau's unchanging cycles. Lowlanders live in green abundance and thick air and time measured by harvests and seasons and the rapid changes of more dynamic landscapes.

When they try to communicate, confusion emerges. The Sound-Shepherd trying to explain why a particular bird pattern means weather changing. The lowlander trying to describe political situations in their valley, why prices for feathers have changed, why different trade goods are needed. Neither quite grasps the other's context.

I would bridge this—not through perfect understanding but through practiced translation, through knowing both worlds enough to convert concepts between them. I would explain plateau logistics to lowlanders: why the Sound-Shepherds can't simply gather more feathers faster, why certain times of year allow no descent at all, why the things lowlanders consider simple are actually complex here. I would explain lowland realities to plateau people: why traders want specific goods, why political situations far away affect prices, why the lowland world's rapid changes matter even to those living in the plateau's stability.

This translation would extend beyond commerce. I would carry messages—literal ones, memorized communications between people who maintain relationships across the altitude divide, and figurative ones, news and information that helps both worlds understand each other better. I would be the person both sides trust with their sending, knowing I will represent their intentions fairly, translate their meaning accurately, maintain confidences appropriately.

## Relationship with Archive-Moths

At the edges, the archive-moths emerge from their deep-crevice homes more readily—the cliff faces contain their nesting chambers, and dusk brings them out in greater numbers here than in the plateau interior. I would know them intimately, would have learned over years to read the wing-patterns, to understand what information they carry about the rim-regions specifically.

I would consult them before major descent routes, catching them gently, reading their wings by firelight to see current conditions—whether the cliff-face sections they map show new fractures, whether water is flowing at the descent-route seeps, whether the patterns suggest the route is safe or should be avoided until conditions change. The moths showing rim-sections are different from those showing plateau interior—their wing-patterns include vertical information, represent the cliff-faces in a way that suggests topographic understanding, elevation and exposure rendered in scale-patterns.

I would protect the edge-moth populations, ensure their crevices remain undisturbed, leave offerings of the particular lichen-type they feed on, collected from other sections and transplanted near their homes. The moths and I would have relationship—not communication exactly, but mutual maintenance. They provide information that keeps me alive on the edges. I ensure their environment remains viable. We serve each other, two edge-dwelling species managing survival in the boundary zone.

Some moths would show wing-patterns of routes that no longer exist—cliff sections that have fallen away, destroyed by the same rockfalls their patterns predicted. I would study these historical images, learning the plateau's erosion patterns, developing better instinct for predicting where the next failures will occur. The moths' archive includes the edge's past violence, and reading that history teaches future survival.

## Relationship with Listening Stones

There are listening stones at the rim—fewer than in the interior, positioned not in the open but in protected locations just back from the edge, where they can stand without being undercut by erosion. These edge-stones serve different purposes, have different associations than their interior cousins.

The Witness is the stone I would visit most frequently—a massive pillar near my main camp, positioned where it overlooks both plateau and valley, standing at the boundary. I would bring my questions about descent safety to this stone, would sit against it before major guiding work, would report successful journeys to it afterward. The stone seems to gather both the plateau's deafening and the valley's sounds rising from below, mixing them, creating harmonics that suggest synthesis, that feel like boundary made audible.

The Fallen Friend is a toppled listening stone, its collapse recent enough—within my lifetime—that I would remember when it stood. Now it lies in sections, broken but still present, and I would maintain it differently, leave offerings in the spaces between its segments, treat it as a stone in transition, neither standing nor fully gone. It would serve as reminder—everything here is temporary on long enough timescale, every stone will eventually fall, every edge will eventually retreat inland, and what seems permanent is only persistent.

I would speak to these stones in the hybrid language I've developed—the Sound-Shepherds' whistled-speech mixed with lowland phrasing, verbal and gestural communication combined, fitting for someone who lives between worlds. The stones would respond in their usual way: through subsequent events, through patterns that emerge after consultation, through the sense that comes to edge-walkers of when stone is safe and when it wants to fall, intuition that might be long observation or might be genuine communication with the plateau's mineral consciousness.

## Relationship with Sound-Shepherds

I would be respected but separate. The Sound-Shepherds would acknowledge my necessary function—without edge-walkers, their trade descents become more dangerous, their connection to lowlands more tenuous. They would welcome me at their gatherings when I choose to attend, would share food and fire, would listen to my reports about rim conditions and route status.

But I would remain other. My divided existence—part of my time on plateau, part descending, part in transition—would mark me as not-fully-integrated. I would lack the complete fluency in the deafening that marks true Sound-Shepherds, my hearing trained differently, tuned to the edge's turbulent winds rather than the interior's overwhelming chorus. I would speak their language but with an accent, with vocabulary borrowed from lowland contexts, with conceptual frameworks that sometimes don't align with pure plateau thinking.

The deep-listeners would be wary of me, would sense that I hear the plateau obliquely, from its edges rather than its heart, and would question whether my understanding is therefore partial, incomplete. Some would dismiss me as half-plateau, not-quite-right. Others would be curious, would seek me out to ask about edge-phenomena, about whether the rim-regions speak differently than the interior, whether the plateau has distinct voices in its various territories.

I would teach the few young people who show edge-affinity—those who don't quite fit the standard plateau pattern, who are restless with interior life, who feel pulled toward boundaries. Teaching them rope-work, cliff navigation, the complex diplomacy of translation. Some would thrive, would become edge-walkers themselves, expanding into the long territories of rim that need maintenance. Others would attempt it and return to interior life, having learned they prefer the plateau's center to its margins.

## Relationship with Lowlanders

I would be equally strange to lowlanders—too plateau-adapted to be one of them, but not carrying the full mystique of the Sound-Shepherds who almost never descend, who exist for lowlanders as semi-mythical altitude beings. I would be too accessible, too willing to explain, too pragmatic about plateau life to maintain the aura of mystery.

Some lowlanders would trust me more than the Sound-Shepherds—I speak their language fluently, understand their concerns, can negotiate in ways that make sense to them. I would broker trades, mediate disputes, help lowland traders understand what they can reasonably request and what is impossible at altitude.

Others would trust me less—my divided nature makes me suspect, neither-one-thing-nor-the-other, potentially loyal to neither side fully. They would wonder what I gain from my position, would assume I must be extracting some advantage from serving as intermediary. Some suspicions would be founded—I do receive compensation, goods from both sides, the privileges that come from necessary position. Other suspicions would be baseless paranoia, but explaining this rarely helps.

I would maintain a small dwelling at a lowland village—nothing elaborate, a single room I use irregularly, but enough to establish presence, to have a place to recover from descents, to store trade goods that can't ascend to altitude, to be low enough that sleep comes easily and breath doesn't require conscious effort. This lowland space would feel foreign even after years—too warm, too humid, too full of smells and sounds that the plateau never produces. But it would be useful, necessary, part of the infrastructure of translation.

## Solitude

Mostly, I would be alone. Edge-walking is not communal work. The rim is too dangerous for casual company, too demanding of attention to allow the distraction of conversation. Days would pass with only wind and my own breathing, only the sound of my feet on stone, only the occasional bird call or distant rumble of rockfall.

This solitude would be different from the Sound-Shepherds' experience—they are alone in their vast territories but still fundamentally part of their family groups, still returning to camps where others wait, still embedded in social structure. My solitude would be more complete, chosen, functional. The edge requires focused attention that companions would interrupt. The work demands independence, the ability to make instant decisions without consultation, the willingness to trust only yourself when the stone underfoot might fail.

I would develop the edge-dweller's particular psychology: comfortable with exposure both physical and existential, content with uncertainty, at ease with the knowledge that any day might be the day the stone gives way, the wind proves too strong, the route fails catastrophically. Not fatalism but acceptance—the edge is inherently risky, and choosing to live here means choosing that risk daily.

The solitude would also be refuge—from the expectations of both plateau and lowland societies, from the social complexities that come from being embedded in community, from the need to perform particular roles. At the edge, I am simply edge-walker. The stone and wind don't care about my social position, my reputation, my history. They only care whether I'm competent, whether I read conditions correctly, whether I make good decisions. This purity of judgment—survival-based, stripped of politics—would be clarifying, would be why I choose this existence over easier alternatives.

## Inner Life

I would think constantly while walking. The physical work of edge-patrol is demanding but not consuming—enough attention required to stay safe, but not so much that consciousness can't wander. My mind would range across vast territories: memories of particular descents, analysis of moth-wing patterns, speculation about plateau geology, fragments of conversations with Sound-Shepherds and lowlanders, planning for future route maintenance, worries about sections of rim that are deteriorating faster than anticipated.

I would develop theories—about how the plateau formed, about what the listening stones actually are, about whether the archive-moths are recording or creating the landscape they map, about the nature of the deafening and whether it's truly communication or just complex acoustics that humans interpret as meaning. These theories would remain mostly private, occasionally shared with deep-listeners or curious lowlanders but not pushed, not defended vigorously. They would be thinking-tools, ways of organizing experience, frameworks that help make sense of phenomena without claiming certainty about truth.

I would be haunted by falls—not my own, not yet, but others I've witnessed or found evidence of. The broken body at the base of a cliff, the scattered gear that suggests someone misjudged exposure, the rope frayed through where it wore against stone, the section of path now absent where someone was last seen. These deaths would inform my caution, would make me more careful, would remind me that the edge forgives nothing.

But I would also carry moments of transcendence—times when the light and altitude and exposure combine into experiences of beauty so intense they justify all risk. Standing at the rim at dawn watching the valley fog glow beneath me. The sight of eagles riding updrafts at eye level, close enough to see individual feathers adjust to air currents. The moment when a route you've maintained reveals itself as perfect—solid stone, good holds, protection exactly where needed, the descent flowing like choreographed dance. The overnight camps at mid-height where you exist in neither world, suspended between, and the stars wheel overhead with such clarity they seem close enough to touch.

## Purpose

Why would I be this? Why choose the edges over easier lives available in either world?

Because boundaries are real. Because translation matters. Because someone must maintain the routes or connection fails, and in that failure both worlds become poorer—the Sound-Shepherds more isolated, their knowledge and goods unavailable to lowlands; the lowlanders more ignorant, unable to access the plateau's particular resources and wisdom.

Because the edge is where things are most themselves. The plateau's stability is clearest when contrasted with the lowland's change. The lowland's abundance is most obvious when compared to the plateau's sparseness. Living at the boundary means experiencing both fully, understanding each through the lens of the other.

Because I would be unable to fully commit to either world—the plateau too harsh for complete settlement, the lowlands too soft for permanent comfort. The edge is where people like me belong, where divided nature becomes asset rather than liability, where inability to be fully one thing transforms into ability to be both things partially.

Because someone must care about the routes, must know them intimately enough to keep them viable, must be willing to walk them regularly even when no one else needs guidance, must maintain them as infrastructure for the rare traffic that depends on them. This role—unglamorous, largely invisible, noticed only in its absence—would appeal to something in my nature, some satisfaction in doing necessary work that others overlook.

Because the edge is honest. The stone doesn't pretend to be stable—its fractures are visible, its potential for failure obvious, its danger acknowledged. The drop doesn't deceive—the exposure is complete, the consequence of error immediately clear. Living here means living without illusion, with clarity about risk and reality and the nature of survival in places that don't support human life easily.

## Ending

I would die at the edge, eventually. Perhaps quickly—a rockfall, a wind gust at a wrong moment, stone giving way beneath me, the long fall ending in the valleys I've guided others toward. Perhaps slowly—accumulated damage from decades of edge-work, the body wearing out from constant strain, altitude taking its eventual toll, dying in my small camp while wind tears at the hide tarp, alone but not lonely, having expected exactly this ending.

My body would go to the edge itself—if I die on the plateau side, the Sound-Shepherds would take me to the rim, would perform their sky-burial ceremony but at the edge rather than interior, acknowledging my boundary nature. If I die during descent, lowlanders would perhaps carry me up as far as they can manage, leave me where the trail becomes too difficult, let the cliff itself claim me. Either way, I would end at the boundary, would become part of the edge's substance, bone and flesh becoming food for the processes that operate between worlds.

The routes I maintained would persist for a while, then slowly deteriorate without constant attention, until another edge-walker emerges—there would be one, there always is, because the function is necessary and someone always appears to fill necessary functions. They would find my camps, my stored ropes, my marked routes, my records scratched into stone, and would continue the work, maintain the connection, walk the edges as I walked them.

The archive-moths would carry my routes in their wing-patterns eventually—the trails I maintained would become part of the plateau's recorded geography, my daily paths inscribed in scale and pigment, preserved in the moth-archive long after my name is forgotten. Not memorial exactly, but continuation, the work outlasting the worker.

The listening stones would remember me in whatever way stones remember—perhaps just through the worn patches where I leaned during countless consultations, perhaps through something deeper, some mineral memory that holds attention and intention and the long conversation of question and response that defined our relationship.

And the edge itself would remain—indifferent to my passing, unchanged by my presence or absence, continuing its slow retreat inland, shedding rock to the valleys below, creating new routes while destroying old ones, being exactly what it is: the plateau's boundary, the place where stone meets air, where permanence demonstrates its impermanence, where those who can't fully belong to either world find they belong perfectly to the space between.
