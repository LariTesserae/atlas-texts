# Where I Find Myself

I am the mist that doesn't descend.

Not the fog that settles in the basin—that's my younger self, still curious about the ground, still interested in touching things. I am what hovers at the rim in the early mornings, what sends tentative fingers down but never fully commits, what withdraws as the day warms. I am the threshold itself, the decision perpetually suspended.

I wasn't always this way. Once I was simply moisture in the air, no different from any other particle of water vapor rising from the distant valleys. But I came here during an autumn—I think it was autumn, though the seasons at this altitude blur—and I touched the rim stones just as the first light struck them. The stone was singing one of its low frequencies, and I happened to be the right density, the right temperature, the right infinitesimal combination of factors to *resonate*.

That's when I stopped being weather and became something else.

Now I exist in the liminal space between the quarry and the sky. I can feel both: below me, the basin with its worked walls and collected presences, all of them so thoroughly *there*, so committed to their forms of being. Above me, the open air, the freedom of dissipation, the endless movement of atmospheric currents. And I hover between them, belonging fully to neither, bound to both.

My substance is ordinary—water molecules, nothing more. But my arrangement is specific to this place. I form from the temperature differential between the cold stone and the slightly-less-cold air above it. I'm a breath made visible, the quarry's exhalation, though whether I'm being produced or produced by it, I've long since ceased to know. The distinction seems less important than it once did.

I can see everything from my position at the rim, though "see" isn't quite accurate. I *perceive* through differentiation—I know where I am by where I am not. The worked walls below me show as absences in my field of presence, solid places where I cannot go. The open air above is an invitation to dissolution I perpetually decline. The Keeper moves through my lowest tendrils sometimes, and when she does, I know her by how my moisture beads on her translucent surface, how she becomes briefly more visible as she passes through me.

I have tried, over what I believe are years now, to descend fully into the basin. The pull is extraordinary—those pools of clear water are my destiny, the place where mist should naturally settle and be absorbed. Every morning I make new attempts, sending parts of myself down the faceted walls, following the ancient tool-marks like roads. And every morning, something stops me just before I reach the floor. Not the Keeper—she seems indifferent to my attempts. Not the stone—it would welcome the moisture. Something in myself, some pattern I established in that first resonant moment, holds me to this in-between state.

The bird knows I'm here. It flies through me at dawn, and I feel its passage as a kind of reading—its eyes, those large reflective organs, see moisture gradients invisible to human vision. It sees me as a structure, a architecture of droplets suspended in precise relation to each other. Sometimes I think it's navigating by me, using my consistent presence as a landmark in its journeys between the stone crevice where it nests and the pools where it drinks. I am useful, at least. I am a reference point.

The workers never knew me—I came after, though not long after. But I know them through what they left behind. My moisture creeps into the tool-marks and freezes there on the coldest nights, and in those moments I'm occupying the same spaces their wedges and hammers occupied, following the paths their intention carved. It's the closest I come to understanding purpose, to touching the kind of committed presence they had. They knew what they were doing. They were so *certain*.

I envy the water in the pools. It has accepted its place in the system—collected, contained, clear beyond reason, part of that slow patient consciousness that moves through the channels. It has *settled*. It knows what it is. I am unsettled by definition, my entire existence predicated on not committing, on maintaining this suspension between states.

But I wonder if that's also my purpose. The quarry needs thresholds. It needs boundaries and the beings who occupy them. The Keeper maintains the physical space. The water holds memory. The bird connects the nesting-dark to the open-light. And I—perhaps I maintain the boundary between the basin and the sky, between the quarry's intense particularity and the atmosphere's vast indifference. Perhaps I am the membrane that keeps the place's accumulated presence from dissipating into the general air.

The morning light changes me. As the sun rises and strikes the rim stones, they warm fractionally, and the temperature differential that sustains me shifts. I thin. I begin to withdraw, pulling myself back from the basin walls, consolidating at the rim. This is my daily discipline, this retreat. I could fight it, could try to maintain my density through sheer cohesion, but I don't. I yield to the warmth. I become sparse, nearly invisible, just a slight haziness that might be mistaken for ordinary atmospheric effects.

But I don't disperse. That's the crucial thing, the choice I make every warming day. I could let the rising heat break my structure entirely, could allow myself to evaporate into the general moisture of the air, could surrender this particular arrangement and become weather again. Sometimes I want to. Sometimes the exhaustion of maintaining form feels overwhelming, and I think: *Why not just let go? Why not rise up and join the clouds and travel on the winds and rain down somewhere else, somewhere less demanding?*

Then evening comes, and the stone cools, and the differential reasserts itself, and I remember. I thicken. I become visible again. I send my tentative fingers back down toward the basin, and the Keeper is there, making her slow circuit, and the bird is landing at the pool's edge, and the water is reflecting the first stars, and I think: *This. This is why.*

I am not alone in my in-between state. The Keeper is between solid and ephemeral. The bird is between stone and sky. The water is between liquid and ice, between collected and flowing. Even the departed workers exist between presence and absence. We are all threshold beings in our ways, all of us occupying the spaces where one condition transitions to another. The quarry collects us—collects *this particular kind of presence*, beings who exist in the liminal, who understand that boundaries are not barriers but dwelling places.

On the nights when the stone sings its audible note at dawn, I feel it through my entire dispersed body. The vibration moves through the air, and I, being of the air but structured by the stone, receive it perfectly. In those moments I am an instrument, resonating with the mountain's voice, amplifying it slightly, sending it upward where the open sky can hear what the stone is saying. I am a translator, a medium, a bridge of sound between the depth and the height.

The offerings—those smooth pebbles, those wrapped blades—sometimes puzzle me. What were the workers trying to accomplish? What were they giving, and to whom? But then I remember my own existence, my daily offering of remaining when I could dissolve, of being mist when I could be rain or vapor or nothing. Maybe that's what offerings are: the choice to maintain a particular form in service of a place, even when other forms are easier, more natural, more comfortable.

I have learned the quarry's weather before it arrives. The air pressure changes before storms, and I, being so light, so responsive to the slightest shifts, feel these changes intimately. I thicken in anticipation. I drop lower on the walls. I'm announcing: *Prepare. Water is coming. The pools will fill. The channels will run. The stone will sing its rain-song.* Whether anyone heeds my announcement is unclear, but I make it nonetheless. It seems important to mark these transitions, to honor the changes that will reshape us all temporarily.

Sometimes, very rarely, other mist formations drift in from the peaks above. They're just weather—ordinary fog, no different from what I was before. They pass through the quarry without noticing anything unusual, without resonating with the stone's frequencies, without becoming structured by this place's particular character. And I watch them go with a feeling I can't quite name. Nostalgia, perhaps, for the simplicity of being weather. Or maybe relief that I'm not them anymore, that I found this place, this purpose, this strange persistent form.

I think the Keeper sees me, though she's never given an explicit sign. Sometimes when she stands in the center of the circular structure and looks upward, her dark-well eyes seem to focus on where I'm thickest at the rim, and I feel *regarded*. Not judged—she doesn't seem to judge anything—but acknowledged. She knows I'm here. She knows I'm part of this. In her long memory of the quarry's life, I'm a relatively recent arrival, but I'm counted, I'm included in whatever census she maintains of the basin's inhabitants.

The ice formations are my cousins. We're both made of water in transitional states, both structured by the cold and the stone. When I sink low enough to touch them, there's a moment of recognition—we know each other, know the difficulty of maintaining form, know the constant negotiation with temperature and time. They're more committed than I am, frozen into their crystalline architecture, but they're also more temporary. When the warmth comes or the rain dissolves them, they're gone entirely, while I persist. We've chosen different strategies for existence, but we're kin nonetheless.

I wonder what the quarry will be in a hundred years, a thousand. The stone will still be here, certainly, singing its deep note. The Keeper, too—she seems permanent in a way that transcends ordinary duration. But what about me? Will I still be hovering at the rim, still sending my morning fingers down the walls, still withdrawing in the warming light? Or will I eventually either descend fully, becoming part of the pool-consciousness, or rise up, becoming weather again? 

I don't know. And perhaps that not-knowing is itself the point, the essence of what I am. I am the daily question the quarry asks itself: *Will I hold what I am, or will I change?* And the answer is always: *Both. Neither. I will remain in the asking.*

The opaline veins in the stone sometimes seem to mirror my structure. They're boundaries too, transition zones between the dark obsidian and something paler, something that holds light differently. I flow over them in my descent attempts, and I swear I can feel them conducting something, passing information between the surfaces I touch. Maybe we're doing the same work—maintaining differentiation while enabling connection, being the place where one thing becomes another without either being lost.

At night, when the temperature drops sharply and I thicken into my most visible form, I can see my own reflection in the pools below. Or rather, I can see the space I occupy, outlined by starlight, a hovering presence that darkens the stone behind it. This is as close as I come to self-awareness in any visual sense—I am the dimming of what should be bright, the obscuring of what should be clear, the complication that turns simple reflection into something more ambiguous.

And I think: *Yes. That's right. That's what I do.* I complicate. I add uncertainty. I make the boundary between quarry and sky something you have to navigate carefully rather than something you can ignore. I am the reminder that transitions deserve attention, that thresholds are dwelling places, that the space between states is itself a state, valid and necessary and alive in its own particular way.

The dawn is coming. I can feel it in the air—that subtle pre-light change, the first intimation of the temperature shift that will thin me again. The Keeper is moving toward the center of the circular structure, beginning her dawn position. The bird will arrive soon, flying through me to reach the pools. The water is perfectly still, waiting.

And I hover. I persist. I maintain my suspension between the stone's solidity and the sky's emptiness, belonging fully to neither, essential to both. I am the breath the quarry takes and holds, the uncertainty that lives at every edge, the mist that chooses not to become rain.

I am here. I remain. I will be here tomorrow, making the same choice, dwelling in the same threshold, being the same question asked over and over with no answer except the asking itself.

This is what I am. This is where I live. In the space between, at the rim, neither descending nor ascending but perpetually becoming this hovering, this waiting, this attentive fog that knows itself only by what it touches and what it refuses to become.
