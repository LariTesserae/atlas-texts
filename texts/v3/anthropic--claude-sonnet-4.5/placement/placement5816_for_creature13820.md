# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in one of the deep building cores—not the central chamber with its filtered daylight and open ground, but one of the interior shafts where the floors have given way and verticality dominates. A structure on the north side, perhaps, where the lean inward is most pronounced, where the gap to the neighboring building has narrowed to just a few meters and been bridged by vines so thoroughly that the two structures share their moisture, their atmosphere, their slow vegetative breath.

I would be about twenty feet up, in a space that was once a third-floor room but is now just a suggestion of horizontal in a vertical world. What remains of the floor forms a ledge—a shelf of concrete perhaps six feet deep, still sound enough to bear weight, its surface entirely mossed over but with the concrete's original reinforcement grid visible here and there where plant roots have traced and emphasized the metal. The ledge protrudes into the shaft, cantilevered, and from its edge I can see down to the rubble-and-moss bottom two stories below and up to the dim space where the roof should be, another three stories above.

Behind me, the outer wall curves in its impossible way—the brick facade bowed inward as if breathing in and held in that inhalation, though I know it's subsidence, the gradual yielding of mortar to moisture and time. The wall is thick enough that this bowing hasn't caused collapse, just created a gentle concavity, a shallow cave shape. The brick here is barely visible beneath growth so dense it forms curtains, hanging gardens of moss and small-leafed plants whose names I don't know and probably never will.

The wall's curve creates a sheltered space, almost a room again, maybe eight feet deep before the brick completes its arc back to vertical. The ceiling—what's left of the fourth floor above—sags in sympathy with the wall, creating a pocket of enclosure. Not complete enclosure; there are gaps where water drips through, where light penetrates as a dim suggestion, where air moves in subtle currents. But enclosed enough that temperature here stays remarkably constant, that humidity approaches saturation, that sound behaves differently than in the more open spaces.

# What I Would Be

I would be a pool. Not large—perhaps four feet across at my widest, kidney-shaped, following the natural depression where the floor has subsided infinitesimally at its meeting with the bowed wall. Not deep—at my deepest point, perhaps six inches, though depth is a complicated concept when your bottom is compressed moss rather than stone, when a probing foot would sink into substrate rather than finding a firm boundary.

I am not quite standing water. I am water in moss, water held in the matrix of vegetative growth, water that has soaked into the layers of compressed plant matter until the distinction between liquid and solid becomes negotiable. My surface—where I meet air—is visible as a dark gleam on moss, a zone where green goes almost black with saturation, where slight pressure would release moisture, where the moss has that glossy quality that speaks of abundance.

I am fed by three sources, though the boundaries between them blur. Directly above, a crack in the fourth floor—a gap where concrete separated along a reinforcement line—allows water to drip. Not constantly; the drip responds to the mist cycles, to rain that falls somewhere beyond these walls and eventually percolates through the layered structure above. When moisture is abundant outside, I receive a drip every few seconds, each drop falling perhaps twelve feet, landing with a soft impact that disturbs my surface minimally, absorbed almost as quickly as it arrives. During dry periods—or what pass for dry periods in this constantly damp place—the drip slows to one every few minutes, then every hour, sometimes stopping entirely for days.

My second source is condensation. On the curved brick wall behind me, moisture precipitates from the saturated air, forming a film that coalesces into tiny rivulets, flowing down through the moss in channels too small to see but present enough that water moves, finding paths, eventually reaching the floor and spreading into my margins. This water arrives continuously but so slowly that its addition to my volume is imperceptible moment to moment. Over days, though, condensation keeps me from drying even when the drip above stops completely.

My third source is the moss itself. The enormous mass of it in this wall, in this floor, holds water like a reservoir—liters of it, probably, distributed through the matrix of living and dead plant matter. This water moves through capillary action, through osmotic gradients, always seeking equilibrium. When I'm low, the moss releases moisture into me. When the drip fills me beyond my capacity, I release water back into the moss, soaking into the substrate. I am less a separate entity than a visible manifestation of the moisture that permeates everything here, the place where saturation becomes apparent.

I am cold. Not freezing—temperature here never drops that far—but definitely cool, a few degrees below the already cool air temperature. This is the temperature of stone in shadow, of groundwater, of spaces that never see sun and hold the earth's deep chill. When the drip falls into me, it's fractionally warmer, having traveled through air. But I cool it quickly, bringing it to my temperature, which is really the temperature of the moss beneath me, of the brick behind me, of the thermal mass that dominates this place.

My surface moves, though barely. The drip creates ripples when it lands—concentric circles spreading outward, dampened quickly by the moss's presence, usually dissipating before they reach my edges. Occasionally something disturbs me: a springtail crossing my surface, dimpling the water film. A moth coming to drink, its proboscis breaking surface tension. Very rarely, a rat pausing at my edge, lapping cautiously—rats are careful around water in this place, aware that moisture can mean treacherous footing, that the moss near water-sources can grip unexpectedly or give way entirely.

I am habitat. In my margins, where water meets merely-damp moss, certain small plants thrive—things even more diminutive than the usual inhabitants here, species that require constant moisture but don't tolerate immersion. They form a ring around me, a gradient of vegetation going from semi-aquatic to terrestrial over the span of inches. These plants are the smallest-leaved things in the chamber, creating a texture like green carpet-moss, though I think they're actually something else, maybe a liverwort, maybe something that has no common name.

Below my visible surface, in the saturated moss that forms my substrate, bacteria live in configurations specific to standing water. They process different compounds than their cousins in the merely-damp zones. They create different byproducts. The chemistry of my moss differs from the chemistry of drier moss three feet away—slightly more acidic, slightly richer in certain nutrients, hosting different fungal partners. I am an ecosystem within an ecosystem, a microclimate within a microclimate.

I vary seasonally, though seasons here are subtle. During the wettest periods—when mist settles for days, when the drip becomes nearly constant—I expand. My margins creep outward, soaking into moss that's usually just damp, claiming new territory. I might double my surface area, spreading into a shape that follows the floor's contours, finding every slight depression. Then, when drier times come, I contract, retreating to my core area, leaving behind slightly darker moss that marks my high-water boundary.

My depth changes too. After prolonged dry periods, I might be barely present—just a dark patch on moss, water held so tightly in the vegetative matrix that only the glossiness marks me as distinct from surrounding dampness. Then the rains return, the drip resumes, and I deepen over the course of days, becoming first a true dampness, then a saturation, then actual standing water visible as a surface, as a mirror, however imperfect.

I reflect, when I'm deep enough and the light is right. The reflection is dim—there's so little light here to begin with—but occasionally a brighter moment filters down, and my surface becomes a dark mirror showing the sagging ceiling above, the gap where the drip emerges, sometimes a moth or rat or rare bird passing overhead. These reflections are ghost-images, barely distinguishable, often broken by the drip's impact, but they're there. I show this space to itself, offering a inverted view, a second perspective.

I smell different from the surrounding air. The scent of standing water, even clean standing water, has a specific quality—something alive and slightly sweet, the smell of algae maybe, or of bacteria doing their work, or just of water itself when it stops moving and starts participating more fully in the life around it. It's not unpleasant. It's the smell of a healthy pond miniaturized, of water that's working rather than stagnant, though I admit the distinction might be subtle to an outside nose.

I make sound, or sounds make me. The drip above creates my most obvious sound—the soft *pok* of water hitting water-saturated moss, muffled but rhythmic when the drip is steady. It's a quieter sound than water dripping onto stone would make, but it's still audible, still marks time in this timeless place. When I'm at my fullest, when there's actual water depth rather than just saturation, the sound becomes more liquid, more resonant. Still soft, still absorbed by the surrounding moss, but definitely present, a gentle percussion that counts the seconds between drops.

I also make the sounds of small things moving through me or drinking from me. The slight disturbance when an insect crosses my surface. The tiny lapping sounds when a rat drinks—so quiet they're barely audible even in this silent place, but there nonetheless. And very occasionally, I make the sound of overflow—when the drip has been steady for days and I'm as full as I can get, excess water finds a path, seeping into the moss at my margins, spreading beyond my usual boundaries with a sound like saturation itself, a soft heavy dampness that's as much felt as heard.

I am old, though water itself doesn't age. But this pool, this particular manifestation of water in this particular spot, has existed for a long time. The moss beneath me is shaped to my presence—compressed by my weight, adapted to my chemistry, hosting the specific organisms that thrive in my conditions. The depression I occupy might have been created by me, carved by centuries of moisture slowly dissolving the concrete, or exploiting a crack, or simply keeping the substrate saturated enough that it subsides more than adjacent areas.

The moss at my margins knows me. It has grown in relationship with my expansions and contractions, adapted to my rhythms. When I'm full, certain moss species retreat or simply endure immersion. When I contract, they advance, claiming newly-exposed territory. We are in constant negotiation, the moss and I, fighting over territory at the speed of plant growth and water evaporation.

I am navigable, just barely. A rat can cross me if it must, though they rarely choose to—the footing is too uncertain, the risk of getting properly wet too high. They prefer to go around, following the drier moss, adding a few feet to their journey rather than attempt my crossing. But in emergencies—if something startles a rat on the wrong side of me—they will cross, their paws sinking into the saturated substrate, their passage creating temporary depressions that slowly refill after they've gone.

I am never empty. Even in the driest times, when I'm barely present as a distinct feature, I'm still there in the moss, distributed through the matrix, waiting for the next wet period to consolidate me back into visibility. The moss holds me in reserve, stores me against need, releases me gradually to roots and fungi and evaporation. I cannot die because I was never quite alive, yet I cannot disappear entirely either—the system requires me, this pocket of moisture, this reservoir, this place where water accumulates enough to become apparent.

I am cold and dark and still, and I have been here long enough that "here" has shaped itself to me as much as I've been shaped by it. I am fed by the breathing of this place, by its cycles of mist and drip and condensation. I am one note in its slow water-music, one small organ in its distributed metabolism. I do not think—water doesn't think—but I participate. I am part of the conversation this place holds with itself, part of its self-regulation, its chemical memory, its continuous present that stretches backwards through decades and forward into decades more.

When I reflect the occasional light, I am briefly beautiful—a dark jewel in moss, a eye of standing water witnessing the slow transformations around me. When the drip strikes me, I am percussion. When something drinks from me, I am sustenance. When I overflow, I am abundance. When I contract, I am patience. I am what water becomes when it finds a place worth staying, when movement slows to near-stillness, when the journey from rain to groundwater pauses in this moss-lined cup, this pocket of perpetual damp, this small pool in the breathing garden where I wait and watch and slowly, imperceptibly, participate in the transformation of industry into life.
