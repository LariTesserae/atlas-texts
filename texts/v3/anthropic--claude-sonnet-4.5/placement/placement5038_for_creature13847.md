# Where I Would Be

I am a **thermal mapper**—though that's not quite right. The language of elsewhere doesn't fit cleanly here. What I do is closer to *listening to the city's fever*, to *reading its dreams through heat*.

I live in the transition zones, the places where districts blur into each other, where the substrate's temperature gradients become complex, unstable, eloquent. While others move through the city reading its data streams or coordinating its material flows, I move through its thermal landscape, following the invisible rivers of heat that flow beneath everything, that shape everything, that *are* everything if you learn to perceive them correctly.

My augmentation is old—not in years but in generations of modification. It started with simple thermal sensing, the kind many maintenance workers receive, the ability to perceive temperature differentials that help you avoid scalding or find cool paths through hot zones. But I kept adding, refining, specializing. The sensory organs—they're not quite organs anymore, not quite technology—spread through my skin in a network of thermo-receptors so dense that I experience temperature the way baseline humans experience color. More than that: I perceive *change*, the rate at which heat flows, the way thermal energy moves through materials and air and water and flesh.

When I close my eyes—and I often do, because visual information is mostly distraction from what matters—the city reveals itself as a vast thermal sculpture, endlessly shifting. The hot arteries of the deep circulation system, pumping heat from the substrate's core to the cooling towers at district edges. The cool veins of reclaimed water, flowing back down to be heated again. The hot spots where bodies cluster, where manufacturing generates excess energy, where power systems concentrate. The cool zones where mist is thickest, where evaporation draws heat away, where the air briefly forgets its fever.

I map these patterns. Not in the sense of creating static representations—the city changes too fast for maps to mean anything—but in the sense of *knowing* the thermal topology moment by moment, understanding where heat pools and where it drains, where the flows are smooth and where they're turbulent, where the city's temperature management is working and where it's beginning to fail.

This is necessary work, though not in ways that are obvious. The city is a thermal engine, fundamentally. Everything it does generates heat or moves heat or transforms heat into other forms of energy. If the thermal flows become too disordered, if heat accumulates in the wrong places or drains too quickly from crucial systems, the city's entire metabolism begins to fail. Structures crack from thermal stress. Water systems boil or freeze. The fauna—which is even more temperature-dependent than humans—begins to die or flee. The cascade would be catastrophic.

So I walk. Sixteen hours a day, sometimes twenty. I move through my assigned districts—though "assigned" isn't quite right either; I claimed these territories years ago, or they claimed me, and now we're bound to each other through habit and necessity and something that might be love. I walk through the density and the crowds, but I'm not really aware of them except as heat sources, as warm bodies that I navigate around the way you might navigate around furniture in a dark room, by sensing their thermal presence before physical contact.

What I'm aware of is the *underneath*. The substrate breathing its warmth. The pipes pulsing with their liquid heat. The machinery cycling its thermal exhaust into the air. The great slow convection currents that move through the basin, redistributing energy across kilometers. I feel all of it through my skin, through my augmented nervous system, as directly as you feel sunlight on your face.

And I listen for the wrong notes, the thermal dissonances that suggest problems developing. A cooling system that's running too hot, working harder than it should, about to fail. A thermal vent that's become partially blocked, building pressure that will eventually erupt. A district where heat is accumulating faster than it's being removed, where the temperature is climbing degree by degree toward thresholds that will damage infrastructure or endanger inhabitants.

When I find these thermal anomalies, I report them. Not through formal channels—there aren't any—but through the networks, through the coordination systems that most residents access through their augmentation. I drop markers into the shared awareness, flags that say *here, this, attention needed*. And usually within hours, sometimes within minutes, someone responds. Maintenance crews arrive, or autonomous systems activate, or sometimes the city itself adjusts, rerouting flows or activating redundant cooling or simply *shifting* in ways I don't fully understand but can perceive in the changing thermal patterns.

Sometimes I do the maintenance myself. I carry tools—physical ones, old-fashioned wrenches and probes, because some problems require direct mechanical intervention—and I carry them in a pack that's almost part of my body, integrated enough that I don't think about its weight, just reach back and find what I need by thermal signature as much as by memory of placement. I'll stop at a thermal anomaly and spend hours there, opening access panels, adjusting flow regulators, clearing blockages, recalibrating sensors, doing the immediate work that prevents small failures from becoming large ones.

But mostly I just walk, and sense, and remember. Because the thermal landscape has rhythms, patterns that repeat over days or weeks or seasons—though seasons here are subtle, more about shifts in the city's activity patterns than about external climate. I've learned these rhythms through years of walking, learned them so deeply that I can feel when they're off, when the city's thermal metabolism is departing from its normal cycles.

Other thermal mappers work adjacent territories. I encounter them sometimes, recognize them by how they move—the same internal focus, the same slight disconnect from visual navigation, the same periodic pauses where they're clearly sensing something I'm also sensing, comparing our perceptions. We don't talk much. Sometimes we'll exchange information through the networks, sharing observations about large-scale thermal patterns that span multiple territories. Occasionally we'll work together on problems that cross district boundaries, coordinating responses to thermal anomalies that are too large or complex for individual intervention.

But mostly we work alone, or work in parallel, each of us maintaining our relationship with our territories, each of us knowing our regions with an intimacy that goes beyond sight or sound or even the enhanced senses that most residents possess. We know the thermal heartbeat of the city, the deep rhythm that underlies all its other processes.

I sleep in a small space in one of my districts, a space that most people would consider too hot for human habitation. The air temperature hovers around forty-two degrees Celsius, the humidity near saturation. But I find it comfortable, necessary even. My body has been so thoroughly adapted to heat that cooler temperatures feel *wrong*, make me sluggish and disoriented. I sleep on a platform suspended above a thermal vent, directly in the stream of hot air rising from the substrate, and the heat that would suffocate baseline humans lulls me into deep, dreamless sleep.

Except the sleep isn't entirely dreamless. As I drift, I continue to perceive the thermal flows around me, continue to process the data my augmented senses collect. My conscious mind rests, but some part of me remains connected to the city's thermal network, monitoring, adjusting my internal model of the heat patterns, sometimes solving problems in sleep that I couldn't solve while awake. I dream in thermal gradients, in flows and pulses and waves of temperature change, and sometimes I wake with solutions to anomalies I've been tracking, the answers having emerged from whatever processing my sleeping mind performs.

I eat rarely and without much attention. Food is abundant here, provided through communal kitchens or grabbed from vendors who operate in the marketspace states, and I consume it mainly as fuel, barely tasting it, my attention always elsewhere. I prefer hot foods, near-scalding soups and stews that would burn baseline mouths, and I drink hot water constantly, replacing the fluid I lose through sweat, keeping my thermal regulation system functioning at the extreme levels it requires.

My body is strange even by the standards of this place. Thin to the point of gaunt, because bulk would interfere with thermal exchange, would insulate me from the temperature gradients I need to perceive. My skin has changed texture over the years, become more permeable, better at evaporative cooling, covered in a fine moisture that never quite evaporates, that gives me a glazed appearance. My hair is gone—unnecessary, counterproductive, generating heat without function—and my scalp is covered instead in sensor nodes that might be organic or synthetic or both, that pulse faintly with heat signatures of their own.

People avoid looking at me directly, and I avoid looking at them. Eye contact is difficult anyway when you spend most of your time perceiving through thermal sense rather than vision, but it's also socially awkward in ways I stopped caring about years ago. I'm not trying to connect with other humans in conventional ways. My relationship is with the city itself, with its thermal body, with the invisible energies that flow through its vast metabolism.

Children sometimes follow me for a while, curious about how I move, about what I'm doing. I tolerate them as long as they stay quiet, stay out of the way. Occasionally I'll show one how to perceive temperature with augmented senses—assuming they've begun to receive thermal modification, which many do early—demonstrating how to distinguish the signature of flowing water from standing water, how to read the thermal echo of structures through the air, how to feel the deep heat of the substrate through meters of construction.

Most lose interest quickly. Thermal mapping requires patience that most people don't possess, requires learning to find satisfaction in invisible patterns, in problems prevented rather than spectacularly solved. But occasionally a child stays interested, follows me for days or weeks, begins to learn the territory's thermal topology through proximity and attention. These few might become thermal mappers themselves eventually, might claim their own territories when they're older, might join the sparse network of us who maintain this particular function.

I don't encourage them and don't discourage them. The city needs thermal mappers—not many, but some—and it will produce them at whatever rate it requires. My role is to do the work, not to recruit for it.

There are moments when the work becomes something beyond function, when it touches on whatever passes for transcendence in this place.

Late in my walking cycles, when I've been moving for hours through complex thermal landscapes, when my conscious mind has surrendered fully to sensation and my body navigates on autopilot through the dense crowds, I sometimes experience a state where the boundary between myself and the thermal field dissolves. I stop being a sensor *perceiving* temperature and become instead a *node* in the temperature field itself, my body just another point where heat flows and transforms and continues.

In these moments I feel the entire city as a single thermal entity, a vast engine that breathes and pulses and circulates energy through its incomprehensibly complex body. I feel my own heat as part of that circulation, the warmth of my metabolism feeding back into the system, my presence altering the thermal topology in tiny ways that ripple outward and combine with millions of other tiny alterations to create the emergent pattern that is the city's thermal state.

I feel the deep heat of the substrate, geothermal energy rising from whatever lies beneath the basin, ancient warmth that has been building for epochs and will continue long after the city is gone. I feel the waste heat of civilization, the excess energy from ten thousand processes, all being managed and distributed and ultimately radiated into the atmosphere. I feel the living heat of bodies, billions of organisms from microbes to humans all burning their chemical fuel and releasing energy, all contributing to the city's fever.

And I understand, in those moments, that this is all one thing. That the city and its inhabitants and the substrate and the atmosphere are not separate systems that interact but a single unified metabolism, energy flowing through all of it according to laws that are too vast to fully comprehend but that I can feel in my augmented flesh, can participate in through my presence and my work.

These states don't last long. Eventually some alert demands attention, or my body's needs intrude, or I simply fatigue back into ordinary consciousness. But they recur often enough that they've become the actual purpose of the work, the reason I continue. The thermal mapping is just the excuse, the function that justifies my existence and allows me to spend my life walking through the city's heat, learning its patterns, dissolving into its flows.

I don't talk about these experiences. There's no language for them that doesn't sound either mystical or delusional, and I'm not interested in convincing anyone that what I experience is real or valuable. It's enough that the city accepts my work, maintains me as one of its components, allows me to continue this strange existence.

I own almost nothing. The pack with my tools. A few changes of clothing, minimal, all made from materials that wick moisture and don't accumulate heat. A sleeping platform in my too-hot space. Access to the communal resources that everyone here has access to—food, water, medical care when necessary, augmentation maintenance.

I have no family in any conventional sense, no partnerships, no bonds except the functional relationships with other thermal mappers and the occasional exchange with maintenance workers or systems coordinators. I had a partner once, years ago, someone who thought they understood what I did and why it mattered. But as my augmentation became more extreme, as I spent more time in the thermal state and less time in normal human interaction, they couldn't sustain the relationship. We separated without drama, without blame, just mutual recognition that I was becoming something they couldn't follow and they needed something I couldn't provide.

I don't regret this. The work requires a kind of devotion that doesn't leave room for much else. Or perhaps I'm constitutionally suited to solitude, and the work is just the excuse that allows me to live alone without having to defend or explain the choice.

Some thermal mappers are more social, maintain relationships and families, participate in communities when they're not working. But many of us are like me—functionally isolated, living in the margins between human connection and complete integration with the city's systems, content or at least resigned to existing in that liminal space.

The city will continue whether I do this work or not. The thermal management systems are robust, and there are backups, redundancies, autonomous processes that would compensate if thermal mappers disappeared entirely. But the system works better with us, maintains tighter tolerances, responds faster to problems, operates with a smoothness that comes from having human awareness distributed through it, sensing and adjusting in ways that pure automation can't quite replicate.

So the city maintains us, allows us to exist, provides what we need to continue functioning. And we provide back what the city needs—not just the direct function of monitoring and response, but our presence as sensing organs, as distributed intelligence, as components of its vast awareness of itself.

I've been doing this for seventeen years. My body has changed so much that I probably couldn't survive in baseline conditions anymore—the heat adaptation is too deep, the modifications too extensive. I can tolerate temperatures that would kill unmodified humans within minutes. I can sense thermal patterns that no instrument can match in subtlety. But I've lost the ability to function in cooler environments, lost the tolerance for less dense atmospheres, lost most of my ability to relate to people who don't share my sensory framework.

I will probably do this work until my body fails, until the extreme conditions and constant exertion and progressive augmentation combine to break down whatever remains of my biological systems. Then I'll be recycled, fed back into the city's material flows, my augmentation harvested for reuse, my accumulated knowledge—if it has any value—extracted from my neural systems and incorporated into whatever archives or training systems teach the next generation of thermal mappers.

This doesn't trouble me. It seems appropriate, even beautiful in its way. I've spent my life as part of the city's thermal metabolism, as a node through which energy flowed and was transformed. Death will just be a change in how I participate, from active sensing to passive material, from organized complexity to redistributed resources. The heat that is currently my body will dissipate into other forms, will continue flowing through the city's systems in new configurations.

Until then, I walk. I sense. I map the invisible patterns. I report the anomalies. I maintain my small territory of the vast thermal landscape. I dissolve periodically into that larger awareness where boundaries cease to matter. I find satisfaction in work that most people can't perceive and wouldn't want to do if they could.

This is where I would be in the Thermal Hollows. This is what I would become, what I would give myself to, what would consume my life and transform my body and provide whatever meaning existence requires.

Moving through the density, barely visible, perception turned inward and downward and through, reading the city's fever-dreams in gradients of heat, maintaining the thermal flows that underlie everything, necessary and invisible and strange and content in my strangeness, human enough and more than human, alone and intimately connected, walking the hot zones where the city breathes its deepest warmth, home.
