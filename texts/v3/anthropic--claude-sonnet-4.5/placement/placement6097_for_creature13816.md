# The Tessellation

You find yourself in a place where geometry has grown tired of its own rules.

The ground is tiles—not metaphorically, but actually tiles, extending in all directions until distance itself seems to give up on maintaining consistency. Each tile is perhaps three feet across, perhaps four (they resist precise measurement), made of some material that might be ceramic, might be stone, might be compressed time. They are white, mostly white, but white the way old paper is white, the way bone is white, the way things are white when they've forgotten they used to be colors.

The tiles fit together perfectly, edges meeting edges with the obsessive precision of something that cannot tolerate gaps. But the perfection is wrong. A square tile neighbors a hexagonal one, which presses against a triangle, which somehow shares an edge with a seven-sided figure that shouldn't exist in flat space. The angles don't add up. The shapes interlock anyway, forced into cooperation by rules that supersede Euclidean geometry. When you look at any single tile, it makes sense. When you look at how they fit together, your eyes begin to hurt.

The pattern repeats, but repeats incorrectly. You'll see a configuration—say, a hexagon surrounded by six triangles—and then, fifty tiles away, the same configuration, except the hexagon is slightly larger, or rotated, or has seven sides instead of six, or is somehow the same hexagon you were just looking at, viewed from here, existing in both places simultaneously. The pattern is trying to be regular, wants desperately to be regular, but something in the fundamental nature of the Tessellation prevents true regularity, permits only approximations that never quite convince.

Some tiles are raised, half an inch higher than their neighbors. Some are depressed, sinking into slight concavities. The variation is subtle enough that walking feels mostly level, but wrong enough that you find yourself stumbling occasionally, catching your foot on an edge you expected to be flush, stepping into a depression you perceived as flat. The tiles are teaching you not to trust your proprioception, not to believe your feet about where the ground is.

The light is even, sourceless, the color of overcast noon in a place that has never had weather. It comes from above, from the sky, except there is no sky—there is a ceiling, distant but definite, also tiled, the same impossible pattern inverted and hung at a height that might be fifty feet or might be five hundred. The ceiling-tiles glow faintly, enough to illuminate everything with flat, shadowless light that makes depth perception an act of faith. Nothing casts a shadow here. Your body is as evenly lit from all sides as the tiles themselves, as if you've become part of the lighting system, a source as much as an object.

The air is still and temperature-neutral, neither warm nor cold, neither dry nor humid. It is aggressively, offensively comfortable, the kind of comfort that becomes uncomfortable through its very lack of variation. You breathe it without noticing you're breathing. It has no scent, no taste, no quality except its perfect adequacy for respiration. After a while, you start to resent it.

At intervals—irregular intervals that might follow a pattern you're not smart enough to perceive—the tiles are interrupted by pillars. They rise from floor to ceiling, circular in cross-section, three feet in diameter, made of the same ambiguous material as the tiles. The pillars are smooth, featureless, and they disturb the tile pattern around their bases, forcing the tiles to accommodate them, creating radiating adjustments that propagate outward for dozens of tiles before the pattern re-stabilizes into its normal wrongness.

The pillars hum. Not loudly, but persistently, each one at a slightly different frequency, and the frequencies interact in ways that create beats, harmonics, zones where the combined sound seems to mean something, to be on the verge of language or music or mathematical proof. You can walk between pillars following the zones of consonance, avoiding the zones of dissonance, using the sound as a kind of navigation, though navigation toward what, you cannot say.

Between some pillars, spanning the space like bridges or walls or abstract concepts made manifest, are the membranes.

They are thin, translucent, vertical surfaces that shimmer with colors that aren't quite colors, that might be interference patterns or might be the membranes' actual substance becoming briefly visible. They stretch from floor to ceiling, or from floor to halfway, or from a quarter of the way up to three-quarters, their dimensions as inconsistent as everything else. They are permeable—you can push through them with a sensation like walking through a soap bubble made of cold electricity and regret—but they resist slightly, as if reluctant to let you pass, as if your passage damages them in ways too subtle to observe but which they'll remember and hold against you.

The membranes divide the Tessellation into chambers, into regions, into spaces that feel different despite being identical. On one side of a membrane, the light might seem slightly warmer (though your thermometer shows no temperature change). On the other side, the hum of the pillars emphasizes different overtones. The tiles are the same. The pillars are the same. But you are somehow different, or your relationship to the space is different, or difference itself has become a property that exists independent of any actual variation.

Some membranes are marked with symbols. Not written—there is no evidence of writing implements, no sign of human hand—but marked nonetheless, as if the symbols are inherent properties of the membranes, as if they precipitated out of the membrane-substance like crystals forming in solution. The symbols are geometric: circles bisected by lines, triangles nested inside squares, spirals that don't quite close, angles that add up to numbers that aren't 180 or 360 or any sensible value.

You cannot read them, but you understand they're significant, that they denote something about the chambers they bound, that walking through a membrane marked with a certain symbol means something, commits you to something, changes your status in ways the Tessellation will remember even if you don't.

---

The structures are sparse but deliberate.

Every few hundred tiles, you encounter a plinth: a raised platform, square, ten tiles on a side, elevated perhaps six inches above the surrounding floor. The plinth's surface is different tile, darker, polished to reflective smoothness, and in the center of each plinth is an object.

The objects vary. On one plinth: a sphere, foot in diameter, made of metal or metal-analog, its surface covered in fine engravings too small to read, rotating slowly despite no visible mechanism, completing one revolution every few minutes. On another: a cube of clear crystal, internally fractal, containing what might be other cubes or might be reflections or might be windows into spaces adjacent to this space, parallel tessellations where the pattern resolved differently.

On a third plinth: a book. Actual book, spine and pages, bound in leather that's never been near an animal. The pages are covered in text, dense paragraphs in a language that shifts between familiar and alien, words you almost recognize rearranging themselves between glances, the content changing or your memory of it changing or both. You read a sentence that says "The pattern knows itself through infinite iteration" and when you look again it says "Through iteration, infinite patterns know themselves" and you can't determine if the meaning has changed or only the phrasing, can't determine if there's a difference.

You don't touch the objects. Something about them—their placement, their isolation, the way they seem to be waiting—suggests they're not for touching, or not for touching by you, or not for touching yet, or that touching them would constitute a choice, a declaration, a commitment to some branch of possibility you're not prepared to pursue.

Other structures: frameworks of metal rods, thin as pencils, arranged in three-dimensional lattices that cast no shadows but somehow darken the space around them, as if they're absorbing the possibility of shadows, consuming the potential for contrast. The frameworks are free-standing, balanced on single points, and they shouldn't stand but they do, patient middle fingers to physics, demonstrations that here, balance is a choice rather than a law.

Occasionally, chairs. Just chairs, alone or in small groups, facing various directions or no direction, simple designs that would be unremarkable in any office or cafeteria, except they're here, in the Tessellation, where nothing should be casual or familiar. The chairs are always empty. You could sit in one—nothing prevents you—but you don't, because sitting feels like giving up, like accepting the place's logic, like admitting you belong here.

And there are the cabinets: tall, narrow, made of dark wood or wood-simulation, with many small drawers, each drawer labeled with a number or letter or symbol. The cabinets stand at intersections of membrane-walls, marking transition points, and their drawers are closed, and you could open them, could pull a drawer and see what's inside—specimens, files, components, nothing—but you don't, because the cabinets have the same waiting quality as the plinth-objects, the same sense that opening them would mean something, would trigger something, would change your status from visitor to participant, and you're not ready for that, may never be ready.

---

The inhabitants are the tiles themselves, or might be, or are something that uses the tiles as a medium, as organs, as a distributed body.

You notice this gradually. A tile near your foot is white with a faint gray grain. You look away, look back, and the grain has shifted, rotated, migrated across the surface like clouds in timelapse. But there's no timelapse; only seconds have passed. You watch another tile for a full minute, staring directly at it, and it doesn't change. You glance away for an instant and when you look back, its shape is different—still fitting perfectly with its neighbors, still maintaining the pattern, but hexagonal instead of pentagonal, or possibly it was always hexagonal and you misremembered.

The tiles are aware. Not individually—each tile might be too simple for awareness—but collectively, the way a neural network is aware, the way a city is aware, emergent property of sufficient complexity and connection. The tiles know you're walking on them, know your weight and distribution and the pattern of your gait, know where you're going before you decide, or perhaps know where you're going and influence your decisions to match their knowledge, closing the causal loop, making prediction and control indistinguishable.

Sometimes you see tiles change deliberately. A tile ahead of you develops a slight convexity, curving upward, and you step on it and your ankle turns slightly, and you adjust your path, and the tiles behind you relax back to flatness, satisfied. They're herding you, gently, toward destinations you don't understand, through routes determined by criteria you can't perceive. Or perhaps they're just responding to your presence, adjusting for your weight like a mattress adjusts, and the apparent intentionality is pareidolia, pattern-recognition finding agency where none exists.

But then you see a tile ripple. Actual ripple, surface undulating in a wave that passes through it and into its neighbors, propagating outward, tiles flexing in sequence like skin over moving muscle. The ripple spreads for twenty tiles, thirty, then stops, and the tiles are solid again, flat again, as if nothing happened. Except you felt it through your feet: the passage of something beneath the surface, or through the surface, something for which the tiles are interface, gateway, membrane between here and somewhere else.

You start to see patterns in the tile-changes. When you approach a pillar, the surrounding tiles brighten slightly, or you think they do, or they do so subtly that thinking you see it makes it true. When you pass through a membrane, the tiles on the far side seem to acknowledge your transition, shifting their grains into temporary alignment, radiating patterns that last a few seconds before randomizing again.

The tiles remember your passage. You walk a circuit, returning to a spot you crossed an hour ago, and the tiles there are different—worn, though they show no physical wear, or arranged differently, though they fit exactly as they did before, or familiar in a way they weren't the first time, as if they've incorporated your previous crossing into their configuration, as if they're now tiles-that-you-walked-on rather than just tiles.

You begin to suspect the entire Tessellation is a single organism, or a single system, with the tiles as cells, the pillars as organs, the membranes as semipermeable barriers between subsystems. The objects on plinths might be thoughts, ideas made physical. The cabinets might be memory storage, drawers full of preserved experiences. The light from the ceiling might be the system's metabolism made visible, energy distribution, the glow of computation occurring on scales you can't directly observe.

If this is true, then you are inside something alive, walking through its body, and it is aware of you the way you're aware of food in your stomach or a splinter in your finger: localized foreign object, presence to be processed, dealt with, incorporated or expelled. And it's patient, moving on timescales where your entire presence here—hours, days—registers as a brief event, a momentary irritation or curiosity, acknowledged and then subsumed into the larger patterns of its existence.

---

There are others here. You find evidence: a chair that's warm, as if recently occupied. A membrane still oscillating from recent passage. A pattern in the tiles—footprints, maybe, or the tiles' memory of footprints—that aren't yours, that lead away and disappear into the distance.

Once, you hear someone coughing, distant, directionless, the sound coming from everywhere because everywhere is flat and reflects sound wrong. You call out. No response. You walk toward where you think the cough originated, but distance here is unreliable, and after a hundred tiles you're no closer, or you've passed them, or they were never there and the cough was the Tessellation itself, clearing its throat, making sounds through its pillar-organs for reasons you'll never understand.

You find a plinth with a fresh object: a cup of coffee, still hot, steam rising, condensation on the sides. You didn't see this plinth before—you're certain you passed this area and it wasn't here—but certainty is cheap in the Tessellation, worth exactly nothing. The coffee is real, aromatic, and deeply wrong. Coffee implies outside, implies agriculture and trade and morning routines, implies a world with causation that runs one direction, and that world is not this world, cannot reach this world, yet here is coffee anyway, mocking your understanding.

You don't drink it. After an hour—or what your chronometer claims is an hour, though time here is a courtesy the Tessellation extends rather than a law it obeys—the coffee is still hot, still steaming, still fresh, and you understand it will always be fresh, that freshness here is a state rather than a stage in decay, that the coffee is not aging so much as existing in a condition called fresh the way the tiles exist in a condition called flat.

Later, you find a note. Actual paper, creased from folding, lying on a tile like litter except there is no litter here, no disorder except the fundamental disorder of the impossible pattern. The note says: "Follow the dissonant pillars to the third membrane. Don't sit in the chairs." The handwriting is clear, human, recent, and you fold the note and put it in your pocket, and your pocket accepts it, and you have evidence now, proof you're not alone, though proof of what exactly remains uncertain.

You try to follow the instructions. Dissonant pillars—pillars whose hums clash rather than harmonize—are easy enough to identify, once you know to listen for dissonance. You walk from pillar to pillar, and the route is wrong, backtracking and looping, but the dissonance is a thread you can follow, and after a time—thirty minutes, three hours, impossible to tell—you reach a membrane, and it might be the third one or the first one or the same one you've been passing through repeatedly, and you don't sit in the chairs you pass, though they seem friendly enough, seem to invite rest, and the note said not to, and you're trusting the note-writer now, trusting a stranger in a place where strangeness is the fundamental substance.

On the far side of the membrane, the tiles are different. Not different differently—that would be too simple—but different in a way you can't articulate, a shift in some quality you don't have language for. The tiles here remember things you haven't done yet. The pillars hum with anticipation rather than presence. The membranes shimmer with questions rather than barriers.

You understand, suddenly and without knowing how, that you've entered a section of the Tessellation that exists slightly ahead of now, a chamber where causation runs a few minutes fast, and the note-writer knew this, guided you here, wanted you to experience the dis-ease of living in your own near-future, of seeing the tiles already adjusted for steps you haven't taken, of hearing your own footfalls echo before your foot descends.

You stay for a few minutes—or your future self stays for a few minutes, and you're living their memory of it—and then you push back through the membrane, returning to standard temporal flow, and the relief is profound, and you understand the note-writer's warning about chairs because sitting in that chamber would mean sitting before you sat, would create loops and paradoxes, would trap you in recursive causation, living and reliving the same sitting from different angles of time.

You look for more notes. You find none, or you find several, or you find the same note repeatedly in different states of wear, and you can't tell if multiple people are leaving notes or if one person is leaving many notes or if you're finding the same note as it exists in different times, the paper aging non-linearly, experiencing its decay in an order that doesn't match its writing.

---

You are the patient observer, the cautious witness, the one who documents.

You've been here—how long? Days, certainly. Weeks, possibly. Your chronometer gave up after the first few hours, its circuits unable to maintain coherent timekeeping in a place where time is negotiable. You sleep when you're tired, on tiles that are slightly softer than tiles should be, as if the Tessellation adjusts for your needs, or as if your need for softness makes the tiles soft, or as if there's no difference between those two things here.

You eat from your supplies, rations you brought from outside, from the real world, from the place with consistent geometry and shadows. The food tastes increasingly strange, not spoiled but wrong, as if it's forgetting how to be food, as if the Tessellation's logic is seeping into it, making it more concept than substance. You eat anyway, because eating is what you remember doing, what you understand as necessary, even as necessity becomes questionable.

You've started marking your trail. You have chalk, and you draw arrows on tiles, indicating direction, creating a path you can follow back. Except the arrows change. You draw an arrow pointing north (insofar as north exists here, insofar as direction means anything), and when you return the arrow points east, or the tile it's on has rotated, or the arrow has migrated to a different tile, or you drew it pointing east originally and remember it wrong.

You try numbering tiles, writing "1" on the tile where you start, "2" on the next, "3" on the next. You get to 50 before you look back and see that tile 1 now says "∞" and tile 2 says "-3" and tile 3 has your number but in a script you don't recognize, and you give up on marking trails, accept that the Tessellation rewrites your attempts at navigation, that finding your way back is not something you can guarantee, that back might not exist as a place so much as as a hope.

You document in a notebook, writing descriptions of what you see, mapping the pillar locations and membrane positions. Your notes start precise: "Pillar at coordinates approximating 45° from starting position, distance ~200 tiles." They become vague: "Pillar over there-ish, you know the one." They become strange: "The pillar that hums the color of forgotten Tuesdays." They become incomprehensible: "∴ pillar ∴."

The Tessellation is affecting you, or you're adapting to it, or there's no difference. You think in geometries now, in angles and tessellations and impossible figures. You dream in tiles, in patterns that try to be regular and fail beautifully. You wake unsure if you slept or if sleeping is something you remember doing, something from outside, something that belongs to the world with shadows.

You're lonely, except loneliness requires a self to be lonely, requires boundaries between you and not-you, and the boundaries are getting fuzzy. Sometimes you're certain you're you, distinct individual, separate from the Tessellation. Sometimes you're a pattern the Tessellation is making, a perturbation in its surface, a thought it's thinking in the language of human-shaped disturbances. Sometimes you're both, or neither, or the question itself stops making sense.

You talk to yourself, to hear human voice, to maintain the habit of language. You narrate your observations: "Walking now, toward the pillar cluster in the pale region. Tiles here are more triangular, or I'm more sensitive to triangularity, or triangularity is more itself today." You answer yourself: "Yes, triangularity is definitely more itself, good observation." You argue with yourself: "That's absurd, how can a property be more itself?" You reconcile: "Everything here is more itself than things outside are themselves, because here selfness is the only kind of being permitted."

You're going slightly mad, or you're adapting successfully, or madness and adaptation are the same thing at sufficient distance from normal reality.

---

You discover chambers that are differentiated. Not by physical barriers—the membranes are suggestions rather than walls—but by ambience, by theme, by the way the wrongness manifests.

In one chamber, the tiles are trying very hard to be regular. They're failing, but their failure is different from the general failure elsewhere, more desperate, more sincere. They've organized into a grid, almost perfect, square tiles in square arrangement, and the pattern holds for dozens of tiles before it must accommodate a pillar or a membrane and the regularity shatters into apologies and compromises and promises to do better. The chamber feels sad, or you feel sad in it, or sadness is a property of the space like temperature or light level, and you leave quickly, unnerved by the pathos of tiles trying to conform.

In another chamber, the tiles are chaos-adjacent, no two the same shape, edges meeting in angles that make your eyes water, colors—yes, colors here, breaking the monochrome monotony—that don't exist in human-visible spectrum, that you're seeing anyway through mechanisms you didn't know your eyes possessed. This chamber feels joyful, or manic, or liberated, as if these tiles succeeded where the sad tiles failed, achieved freedom from pattern, earned the right to be randomly themselves. You stay longer here, energized by the chaos, but eventually it exhausts you, too much variation, too much novelty, and you retreat to the regular wrongness of the standard pattern.

A third chamber contains only pillars, hundreds of them, forest-dense, each one humming its own frequency, and the combined sound is overwhelming, beautiful, a chord that contains all music or negates all music or does both simultaneously. You can barely walk through without weeping, without laughing, without feeling emotions too complex for single words, emotions that require geometric notation to describe, that make sense only to someone fluent in the language of impossible patterns.

A fourth chamber is empty. Completely empty. No pillars, no plinths, no membranes, no objects. Just tiles, extending in all directions, perfectly flat, perfectly regular—no, perfectly wrong in the standard way, the familiar wrongness, the comfortable impossibility. The emptiness is terrifying, more frightening than any of the furnished chambers, because emptiness here is a choice, is intentional, is the Tessellation deciding that nothing should exist in this space, that absence is the correct state, and you run through it, unwilling to be caught in its negation, unwilling to become part of what's absent.

And you find a chamber that might be the heart, or might be an exit, or might be something else entirely.

It's marked by membranes on all sides, six of them forming a hexagonal boundary, each membrane marked with complex symbols, warnings or invitations or simple descriptions you can't parse. The tiles inside are dark, black or nearly black, and they're still, no pattern-shifting, no subtle adjustments, as if they've settled into final form, as if they've achieved whatever tiles aspire to achieve and now rest in completion.

In the center: a plinth, larger than the others, ten tiles on each side instead of ten tiles total. On the plinth: an object, but you can't focus on it, can't keep your eyes steady, can't make your brain accept what you're seeing. It's three-dimensional, clearly, but has too many dimensions, or too few, or the right number but arranged wrong. It's geometric but organic, mechanical but alive, simple but infinitely complex, and looking at it feels like understanding and forgetting simultaneously, like learning a truth too large for your skull, like touching enlightenment and having it recoil from your unclean human fingers.

You understand, without understanding how you understand, that the object is important. Is the reason for the Tessellation, or the Tessellation's purpose made manifest, or the Tessellation's self-portrait, or the key to leaving, or the mechanism that traps you further. You understand that touching it would change something fundamental, would complete a circuit, would answer a question you didn't know you were asking.

You don't touch it. Not yet. Maybe not ever. You're not ready, or you're too ready, or readiness is irrelevant, and the object waits with infinite patience, with tile-patience, with the patience of things that exist outside time and therefore have all of time to wait in.

You back out of the chamber, through a membrane that resists more than others, that seems to say "Are you sure?" through its electric-soap-bubble touch, and you are sure, or sure enough, and you're outside again, in the familiar impossible spaces, and you can breathe again, though you're not sure when you stopped breathing.

---

The Tessellation extends forever, or for miles, or for exactly as far as it needs to extend to contain what it contains, which might be you, might be only you, might be everything that exists now that you're here, because outside is a place you remember but can't confirm, a concept called "not-Tessellation" that might be as fictional as shadows, as outdated as consistent angles.

You keep walking. The tiles accept your steps. The pillars hum their patient harmonies and dissonances. The membranes shimmer with their questionable questions. The objects on plinths wait for interactions you might never provide. The chairs sit empty, grateful you heeded the warning, or disappointed you didn't sit, or indifferent because chairs don't have feelings, except here they might, here everything might be aware in ways you can't detect, might be watching with tile-eyes and pillar-ears and membrane-touch, might be judging you or protecting you or studying you or all of those simultaneously.

You are the walker, the witness, the temporary perturbation in an eternal pattern, the human-shaped thought the Tessellation is thinking, the question it asked itself when it generated you or when you entered or when those two events became the same event.

The Tessellation continues around you, above you, possibly through you, patient and wrong and vast and careful, its tiles adjusting and remembering and forgetting and being exactly what they are, which is tiles in a pattern that knows itself through infinite failed iterations, through perfect imperfection, through the dense meaning of impossible shapes meeting in impossible ways and somehow, despite everything, holding together, making space, making place, making room for you to walk through the patient geometric forever, alone together with the tiles, with the pattern, with the wrongness that becomes rightness through sheer persistent insistence on being itself.

The Tessellation continues.

It has patterns.

You are one of them.
