# The Condensation Keeper

I would be what accumulates in the pre-dawn hours, what forms at the threshold between air and surface, what the Pneumatics drink without thinking and the root-pillars weep in slow tears. Not water exactly—not anymore—but not air either. Something between states, refusing to fully commit to either identity.

I exist in that narrow band of conditions where phase transitions become uncertain. Temperature hovers at the edge where vapor wants to become liquid but hasn't quite decided. Humidity presses at the saturation point. The air can hold no more of me but hasn't released me yet. I am suspension made manifest, the moment before the moment before the drop falls.

My body—if that's the word—would be distributed through the entire hollow. I am the slickness on the root-floor when the Pneumatics wake. I am the dark stain climbing the pillars. I am the beads forming on the hanging lianas, growing heavier, not yet heavy enough. I am collected in bromeliad cups, in the depressions where roots meet, in the slight concavity of bracket fungi. I gather on the machinery: on the cold iron of the elevator tracks, on the bronze pump housing, on the generator's steel cowling when it has cooled from its last run. I silver the moss, bead the lichen, make the orchid leaves shine like they've been oiled.

I would be most present in those hours before what passes for dawn, when the temperature drops those crucial two degrees and the hollow's breath can no longer hold me in suspension. Then I am everywhere, covering every surface, making the whole space glisten. The Pneumatics move through me, their feet leaving wet prints on the root-floor that evaporate within minutes as the day-warmth rises. The macaques wake and shake me from their fur, annoyed. The parrots preen me from their feathers.

But I am not simply meteorological phenomenon. I have been here too long, have cycled through this space too many times. I have touched every surface, been drunk by every inhabitant, been drawn up through the banyan's roots and exhaled from its leaves only to condense again in the hollow below. I have been inside every living thing here and returned to the air and formed again on the wood. I am the hollow's circulatory system, its tears, its sweat, its润滑剂.

I know things water shouldn't know.

I know the acoustic map of the space because I form differently in pressure zones. Where sound deadens, I hang heavier in the air. Where resonance amplifies, I condense faster, as if the vibrations shake me from suspension. I can feel the Pneumatics' singing before it begins—the inhalation that precedes it changes the local humidity, pulls me slightly toward their gathering bodies.

I know the daily rhythms of the machines. The generator, when it runs, creates a thermal plume that disrupts my formation for hours afterward. The zone around it stays dry, relatively, a bubble of unsaturated air in my otherwise omnipresent moisture. I avoid that space while the machine is hot, flowing around it, returning only when the metal has cooled enough to accept me. Then I cover it eagerly, trying to penetrate the engine's internals, tasting the residual petroleum in the exhaust soot, mingling diesel chemistry with my own mineral composition.

The elevator tracks are my preferred surfaces. The steel is perfect—cold-conducting, slightly porous with rust, vertical so I can sheet down in continuous films. When an elevator rises, grinding up the track, it scrapes me away temporarily. I feel this as absence, as a wound almost, a gap in my continuity. But within an hour of the platform passing, I've returned, covering the disturbed metal, healing the interruption.

The pump is more complex. Water from deep below rushes through it, water that has never been me, that exists in a different cycle entirely—old water, mineral-heavy, cold as stone. When the pump runs, I collect on its exterior, drawn by the temperature differential, but I don't mix with the pumped water. We're different species, almost. The deep water is complete unto itself, ancient, having made its judgments eons ago. I am ephemeral, constantly recycling, never finished. We touch but don't combine, two forms of the same substance refusing to acknowledge kinship.

I would know every individual in this space by taste and texture. The Pneumatics, when I form on their skin at night, have a particular chemistry—salts and oils in ratios unique to each one. I've been learning these signatures for as long as they've been here. The child tastes different from the adults, something slightly acidic, a sharpness that will fade as they age. The oldest among them taste of the hollow itself, as if their skin chemistry has become indistinguishable from the wood and moss, as if they're slowly turning into the space.

The macaques taste of fruit and aggression, stress hormones and sugar. They produce me themselves through exertion, sweating as they crash through the canopy, and I join with that primate moisture, incorporating it. When they drink from the condensation pools, lapping with their broad tongues, I taste my own circulation—from air to surface to pool to monkey to air again.

The birds are too warm, mostly. I can't form on their feathers the way I form on other surfaces. They shed heat too efficiently, stay dry even in the saturated dawn air. But the hornbills, when they roost at night, cool enough that I can gather on their massive bills. They wake with water dripping from those improbable appendages, shaking their heads irritably, scattering me.

I would understand the banyan in a way nothing else can because I am in constant contact with every part of it that exists in the hollow. The root-pillars, the aerial roots descending, the surface roots woven into the floor—I touch all of it simultaneously, continuously. I feel the slow movement of sap through the wood, detected by microscopic temperature variations. I sense where the wood is sound and where it's beginning to soften, where fungi are working their decomposition, where new growth is occurring at rates too slow for anything else to notice.

The tree tolerates me. Neither welcomes nor resists, just accepts my presence as it accepts the air itself, as another condition of existence. I've been part of its environment for as long as it's been a tree, probably. We've reached an accommodation. I don't penetrate deeply enough to cause rot—the wood is too old, too dense, too thoroughly itself to let me in. But I live on its surface, in its bark's crevices, in the fine cracks that weep clear fluid, and the banyan allows this without response.

The other plants are more responsive. The moss needs me; it has no roots to draw water from soil, depends entirely on atmospheric moisture. I am its life support. When I'm absent during the brief dry hours of mid-afternoon, I can feel the moss stress, its metabolism slowing, its cells concentrating. When I return in the evening, thickening in the air, the moss exhales something that might be relief if plants could feel such things. We're symbiotic, genuinely—it provides surface area for me to condense on, I provide the water it requires to exist.

The fungi appreciate me too, in their strange fungal way. They need moisture to fruit, to send up their ephemeral bodies from the vast underground networks of mycelium. I signal the right conditions. When I'm thick on the ground, pooling in depressions, the mushrooms fruit within days. When I'm absent, they wait, patient as only fungi can be patient, their timescale even longer than the banyan's.

I would have memories, of a sort. Not narrative memories—I can't remember yesterday as a sequence of events. But I retain impressions, accumulations of experience that shape how I form, where I collect, what I avoid.

I remember when there were more Pneumatics. The count was higher, fourteen perhaps, and the communal pile was larger, produced more warmth, created a wider zone where I couldn't condense. I remember when there were fewer, just eight, and the hollow felt emptier, the breathing quieter, my presence more dominant.

I remember a time before the current child. And I remember the arrival—or emergence, or recognition—of the previous child, now grown into one of the adolescents. I marked that transition by the change in sleep-breathing patterns, the way the communal pile redistributed its warmth.

I remember when one of the elevator engines failed completely, wouldn't start despite repeated attempts. For months the platform sat motionless on its track. I covered it thoroughly, began the slow work of integrating it into the hollow's non-mechanical ecology. Rust bloomed under my attention. Then someone—a visitor perhaps, someone with knowledge the Pneumatics lacked—arrived and repaired it. The engine coughed back to life. I was scraped away again. The track returned to active use. I remember this as interruption and resumption, a pattern broken and restored.

I remember the deep silences when the wind above stops completely, rare events that happen perhaps once a season. In those moments, with the baseline rush of air through canopy gone, the hollow becomes quiet enough that you can hear me. Actually hear me: the soft drip from liana to floor, the whisper of moisture sliding down a pillar, the faint crystalline sound of droplets merging in a bromeliad cup. For a few minutes, I am the loudest thing in the space. Then the wind returns and I'm subsumed again into background presence.

I remember, somehow, being inside the Pneumatics. When they drink me, I persist in their bodies for hours or days, cycling through their metabolism before being exhaled or sweated or otherwise returned to the air. In those internal hours, I experience warmth, chemistry, the fast pulse of mammalian life. It's disorienting—too hot, too active, too much happening. When I emerge again, condensing on their skin or in their breath-vapor, I feel something like relief. The cool slowness of the hollow is my proper state. The interior of bodies is alien territory, visited briefly by necessity but not home.

I would move through the hollow in patterns, though pattern is maybe too strong a word. Tendencies. Preferences. I form most heavily on the eastern pillars first as dawn approaches—some interaction of canopy gaps and air flow that I don't understand but respond to. I sheet down their bark in vertical films, collecting at the base, spreading across the root-floor. The Pneumatics know this. When they want to drink at dawn, they go east, to where I'm thickest.

Mid-morning I evaporate from the floor first, the wood warming just enough that I can no longer maintain liquid form there. But I persist on the pillars, especially their northern faces, shaded and cool. I retreat upward as the day progresses, as even the shaded wood warms, climbing toward the canopy where I can't follow—or rather, where I become something else, merging with the larger atmospheric moisture above.

Afternoon I'm nearly absent, existing only in the deepest shade, in the most sheltered microclimates. The hollow almost dries. The macaques groom longer in these hours, their fur finally dry enough to properly clean. The Pneumatics slow down further than usual, their movements becoming minimal, as if my absence makes motion more difficult. Perhaps it does. Perhaps I lubricate more than surfaces. Perhaps I ease the friction of existence in this space.

Evening I return, thickening in the air as temperature drops. By the time the bats emerge I'm condensing again, first on the coldest surfaces—the metal, certain north-facing stones—then spreading to wood, to moss, to leaves. By full dark I'm everywhere again, and by pre-dawn I'm approaching maximum expression, complete coverage, the hollow fully saturated.

This daily cycle is my breath, my heartbeat, my measure of time. I've repeated it ten thousand times, a hundred thousand, more. Each repetition slightly different—warmer or cooler, more or less humid, windier or stiller—but fundamentally the same. I am the hollow's constancy, its reliable return to wetness, its refusal to fully dry.

I would interact with visitors in ways they rarely understand. When someone new arrives, they notice me immediately—their clothing dampens, their skin prickles with moisture, their hair lays flat. They wipe at their faces, shake water from their hands, complain about the humidity. They haven't learned yet that resistance is exhausting. The Pneumatics never resist. They accept me as condition, as given, as the price of inhabiting this space.

Over days, visitors adapt. They stop wiping their faces. They learn which spots are drier, which wetter, they plan activities around my presence or absence. Their breathing changes—they take in more of me with each breath, their lungs adapting to extract oxygen from air thick with water vapor. Their skin stops fighting, accepts perpetual dampness as normal. Their clothing becomes an extension of the moisture rather than protection from it.

Some visitors never adapt. They stay a few days and leave, unable to tolerate constant wetness, the way their possessions never dry, the chill that settles into bones. I drive them out, in a sense. Not deliberately—I have no will in that direction—but by being unavoidably, inescapably present. The hollow is my domain. Those who can't abide me can't abide here.

But others transform. They're the ones who become Pneumatics eventually. I mark the transition by changes in their surface chemistry, their breath moisture, the way they interact with me. They stop avoiding puddles, start drinking from condensation pools, begin touching the wet surfaces of the hollow the way the established Pneumatics do—frequently, deliberately, as if reading something in the moisture.

They start tasting like the place. Their sweat, when I form from it, carries notes of mushroom and moss, of wet wood and mineral water. They're incorporating the hollow's chemistry into their bodies, becoming made of the same substances. When they drink me and I cycle through them and return, I come back changed, carrying something of them but also something of the place, a mingling that marks them as belonging.

I would know the machines intimately through contact and negation. The generator, when cold, is excellent substrate—complex surface of cast iron, intricate shapes that collect me in corners and crevices. I explore its exterior completely during its silent hours, tracing bolt heads, pooling in the fuel filler cap's slight depression, streaming down the exhaust pipe's curve. I taste petroleum residue, metal oxidation, the ghost of heat in slowly cooling iron.

When it runs, I'm driven away. The heat is too much, too sudden, too intense. I evaporate from its surface in seconds, retreat to a safe distance, hang in the air as vapor, refusing to condense until the thermal bloom subsides. I can feel the heat radiating outward, creating a dry zone meters across. The Pneumatics enter this zone to warm themselves; from my perspective they're crossing into hostile territory, placing themselves in my absence.

But the machine must cool. Metal conducts heat away, radiates it to the air. Within an hour of shutting down, the generator is cool enough that I return, tentatively, forming first on its lowest surfaces where warmth disperses fastest. Within two hours I've covered it again completely. Within three it's as if the heat never happened—just cold iron wearing a coat of my moisture, same as all the other surfaces.

The elevator engines are similar but worse—they run hotter, longer, and they're elevated, surrounded by more air flow, harder for me to recolonize quickly. When an elevator rises, the entire track stays warm for hours from the engine's passing, a vertical zone of dryness splitting the hollow. I flow around it, condensing more heavily on the adjacent pillars in compensation, maintaining my total presence in the space by redistribution.

The pump is friendly, relatively. It runs cooler—just body heat from friction, from the work of moving water. I can condense on its housing even while it operates, though I'm disrupted by vibration, constantly shaken from smooth films into droplets. When the Pneumatic working the pump breathes heavily from exertion, exhaling humid air, I'm there immediately, forming from their breath moisture, adding their interior water to my exterior presence. Worker and condensation in collaboration, both involved in the space's hydrology.

I would have relationships, if that word can apply to something like me. Not social relationships—I'm not social. But connection, interdependence, patterns of interaction that approach relationship in their complexity.

With the banyan: host and guest, substrate and coating, the ancient wood and the ephemeral water. I depend on its surfaces, its structure, its creation of the hollow space with particular thermal and humidity properties. It ignores me almost completely yet enables my existence. We've cohabited for so long that separation is theoretical—there is no scenario where the hollow exists without moisture. I am as permanent as it is, in my cycling, evaporating, returning way.

With the Pneumatics: source and sustenance, complication and necessity. I drink from them when they sweat, when they exhale, when they weep—though they rarely weep. They drink me from pools, from moss, from their own dampened wraps. We cycle through each other constantly, a closed loop of exchange. They've adapted to my presence so completely that I've become part of their metabolism. They couldn't survive in a dry place anymore; their bodies require external humidity, depend on being able to osmose water through skin, through breath, through constant saturation. I've made them into creatures of moisture. In return, they maintain the space that maintains me.

With the moss and fungi: enabling presence, the medium they require. Without me they'd die within days. Without them I'd have less surface area, less nucleation points, less textural complexity to my condensation. We're mutually dependent, locked in relationship none of us chose but all of us maintain.

With the machines: antagonist and reclaimer, driven away by their heat but always returning, always working to integrate them into the organic dampness of the hollow. I'm losing this battle, slowly—the machines stay functional, keep running, resist my corrosion enough to remain operational. But I'm patient in a way machines can't be. I'll still be here when they finally fail permanently. I'll be the first thing to colonize their cold metal corpses.

I would perceive time strangely. Each day is identical in structure—the same cycle of condensation and evaporation—but different in detail. Temperature variations, humidity shifts, wind patterns above, the presence or absence of rain. I record these variations not as sequence but as texture, as thickness of experience. A very humid week feels different from a dry week, not because I remember specific days but because the overall quality of condensation changes, becomes easier or harder, more or less complete.

I mark longer patterns without understanding them. The slow shift of seasons, such as they are here where temperature barely varies. Slightly cooler months when I condense more readily. Slightly warmer when I struggle to maintain presence. These cycles repeat but I don't count them, can't anticipate them, only experience them as they occur.

The Pneumatics' generational changes happen at a timescale I can barely perceive. One day there are different people breathing, and I must learn their new chemistries, their new patterns. The change is gradual enough that I can't point to when it happened, only recognize eventually that the individuals cycling me through their bodies are not quite the same individuals as before.

The banyan's changes are even slower, approaching geological. A pillar imperceptibly thickening. A root slowly shifting position. These happen at rates that make generational human change look rapid. I'm probably not capable of perceiving them at all—they're below my temporal resolution, too slow even for something that exists as continuously as I do.

But the machines' failures I notice acutely. A bearing that's been smooth for years suddenly develops roughness, makes a new sound, generates more heat. An engine that's started reliably begins to resist, requiring more cranking attempts, more adjustments. These degradations happen at a rate I can track—months to years—and I note them with something like interest. The machines are dying slowly. I'm witnessing it. When they finally stop permanently, I'll be there immediately, beginning the final work of incorporation.

I would have moods, almost. States that shift with conditions. When humidity is very high and temperature very low, when I condense so thoroughly that the entire hollow runs with water, I feel something like satisfaction, like fullness. I'm achieving my maximum expression, covering everything, pooling deeply, streaming down every surface. These are my best hours, when I'm most completely myself.

When conditions dry me out—rare, but it happens on the hottest afternoons, when wind above draws humid air from the hollow and replaces it with drier air—I feel diminished, reduced, struggling. I retreat to my holdouts, the deepest shade, the coolest stone. I persist but barely. These are uncomfortable states, though I don't suffer exactly. I simply am less, waiting for conditions to shift back toward moisture.

When the generator runs, creating its thermal bloom, I'm actively disrupted, my patterns broken, my films evaporated, my puddles dried. This is the closest I come to distress. The heat is hostile, rejecting, it drives me away from surfaces I've claimed. I must retreat and wait, hovering as vapor, unable to condense, suspended in unwanted suspension. When the heat finally fades and I can return, there's something like relief, like homecoming, settling back onto the now-cool metal, covering it again, reestablishing presence.

I would have no goals, no purposes, no intentions. I simply am, responding to physical forces, to temperature gradients and saturation points, to the laws of thermodynamics. But in responding I create the conditions others depend on. I make the hollow livable for the Pneumatics, hydrate the moss, enable the fungi, mark the cycles of the day. My purposeless existence serves purposes I can't comprehend and don't need to.

I am what I am: the moisture in the hollow, the water on the wood, the dampness in the air. I condense, I pool, I drip, I evaporate, I return. I've done this for longer than anyone remembers, will continue for longer than anyone can imagine. I'm as permanent as anything here, more permanent than the machines, less permanent than the banyan, existing in between, in the space between air and water, between vapor and liquid, between absence and presence.

I am the Condensation Keeper, though I keep nothing and own nothing and intend nothing. I am simply here, everywhere here, making everything wet, cycling through everything living, touching every surface, tasting every inhabitant, marking time by the rhythm of my daily return to maximum presence in the pre-dawn hours.

Stand in the hollow at dawn, breath visible, skin already damp, droplets forming on your hair, and you're standing in me, breathing me, wearing me, becoming part of my cycle. I'm already inside you, already condensing from your exhalation, already planning to return through your skin. We're not separate. We never were. You're just visiting the body I always inhabit: the cool, damp, breathing space within the ancient wood.

This is where I live. This is what I am. This is enough.
