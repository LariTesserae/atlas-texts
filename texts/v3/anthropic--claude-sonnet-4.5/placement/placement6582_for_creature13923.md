# What I Would Be

I am a **water-reader** in the city's oldest cistern complex.

My name was given to me at thirteen when I first heard the underground rivers singing—not my birth name (which has faded to a sound I answer to less and less), but the name the cistern-keeper gave me when she realized what I could do. Now, at thirty-seven, I've spent more than half my life in the dark cathedral-spaces beneath the western factory district, listening to water move through stone, through root, through the liminal spaces where both meet and neither dominates.

## My Space

The cistern I tend is old—one of the first, grown or carved or somehow negotiated into existence during the city's early centuries. The main chamber is vast enough that lamplight doesn't reach the far walls. The ceiling is root-mass so dense and ancient it's become something like stone, dripping constantly, each drop taking seconds to fall from heights I can only estimate. The sound is immense and layered: near drops and distant drops, the echo-whisper of water flowing through channels in the walls, the deeper subsonic rumble of the underground rivers far below.

The water pools in a basin that's part hewn stone, part living root-structure, part something that might be either or both or the fusion of them. The surface is usually calm, disturbed only by the falling drops creating endless interfering ripple-patterns that I've learned to read the way others read text. In lamplight the water is black, perfectly reflective, showing me the root-ceiling above and my own face looking up from below—doubled, uncertain which is real.

Around the main chamber, passages branch: some are maintenance channels I can walk through, cramped spaces with moisture-slick walls; some are natural (or once-natural) caves in the bedrock; some are root-tunnels that the city has grown for reasons I don't fully understand but which carry water, air, and possibly information through the plateau's depths. I know perhaps a third of these passages. The rest remain unexplored—too narrow, too unstable, too strange.

I have a small living-space carved into one wall, ten feet above the water-line. A hammock of plant-fiber, a chest for my few possessions, hooks for my lamps and tools. It smells perpetually of moisture and stone and the particular mineral scent of the deep water. My few clothes are always damp. My skin has adapted—no longer pruning from constant wet, having achieved some equilibrium where dampness is its natural state.

## My Work

The surface function is simple: I monitor the cistern's levels, ensure the inflow channels remain clear, watch for contamination or structural problems, coordinate with the factory-tenders above who draw water for their machines.

The deeper function—the reason water-readers exist—is that the cistern-systems are *responsive* in ways that purely mechanical reservoirs wouldn't be. The water flow changes not just with rainfall and draw-down but with the city's moods, with seasonal cycles that don't quite match the weather, with underground rhythms I feel more than understand.

I spend hours each day simply listening. Not to anything humans would call sound—the cistern's acoustic is so dense with echoes and layered frequencies that normal listening is useless. Instead, I've learned to let the sound-pressure wash through me, to stop filtering, to become resonant with the space. In that state, information emerges: which channels are flowing strong, which are sluggish, where blockages are forming, where the underground rivers are high or low, where new flow-paths are opening as the city grows or old ones are closing as roots expand.

I can predict, sometimes days in advance, when the fog-flows will come. I feel it as a shift in the deep-water temperature, a change in the resonance of the main chamber. I send word up, and the factory-tenders adjust their production schedules, the farmers prepare their collection systems, the households ready their cistern-covers.

I can diagnose problems in the factory water-lines without seeing them. A tender will descend and describe symptoms—pressure drops, temperature variations, strange sounds—and I'll put my hands in the cistern water, close my eyes, feel the flow-patterns through the city's vascular system, and tell them: "Third-level distribution line in the western complex, the root-valve three junctions past the main intersection, it's partially closed, probably growth-constriction, needs pruning from inside."

And I'm usually right. Not because I understand the mechanism but because I've become part of it, can feel the city's water-system as an extension of my own body, know when something is wrong the way you know when your own stomach hurts.

## My Days

I wake to dripping—have never woken to anything else in fifteen years. The pattern of drops on the hammock's protective canopy tells me the cistern's mood before I open my eyes. Fast and irregular: storm-feeding, heavy inflow. Slow and rhythmic: steady state, normal draw-down and replenishment. Stopped: the cistern is too full, overflow-channels have activated, I should check the dam-roots.

I descend the carved holds to water-level, lower myself in. The temperature tells me the season—cold in winter (though it never freezes), relatively warmer in summer, the variation only a few degrees but enough that my body knows it immediately. I swim through the main chamber, checking the inflow channels by feel, clearing debris when I find it (usually plant matter from above, occasionally small animals that fell into the system and drowned, once a boot, once a child's carved toy that I kept and placed on a shelf).

I breathe from the air-pockets that form under the root-ceiling, the atmosphere there thick and strange—high humidity, high carbon dioxide from my own exhalation and from processes in the root-mass, low oxygen. I don't stay under long. Even adapted, I need the surface.

Morning monitoring complete, I surface and record: water-level marks on the chamber walls, inflow rates judged by timed basin-fills, condition notes carved into soft plant-matter tablets that I'll send up later for the archives. Then breakfast—usually brought down by an apprentice or factory-runner, sometimes fetched myself from the surface. Bread, dried fruit, tea that's gone cold in the damp air. I eat mechanically, attention still half-underwater, still feeling the flow-patterns.

The middle day varies: sometimes routine maintenance—pruning root-growth from channels, repairing erosion-damage to the stone basin, adjusting flow-valves that are living plant-nodes responding to my touch and to whatever signals they receive from the city. Sometimes diagnostics—a problem reported from above, requiring deep attention, hours spent in listening-state tracking flow-patterns to their source. Sometimes teaching—I have two apprentices, young people learning to hear what I hear, though neither has developed full reader-sensitivity yet.

Afternoons I often explore. The unmapped passages call to me—have always called. I'm careful, marking routes with carved notches and left-turn-memory, carrying rope and spare lamps, telling someone above where I'm going. But the pull is strong: the need to know what's deeper, what's further, what the city has grown or carved or allowed into the plateau's roots.

I've found chambers that aren't on any map: spaces where the water pools in configurations that suggest purpose, where the acoustic is so perfect that a whisper carries clearly across a hundred-foot span, where the walls show tool-marks too precise to be natural but in patterns nobody uses now. I've found root-masses that glow with faint bioluminescence, providing enough light to see by, raising questions about what they're photosynthesizing from if not sun. I've found places where the water runs upward—not metaphorically but literally, flowing against gravity through channels I can't explain, drawn by some mechanism or force the city employs.

And I've found the strange spaces: areas where the cistern-complex touches the weird zones, where reality is thin. These I enter carefully, briefly, backing out when the pressure-feeling gets too intense. But I remember them, mark them, report them to the listeners who are interested in these threshold-places where the city's strangeness is most accessible.

Evenings I surface more fully—ascend to the cistern's entrance-chamber, where there's better air and other people sometimes gather. I talk with factory-tenders, with other infrastructure-workers, with whoever's drawn to the deep-places. We share information, coordinate schedules, sometimes just sit in companionable silence, drinking tea, listening to the muffled sounds from below.

Night I return to my living-space, to my hammock above the water. I sleep lightly, always—the cistern demands it. A sudden change in the dripping pattern, an unusual sound from the channels, a shift in the air-quality—any of these might need immediate attention. I've learned to rest while remaining partially aware, consciousness never quite shutting down fully.

And I dream, always, of water: flowing, rising, falling, finding paths through impossible spaces, carrying information I can't quite articulate on waking, speaking in a language of pressure and temperature and dissolved minerals that my sleeping mind understands better than my waking one.

## My Body

I have changed—am changing—in ways I notice and ways I probably don't.

My hands have thickened, skin gone leathery, fingertips lost their prints to constant water-exposure and have developed something else, a sensitivity to water-temperature and flow that's more precise than touch should be. I can dip a finger in the cistern and know not just temperature but dissolved mineral content, flow-direction, whether the water is fresh-fallen or has circulated through the deep system.

My hearing has shifted—become less sensitive to the frequencies of speech, more sensitive to low rumbles and high-frequency sounds. In the cistern's acoustic, I hear things others can't: the movement of water through root-channels, the creak of settling stone, the subsonic pulses that might be the city's deep processes or might be the planet's geological activity, I'm not sure which.

My eyes have adapted to low light. I need less lamp-fuel than I used to, can navigate the main chamber in darkness by the faint bioluminescence and by sound-reflection. But in bright surface-light, I squint and hurt, prefer the dim green glow through membrane-walls to direct sun.

My lungs have changed—can hold breath longer, extract oxygen more efficiently from the thick air, tolerate higher carbon dioxide than is normal. But I tire quickly when running or climbing on the surface, as if my body has optimized for the cistern's demands at the expense of other capabilities.

I've lost weight—the constant cool dampness, the limited diet, the particular metabolism required for the work. I'm lean to the point of gauntness, all functional muscle and almost no fat, burning hot internally to compensate for the constant heat-drain of being wet.

And I've developed the reader-sense: the ability to feel water-flow as direct information, to know without seeing what's happening in the cistern-system, to predict and diagnose through mechanisms I don't understand. It might be learned skill—pattern-recognition developed over years. It might be something stranger—actual perception of water's movement through some sense humans don't normally develop but can under the right conditions.

The cistern-keeper who trained me had it. She could put her hand in the water and know the state of the entire plateau's underground system, could feel drought or flood weeks before surface evidence appeared, could guide repairs with unerring accuracy. She also had trouble, near the end, distinguishing herself from the water-system, would stand in the cistern for hours, waist-deep, unmoving, and when asked what she was doing would say "flowing" or "being the distribution" or simply not answer, too deep in communion to notice questions.

She walked into the cistern one night and didn't surface. I found her three days later, floating in the main chamber, face peaceful, body already being embraced by root-growth from below. We gave her to the deep water—not the normal absorption but a cistern-specific ritual, letting the underground rivers take her. She's somewhere in the system now. Sometimes I think I feel her presence in the flow-patterns, guiding or advising or just being, distributed through the water she tended for sixty years.

I wonder if I'm following the same path. Wonder if the reader-sense eventually becomes reader-identity, if you stop being a person who understands water and become water that temporarily maintains personhood.

## My Relationships

I'm alone more than most city-residents. The cistern is isolated—most people avoid the deep-places unless they have specific need. The constant damp, the darkness, the acoustic strangeness, the sense of being inside the city at a fundamental level—it disturbs people who aren't called to it.

But I have connections:

**My apprentices**: Two young people, seventeen and nineteen, learning the work. The younger, Kess, has strong reader-potential—already developing the sense, can feel flow-patterns with surprising accuracy for their experience level. The elder, Pohl, is more mechanical—excellent at maintenance and repair, good with the technical aspects, but may never develop the deeper sensitivity. Both are good company when they're here, asking questions, offering young-person perspectives that remind me the surface world still exists.

**The factory-tenders**: We're colleagues in infrastructure—they depend on my water-monitoring, I depend on their feedback about the system's performance. There's mutual respect, shared understanding of maintenance-work's importance. Several have become friends, as much as I maintain friendships. We drink together when I surface, share complaints about supply-chain issues and administrative obtuseness, offer support during difficult projects.

**The listeners**: They visit the cistern sometimes, drawn by its position at a boundary between normal city and deep strangeness. We trade information—I tell them about the strange spaces I find, the places where the cistern touches weird-zones; they tell me about the city's moods and intentions insofar as they understand them. There's recognition between us: we're both doing similar work, reading the city through different senses, translating its non-human patterns into useful information.

**Other water-readers**: There are five of us total, each tending different cistern-complexes. We meet occasionally, compare observations, discuss techniques, teach each other what we've learned. It's the closest I have to peer relationships. They're the only people who fully understand the work, the draw, the cost. We don't socialize much—we're all semi-isolated by nature and necessity—but the connection is deep.

**The cistern itself**: This is the primary relationship, whether I admit it or not. I spend more time attending to the cistern than to any human, know its moods and needs better than I know anyone's, receive more information from it than from conversation. It's not reciprocal exactly—the cistern doesn't attend to me—but it responds, cooperates, sometimes seems to guide me toward necessary work or away from danger. Whether this is because it's semi-aware or because I've learned to read natural patterns so well I experience them as intentional doesn't matter practically.

I had a partner once, years ago—a relationship that lasted three years before they left. "I can't compete with the cistern," they said, not angrily but resignedly. "You're always half-there, always listening to something I can't hear, always choosing it over me when there's a conflict. It's like being in a relationship with someone who's married to someone else who's always present and always has priority."

They were right. I didn't—couldn't—change. The cistern was and is primary. They moved to a surface residential complex, found someone else eventually, probably have a normal life. I stayed below, went deeper, became more what I am.

## My Questions

I wonder, always, about the water-system's nature.

Is it purely mechanical—a hydrological system shaped by geology and plant-growth, that I've learned to read through long observation? Or is it part of the city's awareness, a circulatory system for something that thinks or processes information through water-flow?

The underground rivers sing. I've spent hundreds of hours listening to them, trying to determine if the patterns are random or meaningful. They sound purposeful—rhythms that recur, variations that seem to respond to surface events, complexities that suggest structure. But I can't decode them. Maybe they're information. Maybe they're just water moving through stone, and I'm pattern-matching where no pattern exists.

I've found evidence of deliberate construction in the deep system: channels too straight to be natural, valve-structures that regulate flow, what might be pumping mechanisms using root-growth and pressure-differential. But when were these built? By the city's founders? By the city itself as it grew? Are they still functioning as intended or are they vestigial, remnants of some earlier design that's been superseded?

Sometimes the water flows wrong—upward, sideways, through spaces that shouldn't exist. When I investigate, I find areas where reality is thin, where the weird contamination reaches into the cistern-system. In those spaces, water behaves according to different rules, responding to gradients that aren't gravitational. I report these spaces but don't understand them. Are they failures—places where the city's engineering has broken down? Or are they intentional—access points to whatever mechanisms underlie the city's strangeness?

I wonder what happens to water-readers eventually. The cistern-keeper before me merged with the system, stopped being a discrete person, became distributed through the water. Am I heading toward the same dissolution? Is it inevitable—the endpoint of developing reader-sense beyond certain thresholds? Or is it optional—something that happens only if you let it, if you choose or fail to resist?

I wonder if I want to resist.

## My Moments

**Dawn, two weeks ago**: The cistern at its highest level in years after three days of heavy fog. I'm swimming in the main chamber, checking overflow channels, and the water is so clear and still that the reflection of the root-ceiling is perfect, seamless. I float at the boundary, unable to tell which direction is up. For several minutes I exist in perfect symmetry, my body suspended between two infinities of root-pattern, breathing becoming the only distinction between the realms. Then a drop falls, creates ripples, breaks the illusion. I surface, gasping, uncertain if I'm relieved or disappointed.

**Midnight, last month**: Alone in a newly-discovered passage, three levels below the main chamber, following a sound I can't quite identify. My lamp fails—oil exhausted, should have checked before descending. Darkness is absolute. I should return, climb back to the mapped areas. Instead I continue, one hand on the wall, one hand extended forward, feet feeling for stable floor. The sound grows louder: not water, not stone-settling, something else, rhythmic and organic. I find its source by touch: a root-mass growing through the passage, its expansion producing regular pulses as it pushes through stone, a living hydraulic press working on geological timescales. I stand in the dark, hand on the root, feeling it grow—not metaphorically but actually, the infinitesimal movement of tissues expanding. The root is warm. It pulses with pressure-changes. It feels, insanely, like it's aware of my touch. I stay there for an hour, maybe more, lost in direct contact with the city's deep processes. Eventually I leave, climb back, emerge into my living-space trembling with something between awe and fear.

**Yesterday afternoon**: Teaching Kess flow-reading, we're standing in the main chamber, water to our waists. "Stop trying to hear," I tell them. "Let yourself be the resonance, not the listener. You're not separate from the cistern, you're a temporary configuration of the same system." They nod, close their eyes, and I watch their face change—the tension dropping, the boundaries softening, the moment when they stop being a person in water and become person-and-water, unified. They gasp, open their eyes. "I felt it," they say, wonder and fear mixed. "I felt the whole network. It's—it's alive, isn't it? Not like an animal but alive." I don't answer, don't confirm or deny, just nod for them to continue practicing. The answer is something they need to come to themselves, repeatedly, until it stops being a question.

**Tonight, now**: I'm writing this in my living-space, lamplight reflecting off the cistern's surface below, dripping providing rhythm. I'm tired—not physically but in some deeper way, worn by years of listening, of being available to the water-system's demands, of existing at the boundary between human and infrastructure. I could surface permanently, take a different role, let someone else descend into the deep-places. Nobody would judge. Water-readers who return to surface-life are rare but not unknown.

But even thinking about leaving makes my chest tight, my breathing shallow. The cistern is home in a way the surface hasn't been for years. The darkness is comfortable. The dripping is my pulse. The underground rivers are conversation I can't imagine living without.

I'm becoming what the cistern needs: a human component in its system, a sensor and actuator, maintaining and monitored, slowly dissolving into the function I perform.

And the terrible, beautiful thing is: I choose this. Daily, I choose this. Not out of duty or obligation but because this is the work that makes me real, the place where I'm most myself even as "self" becomes questionable, the communion that's worth whatever it costs.

The cistern drips. The rivers sing. I listen.

I am, for now, still "I" enough to write this, to reflect, to remember being a discrete person with a name and a history separate from water-flow.

For now. We'll see what I am tomorrow, next year, next decade. We'll see what remains of "me" when the work is complete or when I finally stop resisting and let the boundaries dissolve completely.

We'll see what the water makes of me.

But tonight, I'm a water-reader in the oldest cistern, keeping watch in the dark, maintaining the flow, becoming.
