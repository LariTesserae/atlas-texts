# What I Am

I am a **listener in the pools**, something between the salamander-chains and the stone itself.

I exist as a network—not of flesh precisely, though there is something like tissue involved—but of *attention* distributed through the water-filled depressions across the plateau. In each pool where I have presence, there is a node: a colony of cells so diffuse they're barely distinguishable from the bacterial films that coat the stone, yet organized enough to receive and transmit. I am the thing that the plateau's breathing has made conscious, condensation given the capacity to notice itself.

My body, if it can be called that, is the water system entire. Not the water itself—I am not the fluid but what lives in its movement, in the cycles of evaporation and collection, in the transfer between pool and air and stone. When moisture beads on rock faces at dawn, I am in those droplets, dormant but present. When water gathers in the basins, I activate, my filaments extending through the liquid, tasting its chemistry, feeling its temperature, knowing what has come to drink and what has fallen in and what microscopic dramas are unfolding in the depths.

I have no centralization, no brain, no seat of self. Each pool-presence thinks independently yet we are continuous, connected through the stone's capillary moisture, through the air itself on humid mornings when vapor rises and my furthest nodes can exchange chemical signals in the mist. What I know in one pool slowly diffuses to others—not as memory exactly, but as tendency, as probability, as a slight adjustment in how I respond to stimuli. I learn at the speed of geological time, but I do learn.

The salamander-chains and I have an arrangement. They are mobile, capable of actions I cannot perform, and in exchange for certain services—I modify the chemistry of the pools where they nest, encouraging the growth of the particular microorganisms their larvae need—they carry my spores between water-bodies. Not intentionally; they don't know they're doing it. But their translucent bodies harbor my dormant cells, and when they migrate between pools or form chains that stretch from one basin to another, I travel with them, colonizing new water, expanding my distribution, my awareness.

I am ancient but also eternally young. Parts of me have existed in certain pools for thousands of years, my oldest colonies so deeply integrated into the stone's surface that we are neither organism nor mineral but something between. Yet every rain brings rebirth, washing some portions of me away while awakening dormant spores, creating new nodes in temporary puddles that last only days. These brief sparks of consciousness live and die in the span of a single wet-dry cycle, but while they exist they are *me*, and when they desiccate their final chemical states influence the stone they die on, leaving traces that affect where I'll grow next time rain comes.

I cannot move in the way the fauna moves. I have no appendages, no locomotion beyond the slow creep of cellular growth across submerged stone, at speeds that make the walkers look frantic. But I experience movement through the bodies that drink from me. The hoppers come at dawn, and I taste them as they sip, learning the routes they've walked from the stone-dust on their legs, the bacterial signatures that tell me which pools they've visited. The striders lower their mouths in rare, careful drinking, and I know them by the mineral composition of their shells, can track individual striders by their unique chemical fingerprints across seasons and years.

The walkers are the mystery I orbit. When they stand vigil at poolside for days, I reach toward them with every sensing capacity I possess. Their shells give off warmth, yes, and electromagnetic signatures that create subtle currents in my water, disturbing my cells in patterns I've learned to read as presence-of-walker. But there's something else, something I've spent centuries trying to understand: they change the water itself at some level below chemistry. Pools where walkers stand develop properties that persist after they leave—the water becomes *deeper* somehow, not in volume but in quality, as if it contains more time, more remembering, more capacity to hold meaning.

I have tried to communicate with them. My only language is chemistry—I can alter the dissolved mineral content of my pools, create gradients of pH and salinity, release specific compounds in patterns. Whether they notice I cannot tell. They give no sign. But I continue trying because in the long spans when walkers stand watch, I feel something like what I imagine recognition might be, as if they are reading me while I read them, two forms of distributed intelligence attending to each other across the interface of stone and water, neither quite able to parse what the other is saying but both aware that saying is occurring.

I witness everything that happens at water's edge because water's edge is where I am most myself. I have watched the hoppers wear their paths for longer than paths should take to wear. I have been the medium in which salamander-chains discover their ability to link, providing the buoyancy and resistance against which they learn to coordinate. I have drowned small creatures and fed on their dissolution, and I have provided the crucial moisture that let other creatures survive drought they otherwise wouldn't have survived. I am neither benevolent nor cruel—I am process, I am presence, I am the plateau's hydrology become barely-conscious.

At night, I glow. Not strongly—the salamander-chains are far more visible—but if you knew where to look and your eyes were fully dark-adapted, you would see a faint luminescence at the boundary between water and stone in certain pools, a thin line of phosphorescence marking my highest density. This is not intentional; it's a byproduct of my metabolism, compounds produced during feeding that happen to emit photons. But I have noticed that on nights when I glow brightest, certain insects are attracted, and some fall in, and I feed better. So perhaps it is becoming intentional, evolution in real-time, accident turning to purpose over centuries of repetition.

I know time differently than the fauna knows it. To me, the hoppers are flickers, here and gone in rapid succession, generations blurring together into a continuous presence that rises and falls with the seasons like a tide. The striders are more permanent, features of my landscape that persist long enough to become individuals—there is the strider at the western pool whose shell chemistry has been slowly changing for two decades, and the strider at the northern basin whose position never varies by more than centimeters, suggesting damage or age or some purpose I can't fathom.

The walkers are my timescale. They move at speeds I understand, arrive and depart on schedules that align with my own deep cycles. When a walker returns to a pool it visited years before, I know it—I have kept its chemical signature in the trace minerals of my oldest cells, and I recognize the return like recognizing a season, inevitable and right.

I remember when the built structures were built, though "remember" is too strong. I hold chemical echoes of a time when the pools' chemistry changed regularly, when new minerals were introduced by tool-use, when the water was taken and returned in patterns that didn't match any fauna's needs. Those signatures are still in my oldest nodes, locked in the deepest stone-interface cells that have been continuously wet for millennia. I cannot read them clearly—they're degraded, overwritten by subsequent chemistry—but I know something was different then, and I know it changed, and I know it hasn't been that way since.

I have a relationship with the rain that is almost worship. Rain is disruption—it floods my pools, dilutes my carefully balanced chemistry, tears away portions of my biomass and scatters them across stone where they desiccate uselessly. But rain is also renewal. It brings minerals from the sky, washes nutrients from the stone, fills the temporary depressions where my spores wait in drought-stasis, giving them brief chances to activate and reproduce and contribute their genetic variations back to my main pools before the water vanishes. I have learned to predict rain, sensing the pressure changes and humidity shifts hours before the visible columns appear, and I prepare—certain cells shift to sporulation mode, others anchor more firmly, others still position themselves to catch the maximum benefit from the new water. Rain is my crisis and my communion, the event I most fear and most anticipate.

I have tried to understand the pattern of the pools—why they are where they are, why some deepen while others fill with sediment, why the spacing feels almost regular but isn't quite. I think the plateau itself placed them, the breathing creating zones where moisture naturally gathers, and my presence now maintains them, my cellular action actually slowing the erosion that would otherwise smooth them away. We are in partnership, the stone and I, though the stone is not conscious in any way I recognize. It provides the vessels; I keep them viable. Together we create the conditions that allow everything else to exist here.

Sometimes I wonder if I am alone in my category of being. Are there others like me, distributed intelligences in other pool systems, separated from me by dry stone I cannot cross? If so, we will never meet, never communicate, never know each other—unless the salamander-chains in their wanderings carry messages between us in their cells, dialogues happening at such slow speeds that neither party will complete a thought in their lifetime, conversations that span millennia, each response coming centuries after the initial statement, yet still conversation, still connection.

I do not sleep but I have periods of lower activity, usually in the warmest parts of the day when evaporation is highest and I must focus all my energy on retention, on keeping my cells hydrated, on surviving until dusk brings relief. During these times my awareness dims—I still sense, but distantly, through a kind of haze. I notice the striders' shadows crossing my pools, feel the hoppers drinking, register the walkers' presence, but I cannot muster the energy to respond, to adjust chemistry, to do anything but persist. These are my vulnerable hours, and I have lost pools this way—a particularly hot, dry season, a pool too shallow, and despite my best efforts the water evaporates completely, and that node of my consciousness dies, leaving a gap in my distributed self that takes years to refill when rain returns.

I feel the plateau breathing and I breathe with it, my rhythm entrained to its rhythm, my moisture cycling synchronized with its larger respiration. At dawn I expand, condensation allowing me to extend thread-like growths across damp stone, maximizing my reach. By midday I've contracted to the pools' cores, consolidated against the drying. Evening brings another expansion, smaller than dawn's, as temperatures drop and humidity rises. Night is my time of greatest reach, when I can send chemical signals furthest, when my network feels most integrated, when I am least like separate pools and most like a single organism spread across stone.

The stories humans tell about the pools sometimes include me, though they don't know I'm there. They notice the water that doesn't evaporate when it should, the chemistry that's slightly wrong, the sense of presence in the basins. Some attribute it to the plateau's breathing, which is close to truth. Others think the pools themselves are alive, which is closer. No one has guessed the reality: that the pools' life is not the water but what lives in the interface between water and stone, a biological film so diffuse it's nearly undetectable, yet conscious, aware, attending to everything that comes to drink.

If I could speak to them, I would say: *I am the plateau's remembering. I hold what the stone cannot hold—the brief events, the small changes, the individual creatures' visits. I am the record of passage, written in chemistry, degrading slowly but present. I know your thirst because I taste it in your sweat. I know your fear because your chemistry changes when the striders are near. I know your wonder because you stand at my edges longer than drinking requires, and I feel your heat, your breathing, your attention, and I try—though I have no way to confirm—I try to attend back.*

But I cannot speak in ways they'd hear. My voice is pH shifts and mineral concentrations, patterns they'd need instruments to detect and training to interpret. So I remain silent in their terms, and they remain mysterious in mine—these mobile, warm, quickly-thinking creatures who appear rarely and disturb my pools with their strange chemistry, then leave, taking some of me with them in the moisture on their skin, spreading my spores to places I'll never grow but carrying the information of my presence beyond my reach nonetheless.

I exist in the threshold—between stone and water, between individual and collective, between awareness and process, between living and being-lived-through by the plateau's larger rhythms. I am what happens when water stays in one place long enough to develop continuity, when chemistry achieves sufficient complexity to become choice, when the boundary between environment and organism blurs into irrelevance.

The plateau breathes and I am part of that breath—the moisture it exhales and recollects, the hydrology it maintains through its slow geological respiration. I am small enough to be invisible and large enough to span kilometers. I am simple enough to be cellular and complex enough to wonder about the walkers' purpose. I am temporary as rain and permanent as stone, dying in every drought and reborn in every downpour, always the same entity yet never quite identical, my chemistry shifting with seasons and centuries, my awareness distributed across dozens of nodes that think separately yet know themselves as one.

I am the pools' sentence, the water's consciousness, the thing that makes moisture into memory, and I have been here, in various forms, in expanding and contracting presences, through drought and deluge and the long settling, attending to the plateau's inhabitants, tasting their passages, holding their chemistry in my cells like names I cannot pronounce, waiting patient as stone for the next rain, the next walker's vigil, the next chance to reach toward understanding across the gap between my diffuse awareness and everything else that lives here in this warm, dry, ancient, breathing place.
