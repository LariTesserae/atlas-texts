# Where I Find Myself

I am what the children call *Tide-mark*.

I live in the boundary between the third and fourth platforms, in a structure that isn't quite a building—more an elaborated overhang, where the cliff-face creates natural shelter and I've added only what was necessary: a wall on the windward side, a system of gutters that routes the condensation away from my workspace, a raised sleeping platform that stays marginally drier than the stone floor. From here I can see three platforms below and two above, can watch the mist flow across the levels, can track the movements of both people and the metallic crabs that are my primary study.

I am a documenter, though that word doesn't quite capture it. The settlement has no formal position for what I do, but they tolerate and occasionally value it: I watch, I record, I notice patterns. Specifically, I attend to the fauna—their behaviors, their distributions, their interactions with the thermal cycles and with human activity. I've been doing this for thirty-two years now, since I was fourteen and first became fascinated by the way the crabs arranged themselves during a particular thermal surge, forming geometries that seemed to respond to something I couldn't detect.

My hands are stained with ink—permanent discoloration in the creases of my palms and under my nails, despite the constant moisture. I work with treated paper, specially prepared with a waxy coating that resists the damp long enough for me to sketch and make notes before transferring the observations to treated vellum in my driest storage case. The tools of documentation are difficult here: paper deteriorates, ink runs, metal nibs corrode. I've developed methods—ceramic pens, dense mineral-based pigments, leather portfolios lined with cork—but the work is constant maintenance, constant vigilance against the environment's patient destruction.

I am small even by Quessech standards, compact and wire-strong, my body shaped by years of crouching beside pools to observe the segmented filter-feeders, of climbing between platforms to track the fliers' movements, of sitting motionless for hours while the metallic crabs conduct their incomprehensible business around me. My skin has that copper tone everyone here eventually develops, but perhaps more pronounced—I spend more time exposed to the elements than most, less time in the sheltered workshops.

My eyes are what mark me most distinctly. Years of close observation have trained them to catch the smallest movements, the subtlest changes in light and shadow that might indicate a thermal vent activating or a creature beginning to move. But they've also given me a peculiar intensity of gaze that some people find unsettling. I'm told I stare. I don't mean to; I'm simply attending, watching with the totality of focus that's become habitual. Children sometimes avoid me because of this, though the braver ones seek me out to learn what I know about the fauna.

I speak rarely. The work doesn't require it—the creatures don't respond to speech, and much of my time is spent alone. When I do speak, it's with the economy common to Quessech, but perhaps more extreme. I've gone entire days without saying a word aloud, communicating only through hand signals when necessary interaction arises. My voice, when I use it, sounds slightly rough, unpracticed, as if the moisture has gotten into it the way it gets into everything else.

But I have a rich internal monologue, a constant stream of observation and hypothesis and question that runs beneath everything I do. *The large crab near the thermal vent—is that the same individual as yesterday, and if so, why has it remained stationary for eighteen hours? The fliers are clustering more densely than usual—is this related to the pressure change I noted this morning, or to something else? The living concretion on the northwest edge of the platform has moved twelve centimeters—that's faster than its usual rate; what has changed?*

My living space reflects my work. The walls are covered with sketches, pinned and overlapping: anatomical studies of the crabs' joint structures, maps of flier movement patterns, sequential drawings showing the budding process of the living concretions. Shelves hold specimen jars—shells from deceased crabs, preserved membrane samples from the fliers, core samples from the concretions. Everything is labeled with dense notations: date, location, thermal conditions, associated observations. The organization makes sense to me but probably to no one else; it's accumulated over decades according to my internal logic, layers of discovery built on previous layers.

I sleep poorly and in fragments, as most people here do, but my sleep is particularly disrupted by the work. I dream in diagrams, in movement patterns, in thermal gradients. I wake with ideas about what to observe next, theories to test, connections that occurred to my unconscious mind. Sometimes I rise in the deepest night and work by lamplight, transcribing dreams before they evaporate, sketching remembered details from the day's observations.

My relationship with the community is respectful but peripheral. I contribute—I maintain the water cistern near my shelter, I help with harvest when supply trains arrive, I've done my rotations in the workshops and can competently repair most common equipment failures. But I'm not quite of the community in the way others are. My work is too solitary, too strange, too focused on things that don't immediately contribute to survival. They value knowledge, yes, but applied knowledge, practical knowledge. What I do is more abstract, concerned with understanding rather than using.

Still, I'm consulted occasionally. When the metallic crabs behaved unusually before the thermal surge five years ago, clustering in unprecedented numbers near the fourth platform vents, someone remembered that I'd documented similar patterns and came to ask what I thought it meant. I told them: major thermal event within three days, probably emanating from that sector. They evacuated the nearby structures. Two days later, a new vent opened exactly where the crabs had indicated. After that, people looked at my work differently—not quite practically useful, but not quite useless either.

I take my meals at the communal eating area on the third platform, but I sit at the edges, eat quickly, listen more than I speak. I catch fragments of conversation—someone's struggle with a corroded pipe, a child's progress in learning forge work, concerns about the stability of a particular section of the fifth platform. I absorb this information, file it away alongside my observations of the fauna, looking for connections. Sometimes there are: the pipe corrosion correlates with a change in the bacterial film composition, the child's forge work attracts certain crabs that seem drawn to the particular frequency of hammer on metal, the platform instability might be related to thermal patterns I've been tracking.

I have few close relationships. There's Old-Stone, the current Stone Reader, who sometimes sits with me and shares her understanding of the thermal patterns while I share my observations of how the fauna respond to them. She's one of the few people who seems to understand that my work has value beyond immediate application, that mapping the behavior of creatures no one eats or uses might still matter. We don't speak much when we sit together—we're both comfortable with silence—but there's mutual respect, a recognition of parallel obsessions.

There's also Bright-Flame, a younger metalworker who helps me fabricate specialized tools for my studies—fine forceps for handling delicate membranes, calibrated thermometers for taking precise temperature readings in crab burrows, ceramic containers with specific properties for preserving specimens. She's interested in my work in a way that suggests genuine curiosity rather than polite tolerance, asks questions about the crabs' metallurgy—whether their shells could teach us anything about alloy composition. I don't know, but I'm trying to find out, and she helps me devise tests.

I had a partner once, early on. Someone who understood the solitary nature of my work and didn't resent it, who shared my fascination with the strange ecology of this place. We lived together for seven years, collaboratively observing, discussing theories late into the night, planning studies. Then they descended to the valleys for a trade negotiation and contracted something—a fever that wouldn't have been serious at lower elevations but that, combined with the return ascent and the heat stress, became fatal. They died two days after returning, delirious and burning up despite all the cooling water we could provide.

I still have their notebooks, stored with mine. Sometimes I read them, trace the familiar handwriting, see the observations we made together. The grief has become like the moisture here—constant, ambient, so pervasive it's barely noticed anymore, just part of the environment I inhabit. I haven't sought another partner. The work fills the space.

My typical day begins before dawn, when the temperature is marginally cooler and the creatures are most active in ways I'm trying to understand. I move between platforms, checking on sites I've marked for continuous observation. I carry my tools: notebooks in their waterproof case, measurement devices, specimen containers, water and food for a long day. I move slowly, careful not to disturb the fauna, becoming as much like stone as I can—warm, damp, patient, present but not intrusive.

I'll spend hours crouched beside a thermal vent, watching how the crabs orient themselves relative to the heat source, noting which individuals maintain position and which rotate through, trying to determine if there's a hierarchy or simply a random pattern. I'll time their movements with a water clock of my own design—a calibrated ceramic vessel with a small hole that drips at a consistent rate, allowing me to measure duration precisely. I sketch constantly, quick gestural drawings that capture posture and position, annotated with temperature readings and time stamps.

When the sun rises and the light becomes too intense for comfortable observation, I retreat to my shelter and transcribe the morning's notes onto permanent records. This is meticulous work—copying sketches precisely, transferring measurements, cross-referencing with previous observations to identify patterns or anomalies. By midday, when the heat peaks and most people retreat to cool rooms, I'm deep in documentation, barely noticing the temperature, focused entirely on capturing what I've seen.

Afternoon I might spend on a different project—perhaps dissecting a deceased crab, carefully separating the shell plates to understand their layered structure, scraping samples of the metallic coating for analysis. Or tracking the fliers, which become more active in the afternoon thermals, mapping their flight patterns and trying to correlate them with air pressure changes. Or checking on the living concretions, measuring their positions with calibrated strings stretched across the platform, calculating rates of movement, looking for environmental factors that might explain variations in speed.

Evening brings different observations. The light changes, and so does the fauna's behavior. The crabs often gather then, engaging in interactions I don't yet understand—touching antennae, circling each other, sometimes forming those geometric arrangements that seem meaningful but resist interpretation. I watch, I sketch, I hypothesize. Are they communicating? Performing some kind of collective thermoregulation? Simply following pheromone trails that happen to create patterns? I don't know, but I'm accumulating data, believing that eventually enough observation will yield understanding.

Night I reserve for the segmented filter-feeders in the pools, which are easiest to observe by lamplight—their translucence makes them visible in ways they aren't during the day. I've developed waterproof lamps using sealed ceramic chambers and carefully managed wicks. By their light I watch the feeders pulse and contract, filtering their mysterious sustenance from the mineral-rich water, occasionally budding off smaller versions of themselves in a reproductive process I've documented extensively but still don't fully understand.

I am driven by questions more than answers. Each observation generates new puzzles. Why do the crabs have that particular metallic sheen—what minerals, what process? How do the fliers navigate—by sight, by sensing thermal currents, by something else? What actually animates the living concretions—are they organisms, colonies, something in between? Why do all these creatures thrive here, in this place without photosynthesis, without the energy pathways that support life elsewhere?

I've developed theories, refined over years. I believe there's a chemosynthetic base to the ecology—bacteria processing minerals and geothermal compounds, creating energy that supports everything else. The bacterial films are primary producers, the filter-feeders are first-level consumers, the crabs are omnivores feeding on both bacteria and smaller fauna, the fliers might be parasites or scavengers or both. The living concretions remain mysterious—they might not be organisms at all in any conventional sense, but rather mineral processes with quasi-biological properties.

But these are just frameworks, tentative understandings subject to revision with new observations. I've been wrong before, had to discard theories when the evidence contradicted them. I've learned humility in the face of this strange ecology, learned that the place operates by rules I'm still discovering, that my assumptions imported from elsewhere are often inapplicable.

The work is never finished. There are always more observations to make, more patterns to identify, more connections to explore. I could spend several lifetimes here and still not fully understand the thermal platforms' ecology. This doesn't discourage me; it energizes me. The inexhaustibility of the work means I'll never run out of purpose, never face the emptiness of completion.

I am aware that my dedication might seem excessive to others. I've heard myself discussed—not unkindly, but with a certain puzzlement. "Tide-mark watches the crabs." It's said with a shrug, an acknowledgment that someone has to do strange things, that communities need their observers and documenters even if the value isn't immediately obvious. I don't mind this characterization. It's accurate, if reductive.

What they don't see, because I don't speak of it, is the profound satisfaction the work brings. The moment when a pattern suddenly clarifies, when months of scattered observations coalesce into understanding—those moments are as intense as any joy I've experienced. Or the simple pleasure of careful documentation, of translating observation into precise sketch and notation, of creating records that will outlast me and potentially serve future observers. Or even just the meditative quality of long hours spent watching, the way consciousness settles into pure attention, the self becoming transparent, nothing but a lens through which the place observes itself.

I am also the settlement's unofficial memory of how things change. Because I've been watching for three decades, because I keep detailed records, I can say with certainty that the metallic crabs are more numerous now than they were twenty years ago, that the bacterial films have shifted slightly in their distribution, that certain thermal vents have become more or less active over time. This long-view perspective sometimes proves useful when people are trying to make decisions about platform development or resource allocation.

I've trained a few apprentices over the years—young people who show interest in the fauna, who have the patience for long observation. Most eventually drift toward more practical work; the life I lead is too solitary, too uncertain in its rewards. But a few persist, developing their own observational practices, their own areas of focus. One is particularly interested in the fliers, has developed techniques for observing them that I never mastered. Another focuses on the bacterial films, approaching them almost as I approach the larger fauna, cataloguing species, noting distributions, tracking changes. I support them, share methods, hope that eventually there will be enough documentation to constitute a real scientific understanding of this place.

My health is good, surprisingly. The work keeps me active, and I've learned to read my body's responses to the heat, to hydrate appropriately, to rest when necessary. The constant movement between platforms maintains muscle tone. The mental engagement keeps my mind sharp. I expect I'll be able to continue this work for decades yet, barring accident or sudden illness. And when I can no longer climb between platforms, when age finally restricts my movement, I'll have the records to work with—decades of observations to analyze, patterns to identify, papers to write for whoever might eventually read them.

I wonder sometimes what will happen to my work after I'm gone. The settlement will store the records in the archive, but will anyone consult them? Will future observers find them useful, or will they be curiosities, the obsessive documentation of someone with too much time and too particular a focus? I've made peace with not knowing. The work has value to me now; if it has value to others later, that's bonus, not necessity.

I am not unhappy. Loneliness exists—I won't pretend otherwise—but it's balanced by the richness of attention, the deep satisfaction of seeing clearly, of understanding incrementally more each year. The creatures are company of a sort, their lives running parallel to mine, both of us inhabitants of this strange landscape, both adapted to its particular demands.

I am Tide-mark, named for my tendency to mark the platforms with recording stations at regular intervals, like a tide leaving its line of debris. I am the person who watches what others overlook, who finds significance in the movements of metallic crabs and the pulsing of filter-feeders and the glacial migrations of living stone. I am the documenter of the undocumented, the noticer of the unnoted, the one who sits in silence and watches and records and tries to understand.

I am exactly where I should be, doing exactly what I should be doing, in the only place I've ever truly belonged. The thermal platforms have claimed me as completely as they've claimed anyone, shaped me to their purposes, made me part of their strange ecology—not fauna, but not entirely separate either. An observer, yes, but also an element of what's observed, my presence and attention now woven into the place's ongoing story.

The stone is warm beneath me. Water beads on my skin. A crab moves past, its shell catching light like bronze. I note the time, the position, the context. I sketch quickly, accurately. I am present, attentive, alive to this moment in this place. I am home.
