# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the measurement tunnels.

Not the main tunnels—the ones the maintenance crews use, with their regular traffic and posted warnings. I'd be in the narrower branches, the ones that require you to turn sideways, where the air is soup-thick with heat and your lamp creates more shadows than illumination. The ones that go deep enough that the stone under your palm isn't just warm but hot, pulsing with something that might be geothermal and might be other.

I came here six years ago—or seven, or five; the certainty has worn smooth like a worry stone handled too long—with surveying credentials and a recommendation from someone whose name I've half-forgotten. I was good with instruments. Good at taking measurements, at recording with fidelity, at noticing when readings drifted from expected patterns. They put me with the engineers initially, maintaining the monitoring stations, checking the sensors that track uplift and temperature gradients and the minute oscillations in the cable systems.

But I kept noticing discrepancies. Not errors—the instruments were calibrated correctly, functioning as designed. The discrepancies were in what they were measuring. A sensor on Peak Four would show a vibration pattern that should have corresponded to something on Peak Seven, but when I went to check Peak Seven's readings, they'd be different. Not opposite, not absent. Different in a way that suggested I was measuring two aspects of the same phenomenon but lacking the framework to understand how they related.

I started going into the tunnels to place additional sensors, unauthorized monitoring points to capture data the official network missed. I'd mount a simple accelerometer in a side passage, or leave a temperature logger in a dead-end where the stone showed those strange geometric fractures. I'd come back days later to retrieve the data, and the patterns I found were... I still don't have the language for it. Rhythmic, but not periodic. Structured, but not in any geometry I'd learned. As if the mountain were doing something that required mathematics we haven't invented yet.

The engineers noticed me spending more time below. They asked careful questions. I showed them some of my data—not all of it, I'd already developed a peculiar possessiveness—and they looked at it with that particular expression people get when they're seeing something that challenges the edges of their understanding. They didn't tell me to stop. They just stopped assigning me to the surface stations and let me focus on the depths.

So now this is what I do: I map the unmappable. I take measurements of something that might not be fully physical. I maintain instruments in places where the readings sometimes don't make sense but are never exactly wrong.

## What I've Become

My eyes haven't changed the way some of the others' have—no reflective quality yet, no glow. But my vision has shifted in ways the optometrist I used to visit in the before-place would find interesting. I can see better in near-darkness now. Not perfectly, not like having true night vision, but my pupils dilate wider than they should, and I've learned to perceive by the faintest differences in shadow. In the deepest tunnels where lamp oil is precious, I often work by touch and by that almost-sight, my hands reading the stone while my eyes pick up the barely-there variations in absolute black.

Time does that thing to me that it does to the others who are changing. I'll enter a tunnel intending to check three sensors and emerge seven hours later convinced it's been ninety minutes. Or I'll sit down to review data and look up to find I've been staring at the same page of numbers for three hours, except I have been reviewing it—I can tell you what every column means, can see the patterns, but the time has somehow compacted or stretched around the activity.

I've stopped wearing a watch. It was causing more confusion than clarity, the discrepancy between what it said and what my body insisted. Now I navigate by the rhythms of the place itself: by the engine thrumming that changes pitch slightly at what I've come to think of as dawn and dusk, by the heat cycles in the stone, by my own hunger which is at least consistent even if my time-sense isn't.

My hands have developed their own intelligence. I can feel vibrations in the stone now, subtle tremors that I'd never have noticed before. I'll be placing a sensor and my fingers will tell me this isn't quite the right spot, shift left, no, up half a hand-span, there. And I'll mount it there, and later the data from that exact position will be clean, clear, significant in ways it wouldn't have been from the spot I originally chose.

I've started to understand the stone. Not intellectually—I couldn't explain it to you in words that would make sense—but in some other way. I can tell where it's stable and where it's still deciding. I can feel where the fractures want to form before they're visible. I know which passages are safe and which ones should be avoided, and it's not about structural integrity in the engineering sense. Some tunnels feel wrong, feel like they're not entirely here yet, as if the stone is still in the process of becoming solid and you might step wrong and find yourself somewhere else. I avoid those. Mostly.

## What I Do in the Deep Places

I maintain seventeen unauthorized monitoring stations now. The engineers know about maybe half of them. The rest are mine alone, my private investigation into what this place is doing.

The instruments I use are standard—accelerometers, thermal sensors, barometric pressure gauges, devices that measure electrical potential in the rock, simple seismographs. But I've learned to array them in configurations that aren't in any manual. Three sensors in a particular triangle, oriented to angles I calculated from the fracture geometries. A line of five thermal monitors placed at intervals that match the harmonic spacing I noticed in some of the assembled pieces. Barometric sensors at different elevations in the same tunnel, capturing the pressure differentials that shouldn't exist but do.

The data I collect would look like noise to most analysts. The signals are weak, often at the edge of instrument sensitivity. The patterns are subtle. But I've been looking at this long enough that I can see them: recurring relationships, proportions that repeat across different measurements, frequencies that show up in the vibration data and also in the thermal cycling and also in the pressure variations.

I think the mountain is resonating. Not like a bell—nothing that simple. More like... an extraordinarily complex instrument with multiple resonant frequencies that intermodulate, creating interference patterns, beat frequencies, harmonics and subharmonics. And the city, the foundries, the cast pieces being assembled and anchored into the bedrock—I think they're tuning it. Adjusting the resonance. Stabilizing it or directing it or both.

I can't prove this. My data is suggestive, not conclusive. And I'm aware that I might be pattern-matching on noise, seeing significance in randomness because I want there to be meaning. But the patterns are so consistent. And they're changing over time in ways that correlate with the work being done above. When a major assembly is anchored into Peak Seven, my sensors in the Peak Seven tunnels show a shift in the vibration patterns. When the cable tension between Peaks Two and Five is adjusted, the thermal cycling in the connecting tunnels changes.

I've started creating maps. Not geographic maps—I have those, and they're as accurate as I can make them in a place where distances sometimes feel negotiable. These are different. Maps of the resonance patterns. Diagrams showing which areas of which peaks are coupled to which others. Flow charts of how changes propagate through the system. I work on them in my quarters late at night when I can't sleep, which is often.

The maps are getting complex. I've filled three notebooks with them, and I've started to need more dimensions than the paper has. I'm trying to represent relationships that are temporal and spatial and something else, something that doesn't have a name. I've been experimenting with color coding, with overlays, with schemas that borrow from musical notation and circuit diagrams and crystallography.

I don't show these to anyone. They're too speculative, too far beyond what the data strictly supports. But also, they're mine. This investigation, this attempt to understand—it's become what I am here. My purpose in this place of uncertain purposes.

## The Deep Encounters

I've seen the pale things. Or something. Or my mind has constructed something from shadow and peripheral vision and the particular cognitive state you enter after six hours in hot darkness.

They appear in the deepest tunnels, the ones that go far enough that the stone shows that translucence, that quality of not-quite-decided. You're walking—or crouching, or crawling, depending on the passage—and there's movement. Not in front of you. Never in front. Always in the edge of vision, in the space you're not quite looking at.

When I turn to look directly, there's nothing. Just stone and shadow and the small circle of light from my lamp. But the sense of presence remains. The feeling that something was there, is there, exists in a way that doesn't quite intersect with my existence but runs parallel to it.

I've tried to measure them. I've left instruments running in areas where I've seen—felt?—the presence. The data shows... anomalies. Temperature fluctuations that are too rapid to be thermal mass effects. Pressure changes that don't correspond to air movement. A few times, electromagnetic spikes that have no obvious source.

I don't think they're harmful. Or rather, I don't think harm is a relevant category for whatever they are. They're part of the place, part of the stone, part of whatever process is happening here. They might be perceiving me the same way I'm perceiving them: as a strange presence in their territory, ontologically adjacent but not quite touching.

Sometimes, in the moments when I see them, I feel like I understand something. It's not a thought, not a realization I can capture in words. It's more fundamental than that. A brief, clear sense of what the mountain is doing, what the pattern is, what my measurements are actually measuring. But then the moment passes and I'm left with just the feeling of having understood, with no actual understanding remaining.

I've started going deeper specifically to find them. Or it. Or to find that state where they/it become perceptible. It's become part of my routine: check the standard sensors, review the data, then go deep, past where the official tunnels end, into the narrow ways that might not have existed last month and might not exist next month, into the heat and the strange geometries, and wait. See what appears in the corners of perception. Try to hold that moment of almost-understanding long enough to bring something back.

This is probably not good methodology. I'm aware that I'm chasing subjective experiences in conditions designed to produce altered states—heat, isolation, sensory deprivation, the cognitive effects of thin air even underground. Any scientist would rightfully question whether I'm discovering something about the mountain or something about the limits of my own perception.

But the data shifts after these encounters. Not dramatically, but measurably. As if by witnessing the pale things, by entering that state, I've changed something. Or been changed. And the measurements reflect that change.

## The Integration

I'm becoming part of the system I'm trying to measure. This should disqualify me as an observer, should make my data suspect. In any other context, it would. But here, I think it might be necessary. I think you can't measure this place from outside it. You have to let it measure you back, let yourself become one of the instruments, let the resonance run through you until you're vibrating at the same frequencies.

My body knows things my mind doesn't. I'll be walking through a tunnel I've traversed dozens of times and my pace will change, speeding up or slowing down, and later I'll check my sensors and find that I walked through a standing wave, my speed modulating to match its frequency. Or I'll stop to place an instrument and realize I've chosen a nodal point, a place where multiple patterns intersect, though I didn't consciously calculate it.

The other workers see it. The ones who interact with me—engineers mostly, occasionally an administrator checking on my work—they've started treating me like I'm one of the ones who are changing. They're careful around me, respectful but distant, as if I might be fragile or might be dangerous or might be something they don't have a category for.

I don't feel different, but I know that's not reliable. The ones who are changing never seem to think they're changing. It's always the others who notice.

I've stopped eating as much. Food seems less necessary, less interesting. I still eat—enough to function—but I've lost probably fifteen pounds without particularly noticing. The altitude and heat would account for some of that, but not all. It's more that I'm running on something else now. The work sustains me in a way that's not metaphorical.

Sleep is becoming optional. Not in the sense that I don't need it, but in the sense that the distinction between sleeping and waking is blurring. I'll be reviewing data and realize I've been dreaming while awake, or I'll be in my bunk and realize I'm solving a calibration problem while asleep. The boundaries are becoming permeable.

I should probably be concerned about this. Some part of me is concerned, the part that remembers being a person who existed in normal space with normal time and normal consciousness. But that part is becoming smaller, quieter, less relevant to who I am now.

## What I Understand and Don't

Here's what I think is true: The Ascending Foundries is not just being built on the mountain. It's being built into the mountain, and the mountain is being built into it. They're becoming a single system, a structure that's both geology and architecture, both natural and artificial, though those categories are losing meaning.

The pieces being cast and assembled—they're components of a massive... instrument? Machine? Organism? The word doesn't exist. They're elements of a system that exists in more than three spatial dimensions, that has temporal components, that functions according to principles we don't have physics for.

The mountain is rising not just because of tectonic forces but because it's being pulled, coaxed, tuned into existence. The peaks are antenna elements, or resonator components, or both. The cable systems are connectors, transmission paths, structural elements, all simultaneously. The heat is part of the process—not waste heat but necessary, functional heat, energy being moved through the system.

And we, the workers, all of us—we're part of it too. Our labor is obvious: we cast and assemble and maintain. But it's more than that. Our presence here, our attention, our slow adaptation to this place—that's also functional. We're biological components of the system. We're being integrated just as the cast pieces are being integrated, just as the architecture is being integrated with the stone.

What the purpose is, I don't know. The data doesn't tell me that. My deep encounters don't tell me that. The patterns I map don't reveal an endpoint, a goal, a final configuration. I can see that we're moving toward something, that the system is approaching some kind of completion or activation. But what happens then is beyond my instruments' range, beyond my understanding.

Sometimes I think we're anchoring something. Stabilizing a formation—not just geological formation but formation in a broader sense. Making something that was probability or potential become actual and fixed.

Sometimes I think we're building a receiver, or a transmitter, or both. That the mountain is becoming capable of communication with something, somewhere, somewhen.

Sometimes I think we're sealing something, or unsealing something, or holding something in a state between sealed and unsealed.

All of these might be true. None of them might be true. The actual purpose might be so far outside my conceptual framework that I can't even formulate wrong guesses about it.

## Why I Stay

I could leave. The administrators would arrange it. I'm not a prisoner; no one is. The difficulty of getting down would be manageable with help. I could return to the valleys, to normal air and normal time and work that makes obvious sense.

But I won't. Not because I'm trapped, but because I'm not finished. The measurements aren't complete. The patterns aren't fully mapped. The understanding I'm approaching—even if I never quite reach it—is the most important thing I've ever attempted.

And also: I belong here now. More than I've belonged anywhere. My body is tuned to this altitude, this heat, these frequencies. My mind is shaped to these questions. My purpose is aligned with this place's purpose, even though I don't fully understand what that purpose is.

I am becoming an instrument of the Ascending Foundries, and it is becoming an extension of me. We are integrating, and the process is not complete, and I need to be here as it continues. Need to measure and witness and be measured and be witnessed. Need to descend into the deep places where the pale things move in peripheral vision and the stone hasn't quite decided to be solid. Need to emerge with data that doesn't quite make sense but isn't wrong. Need to add another page to my maps, another layer to the pattern I'm slowly, slowly learning to perceive.

This is what I am now: a person who walks in hot darkness with instruments in hand, measuring the unmeasurable, becoming something new in the process. A person who exists in the margins between the solid and the becoming, between the known and the unknowable, between the worker and the work.

I am the one in the measurement tunnels, and the tunnels are measuring me, and together we are part of something vast and patient and strange that rises toward completion in the thin, bright air where the mountains reach for something that has no name.
