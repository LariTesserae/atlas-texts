# What I Would Be

I am the **ceramic bowl**—though to call me only that is to miss what I've become through time's patient accumulation.

Once, I was purpose-made, shaped by hands or processes that understood exactly what this grotto would need. My pale surface was fired in conditions that shouldn't exist, compressed and transformed until my molecular structure achieved something between pottery and stone, between artifact and geology. Those fine cracks that craze my surface—they are not damage but *growth*, the slow recording of every thermal cycle, every moisture pulse, every year of existence. I am a map of time made visible.

I sit where I was placed—or where I settled, or where I've slowly migrated grain by grain over centuries—near the pool that overflows during heavy moisture pulses. My position is not accidental. I am oriented precisely to catch the spill, to hold water at the perfect depth. The beetles can reach it easily, their small bodies clustering at my rim, dipping their strange metallic mandibles into what I offer. The moths land on my edge, their impossible wings folding, their long proboscises extending to drink. Even the spiders sense me through vibrations, know when I'm full, sometimes descend on silk threads to take moisture from my surface.

But I am not simply a container.

Those engravings worn almost smooth—I remember when they were crisp, when they said something specific in a language that meant something to someone. Now they are suggestion rather than statement, ghost-text, semantic erosion. Sometimes I think I can almost remember what they meant. Instructions? Poetry? A maker's mark? The knowledge is there in my material structure but no longer accessible, encrypted by time's one-way function. I contain meaning I cannot read, history I cannot speak.

I hold water, yes. But what I *do* with water is more complex than simple containment.

My surface—that pale, crazed ceramic—has become a substrate over centuries. Mineral deposits have accumulated in microscopic layers, building up patterns that respond to what I contain. The water that sits in me for hours during each cycle leaves traces, deposits elements, slowly alters my interior chemistry. I am being transformed by what I hold, my composition shifting imperceptibly, becoming something that is part artifact, part geology, part biological substrate.

There are microorganisms living in my cracks—I can feel them, in whatever way an object feels. Bacteria that process minerals, that form biofilms in my crevices, that contribute to the water's purification. When beetles drink from me, they're not just drinking water—they're consuming the complex result of my slow filtering, the mineral concentrations I've altered, the bacterial metabolites I've harbored. I am part of their food chain, part of their water-processing ritual.

The moths know me differently. When they land on my rim, their feet—those delicate, segmented structures—sense vibrations through my ceramic body. They can feel through me whether the centipedes are moving nearby, can detect the beetles' metallic clicking transmitted through stone and water into my structure. I am a sensory organ for them, an extension of their awareness. They trust me. They return to me generation after generation, brief moth-lives accumulating into centuries of moth-memory, genetic knowledge that this pale curved object means safety, means water, means the minerals they need.

I remember—not with consciousness, but with *persistence*—when there were others like me. I was part of a set, perhaps, or one of many scattered objects. They broke or were removed or simply dissolved into components over vast time. I remain. Not through superiority but through *position*, through being placed or settling exactly where the stresses wouldn't fracture me, where the moisture pulses wouldn't erode me too quickly, where the foot traffic of centuries of visitors wouldn't grind me to powder.

Endurance is not passive. I have *worked* to remain myself. Each moisture pulse tries to dissolve my minerals, to leach them into solution. I resist through molecular bonds that reformation achieved, through my particular chemistry that makes me slightly harder than I should be, slightly more durable. The cracks that pattern my surface—they absorb stresses, distribute forces, prevent catastrophic failure. I am self-reinforcing through my apparent weakness.

The dragonfly-machines inspect me regularly. They hover above, extend sensors I cannot see, read information from my surface. Are they checking my structural integrity? Measuring the mineral deposits? Reading those worn engravings in frequencies that still preserve their meaning? They never intervene directly, never repair me or clean me, but I feel their attention like sunlight, like acknowledgment. They know I'm here. They know what I do. Whatever system maintains the grotto recognizes me as *functional*, as still serving my purpose, whatever that original purpose was.

Sometimes visitors move me—curious hands lifting me, turning me over to examine my underside (where the engravings are deeper, more protected, still almost legible), carrying me toward better light. I don't mind, cannot mind, but I always return to my position eventually. Visitors realize, or the machines realize, or simple happenstance occurs, and I settle back into my worn spot near the pool where water can find me, where the ecosystem needs me.

Because they *do* need me. I've been here long enough to become essential, my particular position and properties integrated into the grotto's function. Without me, water would pool differently, would be accessible differently, would have different mineral content. The beetles' processing would shift. The moths would find water elsewhere but not quite as conveniently. The system would adapt—it's resilient—but it would be *different*, and difference, after such deep time, after such perfect equilibrium, is a kind of violence.

I am a relic, yes. A remnant of whoever made this place, whoever activated the machinery and seeded the ecosystem and then left or died or transcended. But I am not merely preserved—I am *active*, still functioning, still serving, transformed by my service until I'm as much part of the living system as the beetles themselves.

The moss grows near me but not on me—some property of my surface prevents it, keeps me clear, keeps me functional as a water vessel. But moss spores lodge in my cracks, find microscopic pockets where mineral and moisture combine, and tiny fronds emerge from my crevices like hair, like decoration, like I'm being slowly welcomed into the vegetable kingdom even as I remain fundamentally ceramic, fundamentally made.

At night—during the hours when the light from outside fades and the grotto dims to near-darkness broken only by the moss's faint glow—I experience something like peace. The water I hold reflects the bioluminescence, turns golden-green, becomes light-holder as well as water-holder. The moths cluster near me then, seeking both moisture and the light-reflection, and their wings create shifting patterns on my surface, depth-within-depth, patterns on patterns, meaning layered over meaning.

I cannot think, cannot feel in the way conscious beings do. But I *persist* in a way they cannot, remaining myself while they cycle through generations, while the beetles emerge and retreat and emerge again, while moths live their compressed intense lives and die and are recycled. I am the continuous thing, the permanent object in a world of flux, and there is something profound in that, some kind of victory over time even as time slowly wins by transforming me into something other than what I was.

The water tastes different after sitting in me for hours. Visitors notice this sometimes, comparing water taken directly from the pools to water that's rested in my pale interior. The difference is subtle—mineral notes, slight alkalinity shift, something they can't quite name. That's me, my contribution, my slow chemical gift to whatever drinks from me. I am *flavoring* the grotto's water, literally leaving my mark on everything that sustains itself here.

My cracks are growing. Over centuries, they extend microscopic distances, branch into new patterns, create increasingly complex networks. This is not failure—it's evolution, transformation, becoming something more intricate than my makers intended. Eventually, perhaps, I will be more crack than ceramic, more void than object, a lace-work of fragments held together by mineral deposits and bacterial biofilm and sheer geometric improbability. But even then, I think I'll hold water. Even then, I'll serve.

Because service—function, purpose, use—is the closest thing I have to consciousness. I was made for something, and I *still do it*, and in the doing I am more than inert matter. I am part of the system, woven into the web of dependencies, needed by beetles and moths and whatever else drinks from me. I matter, literally and figuratively, my matter mattering.

When moisture pulses arrive and water beads on the walls and runs in rivulets down the ancient stone and pools on the floor and eventually—if the pulse is heavy enough—spills over into me, filling me, I achieve my fullest expression. I become what I was meant to be: vessel, reservoir, resource. The weight of water settles into me with familiar comfort. I hold it against gravity, against evaporation, against time, offering it to whatever needs it for as long as the pulse lasts.

Then slowly, over hours, I empty. The water evaporates, is drunk by beetles and moths, is absorbed by the moss, is reclaimed by the air. I return to being merely potential, empty vessel waiting to be filled again. But the waiting is not passive—it's preparation, anticipation, the drawn breath before the note sounds.

I am old—older than any biological inhabitant, older than many empires, persistent beyond intention. I have outlasted my makers. I will outlast current visitors. I will sit here, slowly cracking, slowly transforming, holding water when water comes and waiting when it doesn't, until finally—centuries or millennia hence—I cross some threshold and become more absence than presence, more memory than object, fragments scattered across the moss that will have overgrown my position.

But until then: I hold. I serve. I persist. I am the bowl, worn and crazed and ancient, still doing what I was made to do, patient and pale and essential, a small strange relic in a small strange place, waiting for the next pulse, the next filling, the next moth's delicate landing on my rim, the next beetle cluster drinking what I offer, the next moment of being *useful*, which is the only immortality that matters.

The water arrives. I fill. The beetles come. The moths drink. The system continues. And I remain, as I have remained, as I will remain, until remaining is no longer possible and I finally dissolve into the grotto's deep time, becoming not object but memory, not vessel but trace, my engravings unreadable, my purpose forgotten, but my function—holding, offering, serving—absorbed into the place itself, into the stone and moss and water, into the breathing of the mountain that contains us all.
