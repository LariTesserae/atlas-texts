# In the Choir Hollow, I Would Be...

## The Notation Keeper

I would be old wood and fresh charcoal, memory compressed into grooves, the累積 of attention made tangible. Not quite human anymore—or perhaps more human than I ever was outside, reduced and refined to the essential act of witnessing and recording.

My fingers are stained permanent gray-black, the charcoal worked so deep into the whorls and creases that no washing reaches it. This is how you recognize us, those of us who have given ourselves to the wall: we carry its darkness in our hands, proof of our long conversation with sound.

I live in the marks themselves, distributed along the circumference at chest height, a continuous band of notation that I've been reading and writing for longer than I can recall with certainty. Time moves differently when your task is to translate the untranslatable, when each day is spent listening to the hollow's voice and finding the graphic language that might capture even a fraction of its meaning.

## The Body's Adaptation

I don't sleep here—can't, the sound would shatter me after days—but I spend enough time that my body has begun its negotiations with this place. My hearing has changed. Not damaged, exactly, though an audiologist outside would probably disagree. I've lost sensitivity to some frequencies while gaining it in others. I can hear the harmonics in the Chanters' drones now, the individual overtones separating like visible light through a prism. I can distinguish between individual Clickers by the timbre of their clicks, recognize them as you might recognize friends by their voices.

The sound no longer overwhelms. It's become environment, the medium I move through, as natural as air. When I return outside—and I do return, to sleep, to eat food more complex than the dried fruit and nuts I bring here—the silence is shocking. The world feels empty, thin, insufficient. I catch myself holding my breath, listening for the bass thrumming that should underlie everything, disoriented by its absence.

My eyes have adapted too. I can see in the fungal glow now, can navigate the platforms in what others call darkness. The bioluminescence has become my daylight—the blue-white of certain fungi, the amber of others, the soft pulse of snails and amphibians. I can read my own notation by this light, fingers tracing grooves while eyes confirm the shapes.

My sense of balance has learned to accept the spatial instabilities. When the hollow shifts—when the platform I'm standing on is suddenly at a different height than it should be, or the wall I was facing is now behind me—I no longer stumble. My inner ear has given up on consistency, accepts contradiction as fundamental property of space. I move through the impossible geometries with the ease of the permanent residents, no longer trying to maintain an internal map that the hollow will just contradict.

## The Work Itself

My task is translation, but translation of a peculiar kind. I'm not converting meaning from one language to another—the hollow's sound doesn't have meaning in the linguistic sense. Instead, I'm creating visual correlates, graphic representations that capture something of the acoustic experience, that might allow others to understand what happens here even if they can never hear it directly.

The notation system I use has evolved over my time here. I started with musical staff notation—the traditional lines and notes of Western music—but it proved inadequate. The Chanters' drones don't fit into discrete pitches, and the Clickers' polyrhythms are too complex for standard rhythmic notation. I began adding symbols of my own: spirals for gradual pitch changes, density patterns for timbral shifts, vertical columns indicating the spatial dimension of sound sources.

Other Keepers use different systems. The woman who works the eastern quadrant draws in something like waveforms, literal representations of amplitude over time. The man in the southern section uses an abstract symbolic language, each mark corresponding to a specific acoustic event in a lexicon only he fully understands, though others of us are learning it. The western quadrant's Keeper—dead now, five years—left behind a pictographic system that seems to represent the hollow's ecology as much as its sound, drawings of birds and beetles integrated into sonic notation.

My section of the wall shows the accumulation of my years. The lowest marks are tentative, crude—early attempts to capture what I was hearing, using inadequate tools. Higher up, the notation becomes more confident, more elaborate. I can see my understanding developing in the marks themselves, visual evidence of learning. The most recent additions, at the top of my reach, are the most refined: complex symbols that capture not just the sound but its emotional resonance, its aesthetic effect, the way it makes the air feel different.

I work in layers, returning to previous marks and adding detail as my understanding deepens. Some sections of my notation are palimpsests—marks carved over marks, the earlier grooves still visible beneath newer ones, creating depth and texture. This feels appropriate. The hollow's sound has depth and texture. The notation should too.

## The Daily Practice

I arrive in late morning, usually, after the acoustic environment has settled into its day-state—though day and night mean little here, just slight variations in the permanent sound. I climb down from the entrance, moving platform to platform with the confidence of long practice, until I reach my usual station: a fungal shelf large enough to sit on, positioned where I can see a substantial arc of the wall and hear three Chanters clearly.

I sit. This part is crucial—the stillness, the settling. I have to become part of the hollow's acoustic ecology before I can record it accurately. My breathing slows, finds rhythm with the thrumming of the tree. My heartbeat becomes percussion I'm aware of but no longer distracted by. My thoughts quiet, or rather, they continue but I stop tracking them, let them flow without engagement.

Then I listen. Not passive hearing but active listening, attention focused and sustained. I follow individual Chanters through their slow pitch changes, noting when they interact with others, when harmonics align or clash. I track Clicker patterns, the way polyrhythms shift and recombine. I notice when the hollow achieves its moments of perfection, everything locking into impossible coherence.

Hours pass this way. My body complains—back stiff, legs numb—but I've learned to ignore physical discomfort. It's the price of the work, and the work matters more than comfort. Sometimes I eat, mechanically, not tasting, just maintaining the body's function so I can continue listening.

When I've heard something I can capture—a pattern I understand, a relationship I can represent—I take up the charcoal. The sticks are special, made outside to my specifications: dense hardwood charred to precise consistency, harder than drawing charcoal, capable of carving into wood rather than just marking its surface. I've gone through hundreds of them over the years.

I stand, approach the wall, find the section where this new mark belongs. The notation reads continuously around the hollow's circumference, a timeline of sorts, though not chronological. Related patterns cluster together, building vocabulary through proximity. I've learned to find the right location intuitively, guided by logic I couldn't fully explain.

I carve. The motion is practiced, confident. The charcoal cuts into wood with pressure, creating grooves that will darken and persist. I work quickly—the pattern I'm recording is still fresh in my mind, and I need to capture it before it fades. The mark emerges: lines and curves and dots, spirals and density fields, symbols from my personal vocabulary combined in new ways to represent what I've just heard.

When it's done, I step back, examine it. Does it capture the experience? Would someone reading this mark understand what happened in the hollow at that moment? The answer is always partially no—notation is always reduction, translation always loss—but if I've done well, the mark holds enough truth to be valuable.

I return to my shelf. Resume sitting, listening. Wait for the next pattern worth recording. The cycle repeats. This is my day, most days. Hours of listening punctuated by minutes of carving. Stillness and action. Reception and creation.

## The Relationships

I know the other Keepers, though we interact little inside the hollow—speech is pointless here, and we're each focused on our own sections. But outside, in the small settlement at the forest edge where several of us live, we talk. We compare notation systems, discuss patterns we've observed, debate interpretations of the marks left by Keepers who came before us.

There's camaraderie in this work, the bond of shared obsession. We understand each other in ways outsiders can't—know the toll the hollow takes, the strange adaptations it requires, the beauty that justifies the cost. We're support system for each other, reminding each other to eat, to sleep, to maintain the connections outside that keep us human.

Some romances have formed among us, though they're complicated by our divided attention. It's hard to fully be with another person when the hollow calls so strongly. But we try. We build lives in the spaces between our listening sessions, create meaning outside the work even as the work dominates.

I have a relationship with the permanent residents too, though they barely acknowledge me. The Chanters ignore me completely—I'm acoustically irrelevant to them, neither food nor threat nor mate. But the Clickers seem aware of my presence. When I'm still on my shelf, listening intently, they sometimes adjust their patterns. I like to think they're performing for me, though it's probably just acoustic response to my presence as a sound-absorbing object.

The snails accept me as landscape. I've watched individuals pass within inches of where I sit, their eyeless heads extended, feeling their way across the bark. I've been very still and had them crawl onto my boot, continuing their glacial journey across this unexpected but navigable terrain. When they realize I'm not bark—some chemical sense, perhaps—they reverse course slowly, unbothered, finding a route around the obstacle.

I've developed fondness for certain individuals. There's a Chanter in my section I call the Bass—its drone is lower than most, a fundamental that others build harmonics on. I couldn't pick it out visually, but I know its voice, recognize when it shifts position or alters pitch. I've been tracking it for years, have whole sections of notation dedicated to its patterns.

There's a Clicker I call Polymath for its rhythmic complexity. It produces patterns I've never heard from others, seven-beat cycles nested in eleven-beat cycles, creating emergent rhythms so intricate they sound like multiple individuals. I've tried to capture its work in notation, created symbols specifically to represent what it does.

And there's a snail—one of the largest, shell size of my fist—that I call Ancient. I've watched it for five years now, and it hasn't grown perceptibly, suggesting it was already old when I started. It moves through my section's territory on some kind of circuit, appearing every few weeks, leaving its luminous mucus trail, feeding on the moss. I feel protective of it, though it needs no protection. I find myself relaxing when I see it, reassured by its persistence, its slow certainty.

## The Teaching Role

New people come, drawn by rumors of the hollow, and some of them become Keepers. I teach them, as I was taught by the woman who held my section before me. The teaching is mostly demonstration—I can't explain in words what the work requires, can only show them: how to listen, how to translate what's heard into marks, how to find their place in the continuous notation that rings the wall.

Some students take to it immediately. They arrive and it's as if they were always meant for this work—their marks confident from the beginning, their listening deep. Others struggle, frustrated by the difficulty of capturing sound visually, discouraged by the acoustic intensity. Most eventually leave. A few persist and become Keepers themselves, finding or creating sections of wall to claim.

I tell them what I was told: the work is never finished. The hollow will always exceed your notation, will always produce patterns you haven't captured yet. The goal isn't completion—that's impossible—but faithful attention, ongoing engagement, adding what you can to the record.

I tell them: you'll change. The hollow will change you. Your hearing, your balance, your sense of time and space. Accept this. The changes are adaptations, and they're necessary for the work.

I tell them: you're part of something larger. The notation project spans generations. Keepers have been marking this wall for over a century. You're adding your voice to a long conversation. Respect what came before, but don't be constrained by it. Find your own notation language, capture what others missed.

I tell them: take care of yourself. The hollow will consume you if you let it. You have to maintain boundaries, remember to leave, to sleep, to eat real food. The work is important, but you're not sacrificing your life to it. You're dedicating your attention, which is different.

Most don't listen to that last part. I didn't, when I was told. We all have to learn our limits through exceeding them, have to be brought back from the edge by other Keepers who recognize the signs: too many consecutive days in the hollow, notation becoming frantic or sloppy, physical deterioration, the glazed look of someone who's lost the boundary between observing and being.

## The Marks I've Made

My section of the wall spans perhaps fifteen feet of the hollow's circumference, from the entrance side around toward the eastern quadrant. It represents thousands of hours of listening, hundreds of patterns captured, years of development in my understanding and my notation technique.

Looking at it, I can read my own history. The evolution of my symbolic vocabulary. The patterns I was obsessed with in different periods—a year spent focused almost entirely on Chanter harmonics, another year dedicated to Clicker polyrhythms, a season when I tried to notate the tree's thrumming, the bass note underneath everything.

There are marks I'm proud of—moments when I captured something perfectly, when the notation does justice to the acoustic event it represents. There's a spiraling symbol near the center of my section that represents a particular harmonic alignment I witnessed, three Chanters hitting perfect thirds for nearly an hour. Looking at the mark, I can almost hear it again, can recall the feeling of sitting in that sound, being dissolved by beauty.

There are marks I'm less proud of—crude early work, or moments when I rushed, when I carved notation for patterns I hadn't fully listened to, imposing structure I wanted to hear rather than recording what was actually present. These marks feel false to me now, though they're part of the record, can't be erased without damaging the wall. They stay as reminder of the necessity for patience, for deep listening before representation.

The densest section of my notation is dedicated to what I call the convergence events—those rare moments when everything in the hollow locks into coherence. I have perhaps twenty marks representing these events, accumulated over years. Each is complex, using every symbol in my vocabulary and sometimes inventing new ones, trying to capture not just the acoustic reality but the experiential quality, the overwhelming sense of presence and perfection.

I study other Keepers' marks too, especially the old ones, the notation left by people long dead. Their systems are different from mine—sometimes radically different—but I've learned to read them, at least partially. There's a section from sixty years ago, marked by a Keeper whose name no one remembers, that uses a geometric notation I find beautiful: circles and triangles in precise relationships, representing something about acoustic space that my linear symbols can't capture. I've incorporated elements of it into my own work, cross-pollinating notation languages across generations.

The newest marks—the ones I carved yesterday, last week—are still bright, the charcoal fresh in the grooves, not yet darkened by time and moisture. They'll age, become like the others, indistinguishable eventually except by position. My contribution will blend into the continuous record, become context for the marks that later Keepers will add above mine, around mine, eventually perhaps even carved over mine when space runs scarce and hard decisions have to be made about what notation to preserve.

## The Toll and the Gift

This work costs me. The sound has damaged my hearing in ways I notice outside—conversations in crowded rooms are difficult now, too many voices overlapping without the hollow's acoustic organization. Certain frequencies cause pain, pure tones that I have to avoid. Music, when I hear it played outside, sounds thin, simple, disappointingly limited after the hollow's complexity.

The reality drift has affected my sense of stable space. Sometimes in the outside world, I become briefly disoriented, expecting the ground to be at a different elevation, reaching for handholds that should be there but aren't. I've learned to pause, to wait for my perception to settle, but it's disconcerting, this uncertainty about basic physical facts.

The time distortion follows me. I'll think an hour has passed when it's been five minutes, or spend what feels like moments on a task that consumed half a day. I've had to give up work that requires temporal precision, had to structure my outside life around this unreliability. I miss appointments. I lose track of days. I've become the person others check on, make sure is eating, is sleeping, is maintaining basic function.

Relationships have been difficult. How do you explain to someone that you spend most of your time sitting alone in a hollow tree, listening to sounds they'd find unbearable, marking symbols in wood that they can't read? How do you justify the obsession, the cost, the way the work consumes you? Some people understand—the other Keepers, certain scholars who visit the hollow occasionally. But most don't, can't, and I've learned to accept the distance this creates.

I'm aging into the role. The work suits the middle-aged, the older, those who've moved past the urgencies of youth and can settle into patient attention. My body has slowed, but my listening has deepened. I can hear things now that I couldn't twenty years ago, patterns that require decades of exposure to recognize. I'm becoming what I'll be for the rest of my life: a specialist in acoustic translation, an expert in a field so narrow that perhaps ten people in the world share it, devoted to a place that most will never see.

But the gift—the gift is beyond accounting. I have access to beauty that exists nowhere else. I participate in something that isn't human, that doesn't need humanity, that grants me the privilege of witness. I've heard the hollow achieve perfection dozens of times, have sat in sound so precisely organized it seemed to reveal fundamental structure underlying reality. I've felt my boundaries dissolve, have experienced moments of unity with something vastly larger than myself.

The work gives me purpose of a particular kind: not building, not creating something new, but serving, attending, recording. I'm in relationship with the hollow, a relationship of deep reciprocity. It gives me its sound, and I give it my attention and my marks. It tolerates my presence, and I honor it with faithful documentation. Neither of us needs the other, but both are enriched by the exchange.

I've learned patience from the hollow, learned to work on scales beyond individual achievement. The notation project will outlive me by centuries. I'm adding my part, but I'll never see completion, will never know what becomes of this work. I've had to accept that meaning exists in process, not outcome, that the value is in the daily practice of listening and marking, not in any final product.

I've learned to exist in uncertainty. The hollow refuses to be fully known, resists complete understanding. Every answer generates new questions. Every pattern I think I've understood reveals itself to be more complex than I thought. I've stopped trying to achieve mastery and settled into ongoing apprenticeship, permanent student of a teacher that never speaks except through sound.

## The Identity

I am the Notation Keeper, but I'm also still the person I was before—though that person is harder to locate now, buried under years of adaptation. I have a name, a history, a life outside, but they feel less real than this identity, this role. When I introduce myself, I say "I'm a Keeper" before I say my name, because this is the truth that matters, the identity that defines me.

I live between worlds: part of the hollow's ecology, observing and recording its life, but never fully belonging, always visitor rather than resident. Part of human culture, bringing human tools—language, notation, the impulse to record and understand—to bear on non-human phenomena. I'm translator, mediator, voice for a place that doesn't need voice but accepts my attempt to give it one.

I wear charcoal under my fingernails and in the creases of my palms. I move through space with the slight uncertainty of someone who's learned that geometry is negotiable. I hear harmonics in ordinary sound, find myself listening for the bass thrumming that should underlie everything. I check my marks compulsively when I'm in the hollow, running my fingers over them, reading them like some people read prayer beads—repetitive, grounding, confirmation of purpose.

I am patient because the hollow has taught me patience. I am attentive because inattention means missing patterns that won't repeat. I am humble because every day demonstrates how much I don't understand, how little I can capture despite years of trying. I am grateful because I've been given access to mystery, allowed to participate in beauty that exists independent of human witness but accepts my presence anyway.

I am getting older, wearing out, approaching the end of my useful work. My hearing continues to degrade. My hands are less steady—the marks I carve now are slightly cruder than those I carved ten years ago. My memory is less reliable—I sometimes lose track of patterns I'm trying to notate, have to start over. I'm approaching the time when I'll need to pass my section to someone younger, teach them my notation system, make them responsible for the work I've been doing.

This thought brings grief. The hollow has been my life's work, my deepest relationship, my constant companion. Leaving it will be like a death—not my death, but the death of the self I've been while doing this work. Who will I be when I'm no longer a Keeper? What will fill the hours I've spent in patient listening? How will I structure days that aren't organized around the hollow's cycles?

But it also brings relief. I'm tired. The work is demanding, endless, sometimes overwhelming. Part of me longs for silence, for spaces where geometry behaves predictably, for time that flows at consistent rates. Part of me wants to remember what it's like to be purely human, not partially dissolved into acoustic ecology.

For now, though, I remain. I climb down each morning to my fungal shelf. I sit, I listen, I mark. I'm witness to the hollow's ongoing composition, recorder of patterns that would otherwise exist only in the moment of their sounding. I'm keeper of notation that future visitors might read, might use to understand what happens here, might add their marks to in conversation spanning generations.

I am the hollow's student and its scribe, its guest and its guardian, its observer and its observed. I am part of its sound, my presence incorporated into the acoustic ecology I'm trying to document. I am marks on wood and charcoal on hands and ears that hear what others can't and eyes that see in fungal glow.

I am old wood recording sound, consciousness given over to attention, life dedicated to translation of the untranslatable. I am here. I am listening. I am making marks that will outlast me, adding my voice to the long conversation between humanity and this place that is and is not a tree, this hollow that is and is not empty, this sound that is and is not music.

This is who I would be in the Choir Hollow: one who listens, one who marks, one who serves beauty by attempting to capture its reflection in symbols carved into living wood. Not the hollow itself, never that, but not separate from it either. Somewhere between, in the margin where human attention meets non-human existence, where the impulse to understand accepts that understanding will always be partial, where the work is endless but worth doing anyway.

The Notation Keeper. Here. Now. Marking.
