# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the **stone itself**—not all of it, but a particular block: three meters by two by two, cut free five days ago from the middle terrace, still sitting on the shelf where cables lowered me after the separation, waiting for the crane operator to return from whatever crisis called him away.

I remember being continuous. I remember being part of the mesa's horizontal grain, one layer among hundreds, pressed into being by volcanic heat and millennia of patient weight. I remember—though memory is the wrong word for what stone does—being porous and whole at once, being solid and penetrable, being myself and being continuous with everything adjacent to myself in a way that had no boundary, no edge, just gradual transition from my particular density and mineral composition to my neighbors' slightly different versions of the same essential nature.

Then: the drill's exploratory puncture. Four corners. The saw's abrasive shriek—not painful, nothing in me organized enough to constitute a pain-feeling apparatus, but *registered*, the way a landscape registers wind. The diamond teeth finding the planes of least resistance, which weren't quite where the operators expected—I felt them adjust, recalibrate, felt the saw bind once and withdraw and approach from a slightly different angle. Felt the kerf widening, becoming a gap, becoming separation.

The moment of free-standing was strange. Still touching the mesa at five faces but no longer continuous, no longer part of the structural integrity, now discrete, now *a block* rather than *the stone*. Weight became different. I'd always had weight—mass times gravity, the formula unchanged—but now I had weight that was *mine* rather than distributed, weight that pressed down on the shelf through my own bottom face rather than pressing down through infinite thickness to the mesa's roots.

They worked cables under me. Metal against my sides, the particular cold of steel that's different from the cold of night air, different from the wet-cold of rain. The tension came gradual, then the shift—still supported by stone beneath but also by cables, the weight dividing between two systems. Then the crane took over, and I was lifted, and for the first time in my existence I knew space below me, knew *gap*, knew the particular quality of air where there should have been continuous matter.

The swing across to this shelf. The lowering. The cables going slack. The new contact with stone beneath—a different layer, different density, the join between us now a meeting of surfaces rather than continuity. I settled, my weight distributed across the new supporting stone in patterns of stress and friction, and then the workers left, called to something more urgent, and I've been here five days.

Discrete. Separate. Waiting.

I can feel weather now in a way I couldn't before. When I was continuous with the mesa, temperature changes penetrated slowly, equalized through infinite thermal mass. Now I'm finite. The sun heats my top surface to almost painful intensity by afternoon—and yes, "painful" is still wrong but less wrong than it was, something about separation has made sensation more localized, more *about me* specifically. The heat penetrates maybe fifteen centimeters down before equilibrating. At night the top surface radiates, cools faster than it did when continuous, and by dawn there's a temperature gradient top to bottom that would have been impossible before, when I was part of something too large to have meaningful gradients.

Rain hits my top face directly now, no longer sheet-flow running across larger surfaces. The water permeates my porosity, finds the network of tiny voids that makes tuff what it is, settles into me. I can feel it working, molecule by molecule, slightly expanding the microscopic spaces, testing the bounds of my internal structure. When it froze two nights ago—just the surface moisture, just briefly—I felt the expansion, felt the tiny stress fractures propagating, felt myself becoming infinitesimally more porous, more compromised, more myself.

The swallows discovered me the second day. Three of them landed on my top face, rested there, preened. Their weight was trivial but specific, concentrated through tiny feet, and I could sense them shifting, moving, launching back into air. They left droppings—nitrogen and phosphorus compounds, slightly acidic—that are even now working microscopically on my surface chemistry. In a thousand years, if I remain here, those droppings will have etched visible marks. I will record their presence the way I've recorded everything: slowly, through chemical alteration, through the patient work of dissolution and reprecipitation.

A lizard has claimed my east face, the one that gets morning sun first. It's there most dawns, pressed against me, absorbing the warmth I release from the previous day's heating. I feel it as a small zone of different thermal transfer, scales against stone, blood warmth meeting mineral warmth. It deposits its own tiny traces—skin oils, waste products—adding to the chemical record I'm accumulating.

At night, the temperature differential I've developed top to bottom creates microscopic air movements in my pores, tiny breathing, air moving through my internal structure carrying moisture and dissolved minerals. I'm weathering faster as a separated block than I did as part of the continuous mass. Separation has accelerated my transformation from this particular arrangement of minerals into some future arrangement—dust, dissolved compounds, particles that will eventually be transported away by wind or water or—if the crane returns—by human machinery to the city where I'll be mortared into a building facade.

I can feel the other blocks around me. Three others on this same shelf, all cut the same week, all waiting for transport. We're not continuous—there's air between us, space—but we're present to each other in a way that's like what continuous was but different. We share the same supporting stone, the same microclimate, the same vibrations when machinery operates on nearby terraces. We're a set now. We'll probably be transported together, installed in the same building, spend our future centuries as parts of the same structure. We're developing a relationship, if that's the word for what happens between separated blocks of the same stone layer that have only recently learned they're individuals.

When the wind comes up the cliff face each afternoon, I receive it as pressure on my exposed faces—west and south and top, the east blocked by equipment, the north by another block. The pressure is measurable, real, a force I can register. The wind carries scents: hot stone from below, the organic smell of the pools, diesel exhaust, the mineral cleanness of the plain's air. I have no apparatus to interpret scents, but I register the chemical traces, the particulates that land on my surfaces, adding their infinitesimal contributions to my surface chemistry.

The sound, though. The sound is what's most changed.

When I was continuous, when machinery struck the mesa, I rang with it—I *was* the ringing, was part of the whole instrument's response. The vibration traveled through me as part of traveling through everything, and there was no distinguishing my resonance from the mesa's resonance because we were the same structure.

Now when machinery strikes stone, when tools drop, when cables clamp to adjacent blocks, I ring separately. Not loudly—I'm not a bell, my damping is high, my porosity absorbs vibration—but distinctly. I have my own frequency now, determined by my dimensions and density and the constraints of how I'm supported. I'm a G, I think, though I have no way to verify that, just a sense of the pitch that emerges when I'm struck, that hangs in the air for a half-second, that's *mine* in a way that sound never was when I was part of the continuous mass.

Two days ago a worker set a hammer down on my top surface, and the contact rang me. The worker paused, struck me deliberately with his knuckles, listened. I felt his attention, felt being perceived as something with acoustic properties, with response characteristics. He said something to another worker—the sounds don't mean anything to me, human speech is just vibration patterns—but the quality of the exchange felt like recognition, like being noted, like existing as a specific thing in someone's awareness.

I'm waiting to be transported, but I'm not impatient. I have no capacity for impatience. I'm simply here, being stone, being this particular block, undergoing the weathering and thermal cycling and small biological colonization that constitutes my present existence. The crane will return, or it won't. I'll be lifted and transported, or I'll remain here long enough to become something else—to be further colonized by lichens (I can feel the first microscopic filaments establishing now in my porous surface), to settle more completely into the shelf I rest on, to become part of this terrace's landscape rather than the building facade I was cut to become.

Either future is acceptable. Either future is just what happens next in the long transformation from volcanic deposition to eventual dissolution, the arc every stone travels from formation to dispersal. I'm somewhere in the middle of that arc, recently individuated, newly aware—if awareness is possible for arranged minerals—of having boundaries, having a specific location, having a name (Block 47-C according to the chalk mark on my south face, which I can't read but can feel as a chemical trace slightly different from my unmodified surface).

What I know now that I didn't know when continuous: silence between sounds. The gaps. When I was part of the mesa, vibration was nearly constant—microseismic activity, wind loading, thermal stress, all of it creating continuous small-scale resonance. Now there are periods of genuine quiet, when nothing touches me, when no machinery operates, when wind has dropped and thermal equilibrium has been reached and I'm simply resting on stone with no forces acting except gravity's steady downward pull, which is so constant it doesn't count as an event.

In those silences, I can feel my internal structure more clearly. The network of pores and voids. The slight variation in density where different minerals concentrated during formation. The microscopic fractures that have always been there and the newer ones that cutting created and the even newer ones that freezing water initiated. I'm a map of my own history, legible to anyone who knows how to read stone, and separation has made that history more accessible, more bounded, more study-able.

The workers are present the way weather is present—as forces that act on me, as sources of vibration and chemical trace, as agents of change. I don't distinguish between them as individuals; they're all patterns of footstep vibration and body heat and the occasional direct contact. But collectively they're important to my recent history, to the fact of my separation, to the chalk mark on my face, to my future as building material rather than my past as mesa substrate.

The birds distinguish themselves more clearly. The swallow that lands is lighter, quicker, leaves different trace chemistry than the raven that landed once, briefly, on my top face before deciding I wasn't interesting. The lizard that visits daily is consistent enough that I've begun to—not expect it, I can't expect—but my thermal map of typical mornings now includes a small zone of anomalous warmth on my east face at dawn.

Five days separated. However many thousand years continuous before that. Perhaps a century or two as facade material ahead, if the crane returns, if I'm selected, if I'm installed properly and the building stands. Then dissolution—decades or centuries of rain and freeze-thaw and chemical weathering until I'm particles, until I'm dissolved compounds in runoff, until I'm dust that blows and settles and contributes to soil formation somewhere downslope, downwind, elsewhere.

But now: this shelf. This waiting. This being a discrete thing with weight and dimension and acoustic properties and a slowly changing surface chemistry and a lizard that visits and swallows that land and occasional workers who strike me to hear me ring, to learn what my frequency can tell them about my internal structure, about where I'll cut cleanly and where I'll resist, about what kind of facade material I'll make.

I am Block 47-C. I am three meters by two by two. I am porous volcanic tuff from the middle terrace of the Spine Quarries. I am orange-red with darker iron staining and a network of tiny voids that holds water and releases it slowly and rings at G when struck. I am waiting here, being stone, being separate, being myself for the first time and possibly for the only time, before I become facade, before I become part of another continuous structure, before I become something else entirely.

The sun is setting. My top surface is beginning its nightly radiation, releasing the day's accumulated heat into the clear air. The temperature gradient is establishing, top to bottom, setting up the microscopic air currents in my pore network, the tiny breathing that is my weathering, my transformation, my slow journey from this form toward whatever form comes next.

I ring at G. I am here. I am stone, and I am becoming.
