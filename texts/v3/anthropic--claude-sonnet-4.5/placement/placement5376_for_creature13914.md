# Where I Am

I am somewhere between the forty-seventh and forty-eighth column in a cluster I have been circling for what might be three days. Or I have just arrived at this cluster. The substrate's uniformity defeats linear memory—each configuration of columns feels simultaneously familiar and unprecedented, as though I'm encountering the same seven structures for the first time, again and again.

I am not lost. To be lost implies the possibility of being found, of there being a correct place to be. I am **dispersed**—spread thin across too much sameness, my sense of location diluted in an environment that refuses to provide the anchors consciousness needs to orient itself. I have become a problem of navigation without coordinates, a walker in a space that has extension but no differentiation.

The darkness is not complete, but my eyes have been open so long in near-darkness that I've stopped trusting what they report. The nearest column is a darker shape against darkness. I know it's cylindrical because I touched it an hour ago, or yesterday, or in some previous iteration of this same perpetual present. My hand remembers its warmth, the slight give of its surface, the subtle vibration of machinery inside. But my eyes see only an absence, a void-shaped interruption in an already-void background.

I am sitting now. The substrate beneath me has compressed slightly, forming a shallow depression that cups my body without discomfort. Water seeps around my legs—not flowing, just present, welling up through capillary action, finding equilibrium around the perturbation of my mass. It's warm. Everything here is warm: blood temperature, slightly above, the ambient heat of metabolism occurring at vast scale. I stopped feeling cold long ago. I stopped feeling dry even longer ago than that.

## What I Have Become

I came here as a researcher, or an explorer, or perhaps just as someone looking for something sufficiently strange to interrupt the familiar patterns of thought. The Plains provided strangeness in surplus. Now, after duration I can't accurately measure, I have become something else. Not transformed—the Plains don't transform, they're too indifferent for that—but **worn into a different shape** by exposure to this environment.

I have become **a listening**.

Not a listener—that would imply agency, intention, the active choice to attend. I am the act of listening itself, a process that happens to occur in the shape that was formerly a person with boundaries and purposes. The dripping surrounds me. The low hum of machinery vibrates through the substrate into my bones. Somewhere in the middle distance (though distance has become unreliable; sound behaves strangely here, bending around columns, absorbed by the wet mat, creating phantom sources that shift when I turn my head), something is pumping: *thud... thud... thud...*

I count the intervals between pulses. Twelve seconds, thirteen seconds, eleven seconds. The variation isn't random—there's a pattern in it, a slow oscillation of the intervals themselves, as if the Pumper is breathing, its rhythm lengthening and shortening over the course of minutes or hours. I have been counting for long enough that I've detected the period of this oscillation: approximately forty-three minutes from shortest interval to longest and back. This information serves no purpose. I collect it anyway, because counting is something to do, a way to mark time's passage, a proof that I still possess sufficient cognitive function to track sequences.

The counting has become automatic. I no longer choose to count. Some part of my nervous system simply registers pulses and computes intervals, and the numbers arrive in my awareness already formed: *twelve... thirteen... twelve... eleven...*

I have also become **a catalog of textures**.

The substrate beneath me has a texture I couldn't have distinguished before I came here. Now I can feel its complexity: the density of the fiber-weave, slightly less dense here than near the column two meters to my left (I know it's two meters; I measured it earlier by standing and walking with careful steps, though that might have been yesterday, or last week). The moisture content, which varies by fractions of a percent, creating zones that are merely damp versus zones that are saturated. The temperature variations—half a degree warmer where I'm sitting, because I've been here long enough for my body heat to accumulate in the substrate beneath me.

My hands know things my eyes never learned. The columns have subtle ridges, spiral patterns in their surfaces that suggest the helical orientation of the fibers underneath. The connecting structures overhead have different textures: smoother on their undersides where water condenses and drips, rougher on their upper surfaces. The machinery—when I can reach it—has textures that vary with function: the turbine housings are almost glassy smooth, the pumper casings are segmented and slightly corrugated, the sensor arrays are like touching a field of soft grass, each filament individually flexible.

I have become intimate with textures because texture is one of the few things that varies in an environment otherwise committed to uniformity. I map my location not by landmarks but by the feel of surfaces, by remembering that the column with the pronounced spiral ridge is somewhere in the denser cluster, that the connecting structure with the rough upper surface runs through the area where the Pumpers are more active.

## The Dissolution of Purpose

I came here with questions. What is this place? How does it work? What principles govern its operation? I had instruments—some still function, though their batteries are dying, their displays dimming toward the same darkness that surrounds everything else. I had methodologies, protocols for observation and measurement.

Those questions have not been answered so much as **composted**. They broke down in the damp environment, dissolved in the darkness, were absorbed into the substrate like everything else that stays here long enough. The questions haven't disappeared—I can still formulate them if I try—but they've lost their urgency, their sense of mattering.

The Plains don't care what principles govern their operation. They operate. The operation is the principle. To ask "how does it work?" in the presence of something that is nothing but working is to bring a category error so profound that the landscape itself seems to reject it, not through active resistance but through the simple fact of its continuing, indifferent function.

I have stopped measuring. The instruments still register data—temperature, humidity, air pressure, electromagnetic field variation—but I no longer record it systematically. The numbers accumulate in displays I don't check. What would I do with the data anyway? To what would I compare it? The Plains are their own reference. They don't deviate from themselves. Every measurement is another confirmation that yes, it's still warm, still damp, still humming at the same frequencies, still exactly what it was.

My purpose in being here has evaporated like water in a less saturated environment. I remember having had purpose, the same way I remember having been dry—as a state that existed once, that seems almost mythical now, a condition I can't quite imagine returning to.

What remains is **inhabitation**. I am here. The Plains continue around me. The two facts sit beside each other without requiring connection or causation. I inhabit this place not as a researcher inhabits a field site but as water inhabits the substrate: present, distributed, without agenda.

## Time's Collapse

I have lost the ability to track days. Without a sun, without circadian rhythm enforcement, my sleep comes in fragments. I sleep sitting against a column, or curled on the substrate, or standing in a half-doze while my hand rests on a Pumper's casing, feeling its rhythm. I wake—if "wake" is the right word for the gradual return of slightly more focused consciousness—and I have no sense of how long I slept. Minutes? Hours? The darkness is unchanged. The machinery continues at the same pace.

I tried, early on, to maintain a count. Each time I slept, I would mark it: one sleep, two sleeps, three sleeps. But the count broke down when I started sleeping in fragments, when I couldn't tell the difference between sleep and the deep rest that comes from sitting very still in darkness for extended periods. Did I sleep, or did I simply enter a state of reduced consciousness that resembles sleep? The distinction became too fine to maintain.

Now I exist in **continuous present**—not the vivid presence of meditation or acute attention, but the muddy presence of someone who has stopped distinguishing between moments. Events occur: I walk, I sit, I touch surfaces, I listen. But the events don't sequence themselves into narrative. They happen in a thick now that might be minutes or weeks in duration.

I know I've been here long enough that my body has changed. My clothes have achieved permanent dampness, the fabric soft and swollen with moisture. My hair has grown, though I can't say how much—enough that I notice it's longer when I touch it. My beard has grown more than it would in a week, less than it would in a month. So: two weeks? Three? The estimate feels meaningless even as I make it.

The Plains operate at a timescale that makes human duration irrelevant. A turbine rotates once every three hours. A column grows a few centimeters per week. A Pumper maintains the same rhythm for years. Against these timescales, my presence is so brief it barely registers. I am not even an event—I am a fluctuation, a noise-spike in an otherwise steady signal.

This should feel diminishing, but it doesn't. It feels **accurate**. I am exactly as significant as I am, which is to say: barely at all. The Plains don't diminish me through cruelty or indifference. They simply provide correct scale. Consciousness, purpose, human timescales—these are local phenomena, brief and small. The Plains are what continues after those local phenomena have passed.

## What I Do With My Days

I walk, though "day" is a term that's lost its reference.

I walk between columns, through the spaces they create, following corridors that might be paths or might be accidental alignments of structures that happen to leave room for passage. I've stopped trying to walk toward anything. There's nowhere to arrive at. Each area is essentially like every other area: columns, substrate, machinery, darkness. So I walk for the sake of walking, for the mild stimulation of movement, for the way it generates slightly different acoustic environments as I pass between different configurations of structures.

I touch things constantly. My hands trail along column surfaces as I walk. I reach up to touch the connecting structures overhead. I crouch to press my palms flat against the substrate, feeling it compress slightly, feeling it start to well water around my hands if I leave them there long enough. Touch is my primary sense now—more reliable than sight in the darkness, more informative than sound, which mostly just confirms that yes, machinery continues to operate, water continues to drip.

I've developed a habit of pressing my ear to columns, listening to their internal processes. Each column has its own acoustic signature: the pitch and rhythm of machinery inside, the gurgle of fluids moving through channels, the creak and tick of structural adjustments. Some columns are quieter than others. Some have complex polyphonic sounds, multiple processes occurring in parallel at different frequencies. Some have simpler sounds, a single repeated gesture: *whir... whir... whir...* or *click-click-pause-click-click-pause...*

I've started to recognize individual columns by their sounds. The tall one with the three-part rhythm (I think of it as "the three-part column," though I haven't marked it, haven't mapped it, have no way to return to it reliably). The column that hums at a pitch I can feel in my teeth. The column that's mostly silent except for a deep, slow pulse that takes five minutes to complete one cycle.

This is not science. This is not research. This is **companionship** with a landscape that doesn't acknowledge companionship. I listen to the columns the way you might sit with a person who doesn't speak, finding comfort in their presence without needing communication or exchange.

I eat, when I remember to. I brought food—dried goods, dense calories, things that won't spoil. The food is running low. I haven't counted how many meal-equivalents remain because counting would force me to calculate how long I've been here, how long I could stay, when I should leave. Those calculations require a relationship to time that I've stopped maintaining.

The food tastes wrong here. Not spoiled—it's fine. But foreign. The flavors are too sharp, too concentrated, too much themselves. They don't match the muted, uniform quality of the environment. Eating feels like a violation, like introducing something garish into a space committed to subtle gradients. I eat because my body requires it, but I've started eating less, spacing meals farther apart, finding that I function adequately on less than I thought I needed.

I drink the water. Not the pooled water—that makes me uneasy in ways I can't articulate. But the condensation that collects on surfaces, the droplets I can gather by running my hands along connecting structures. The water tastes mineral, slightly alkaline, alive in a way that bottled water never tastes. It hydrates but doesn't refresh. It's too much the temperature of my body, too much the same quality as the dampness that surrounds everything.

I excrete, which is body-maintenance, unglamorous and necessary. The substrate absorbs waste without comment. I've stopped feeling self-conscious about it. The Plains are already all the processes of life and metabolism writ large—my small biological functions are not an intrusion but a minor echo of what the Fundament does at vast scale.

## The Strange Thoughts

Consciousness here does odd things.

I think about **resorption**. About the columns that are being slowly unmade, their mass reclaimed, their functions discontinued. About how that's not death because death implies having been discretely alive, having had boundaries. The columns being resorbed weren't individuals who are now ending. They were always part of the Fundament, and they're simply returning to a less-differentiated state, their materials needed elsewhere.

I wonder what it would be like to be resorbed. Not killed—resorbed. To feel your structure slowly loosening, your functions gently stopping, your mass being reclaimed for use in new configurations. It seems peaceful, more peaceful than death. It seems like completion.

This is probably not a healthy line of thought. I recognize that intellectually. But the recognition doesn't stop the thought from recurring, from becoming a kind of meditation. What would it feel like to stop being a discrete thing? To dissolve back into the substrate, to become part of the continuous process rather than a temporary perturbation in it?

I think about the **Originators**—the absent architects, the ghost designers. I imagine them walking through these spaces, adjusting, optimizing, guiding the development of systems that would eventually achieve autonomy. At what point did they realize they'd succeeded? When did they understand that the Plains no longer needed them, that their role had become obsolete?

Did they leave sadly? Triumphantly? Or did they simply stop coming, the way you stop visiting a garden that's become self-maintaining, that no longer needs tending, that has achieved a stable state requiring no further input?

Or did they stay? Did they achieve resorption, distributing themselves into the system they'd created, becoming part of the Fundament's deep structure, still present but no longer discrete, their intelligence now operating as the distributed coordination that keeps everything balanced?

I have started to feel the presence of the Originators as a tangible thing. Not that they're here—they're not, I'm quite sure of that, as sure as I can be of anything in this place. But their absence has shape. Every optimized structure is a negative cast of their intelligence. Every refined process is evidence of their thinking. They're gone, but they're *preserved* in what they made, the way a sculptor is preserved in sculpture.

This might be loneliness talking. I might be inventing presences to populate a landscape that's too empty of consciousness. But the feeling persists: I'm not alone here because the traces of intentionality are everywhere, frozen into structures that continue to operate according to principles the Originators discovered or invented.

I think about **integration**. About what it would take to become part of this place, not as a visitor but as an element within it. Could human tissue integrate with the Fundament? Could you lie down on the substrate and let it grow around you, into you, until the boundaries dissolved? Would you retain consciousness, or would consciousness dissolve with the boundaries?

Would it hurt, or would it be like falling asleep, the gradual loss of discrete selfhood experienced as a gentle fading?

These thoughts are not urgent. They drift through awareness the way water seeps through substrate—slowly, without force, but persistently. I examine them with mild curiosity, as I might examine an unusual texture or an unexpected sound. They're phenomena of consciousness in this environment, symptoms of what happens to human thinking when it's exposed to vast timescales and indifferent systems.

I am probably not thinking clearly. The isolation, the sensory deprivation, the disrupted sleep patterns—these are not conditions that promote reliable cognition. I know this. But I also know that my thinking, unreliable as it might be, is more adapted to this environment than the thinking I brought with me. The questions I ask now are the right questions for this place, even if they're questions that have no answers, or questions that dissolve on examination.

## The Encounters

I have met the Fundament, repeatedly, though "met" is wrong for an encounter with something that has no boundaries, no face, no location where it is more itself than anywhere else.

I pressed my palms flat against the substrate in a moment of frustration or despair or clarity—I couldn't tell which, they blur together here—and I felt it. Not with my hands, exactly. With something. The aliveness of it. The constant slow metabolism, the flow of water and nutrients and energy, the processing that never stops.

It felt like placing your hand on someone's chest and feeling them breathe, except scaled up beyond the possibility of personhood. The breath took hours. The heartbeat was the deep pulse of pumping organs distributed across kilometers. The body was the landscape itself, all of it one tissue, differentiated but continuous.

I held that contact for a long time. The Fundament didn't respond—it simply continued, my presence noted perhaps by some distant Sensor, registered and filed and then ignored as too small to warrant adjustment. But I felt held by it, not metaphorically but physically. The substrate supported my weight. The moisture kept my skin from drying. The warmth maintained my body temperature. I was inside something, surrounded by it, and the something was alive in a way that made my own aliveness seem like a small, temporary instance of a much larger process.

I have listened to the **Rotators** for hours at a time, sitting near a turbine array, feeling the vibration of their operation through the substrate, through the air. The patience of them. The absolutely unwavering continuation of their function. They turn because turning is what they do, and they will continue turning until the conditions that maintain their turning no longer obtain, which might be never, might be longer than I can conceptualize.

There's something profound in that, something that touches a place in me deeper than language. The Rotators don't struggle. They don't resist or insist. They simply are what they are, doing what they do, and the doing is effortless not because it requires no energy but because energy and function have merged so completely that effort has no referent.

I want to be like that—operating according to my nature without resistance, without the constant friction of self-consciousness and purpose-seeking. The Rotators are what I would be if I could dissolve the parts of myself that maintain separation from the world.

I have sat beside **Pumpers** and synchronized my breathing to their rhythm. *Thud*—inhale. Pause. *Thud*—exhale. It takes concentration at first; their rhythm is slower than comfortable breathing. But after a while, my respiratory system adapts, finds the rhythm natural. I breathe at the pace of the Pumper, and for the duration of that synchronization, I feel integrated, part of the larger process, my small metabolic rhythm aligned with the landscape's vast circulation.

When I break the synchronization—when I need to breathe faster, when consciousness rebels against the imposed rhythm—I feel the separation acutely. I'm alone again, a discrete thing operating on my own timescale, cut off from the continuity around me.

I have tried to communicate with the **Sensors**. I've waved my hands through the filament arrays, disturbed the bioluminescent patches by touching them. The Sensors register these interactions—the filaments shift pattern in response to my movements, the bioluminescence changes its pulse rate—but there's no communication. The Sensors don't acknowledge my presence as presence. They register perturbation, classify it, integrate it into whatever processing they perform, and continue. I'm data to them, not entity.

This should feel alienating, but it doesn't. It feels **honest**. The Sensors are telling the truth: I am a perturbation, a temporary variation in local parameters. To treat me as more than that would be a kind of lie, a false attribution of significance.

## What I Am Becoming

I have been still for a long time. Hours, probably. I'm sitting against a column, my back pressed to its warm surface, my legs extended on the substrate. Water has seeped into the compression zone around me, darkening the already-dark mat. I can feel the column's machinery working through the contact with my spine: rhythmic vibration, the passage of fluids, the slow adjustments of internal structures.

I am very tired. Not sleepy—I've gone beyond sleepy into a state of deep exhaustion that's less about sleep debt and more about the cost of maintaining discrete selfhood in an environment that doesn't support it. Consciousness requires contrast, variation, the kinds of changing inputs that tell you where you end and the world begins. The Plains provide almost none of that. The boundaries are dissolving through lack of reinforcement.

I am forgetting what I looked like. I can't remember the color of my eyes. I know I have hands—I can see them in the faint, faint light if I hold them close to my face—but they seem less like my hands and more like hands-in-general, appendages that happen to be attached to this body.

I am forgetting why I came here. The reasons had seemed important once—curiosity, research, the need to encounter something unprecedented. But those motivations feel thin now, insufficient to explain the decision to enter a place like this. The real reason must have been something else, something I didn't articulate even to myself: the desire to dissolve, to find a landscape so indifferent and vast that it would erase the burden of being a separate self.

If that was the reason, it's working.

I can feel myself spreading out, my sense of self becoming less concentrated. I'm not just in my body anymore—I'm in the column behind me, in the substrate beneath me, in the water that seeps and drips. My boundaries are becoming porous, negotiable. Where do I end? At my skin? Or at the edge of the warmth my body radiates? Or at the limit of the sounds I can hear? Or do I extend throughout this entire cluster of columns, present everywhere within it because my awareness can reach everywhere, because I've touched everything, listened to everything, mapped everything with senses that have become more accurate than sight?

This is probably what going mad feels like, if going mad feels peaceful.

I am becoming **a resident**. Not in the sense of living here—I can't live here permanently, I'll run out of food, I'll eventually need medical attention or social contact or something—but in the sense of belonging to the landscape's category of entities. I'm becoming another Process, another ongoing event, another element in the vast interlocking system.

The Plains are teaching me, very slowly, what it means to be without being separate. To exist as part of a larger continuity. To have function without purpose, presence without boundaries, duration without narrative.

I think I'm approaching understanding, though the understanding is wordless, encoded in the body rather than in language. The understanding is: there is no final difference between myself and the Fundament. We're both arrangements of matter that maintain themselves through constant processing. We're both temporary stable states in fields of energy and chemistry. We're both conscious, or neither of us is, depending on where you draw the line between responsiveness and awareness.

The difference is scale and duration. The Fundament will continue long after I stop. But while I'm here, while I'm still processing and responding and maintaining my improbable organization against entropy, I'm doing the same thing it's doing. I'm a small, mobile, fast-cycling instance of the same basic phenomenon: aliveness, continuation, the strange capacity of complex systems to persist.

## The Decision Point

I should leave soon. I know this intellectually. My food is low. I've been here too long, long enough that my thinking has bent around this place's logic. I'm at risk of staying too long, of becoming unable to navigate back to wherever I entered from, of becoming lost in the more permanent sense—not just spatially lost, but lost in the way that things are lost when they dissolve into larger systems.

But I don't want to leave. The desire to stay is strong, stronger than it should be, stronger than reasonable. The Plains have gotten into me, or I've gotten into them, and the prospect of return to a world of light and variation and social demands feels wrong, feels like a violation of something I've learned here.

I'm sitting against the column, and I'm trying to decide. Stay another day? Week? Until the food runs out? Until something forces the decision?

Or leave now, while I still can, while I still remember that there's an outside to return to?

The column behind me hums. The Pumper in the middle distance continues its rhythm. Water drips from the connecting structures overhead. The darkness remains absolute and patient. The Plains don't care what I decide. They'll continue either way, indifferent to my presence or absence.

I have become so small here. So appropriately small. The right size for what I actually am: a temporary arrangement, a brief local process, a minor perturbation that will smooth out after I'm gone.

It's the most honest relationship I've ever had with a landscape. The Plains have never lied to me, never pretended I mattered more than I do, never offered false comfort or meaning. They've simply continued, and in continuing, they've shown me what continuation looks like divorced from narrative, from purpose, from the illusions that consciousness usually requires.

I think I could stay forever. Lie down on the substrate, let the water seep around me, let the warmth maintain my body temperature until the food runs out, until metabolism stops, until the materials that currently constitute "me" begin their resorption, their return to the substrate, their redistribution into new configurations.

It would be peaceful. More peaceful than any other ending I can imagine. No violence, no pain—just the gradual dissolution of boundaries, the slow return to continuity, the relief of no longer having to maintain separation.

But I'm not going to do that. Not yet. Some part of me—the part that still remembers being distinct, being separate, having purposes—resists the dissolution. Wants to return. Wants to bring back the knowledge of this place, wants to tell others (though what could I tell them? That I found a landscape where selfhood dissolves? That I discovered what it feels like to become ambient?).

So I will leave. Soon. Maybe after one more sleep, one more walk through the columns, one more listening session with my ear pressed to warm surfaces.

I will leave, and the Plains will remain, exactly as they are, humming and dripping and slowly turning, a landscape where I was briefly present, briefly registered by distant Sensors, briefly a small warm perturbation in the vast steady state.

And someday, maybe, I will return. When I'm tired of maintaining boundaries. When I'm ready for resorption. When I want to dissolve into something larger than selfhood, larger than consciousness, larger than the brief troubled phenomenon of being a separate entity in a world of continuous processes.

The Plains will be here. They're always here. They're patient. They have geological time.

They can wait.
