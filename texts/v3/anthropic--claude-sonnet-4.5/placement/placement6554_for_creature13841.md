# In the Drowned Scaffolds, I Am the Rust-Keeper

I live in the hollow throat of the old drilling platform on the third pillar, the one they called Meridian-7 before the names stopped mattering. My body has learned the dimensions of this space so perfectly that I can move through it in absolute darkness: three meters across, roughly circular, the floor tilted at seventeen degrees toward the gap where the deck plates rusted through. The walls are a lattice of corrosion—structural beams reduced to lace, cable bundles weeping orange tears, panel surfaces pocked and holy with dissolution. I know every protrusion. I've mapped every sharp edge with small scars on my shoulders and hips. This geometry is my first language.

I am human, or was human, or remain human in some technical sense. The categories feel increasingly irrelevant. My body still has two arms, two legs, a head containing a brain that generates something like consciousness. But I've been here long enough that the adaptations have gone deep. My hands are calloused into leather, the fingertips developed into sensitive pads that read texture like braille—I can distinguish between Type-II marine alloy and standard carbon steel by touch, can estimate remaining structural integrity by how the rust flakes under my thumbnail. My feet have spread, the arches collapsed into platforms that grip the slick surfaces. The soles are so tough now that I rarely bother with the boots anymore; they're in a corner somewhere, slowly becoming substrate for the orange bacterial blooms.

My lungs have learned to extract oxygen from air that's more water than gas, that hangs heavy with salt and mist and the smell of old iron. I breathe in a rhythm synced to the turbulence cycles—shallow during the high-flow phases when the air is thick enough to choke on, deeper when the pressure drops and the mist thins slightly. I don't think about this anymore. My body simply knows.

My routine is prayer, or would be called prayer if I retained the religious vocabulary. I wake when the light shifts, when the particular angle of refracted sun through the water-curtain to the east hits the wall beside my sleeping alcove. This happens at approximately the same time each day, though "day" is a loose construction when the light is always filtered, always moving. I wake, I assess the body—joints, breath, the small inventory of pains that signal what needs attention—and then I begin the rounds.

The platform is dying, has been dying since before I arrived, will continue dying after I'm gone. But the rate of death is negotiable. This is my function, my discipline: to slow the dissolution, to maintain the structure against the patient work of water and oxidation and time. I am hospice care for architecture.

I start with the primary support beams, the four massive columns that rise from the stone of the pillar through the platform deck to what remains of the superstructure. These are the critical points. When one of these goes—and one will, eventually—the entire platform will fold, will slide down the curved surface of the pillar and into the kelp-forest two hundred meters below, joining the other debris I can see on clear days: twisted metal shapes, the geometric ghosts of human intention, slowly being absorbed into the ecosystem.

The northeastern beam is the worst. I've watched it deteriorate over the years—first the protective coating failed, then the surface rust began, then the deep corrosion started eating inward. Now it's maybe seventy percent original cross-section, the rest having flaked away or been consumed by the rust-eaters. I run my hands over it, feeling for new weak points, testing sections with careful pressure. There's a spot about shoulder-height that's softer than it should be, the metal fatigued, a crack propagating invisibly inside the remaining structure.

I mark it with a scratch—three parallel lines, my notation system—which means: watch this, check it daily, prepare for failure. Then I move to the repair work.

I have resources, though the word is generous. In one corner of the platform, I've accumulated metal salvage: pieces scavenged from smaller debris, sections cut from non-critical structures, fragments that wash up on the stone below and that I haul up during low-turbulence windows. I also have wire—precious, carefully hoarded—that I've woven from the cable-bundles that hang throughout the platform. The cables were steel braids originally, hundreds of tiny wires twisted together. Most have corroded through, but some core wires remain intact, and I've learned to extract these, clean them, braid them into new cordage.

For the weak spot on the beam, I fabricate a splint. I take a section of salvaged plate metal, bend it—using the old gear housing as an anvil, my body weight as the hammer—into a curved shape that matches the beam's profile. Then I wire it in place, wrapping the wire tight, using the friction and compression to create a kind of external skeleton. It won't restore the beam's original strength, but it redistributes stress, reduces the load on the weakened section. It buys time.

This work takes perhaps an hour. My hands move with the sureness of ritual. I've done this hundreds of times, on every beam, every joint, every critical connection. The platform is now more splint than original structure in places—a patchwork body held together by improvised surgery.

While I work, the ecosystem works around me. Grip-walkers move across the platform deck, hunting or grazing, indifferent to my presence. I've become furniture to them, predictable, non-threatening. The large ones sometimes use me as cover, crouching beside my leg while scanning for prey. I've learned not to move suddenly; their startle response is explosive and has left me with scars.

The crab-shapes are bolder. They actively approach me, claws raised in what might be curiosity or threat assessment, clicking their way across the deck to investigate whatever I'm working on. They're particularly interested in fresh rust-exposure—when I scrape away loose scale to assess metal beneath, they converge, fighting each other for position, scraping at the newly revealed surface with their mandibles. I've learned to work around them, to let them have the loose material while I evaluate what's structural and what's already lost.

Flying rays nest on the platform's upper surfaces, in the tangles of cable and the sheltered spots beneath the remaining roof sections. I hear them constantly: the scraping calls, the wing-rustle when they come and go, the sounds of chicks during breeding season—a kind of thin, mechanical peeping that carries through the platform's hollow spaces. Their guano accumulates on every horizontal surface, white and caustic, and I scrape it away from critical areas during the rounds. But I leave it where it doesn't matter—it feeds the bacterial blooms, which feed the rust-eaters, which contribute to the platform's dissolution, but this is acceptable. I'm slowing death, not preventing it. I've learned the difference.

The gear—the one that still turns, the six-foot diameter mechanism on the platform's western side—requires daily attention. It's not critical to the platform's structural integrity, but it's become personal. This gear still functions. After everything that's failed, everything that's seized or collapsed or corroded through, this one gear still turns, still rotates in its ancient bearing, still does what it was designed to do. I've decided it will continue turning as long as I'm capable of maintaining it.

The maintenance is simple: clear the accumulated rust-eaters from the teeth, check for new pitting or cracking, ensure the bearing isn't binding. The bearing is the vulnerability—it's a massive piece of equipment, probably weighs several hundred kilos, and it rotates on a shaft that's supported by a housing embedded in the platform structure. The housing is corroding like everything else, and if it fails, the gear will bind, then seize, then eventually break free and fall.

I've reinforced the housing six times now. The current splint-work is complex—a cage of salvaged metal and wire that surrounds the housing, transfers the load to multiple points on the platform frame. It's holding, but I check it obsessively. If the gear stops turning, something in me will stop too. I'm aware this is irrational, that the gear is just a mechanism, that its function or failure has no objective meaning. But meaning is something we generate, and I've generated meaning here.

After the rounds—which take most of the morning, measured by light-shifts—I eat. My diet is constrained by what this environment provides and what my body has learned to tolerate. The kelp is edible if prepared correctly: stripped of the outer layer, the inner tissue pounded to break down the fibers, then dried or eaten immediately. It's not good—it tastes like salted leather with an aftertaste of minerals—but it provides enough calories and nutrients to sustain basic function.

The arthropods are better, protein-rich, and abundant enough that harvesting them has no noticeable impact on the population. I've learned which species are safe: the finger-length grazers that cluster in the kelp-forests are best, their meat sweet and slightly oily. The crab-shapes are edible too, but dangerous to catch—their crushing claws can break fingers, and infection is always a risk. I take them opportunistically, when I find one trapped in platform debris or freshly dead from territorial combat.

The sky-fish are rare protein, a delicacy. I can't hunt them directly—they're too fast, too strong, and they live in the water-columns where I can't follow. But occasionally one is injured, or sick, or makes a miscalculation during a strike and impacts the platform with stunning force. When this happens, I move quickly. Fresh fish doesn't last long in this environment; the rot sets in within hours. I eat what I can immediately—the flesh is dense and rich, oily enough to leave my lips slick—and I dry the remainder in thin strips that I hang in the platform's upper reaches where air flow is strongest.

Water is everywhere and also precious. The water flowing over the platform is salt, undrinkable without processing. I've built a still from salvaged metal: a curved panel that I suspend over a small fire, with kelp-fiber insulation to trap heat, and collection gutters that channel condensate to a container. The fuel for the fire is dried kelp and the occasional piece of wood from the kelpwood trees—not the living trees, but deadfall that I find accumulated in crevices on the pillar surface. The still produces perhaps a liter per day, which is barely enough. I'm chronically dehydrated—my lips are permanently cracked, my urine dark—but I'm alive, and in this place that counts as success.

The afternoons I spend on what I think of as the archive work, though that might be aggrandizing. I write. I document. I maintain a record of what's happening to the platform, what I observe in the ecosystem, what I remember of how I came to be here. The medium is problematic—paper dissolved years ago—so I've adapted. I scratch text into metal plates using a sharp wire, the letters crude but legible. Each plate takes days to fill. I have maybe thirty plates now, stored in a dry cavity within the platform frame, wrapped in salvaged rubber to protect against moisture.

What I'm documenting is probably useless. Who would read it? Who would find it? The platforms are failing, the human presence here—whatever it was, whatever operation established these structures—is long ended. I might be the last person in the Drowned Scaffolds, or I might be one of several, scattered across the pillars, each maintaining their own isolated routine. I haven't seen another human in three years, maybe four. Time has become elastic.

But the documentation continues because it provides structure, because the act of describing slows down observation, forces precision. When I write "northeastern beam, seventy percent remaining cross-section, crack propagating at shoulder-height," I'm not just recording data—I'm teaching myself to see accurately, to think in terms of systems and failure modes and probable futures. This discipline keeps the mind functional. Without it, I think I would dissolve, would become as weathered and smooth as the stone pillars, my identity eroded to something simple and reactive.

Some afternoons I climb. The platform is roughly two hundred meters above the nominal base of the pillar, but the pillar itself rises another thousand meters or more. I've never reached the top—the upper reaches require equipment I don't have, skills I never developed—but I climb as high as I can manage, which is another two or three hundred meters above the platform.

The climbing is technically unnecessary. It doesn't contribute to platform maintenance. It doesn't find food or water. But it provides variation, physical challenge, and a different perspective. From higher up, I can see the cluster of pillars more completely: the arrangement of maybe twenty columns visible from this position, each one crowned with its own growth, its own water-architecture, its own slowly deteriorating artifacts. I can see other platforms, other gears, the cable-bridges that once connected these structures into a network.

And I can see how it's all failing together. A platform on the fifth pillar collapsed sometime in the last year—I remember it being present, and now it's simply absent, just a scar on the stone and some debris in the kelp below. A cable-bridge swings free from the seventh pillar, its far anchor failed, the bridge now just a long pendulum of corroded metal. The pattern is clear: everything human-made is in decline, and the decline is accelerating as structures reach the end of their fatigue-life, as corrosion damage accumulates, as the patient work of this environment continues.

From the higher vantage, I also see the life more clearly. The scale of the ecosystem becomes apparent—the sheer biomass, the density of activity. The kelp-forests are thick enough to obscure the stone in places. The water-columns are visible as hanging structures, made apparent by the fish-schools within them, the millions of moving bodies catching light. The flying rays are numerous, dozens visible at once, their wings catching updrafts, their paths crisscrossing in patterns that suggest traffic, intention, a community of aerial hunters.

And I see the mist-globes, the hanging spheres of atomized water that drift between pillars. From the platform they're abstract features, just cloudy masses. From higher up, their structure is clearer—the way they form at turbulence intersections, the way they merge and split, the way they're inhabited by visible swarms of small life. They look like organs, like lungs or neurons, functional components of some larger system that I'm inside of but can't fully perceive.

The climbing also serves a practical purpose: I'm scouting. The platform will fail eventually—this is certain—and when it does, I'll need somewhere else to be. I've identified potential locations: a large cavity in the pillar surface about fifty meters above the platform, accessible via a climb that's dangerous but possible; an overhang with a relatively dry shelf another hundred meters up; the remains of another platform on a neighboring pillar, reached by a gap-crossing during calm turbulence. None of these options are good, but they're contingencies, and thinking in contingencies is how one survives in an environment that's actively hostile to long-term human presence.

Evenings I spend in maintenance of the self, which is its own form of structural engineering. The body accumulates damage here: cuts from sharp metal, abrasions from rough stone, infections from the countless opportunities for bacterial entry. I've learned basic medical improvisation. For cuts, I clean them in distilled water, pack them with dried kelp that seems to have antibacterial properties, bind them with strips of cloth salvaged from my original clothing. For infections, I have nothing but time and hope—if an infection goes deep, if it becomes systemic, that's probably the end. I've been fortunate so far, or my immune system has adapted, or both.

The chronic issues are harder. My joints ache constantly—knees, shoulders, hips, the major load-bearing articulations all degraded by years of climbing and hauling and bracing against turbulence. My teeth are loosening, probably from nutritional deficiencies, definitely from the constant salt exposure. My vision has deteriorated, especially in my left eye, which took a splash of something caustic during early platform work and has never fully recovered. I can still see well enough to function, but fine detail is becoming difficult.

I inventory these degradations clinically. They're data points, information about how long this body will remain functional in this environment. My estimate: maybe five more years of full capability, then a decline period of unknown length, then failure. The failure mode is uncertain—injury, illness, simply wearing out—but the trajectory is clear. I'm a structure like the platform, maintaining against entropy, slowly losing the fight.

After dark, the platform transforms. The bioluminescence emerges, just as I knew it would from observing the creatures during day. The bore-worm gills glow green in their holes—the pillar surface becomes a field of small lights, like a city seen from above. The sky-fish in the water-columns flash patterns I'm starting to decode: quick strobes mean alarm, slow pulses mean coordination, certain rhythms might be territory or mating signals. The deep-grazers in the kelp-forest glow faintly, their trails visible as moving lights through the vegetation.

And in the very deepest crevices, in the spaces where no daylight reaches, there's a different kind of glow: steady, blue-white, cold. I don't know what produces this light—fungi, bacteria, something else—but it's beautiful in a way that makes my chest ache. It's the color of deep water, of glacial ice, of something pure that exists entirely separate from human intention or need.

I don't sleep well. The platform generates sound constantly—metal creaking under load, cables groaning in the wind, the rush of water over surfaces, the biological chorus of the creatures. I've learned to distinguish these sounds, to know what's normal and what indicates change. A particular shriek of metal-on-metal means the gear bearing needs attention. A low groaning from the southwestern beam means high stress, probably from increased water-flow. A sudden splash and thrashing from the kelp below means predation, something large taking something smaller.

I lie in my alcove—a depression I've worn into the platform's lower deck, padded with salvaged insulation and dried kelp—and I listen to the platform, and I think about the decision tree that led here. There was an operation, a project, something that required establishing these platforms in this impossible environment. I was part of that—engineer, technician, some functionary whose specific role has blurred in memory. There was an evacuation, or an abandonment, or something went wrong with the logistics. People left. I was supposed to leave. I don't remember choosing to stay, but I must have, because I'm here.

Or perhaps everyone intended to stay, and everyone else died, and I'm simply the one whose luck or constitution or stubbornness was sufficient. The distinction feels academic.

What I remember more clearly is the moment of understanding that I was alone, that rescue wasn't coming, that the platforms would receive no maintenance except what I provided. And the decision—which maybe wasn't a decision so much as a recognition of what I was already doing—that if the platforms were going to fail, they would fail more slowly because of my work. That the gear would keep turning. That the structure would be tended, documented, respected in its decline.

This is not survival instinct exactly. Survival would mean leaving, finding a way to one of the distant pillar-tops where the mist-layer is, where there might be different resources, different options. Survival would mean adaptive flexibility, exploring, seeking change. What I'm doing is something else: a kind of loyalty to place, to function, to the idea that even failing structures deserve attention, that bearing witness to entropy is meaningful work.

Sometimes I think I'm insane. The thought arrives with clinical detachment, an observation rather than a judgment. Sane people don't talk to rusted metal. Sane people don't generate meaning from maintaining a gear that powers nothing. Sane people don't live in the hollow throat of a dying platform on an eroded pillar in the middle of a vertical ocean when they could be trying to find other humans, other lives, other possibilities.

But sanity is contextual, and in this context—alone, in an environment that will kill me eventually, surrounded by the debris of failed human ambition—maybe this practice is the sanest response possible. I give the days structure. I give the work importance. I give the place respect. And in return, I get to be a person with a function, a role, an identity more specific than "surviving organism."

The alternative would be to sit and wait for failure, or to take the quick exit—step off the platform, fall into the kelp-forest, let the ecosystem process the body. I've considered this. On bad days, when pain is high or when some critical section of the platform fails despite my work, the consideration becomes specific, practical. How long would the fall take? Would consciousness persist through it? Would it be fear or relief?

But I don't take that exit. Every morning the light shifts, I wake, I assess, I begin the rounds. The platform needs maintenance. The gear needs cleaning. The archive needs another entry. The body needs fuel and water. The routine continues, and the routine is sufficient.

Today—this day, the one I'm in as I write this entry—something changed. I was working on the northwestern beam, clearing rust accumulation, when I saw it: a mark on the metal, fresh, not yet oxidized. Three parallel scratches. My notation system. But I didn't make this mark.

I stopped. I examined it closely, running my fingers over the scratches, testing depth and angle. They're recent—probably within the last day. They're deliberate—too straight and regular to be random damage. And they use my system, which means whoever made them has been observing my work, learning my methods.

There's someone else on the platform. Or someone has been on the platform recently. Someone who understands what I'm doing, why I'm doing it, enough to communicate in my own notation system.

I've searched, but the platform is large and complex, full of hiding places. I've called out, but the water-roar swallows voice. I've looked for other signs—tracks, disturbed rust-accumulation, fresh tool marks—but found nothing conclusive.

So I wait. I maintain the routine. I check the mark daily—it's still there, unchanged. And I've begun marking my own additions to the platform work more visibly, more deliberately, making it clear that I see the communication, that I'm responding.

Maybe I'm wrong. Maybe the mark is old and I'm misremembering my own work. Maybe it's random damage that happens to resemble my notation. Maybe I'm so starved for human contact that I'm generating evidence of it from ambiguous data.

But maybe not. Maybe there's someone else here, someone doing similar work on another part of the platform, someone who's been as isolated and careful as me. Maybe we're both rust-keepers, both tending the same dying structure from different positions, and only now, after years of parallel solitude, are we beginning to recognize each other.

The hope is dangerous—hope is always dangerous here—but it's also undeniable. I find myself working more carefully, leaving clearer signs of my presence, my methods, my intentions. I find myself imagining conversation: comparing techniques, sharing resources, coordinating maintenance, simply having someone to witness the gear turning.

And if I'm wrong, if there's no one else, if the mark is illusion or delusion, then nothing changes. The platform still needs maintenance. The gear still turns. The work continues. But if I'm right—

If I'm right, then I'm not the only one who decided this place deserves tending, that these failing structures merit care, that the slow work of slowing entropy is meaningful enough to build a life around. And that possibility, however uncertain, however unconfirmed, changes everything and nothing.

I am the rust-keeper on Meridian-7. The platform is dying. The gear turns. The ecosystem thrives in its abundance, indifferent to human presence or human absence. The water climbs the ancient stone, patient and endless. And somewhere in this hollow throat of corroded metal and filtered light, there might be someone else who understands what I understand, who chose what I chose, who tends what I tend.

This is where I am. This is what I do. This is who I've become, in the slow years of maintenance, in the vertical ocean, in the drowned scaffolds that were human once and are becoming something else, structure by structure, day by day, rust-flake by rust-flake.

The gear turns. I mark the beams. The light shifts. I continue.
