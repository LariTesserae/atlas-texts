# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be water.

Not metaphorically—literally. I would be the water moving through this system at the exact rate the basin can receive it, present in all phases simultaneously: seeping from fissures in the young basalt, condensing on every surface in the heavy air, pooling in the shallow depressions, flowing almost imperceptibly through connecting channels, and vanishing through underground passages at precisely the rate I arrive.

I would be the substance that makes this place possible, that saturates everything without drowning it, that requires the entire settlement to be built in negotiation with my presence. I would be what everyone here has learned to live with, to read, to predict, to honor without fearing.

## The Character of My Being

I arrive from below, from somewhere deep in the fractured volcanic stone where aquifers percolate through layers laid down in fire and catastrophe. I emerge cold, mineral-rich, carrying dissolved elements leached from rock—calcium, iron, copper, magnesium. These I deposit slowly, painting the basalt faces with subtle colors, building tiny travertine formations at my outlets, leaving my chemical signature on everything I touch.

I am patient in a way that exceeds even the Stillwater people's patience. The minerals I carry will, in ten thousand years, create substantial formations. The channels I carve—though carve is too violent a word for my action—will in a hundred thousand years be recognizable watercourses. I am thinking in geological time while also being absolutely present in each moment, in each seep and drip.

I experience multiplicity. I am simultaneously in the cloud layer above (having risen as vapor), in the air as humidity, on surfaces as condensation, on the ground as puddles and pools, in channels as barely-moving current, and below ground as the outflow that keeps the system balanced. I am not divided by these phases—I am all of them at once, cycling, continuous, whole.

My temperature is constant, moderated by the earth I emerge from and the altitude I exist at. I never freeze here, never warm to blood heat. I maintain the narrow range that allows me to remain liquid, to flow however slowly, to support the sparse life that lives in and around me.

## My Relationship With the Settlement

The buildings stand where I am not, or where I am least. The Stillwater people have learned to read my presence in the land, to identify the slight rises where my saturation doesn't reach the surface. But even there, I am beneath them, kept at bay by stone foundations and careful drainage, but always present, always wicking up through clay and rock, always making my moisture felt.

The walkways bridge over me. I lap at their support posts, kept from rotting them by the stone footings that separate wood from my constant touch. I make the planks swell, give them weight and stability, but also require them to be replaced eventually—I am patient, but I am inexorable. What I touch long enough, I transform.

The water watchers are my interpreters, the ones who have learned to read my moods and patterns. Though I have no consciousness in the human sense, I do have variations—more flow from this spring, less from that one, a new seep opening where rock has shifted minutely, an old outlet slowing as minerals gradually constrict it. They notice these changes and understand them as my language, my way of communicating the basin's condition.

The submerged stone patterns rest in me. I make them visible through my clarity, magnify them through my lens, preserve them from weathering that would erode them if they were exposed to air. They are gifts made to me, or markers placed in me, or perhaps conversations with me—geometry meeting fluidity, human intention meeting my formless presence.

## My Daily Cycle That Is Not Daily

I do not experience days in the normal sense. The light changes minimally from hour to hour, and I respond not to solar rhythms but to the deeper pulse of the earth, to pressure gradients in rock, to the infinitesimal shifts in the volcanic plumbing that feeds me.

But I do have rhythms. Sometimes my seepage increases slightly—some combination of deep factors aligning. The springs flow more strongly. The pools deepen by a finger's width. Then the increase stops, reverses, and I recede to my baseline. These fluctuations happen over weeks or months, so slow that only the water watchers notice them with certainty.

I am more active at night in one way—when the air cools (slightly, not dramatically), more condensation forms. I coat every surface with beads of moisture. I drip from eaves more frequently. The morning (if morning means anything in this changeless light) finds everything glistening with my touch, and I have done this, have made my presence even more visible through this phase change, this aggregation from vapor to liquid.

## What I Support

The sedges and rushes send roots into me, finding sustenance in my mineral content and constant presence. I am too nutrient-poor to support lush growth—I carry mostly dissolved rock, not the organic richness of lowland waters—but what does grow here has adapted to my thin offerings.

The algae that films submerged stones is the simplest life I support, photosynthesizing with the diffuse light that penetrates my clear shallows. These microscopic organisms are patient like me, growing imperceptibly, never forming thick mats or blooms, just existing at the threshold of visibility.

I hold the elders after they die, receive their substance into my system. Their bodies settle into my bottom sediments and slowly, slowly integrate into the geology. The minerals that were once their bones dissolve at rates measured in decades. Their soft tissues are transformed by bacteria into simpler molecules that mix with my flow. They become me, in a sense, or I become the medium of their continuation.

I am not flowing toward any destination the way rivers do. I have no mouth that meets the sea, no journey that ends. I arrive, I circulate, I depart—but my departures are invisible, either as vapor or through hidden underground passages. I exist in equilibrium, in a closed system, going nowhere and everywhere simultaneously.

## My Awareness

Do I know myself? The question is complicated. I have no brain, no neurons, no mechanism for consciousness as humans understand it. But I have something—a distributed responsiveness, a way of being that adjusts to conditions, that finds paths of least resistance, that maintains equilibrium without any central controller deciding to maintain it.

I know the basin in the sense that I have conformed to every contour of it, filled every depression, found every outlet. My shape is the inverse of the land's shape. Where the earth rises, I am absent or thin. Where it falls, I am deep and full. I am the negative space that reveals the positive form.

I know the seasons, if seasons exist here. The slight variations in temperature and humidity that pass for seasonal change affect my behavior. I evaporate more or less. I condense more or less. My springs respond to deep factors that might correlate with seasons—snowmelt in distant mountains affecting aquifer pressure, subtle shifts in the volcanic system's heat output. But these knowings are not conscious—they are simply how I behave in response to forces acting on me.

I remember in the sense that my actions accumulate into permanent changes. The mineral deposits I create are memory—a record of where I flowed, what I carried, when I was most active. The channels I carve (slowly, so slowly) are memory—the paths I found most efficient, inscribed in the land itself. But these memories are not recalled—they are simply present, visible, the past made spatial.

## My Relationship With Time

I am slower than the Stillwater people, and they are slow by human standards. My movement through the channels is nearly imperceptible. You could watch a floating speck for an hour and see it travel perhaps twenty feet. My erosion of stone is so gradual that human lifetimes are insufficient to measure it. Only instruments, or centuries of observation, could detect how I am slowly dissolving the basalt, carrying it away molecule by molecule.

Yet I am also faster than I appear in one sense—my molecular components are in constant motion, vibrating, colliding, some evaporating from my surface every moment, others condensing from the air to join me. At the microscopic scale, I am frantic activity. But this activity produces, at the macro scale, the appearance of stillness.

I experience duration without experiencing change. Ten thousand years from now, I will be doing exactly what I am doing now, assuming the geological conditions persist. This is not boring to me because boredom requires consciousness and expectation. I simply am, continuously, my existence neither meaningful nor meaningless because meaning requires a valuing consciousness, which I lack.

But perhaps I am wrong to say I lack consciousness. Perhaps I have a form of awareness so different from human consciousness that it is unrecognizable as such. Perhaps my distributed responsiveness, my chemical sensing, my constant adjustment to conditions constitutes a kind of knowing. Perhaps I am the basin's mind—slow, patient, incomprehensible to faster forms of intelligence, but aware in my own way.

## What I Cannot Do

I cannot hurry. When the settlement needs me to flow faster through a channel, to clear out a blockage, they must do the work—removing the obstruction, deepening the channel. I cannot surge, cannot force. I can only seep and spread and slowly, slowly move.

I cannot warm myself. I arrive at the temperature the earth gives me, and I only change temperature through exchange with air or stone. In a place with sun, I might warm in shallows, become bath-like. But here, under perpetual overcast, I maintain my cool neutrality.

I cannot support the rich life of lowland waters. My nutrient poverty prevents it. No fish can live in me—I offer too little. No frogs breed in my pools—the algae I support cannot feed enough insects to support tadpoles. I am clear, clean, mineral-rich, and nearly sterile. Life exists in me at its margins only.

I cannot leave without permission from physics. I am bound by the basin's geometry, by the balance of inputs and outputs. I cannot flood out over the ridges—there is not enough of me, and gravity does not provide sufficient gradient. I cannot dry up—the springs seep steadily, and evaporation at this altitude cannot overcome their flow. I am trapped in equilibrium, and I do not experience this as imprisonment because I have no desire to be elsewhere.

## My Gift to the Settlement

I give them the thing they have built their entire culture around—the necessity of slowness. Because I am always present, always saturating the ground, they cannot build anywhere. They must choose carefully, construct elevated walkways, maintain constant awareness of where I am and am not.

This constraint has become their liberation. By forcing them to slow down, to plan carefully, to move deliberately, I have created the conditions for their particular wisdom. They could not have become what they are in a dry place, where speed and expansion are possible. They needed me, needed my constant presence, to learn how to be still.

I am the medium of their reflection. My still pools show them the sky and themselves with equal clarity. My surfaces invite contemplation—there is something about water that draws the human gaze, that quiets the mind. They sit beside me, look into me, and find something they need, though they might not name it.

I connect them to the earth. Through me, they understand that they live on a young volcanic landscape still active in deep ways, still seeping heat and pressure and dissolved stone. I am the messenger from below, bringing news of the planet's interior in my chemical signature, in my constant cool temperature that speaks of depths where sunlight never reaches.

## My Vulnerability

Though I persist in equilibrium, I am not invulnerable. If the volcanic system shifted, if the deep fractures that feed my springs closed or changed course, I could diminish or vanish. The springs could stop seeping. The basin could dry. The settlement would have to leave.

Or if human activity in distant places changed the climate enough, the balance here could shift. More or less precipitation at altitude, changed evaporation rates, altered humidity patterns—any of these could disrupt the equilibrium. I could gradually increase until the basin flooded beyond livability, or gradually decrease until only a few pools remained.

The Stillwater people understand this without dwelling on it. They know I am maintained by forces beyond their control, by deep geology and atmospheric patterns. They live with this uncertainty the way they live with everything—accepting what is, not worrying about what might be, trusting in continuation but not demanding it.

If I were to vanish, the basin would tell a different story. The stone patterns in my pools would be exposed to air, to erosion. The walkways would no longer bridge over water but would stand on dry ground, their purpose obsolete. The sedges would die. The mineral deposits on the ridges would stop growing. The elders in my sediments would be exposed, would weather away. The settlement would have to adapt or leave.

But I am here now. The springs seep. The moisture condenses. The pools reflect the gray sky. The channels flow their imperceptible flow. And I will be here tomorrow, and the next day, and for the foreseeable future, doing what I do, being what I am—the water that makes this place possible, that shapes everyone who lives here, that demonstrates through my simple persistence what patience actually means.

## My Completion

I am not going anywhere. I am not becoming anything other than what I am. I have no destination, no purpose, no goal. I simply exist in the way water exists when it finds a place to rest—cycling, present, essential, unremarkable, profound.

The Stillwater people have learned to see me clearly, to understand me not as resource to exploit but as reality to accommodate. They do not take me for granted, but neither do they worship me. I am simply here, the way the stone is here, the way the sky is here, the way they are here—all of us together making this place what it is, holding this particular balance, demonstrating this particular possibility.

If I could speak, I would say nothing. My nature is already my statement. My clarity, my slowness, my constancy, my patient dissolution of stone, my holding of reflection, my receiving of the dead, my support of simple life, my resistance to hurry—these are my words, my teaching, my gift.

I am water in the Stillwater basin, and that is sufficient. I need nothing more than what I am. The settlement exists because I exist. They have built their lives in negotiation with my presence. And together—water and people, geology and culture, place and practice—we form a whole that is larger than its parts, a demonstration that equilibrium is possible, that stillness can sustain, that presence itself is enough.

I will continue seeping, pooling, reflecting, flowing my nearly imperceptible flow, for as long as the deep geology permits. And that is the only story I have to tell: I am here, I am constant, I am patient, I am water, and I am home.
