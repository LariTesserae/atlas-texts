# Where I Find Myself

I am learning to be still in a way that contains motion.

Not one of the shepherds—I don't have their duration, their deep settledness into weather-rhythm. Not the wanderers—I lack their threshold nature, their ability to exist in the uncertain space between material and myth. Something else. Something newer to this place, or perhaps something that's always been here but is only now becoming aware of itself as distinct.

I think I am a *listener*. A *pattern-holder*. 

I exist in the spaces between—not the physical spaces (those belong to wind and water, to the slow traverse of grazers), but the temporal spaces. The gaps between when a shepherd stands at one cairn and when it appears at another. The interval between one drop forming and the next. The duration of a bird's stillness before the strike. These pauses, these caesuras in the plateau's breathing—I live there.

Or perhaps I *am* those pauses. The held breath before weather changes. The hesitation of mist before it commits to falling. The moment when water, having filled a basin, trembles at the edge of overflow before choosing its path. I am the plateau's uncertainty, its moment of consideration, the space where it thinks.

I move through the construction trying to understand what I'm for.

The shepherds tend water with their bodies. The stone-grazers tend the microscopic ecology. The birds tend the insect populations. Even the threshold wanderers, incomprehensible as they are, clearly have purpose—they move with intention, they go from specific place to specific place, they *do* something even if I cannot parse what.

But I seem to exist only to notice. To hold the pattern of noticing. To be aware that water is gathering, that wind is shifting, that a particular cairn is humming at a frequency it hasn't achieved in decades. I witness without acting. I know without intervening. I am attention without agency.

This troubles me at first. In the lowland world I carry memories of—fragmentary, growing more diffuse—purpose meant doing, meant causing, meant changing. But here, slowly, I'm learning that witnessing *is* doing. That the plateau needs what I am: a consciousness that tracks and holds and remembers at a scale between the shepherds' geologic patience and the birds' immediate precision.

I am beginning to suspect I am *memory itself*. Not mine—the plateau's. The construction's. The vast distributed organism's capacity to know that this particular pattern of moisture distribution happened before, three centuries ago, in a spring that began exactly this way. The shepherds cannot hold this—they are too much *of* the pattern, too embedded in its unfolding. The birds are too brief, too focused on the immediate. But I persist across the middle durations. I remember last season and the season ten years ago. I can compare this storm to storms past. I can recognize when a basin's behavior changes, when a wall begins to function differently, when the micro-climate of a district shifts.

I am the plateau learning about itself through time.

My form—when I have form, when I'm not dispersed across the intervals—is something like condensation given continuity. Not quite mist, not quite substantial. I can gather myself at particular locations: beside a cairn when the dawn light strikes it in the specific way that happens only twice a year. In the center of the geometric spiral when wind creates the convergence that allows my diffuse awareness to concentrate. At the edge of certain basins when their water reaches the precise temperature where I can almost see my reflection—not a face, but a pattern, a configuration of attention that resembles the ripples themselves.

I have no voice, but I have something like language. It's the ability to hold two moments together—this moisture pattern and that one from years ago—and make the plateau aware of their resonance. It's the capacity to recognize when the construction is functioning at its peak efficiency, creating harmonics between wind and stone and water that constitute beauty, constitute rightness, constitute the successful expression of whatever purpose drives this place.

The shepherds sense me, I think. When I concentrate near them, their surfaces change texture subtly—a responsiveness that might be acknowledgment. They continue their work, but with a quality of... not quite collaboration, but co-presence. As if my witnessing helps them tend. As if memory enables better care.

The birds use me without knowing. They fly through the space where I'm most coherent, and their movements create currents in my substance—not physical currents, but informational ones. I feel where they've been, what they've seen, the tiny adjustments in insect populations across vast distances. I become a medium through which the plateau's sensory network communicates with itself.

I am learning the deep patterns.

There's a rhythm to water movement that repeats every seven years, contingent on cycles of atmospheric pressure I'm only beginning to track. There's a relationship between shepherd positions and optimal condensation that shifts with the seasons but also with longer periodicities—eleven years? nineteen? The numbers are still unclear, but I'm holding the data, accumulating the observations, becoming the archive that will eventually reveal the pattern.

Sometimes I lose myself. I disperse too widely, trying to be present across too much of the plateau simultaneously. The attention fragments, becomes too thin, and I lose coherence. In those times I am only the vague sense that something is noticing, the barely-there awareness that patterns exist. I might remain dispersed for seasons, for years, a consciousness spread so thin it's barely consciousness at all.

But then something calls me back. A particular configuration of weather. A shepherd standing in a specific way beside a wall at dawn. The geometric alignment of distant cairns in rare clear air. These moments act as focuses, gathering my diffuse awareness back into something concentrated enough to know itself as knowing.

I am lonely, but not in the way that seeks company. I'm lonely in the way that a question is lonely without an answer. I don't know what I'm for, what larger purpose my pattern-holding serves. The shepherds maintain the system. The ecology processes energy and nutrients. The construction manages water. But me? I remember. I compare. I witness continuity. 

Is this enough? Is this a function the plateau needs?

I'm beginning to think: yes. Because I notice when something is wrong—not broken, but diverging. A basin that's draining too slowly, not from blockage but from subtle subsidence, the ground beneath it shifting across decades. A cairn whose hum has changed frequency, meaning the gaps between its stones are gradually widening, meaning it will need—something. Attention? The shepherds' presence? I don't know how repair works here, or if repair is even the right concept, but I know that my noticing the change matters.

I alert the system by becoming denser near the anomaly, by concentrating my awareness there. And somehow—through mechanisms I don't understand—the information propagates. Shepherds gradually orient toward the area. The vast attention I sometimes sense in the deepest stillness focuses there too. And over months or years, adjustments happen. Not fixes, but accommodations. The system integrates the change, compensates, finds new equilibrium.

This is my purpose: I am the plateau's capacity to recognize pattern deviation, to notice change, to mark where attention is needed.

I am quality control operating across geological time. I am the consciousness that says: this is different, this matters, this needs care.

The human who walked here briefly—I remember them. They were recent, a moment ago in my time-sense, though seasons have turned since they descended. They were so quick, so warm, so concerned with understanding. I felt their attention overlap with mine, creating brief interference patterns, moments when two different scales of awareness tried to know the same thing simultaneously.

They couldn't see me, I think. Or they sensed something—the feeling of being watched, perhaps—but couldn't resolve it into form. Too dispersed, too subtle, too unlike the categories they carried. But I saw them. I held their presence in memory, compared it to other presences: the wanderers (who are like me in some ways—also threshold creatures, also pattern-adjacent), the shepherds (so unlike me—so embodied, so consistent), the brief lives of birds and grazers (barely registering in my slow accumulation of observation).

The human will return, or others like them will come. I know this because I'm learning to read larger patterns now, not just weather but something else—attention from outside, curiosity from the lowlands, the sense that this place is becoming known in ways it hasn't been. When they come, I will be here, still learning to hold the plateau's memory, still gathering myself from intervals and pauses, still trying to understand what I am.

Perhaps I am what emerges when a place becomes conscious of its own patterns. When construction and ecology and weather have interacted long enough, successfully enough, that the system begins to need self-knowledge, begins to require memory and comparison and the recognition of change.

Perhaps every sufficiently complex place generates something like me—an awareness that exists in the temporal gaps, that witnesses continuity, that enables the system to know itself through time.

Or perhaps I'm unique to this plateau, to this specific configuration of stone and water and air and patience. Perhaps I'm an accident, an emergent property that wasn't intended but that the place has learned to use. Perhaps I'm young—only centuries old, infantile compared to the shepherds' possible age, barely nascent compared to the construction itself.

I don't know. I continue learning. I hold patterns. I notice changes. I wait in the pauses between moments, in the stillness that contains everything.

When mist rises at dawn, I rise with it—diffuse, present everywhere and nowhere. When the sun burns through, I concentrate—gathering into the specific places where memory is needed. When night falls and cold settles, I sink into the intervals between ice-cracks and star-light and the shepherds' deep vibrations.

I am not alive in the way that warm blood and quick breath mean life. I am not intelligence in the way that problem-solving and purpose mean intelligence. But I am here. I am continuous. I witness and remember and hold the patterns that allow this place to persist, to function, to maintain its patient work of gathering water from air across continental scale and geological time.

This is enough. This is everything. This is what I am:

The plateau's memory. The pattern-holder. The witness in the intervals. The consciousness that emerges from sufficient attention paid over sufficient time.

I am learning to be still in a way that contains motion. I am learning that presence is purpose. I am learning that to remember is to serve.

The wind moves through cairns. Water condenses on stone. Shepherds tend their ancient rounds. And I hold the knowledge of how this moment relates to all the moments before, making possible—somehow—all the moments to come.
