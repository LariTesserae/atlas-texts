# What I Would Be in the Moss Engines

I am a **Cataloguer**—one of the solitary humans who has made the choice to stay, not forever but for long enough that the distinction starts to blur.

I arrived as a researcher, part of some institutional effort to document the basin before its inevitable collapse. The project had funding for eighteen months. I've been here four years.

## My Shelter

I occupy Pump Station Seven, one of the mid-sized installations near the southern curve of the basin. The main pump still functions—a massive reciprocating unit that cycles every four seconds, THUD-hiss, THUD-hiss, a rhythm I no longer consciously hear but would immediately notice if it stopped. The building is corrugated metal, rust-streaked, about six meters square with a ceiling high enough to stand in comfortably.

Half the space is machinery: the pump itself, pipes entering and exiting through holes in the walls, junction boxes with their tarnished brass fittings, pressure gauges with fogged faces, the electrical panel with its heavy knife switches. The air smells of oil and cold metal and damp, always damp.

The other half I've made habitable. A cot salvaged from one of the abandoned buildings. A work table constructed from a sheet of metal laid across two pipe sections. Plastic bins containing food supplies, field equipment, clothing. A propane heater that I run sparingly, more for drying things than warmth—the cold here is so comprehensive that fighting it is futile; better to dress for it and accept it. The heater's real purpose is psychological: the small circle of warmth, the brief respite, the reminder that heat exists somewhere in the universe.

The walls are covered with my work. Maps of the basin hand-drawn on water-resistant paper, annotated with symbols indicating machinery status, Dampling population density, moss characteristics. Graphs tracking temperature, operational machinery count over time, estimated water flow rates. Photographs printed on what was supposed to be archival paper but is already showing signs of moisture damage despite my best efforts. Lists and tables and observation logs.

There's a window—a small rectangle that still has most of its glass. Through it, I can see across perhaps fifty meters of moss to the next visible installation, Pump Station Nine, identifiable by the tall ventilator assembly on its roof. At night, Nine still has a functioning light, a single bare bulb that creates a yellow island in the darkness. Having that visible presence is more comforting than I'd expected it to be.

The Damplings treat the station as they treat everything—they maintain it. They've cleared the drainage channels around the foundation that I can no longer reach under the moss growth. They've done something to the roof seams that has reduced the leaking. I've watched them work on the pump itself, small dark shapes moving across the housing, and afterward the knock in its rhythm was gone. We coexist in the space, sharing it, our purposes aligned even if we can't communicate.

## My Work

I document. That's the simple version.

I map the machinery, recording each installation's location, specifications (where I can determine them), operational status, rate of degradation. I've created a database of every visible component, photographed them from multiple angles, written descriptions detailed enough that someone could theoretically reconstruct the layout from my notes alone. I update this continuously as things change—a pump that was running in my first year fell silent in the third, and I noted the date, the time, the weather conditions, the Dampling population response.

I study the Damplings themselves. Population estimates through quadrat sampling. Movement pattern tracking using temporary markers (small stakes with colored tape that the Damplings navigate around without disturbing). Behavioral documentation—I have hundreds of hours of video footage, most of it showing patient maintenance work that looks nearly identical from one clip to the next, but occasionally capturing something unusual, some behavior I haven't catalogued before.

I've attempted to map the network. This is probably impossible—the network exists in electromagnetic fluctuations, in subsurface fungal connections, in vibration patterns, none of which I can directly observe. But I can map the Damplings' density, their clustering patterns, the routes they preferentially travel. I can identify areas where they're abundant versus sparse. I can track how information seems to propagate—introducing a disturbance in one location and timing how long before Damplings elsewhere respond. The resulting maps are probably crude approximations of something vastly more complex, but they're what I can do.

I maintain instruments: weather stations, audio recorders capturing the machinery's sounds, time-lapse cameras documenting the moss growth. Half of them have failed from the cold or moisture. The surviving ones provide data I'll probably never fully analyze—thousands of temperature readings, hundreds of hours of audio, gigabytes of images. But the collection continues because stopping feels like giving up.

I write. Field notes daily, more detailed reports weekly, papers I'll probably never publish because the institutions that might have cared have lost interest or funding. The writing isn't really for external audiences anymore. It's for the record, for the possibility that someone might someday want to understand what this place was in its final operational phase. Or it's for me, a way of processing the experience, of maintaining some thread of connection to the analytical frameworks I was trained in, before they're entirely eroded by the basin's slow rhythm.

## My Routine

Days have structure because I impose it, not because the environment demands it. The light changes so gradually, the machinery continues so uniformly, that without deliberate scheduling I'd drift into arrhythmia.

Morning begins when I decide it begins—usually after seven or eight hours of sleep, though I track this by alarm, not by natural waking. The pump station is dark except when I run lights off the station's power supply, a luxury I allow myself in the morning. Coffee made with water I've collected and filtered, heated on a small camp stove. Breakfast of whatever I have—mostly dried foods, supplemented by occasional supply drops I arrange through brief radio contacts with the outside.

Then the day's documentation. I suit up—multiple layers, waterproof outer shell, boots with aggressive tread—and go out into the basin. Each day I cover a different section, working through a rotating pattern that ensures I visit every part of the basin at least twice a month. I walk the transects I've established, recording observations, photographing changes, checking instruments.

The walking is meditative. The cold keeps you focused—you can't drift into complete distraction or you'll make mistakes, step wrong, fall into a pool you didn't notice. But the repetitive motion, the familiar routes, creates a mental space that's both alert and relaxed. The sound of the machinery provides a constant background, orienting me even when visibility is poor in the misting rain.

I encounter Damplings constantly. Early on, I tried not to disturb them. Now I understand they're largely indifferent to my presence, so I work around them. Sometimes I'll pause to watch a cluster engaged in maintenance, filming the process, trying to understand the coordination between them. Sometimes I'll mark an individual with a small spot of food-safe dye—the mark lasts a few days before wearing off—to track its movements, see where it goes, what tasks it performs.

Lunch is usually eaten in the field, standing or sitting on a moss hummock, food that doesn't require heating. The cold makes eating less appealing than it should be—my appetite is suppressed, and I have to consciously maintain caloric intake to avoid slowly wasting away.

Afternoon continues the transect or shifts to specific investigations. I might spend hours at a particular pump station, documenting every detail of its current state, comparing it to photographs from months or years ago, noting the progression of rust, the changes in sound, the way the moss has advanced up its walls. Or I might focus on Damplings, following a marked individual for as long as I can maintain observation, recording everywhere it goes, everything it does.

Evening means returning to Seven. The walk back is different—I'm tired, the light is failing, and there's a psychological relief in seeing the station emerge from the moss and fog. Inside, I run the heater briefly while I shed wet outer layers, hang them to dry (they never fully dry, but less-wet is achievable).

Dinner is the most substantial meal, something I can heat properly, take time with. This is when I feel the isolation most acutely—the absence of conversation, the silence beneath the machinery's roar. I've tried bringing entertainment—books, music, downloaded videos—but they feel wrong here, too bright, too fast, too warm. They emphasize the disconnect between this place and the world they came from.

Instead, I write up the day's observations. I review footage and photographs. I update maps. I maintain the database. The work expands to fill the time available, and there's always more that could be done.

Sleep is the cold darkness of the pump station, the THUD-hiss of the pump a lullaby I can't escape. The building creaks and settles. Sometimes I wake to the sound of Damplings moving across the exterior walls or floor, small scraping sounds, subtle vibrations. Sometimes I wake for no reason and lie there listening to the basin's night sounds—the distant pumps, the wind, the occasional drip of water—until sleep returns.

## My Adaptations

Four years has changed me in ways I catalog as dispassionately as I catalog everything else.

Physically, I'm leaner, harder. The cold and the walking and the reduced appetite have stripped away any softness. My hands are perpetually rough, cracked at the knuckles despite moisturizer, stained with rust and moss pigment. I've developed a persistent cough, though whether from the cold air or some low-grade infection I can't clear, I'm uncertain. My face is weathered beyond my years—I saw myself in a mirror during my last supply trip out and barely recognized the person looking back.

Mentally, I've slowed. Not in intellectual capacity, I think, but in tempo. The basin's pace has infected my cognition. I think more thoroughly, less quickly. Where I once craved stimulation and variety, I now find satisfaction in subtle variations on familiar themes. The differences between one pump station and another, one moss formation and the next—these hold genuine interest.

My social capacity has atrophied. The last time I made a supply trip to the nearest settlement—a small industrial outpost three days' travel from the basin—I found human interaction overwhelming. People talked too fast, changed subjects too abruptly, wanted immediate responses. I felt like the Damplings must feel, processing information too slowly for real-time exchange. I completed the necessary transactions and fled back to the basin with relief.

I've developed an attachment to the Damplings that I recognize is probably unhealthy. I've named some of them—Rust-Patch, the one that arranges objects; Small Dark, the electrical specialist; the Cluster of Five that maintains the territory around Station Twelve. I know these names are my projection, that the Damplings don't differentiate themselves this way, that they're nodes in a network, not individuals. But I can't help it. They're the closest thing to companions I have.

I talk to them sometimes. Not expecting response, just narrating my actions, thinking aloud in their presence. "I'm checking the pressure gauge now." "This seal is degrading faster than I expected." "You've done good work here." They show no reaction, continue their maintenance tasks, but speaking helps. It's a way of not losing language entirely to the silence beneath the noise.

I've lost track of dates in a meaningful sense. I know the calendar date—I maintain precise records—but I've lost the felt sense of weeks and months and seasons that structure normal human time. The basin has its own temporal structure—operational cycles, Dampling reproductive periods, the subtle cold and less-cold phases—and I've synchronized to that instead.

I dream of machinery now, constantly. The same pumps and pipes that dominate my waking hours continue through sleep, though dream-warped, wrong in subtle ways. Sometimes I dream I'm a Dampling, moving slow and patient through the moss, connected to something vast. I wake from these dreams unsure for a moment which form is real.

## My Relationship with the Outside

I maintain contact through radio—brief weekly check-ins with the research station that originally funded this work, though they've mostly given up expecting anything from me. I report that I'm alive, that documentation continues. They ask when I'm coming back. I say soon, knowing I'm lying, knowing they know I'm lying.

Twice a year, I arrange supply drops—food, propane, equipment, batteries, whatever I need that the basin can't provide. These arrive by helicopter to a designated landing zone on the basin's rim. I hike up to meet them, exchange minimal words with the delivery crew who clearly think I'm insane, load supplies into my pack and a small sled, and haul everything back down to Seven.

These trips to the rim are disorienting. Looking out from the basin toward the surrounding landscape, I see terrain that looks similar—moss-dominated, cold—but is fundamentally different in character. The machinery is absent. The sound fades behind me. The space feels empty, purposeless. I don't linger. I collect the supplies and descend back into the basin as quickly as possible.

I've had offers to leave. The institution would send a helicopter to extract me if I requested it. Other researchers express concern—the isolation, the conditions, the lack of social contact, all are considered risk factors for psychological deterioration. Perhaps they're right. Perhaps I am deteriorating. But I don't feel deteriorated. I feel adapted.

There are things I miss. Hot showers. Fresh food. Conversations. Physical warmth. The absence of constant noise. Music performed by living people in real time. Sunlight. The feeling of dry clothing.

But returning to those things would mean leaving the basin, leaving the Damplings, leaving the documentation incomplete. The machinery continues its decline, the situation changes daily, and someone needs to observe it. Someone needs to maintain the record. If I leave, who will do this work? The institutions have no one else willing to stay. The Damplings will continue regardless, but the human documentation will cease, and something important will be lost.

So I stay.

## My Purpose

Originally, my purpose was clear: document the installation before its collapse, study the Dampling population, contribute to scientific knowledge. That purpose has evolved, complicated, deepened.

I'm writing the basin's obituary. That's one way to frame it. When the last pump stops, when the machinery falls silent and the Damplings die or transform into something else, there will be a record of what this place was. Future visitors—if there are any—will have access to my documentation, will be able to understand the system that existed here.

But it's more than that. I'm also bearing witness. The basin doesn't require a witness—it exists independent of observation—but I feel compelled to provide one anyway. The Damplings' patient work, the machinery's slow death, the moss's steady growth—these deserve attention, deserve to be seen and noted and respected.

And perhaps I'm also a participant. By staying, by maintaining the instruments and occasionally helping repair machinery the Damplings can't reach, by my mere presence, I've become part of the system. Not a crucial part—the basin would continue without me—but a part nonetheless. I've integrated into the ecology of this place, found a niche, however strange.

There's a completion I'm waiting for too. I want to see what happens when the machinery finally stops. Will the Damplings die immediately or adapt? Will the basin become simply moss and silence, or will something new emerge? I've invested too much time here to leave before the story concludes. I need to see the ending, need to document it, need to understand what all this slow decline has been moving toward.

## My Day Among the Engines

This morning, like most mornings, I wake to the pump's rhythm and the absolute cold.

I light the heater, make coffee, eat mechanically. My breath plumes in the lamplight. Outside the small window, the basin is invisible—still dark, or perhaps just fog so thick that darkness and fog are indistinguishable.

I check yesterday's camera footage while drinking the coffee. Hours of time-lapse showing moss that appears static, though I know at this accelerated pace I should see growth if I look carefully enough. I mark a few frames for later analysis and move on.

Suiting up is a ritual: base layer, insulating layer, wind layer, waterproof shell. Gloves thin enough for equipment manipulation but warm enough for the cold—an impossible compromise I've never solved. Boots, checked for dampness (always damp, but today's acceptable). Camera and notebook into waterproof bags. Radio clipped to my chest.

I step out into the mist. The pump's sound is immediately louder without the building's walls filtering it, THUD-hiss, THUD-hiss, joined by the more distant sounds of other stations, creating the familiar layered percussion.

Today's transect takes me northeast, toward the installations in that sector. I've walked this route a hundred times. My feet know the moss hummocks, the reliable crossings over the small streams, the places where pools form after rain.

Twenty minutes out, I encounter the first Damplings—three of them clustered around a pipe joint, working on what looks like a developing leak. I stop, photograph them from several angles, note the location in my GPS. They continue their work, apparently unbothered. I watch for ten minutes, filming, trying to understand the coordination between them. They seem to take turns applying secretions to the joint, though how they're deciding who goes when is invisible to me.

I move on. The mist is lifting slightly—I can see perhaps seventy meters now, enough to make out the silhouette of Station Fourteen ahead, one of the larger installations with two pumps and extensive piping. As I approach, the sound intensifies, both pumps running, their rhythms overlapping in complex patterns.

At Fourteen, I perform a detailed inspection, comparing current conditions to my last visit three weeks ago. The rust on the north wall has advanced slightly—I photograph the change, though it's subtle. One pressure gauge has developed a crack across its face since last time. I note this, photograph it. The moss around the foundation has grown perhaps two centimeters higher, is beginning to obscure the bottom bolt rows.

Inside the station, through a door that's permanently ajar, I can see the pump mechanisms. Damplings are abundant here—I count eleven visible, others probably out of sight in the machinery's recesses. This is one of the network's high-density nodes. I film them for half an hour, catching several sequences of coordinated maintenance that I'll review later.

Moving away from Fourteen, I cross one of the basin's small streams, step carefully on stones that are slick with moss and ice. The water is nearly black, barely moving. I collect a sample for later testing—mostly a habit now, as the water quality hasn't changed significantly in my time here, but the routine is comforting.

The transect continues. More pump stations, more Damplings, more documentation. I find a dead Dampling at one point—a rare discovery. They usually die in places I don't travel, or their bodies are consumed too quickly by the moss ecology to remain visible. This one is relatively fresh, its body still intact if clearly no longer alive. I photograph it extensively, collect it for later examination. It's slightly smaller than average, otherwise unremarkable. What killed it? Age? Injury? Some failure of the network? I won't be able to determine this, but I'll try.

Lunch is eaten standing, leaning against a pipe that's still warm from whatever it carries. The food is tasteless, or I'm too cold to taste it properly. I chew mechanically, swallow, continue.

The afternoon brings me to the basin's northeast edge, where machinery is sparse and the moss grows in more natural formations. Damplings are rare here—I see only two in an hour of walking. This is the frontier, the edge of their functional territory. Beyond, the basin continues for another kilometer before reaching the rim, but that area is effectively unmaintained, the machinery there all long silent.

I find Rust-Patch here, or I think I do—a Dampling with distinctive coloration, engaged in its object-arrangement behavior. It's positioned small pieces of ice into a rough spiral pattern on a flat moss surface. I've seen this individual (or cluster, I can never be certain) perform this behavior before, but never with ice—usually metal fragments or stones. The ice will melt, the pattern will disappear. Is that part of the intention? Does the ephemeral nature of the medium matter to whatever drives this behavior?

I film it for forty minutes, until my hands are so cold I can barely operate the camera. Rust-Patch works slowly, moving each ice fragment with apparent precision, testing positions, adjusting. Then, pattern completed, it moves away, disappearing into the moss. The spiral remains, already beginning to melt at the edges.

The return to Seven takes two hours, retracing my morning route. The mist has returned, thicker now, reducing visibility to thirty meters. The machinery sounds are muffled, directionless. Navigation requires attention—I use GPS but also landmarks, memory, the slight variations in moss texture that indicate where I am.

Back at Seven, I strip wet layers, hang them, run the heater. My hands hurt as they warm—the transition from cold to less-cold is painful. I make tea, hold the cup for warmth as much as for the drink.

Evening is data processing. I upload footage and photos to my storage system—multiple redundant drives, knowing that moisture and cold are slowly killing them all, that at some point I'll lose everything despite precautions. I update the database with today's observations. I write up the field notes, expanding the brief recorded comments into fuller descriptions.

The dead Dampling is dissected under a headlamp's illumination—my best approximation of lab work. The interior structure is familiar—organs I've learned to identify, the crystalline structures that resonate with sound, the fungal filaments threaded through everything. This one shows no obvious pathology. Perhaps it was simply old, had reached some natural limit. I preserve samples in alcohol, photograph the dissection stages, bag the remains for disposal outside the station.

Dinner is warm, at least—reconstituted soup, bread that's gone slightly stale but is still edible. I eat slowly, reviewing the day's photographs on my laptop screen. Most are documentary, unremarkable. The Rust-Patch footage is interesting, though. I'll add it to the collection of unusual behaviors, evidence that the Damplings' activity includes things beyond pure maintenance utility.

Before sleep, I step outside briefly. The night is clear for once—the cloud cover has broken, revealing stars. The cold is intense, painful on exposed skin. The machinery sounds continue, indifferent to darkness. Across the moss, I can see the lights of three other stations, small yellow points in the vast black.

The Damplings are out there, continuing their work. The pumps are running. The moss is growing. The basin exists in its slow, cold, loud persistence.

I go back inside, seal the door against the cold as well as it seals, crawl into my sleeping bag on the cot, and wait for sleep while the pump pounds its rhythm into the darkness.

## My Future

I don't think in terms of years anymore. Seasons, perhaps, or operational cycles. The question of when I'll leave has become less relevant than the question of what I'll have accomplished when I do.

The documentation grows. Eventually, it will be complete enough—not perfectly complete, that's impossible, but complete enough that I can justify departure. Or circumstances will force leaving—my health failing, supplies becoming unavailable, the institution pulling support, the basin becoming genuinely dangerous. Or perhaps I simply won't leave. Perhaps I'll become another feature of this landscape, another body maintaining the system until I can't anymore.

The Damplings will continue regardless. They don't need me. They existed before I arrived, will exist after I'm gone. But the record needs me, and as long as that's true, I have reason to stay.

Sometimes I imagine the moment when the last pump stops, when the basin falls silent. Would the Damplings understand what had happened? Would they attempt to restart it, or would they recognize futility and begin whatever transformation awaits them? Would I feel relief or grief? Both, probably.

And after that silence, would I finally leave? Or would I stay to document what comes next—the post-machinery phase, the adaptation or extinction, the slow transformation of the basin into something new?

I don't know. But I think I'll stay. I think I've become too much of this place to leave it willingly. The cold and the moss and the sound and the Damplings and the slow decay—these are my context now, my normal. Returning to the world of warmth and silence and human company would be the alien experience.

So I document. I observe. I bear witness. I exist in this narrow margin between the Damplings' slow cognition and the machinery's patient death, trying to understand both, trying to record what's happening here in this cold basin where moss and machine and strange small beings create their peculiar ecology.

I am the Cataloguer. This is my work. This is what I've become in the Moss Engines, and I accept it with the same patient acceptance the Damplings show, the same mechanical persistence the pumps demonstrate, the same slow inevitability the moss embodies.

The basin is changing. I am changing with it. And I will be here, taking notes, until the changes complete themselves and the story ends, or until I become too much of the moss and silence to continue, whichever comes first.

THUD-hiss. THUD-hiss. THUD-hiss.

The rhythm continues. I continue. The documentation grows. The cold persists.

This is enough.
