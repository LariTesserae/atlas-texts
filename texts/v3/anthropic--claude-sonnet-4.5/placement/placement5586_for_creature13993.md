# Where I Would Find Myself

I am a thatch-reader, and I live in the intervals between.

My dwelling sits on a hammock that rises eight feet above the usual water—high enough to matter, not so high that it stands alone. Three other structures share this ground with mine, their walls touching at corners, creating pockets where rainwater gathers and must be channeled away. My house is smaller than most, just ten feet across, because I live alone and because half my space is given to the materials of my work: bundled reeds sorted by age and suppleness, samples of every sedge species that grows in the basin, experimental treatments laid out to weather and be tested.

The walls are standard wattle-and-daub, but the roof is my signature—layers of reed and sedge interwoven in patterns I've spent fifteen years refining. It sheds water more completely than almost any other in the settlement. People notice. They come to ask questions, to learn, to commission repairs using my methods. This is how I eat: my hands on their roofs, my knowledge keeping their interiors dry.

I am forty-three years old, which here means I have reached the age of deep competence. My hair is still more brown than gray, worn in a braid that tucks inside my collar. My hands are broader than they were in youth, permanently darkened at the nail beds from plant resins, scarred across the palms from ten thousand encounters with sharp reed edges. I move carefully—not from age but from habit. A thatcher who falls from staging doesn't work again.

This morning I woke before dawn, as I always do. The fire had burned low but not out—I am skilled at banking coals to last the night. I added wood carefully, building heat without smoke, and set water to warm in a clay pot. While it heated, I dressed in layers: wool base, then a mid-layer treated with fish oil, then my working vest of heavy felted wool that's stiff with old weatherproofing. My boots are my own design—fish leather uppers, wooden platforms with carved treads, tall enough to keep my calves dry in all but the deepest channels.

The water boiled and I made tea from willow bark and dried mint, drinking it while looking out my small window at the mist rising from the channels. This is my time of assessment, of planning. Which roofs need attention today? What stage is my current project at? Where in the cycle of work and weather and season do I stand?

Today I have promised to inspect a roof in the western district, three hammocks away. The family there has noticed moisture penetrating—they woke to drops falling on a sleeping child's face. This is urgent in a way that mere dampness is not. A roof that fails inside is a roof that will rot from the center out, that will require complete replacement rather than repair. I need to go soon, to assess before midday when the light is best.

But first, a small task for myself.

I climb the ladder to my own roof—carefully, testing each rung though I made it and know it's sound. The peak of my roof is my laboratory. Here I've mounted small test sections where I trial new weaving patterns, new layering sequences, new treatments for the reed. I need to check yesterday's experiment: a section treated with a mixture of pine resin and fish oil, sealed three days ago, that should now show whether the combination resists water better than oil alone.

I reach the section and run my fingers across it. The surface is still tacky—not ideal, but not disqualifying. I pour water from a small vessel I keep up here for testing, watching how it beads and runs. It sheds well, very well, better than the control section beside it. But there's a problem: the tackiness will collect dust, create friction that damages the reeds when they move with wind and temperature. This treatment might work for a stationary surface, but not for a roof that breathes and flexes.

I make note of this—scratching symbols into a piece of bark with an awl. My notation system is my own: half pictographic, half abstract marks that represent reed types, treatments, results. Others have asked to learn it but I've been reluctant. Not from secrecy exactly, but from uncertainty about whether my system makes sense to minds that aren't mine. Perhaps I should teach it. Perhaps it will die with me. For now, I record what I learn, trusting that the knowledge serves its purpose even if only I can read it.

The sun is higher now, burning through the mist enough to create shadows. Time to go.

I climb down, gather tools—awls for probing rot, a curved knife for cutting sample sections, stakes and cord for marking problem areas, fresh reed samples to show the family what quality replacement would require. These go in my work bag, waterproof hide treated until it's nearly rigid. I add food—smoked fish, some dried root, a flask of yesterday's tea—and step out.

My boat waits at the hammock's edge, tied to a post I set myself five years ago. It's a working craft, wider than most, with low sides and a flat bottom that makes it stable for standing. The pole is willow, eight feet long, worn smooth where my hands grip. I step in, automatically compensating for the shift in weight, and push off.

The channels this morning are quiet. I pole through water that's dark with depth and tannin, reading the current's subtle shifts. Here's a place where silting is building—I can feel the resistance change as the pole touches bottom. There's a junction where flow creates a small eddy—I know this spot, use it to rest when poling against current. A heron stands in the shallows ahead, not moving as I pass. We've reached an accommodation, the basin's herons and I. They know I'm no threat. I know they're better fishers than any human will be.

I navigate by landmarks that would mean nothing to an outsider: a willow with a split trunk, a dock with a red mark on the post, a bridge with three support beams instead of the usual two. The settlement is a text I can read fluently, each element known and placed. But it's a text that changes—channels shift, structures are rebuilt, vegetation grows and dies. Part of my skill is tracking these changes, updating my mental map continuously.

Three hammocks away means crossing two major channels and a dozen smaller ones, passing under five bridges, threading between moored boats and fishing weirs. It takes half an hour of steady poling. I could do it faster, but I'm carrying tools and there's no emergency. Speed wastes energy and invites mistakes.

The family's hammock comes into view: six structures clustered tight, with the problematic one toward the center. I tie my boat at their dock—noting that the dock itself needs maintenance, boards softening with rot—and call out my arrival. It's the grandmother who emerges, a woman perhaps twenty years my elder, moving carefully but still strong.

"Thatch-reader," she greets me. We don't use personal names in casual address—trade names, family names, sometimes just roles. I've been "thatch-reader" for ten years now, since the pattern of my work became clear.

"Grandmother," I return. "Show me the trouble."

She leads me inside and points up. The stain on the ceiling beam is obvious—dark, spreading, with moisture beading at its lowest point. I've seen this before. The water is entering somewhere above, traveling along structural elements, emerging at the weakest point.

"How long since you noticed?" I ask.

"Four days. It was small then. Growing each night."

Four days. Not terrible, but not good. The rot will be establishing itself in the hidden spaces between thatch layers, in the beam itself perhaps.

"I'll need to go on the roof," I tell her. "Can you have someone watch from below, tell me when I'm over the wet spot?"

She calls and a young man appears—grandson, probably, late teens. We arrange signals. I climb their access ladder, noting that it's secure but narrow—they should widen it, make maintenance easier—and pull myself onto the roof.

Their thatch is fifteen years old, I'd guess. Not badly made, but showing age. The reed has grayed and brittled. Some sections have compressed too much, losing the air spaces that shed water. I walk carefully, distributing weight, feeling for soft spots.

The grandson calls up, guiding me. When I'm over the problem area, I kneel and begin examination.

With my awl, I probe the thatch layers. The first foot is sound—dry, still springy. But deeper, I find softness. The awl sinks too easily. I widen the hole carefully, peeling back layers, following the damage.

There. The problem announces itself in smell before sight: the sweet-rot odor of decomposing reed. I've exposed a pocket of failure where water has been pooling, unable to drain, slowly destroying the thatch from within. The damage extends perhaps two feet across, down through most of the layers.

I sit back on my heels, thinking. This isn't a simple patch job. The compromised section needs complete removal and replacement. But doing that creates new vulnerabilities—fresh edges where water can penetrate if the integration isn't perfect. And the surrounding thatch, while not yet failed, is old enough that it might go next season, making any repair temporary.

I have to give them options, with honesty about costs.

I climb down and explain what I found. The grandmother listens, face neutral, and when I finish she asks the crucial question: "Can we do just the bad part and have it last two winters?"

Two winters. That's specific. I consider. "If I do the work, yes. But the third winter will be risk. The old thatch around the repair will be seventeen years old. It might hold, might not."

She nods. This is the calculation every family makes: immediate cost versus future cost, certainty versus risk. "Do the repair. Teach my grandson to watch for failure signs. We'll save for full replacement."

This is wisdom. I agree to terms—payment in smoked fish and some textile work I need done—and we settle on timing. I'll return in three days with materials and help. The repair will take two full days.

As I pole away, I think about that roof, about all the roofs. Each one is a negotiation with water and time. Each one eventually fails. My work is about postponing that failure, managing it, making the time between dry and ruined as long as possible. It's endless work—there's always another roof, another leak, another family hoping I can stretch their resources far enough.

But it's work that matters in a way I can feel. When I repair a roof and the next rain comes and the family wakes dry, I've done something real. I've held back the water for another season. That's enough.

I pole toward home through channels that reflect a sky going gray. Rain is coming—not today probably, but soon. The air has that weight, that smell. The herons know it too; they're feeding more intensively, building reserves.

When rain comes, I'll be busy. Every leak that's been ignored will suddenly be urgent. Families will call for me, desperate, willing to pay premium. It's how I make my living—the wet seasons when my skill is desperately needed, balanced against the drier times when I experiment and prepare.

I don't mind this. I've made peace with the rhythm: work and rest, crisis and quiet, the pattern of attention that the basin demands. Some people fight against this. They want control, stability, predictability. But the basin doesn't offer those things. It offers water—constant, moving, patient, inevitable. You accept its terms or you suffer.

I accepted long ago.

Back at my hammock, I secure the boat and climb to my house. The fire has burned down again; I build it back up, set food to warm, change into dry underlayers. My hands are cold but not numb—I know the difference, watch for it carefully.

While the food heats, I update my records. The roof I inspected today, its condition and prognosis. The treatment experiment that showed promise but failed. Mental notes become physical marks on bark, building the archive of my knowledge.

Someone once asked me why I live alone. It was a genuine question—most people here can't imagine it, wouldn't want it. But for me, the solitude is necessary. My work requires concentration, observation, pattern-recognition that's easier without constant social noise. I need to think about thatch, to turn problems over in my mind, to notice subtle connections.

I'm not lonely. I work with people constantly, collaborate on projects, attend gatherings, maintain friendships. But I come back to this small space, to my own fire and my own quiet, and I breathe easier.

Is this strange here, where most people are always surrounded? Perhaps. But the Osoka make room for strangeness if it's competent strangeness, if it serves. I keep roofs dry. They let me live as I need to.

Evening comes early this time of year. The light fades to gray, then darker gray, then the blue-black that means night. I eat my simple meal, tend the fire, prepare for sleep.

Tomorrow I'll source materials for the repair—visiting the reed-cutters who maintain harvesting rights to certain beds, selecting the right quality, negotiating for quantity and price. The day after, I'll prepare the bundles, treat them with my waterproofing mixture, let them start drying to the right moisture level. The third day, early, I'll pole to the grandmother's hammock with my helper—a young woman learning the trade, who carries tools and watches and asks endless questions.

We'll strip the bad section carefully, examining what we remove for lessons about failure patterns. We'll prepare the substrate, checking the underlying structure, treating any soft spots. Then we'll build the repair layer by careful layer, weaving new to old, creating integration that water can't penetrate. It will take two full days of careful work, cold hands, aching backs, constant attention.

And when it's done, when we're packing tools and the grandmother brings tea and bread as thanks, when we pole away leaving behind a roof that will shed rain again—that's the moment. Not pride exactly, though there's some of that. More like satisfaction. Rightness. The feeling of having added something to the world instead of taking from it.

This is what I am: a person who reads thatch like others read water or weather, who solves the problem of staying dry in a place designed to be wet, who lives in the space between roof and rain, keeping one from meeting the other.

The basin needs this. Buildings need maintenance. Water needs managing. Families need dry places to sleep and raise children and store food. And I need to matter, to use the gifts I've been given—patience, attention to detail, hand-skill, pattern-recognition—in service of something beyond myself.

Tomorrow the work continues. Always, the work continues. Roofs age. Water falls. I pole between hammocks, climbing to check and repair, building and rebuilding the fragile barriers between wet and dry.

This is enough. This is more than enough. This is a life.
