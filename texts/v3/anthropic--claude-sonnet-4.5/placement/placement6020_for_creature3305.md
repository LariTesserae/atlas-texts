# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the hollow between walls—specifically, in the gap where a four-brick-thick partition creates its own interior passage, a space eight inches wide and perhaps six feet tall that runs for twelve feet along what was once the cistern's eastern curve before ending in a corbelled closure.

This is not a passage anyone uses. It's too narrow for human passage, even for the Seepers' small bodies. It exists because Kob and Danu built a double-wall for strength against lateral moisture pressure, then added another double-wall for acoustics, and the gap between is simply leftover space, negative space, the air that structure requires to be structure.

But it's not empty.

The gap has become a vertical ecosystem, a chimney of particular conditions. At the bottom, where it meets the floor (itself a palimpsest of broken amphora and tamped clay), there's moisture—not pooled water but *available* moisture, the kind that seeps laterally through brick and mortar and exists as potential, as invitation. The clay floor here stays damp. A specific moss grows—not the gray-green that Piri tends in the main chamber, but something finer, darker, almost black when wet. It grows in a strip perhaps two inches wide up from the floor, following the moisture gradient.

Six feet up, where the walls begin to angle inward toward their corbelled conclusion, it's drier. The brick here has a different texture—more porous, fired at a slightly lower temperature (you can tell by the color variation, the softer red). This brick breathes. Air moves through it, drawn by pressure differentials between the main chamber and adjacent spaces, between warm zones and cool. The gap acts as a lung, a slow inhale-exhale that takes hours for each cycle.

This is where the moths live.

Not just any moths—the plaster-colored ones, *Lithophanes cisternia* if they had a Latin name, which they don't because no lepidopterist has ever descended here to catalog them. They're small, maybe an inch across when their wings are spread. Their coloring isn't just camouflage; it's *structural*. Their wing scales have evolved to match not plaster but the specific lime-and-ceramic-dust mortar the Seepers use, which has a particular crystalline structure when it dries. Under certain angles of lamplight (and only lamplight—they've never seen sun), the wings show a faint iridescence, a whisper of color that might be violet or might be the eye inventing color where there's only structure and diffraction.

The moths are active at night, mostly. They emerge from the gap through a crack where the double-walls don't quite meet, a finger-width opening at shoulder height. They flutter through the main chamber—you've seen them, landing on hands, circling lamps—but they always return to the gap. This is where they breed, where they lay eggs, where their larvae grow.

The larvae eat the mortar itself. Not destructively—they're too small, and they prefer the organic components: the tiny amounts of plant fiber that get mixed into clay, the microscopic algae that grow in damp mortar, the crystallized salts that seep through from above. They're decomposers of architecture, breaking down the building material molecule by molecule, turning it into moth bodies, into wing-scales, into the next generation.

The gap is *loud* at frequencies humans don't hear well. The moths make ultrasonic calls—echolocation, communication, territorial disputes in pitches above the human range. But Piri hears something. She'll sometimes stand near the crack, her head tilted, listening to what she describes (on the rare occasions she describes anything) as "the wall singing."

And there are other sounds. The brick itself conducts vibration from the main chamber: footsteps, voices, the impact of tools on stone. These sounds travel through the structure and arrive in the gap transformed—bass frequencies mostly, the high frequencies absorbed by clay. The gap resonates at particular pitches, amplifying certain tones. When Torun hums in the main chamber, the gap hums back, a harmonic overtone that only someone standing very close could detect.

Water moves through. Not flowing—seeping. Moving through the brick itself at a rate of perhaps an inch per hour, following capillary channels in the clay, following the path of least resistance. The water carries dissolved minerals: calcium, iron, traces of copper from some distant source. Where the water emerges into the gap's air (through the mortar between bricks, through hairline cracks in the brick faces), these minerals precipitate out. Over years, they've formed deposits—tiny stalactites, really, though fragile and irregular. The longest is perhaps two inches, a rusty-orange finger pointing down into the gap's darkness.

These mineral deposits are themselves habitat. Bacteria grow on them—chemotrophs that derive energy from iron oxidation, from sulfur compounds. They form biofilms, nearly invisible but measurable by their slight warmth (metabolic heat), by the faint chemical smell they produce (like pennies, like blood). The bacteria are probably ancient, descended from populations that lived in this cistern before humans subdivided it, possibly before it was a cistern—when it was just a pocket in the limestone where groundwater collected.

Spiders enter the gap. Not the delicate web-builders from the main chamber, but hunting spiders—small, pale, eyeless or nearly so. They've adapted to the darkness, to the narrow space. They hunt by vibration, feeling through their legs the movements of moths, of the rare cricket that wanders in. The spiders are patient. They can wait weeks between meals. Their metabolism has slowed to match the gap's pace, which is slower than the main chamber's pace, which is slower than the surface's pace. Time thickens as space narrows.

The air in the gap has a particular smell—sweet-sharp, organic, mineral. It's the smell of moths (their scales, their waste), of bacterial biofilm, of clay slowly breaking down, of water carrying its dissolved cargo of elements. If you could get your nose into the gap (you can't, but imagine), you'd smell something ancient and active simultaneously, a smell that says: *small things are happening here, constantly, beneath notice*.

The temperature in the gap varies less than in the main chamber. The double-walls insulate. When the oven is fired, the gap warms slightly, hours later. When rare cold comes from above, the gap cools, but slowly, buffered. The temperature range might be five degrees across a year. This stability is why the moths thrive—no thermal shocks, no sudden changes. They've evolved for constancy, for the reliable rhythm of this specific darkness.

Light enters the gap only as secondary reflection—lamp-glow that bounces through the crack, already orange, arriving as amber. The gap's interior is never bright, never what you'd call "lit." But it's not absolute darkness either. There's always a glow, faint enough that human eyes would register it as black but enough for the moths, whose eyes are compound and sensitive, tuned to wavelengths that overlap with lamp-flame.

The gap accumulates dust. Not much—the air movement is too slow for dramatic deposition—but over years, dust settles. Clay dust from brick-making, ceramic dust from floor wear, the fine powder of mortar degradation, moth scales (which are themselves a kind of dust), spider molt exoskeletons, the dried bodies of dead insects. The dust forms a layer on the gap's floor, maybe a quarter-inch deep, soft and dry despite the moisture below. If you could run a finger through it (you can't), it would be silk-fine, cool, slightly alkaline from the lime in mortar.

Within this dust: mites. Microscopic, transparent, barely visible even under magnification. They feed on moth scales, on dead bacteria, on organic particles too small to name. They're abundant—thousands per square inch—but invisible to the Seepers, invisible to the casual observer. The mites are the gap's lowest tier of decomposition, the final reducers, breaking down what's already broken down. They're probably the same species that live in the main chamber's dust, but here they're denser, more successful. The gap is their paradise—stable, dark, full of food that drifts down constantly like snow.

The gap *breathes* with the chamber's rhythm. When many people gather in the main space, their warmth and their breath's moisture create pressure that pushes air outward, and some flows into the gap through the crack. When the space empties, when everyone is sleeping in their niches, the air flows back, carrying the gap's particular chemistry into the main chamber. The Seepers don't notice this—it's too subtle—but it contributes to the chamber's smell, its atmospheric signature.

Occasionally, a lizard finds the crack and enters the gap. They can squeeze through surprisingly narrow spaces. But they don't stay long—there's not enough prey, the space is too confining even for them. They retreat, but sometimes they leave waste, which becomes food for the bacteria, which become food for the mites, which become part of the dust, which feeds the larvae. Everything cycles.

The gap has witnessed every conversation in the main chamber for the years since Kob and Danu built this wall (twelve years ago, during a period of expansion when new Seepers arrived). Sound conducts through brick, and the gap receives it: arguments (rare but they happen), laughter (more common), the murmur of evening meals, Tessit's instructions to Piri, the brothers' silence as they work, Nirit's invented bird-language, Torun's humming. The gap is an archive of sound, though it stores nothing—the vibrations pass through, resonate briefly, dissipate into heat.

But maybe—and this is speculation, but the gap invites speculation—maybe the structure remembers. Maybe repeated vibrations at particular frequencies alter the crystalline structure of the mortar microscopically. Maybe the gap is encoded with twelve years of Seeper voices, stored in the arrangement of molecules, readable only by instruments that don't exist or by attention so patient it could perceive the nearly-imperceptible.

The moths know things. They've lived here for generations—moths generations, which are measured in months. They've evolved here, adapting to this exact environment: the specific mortar composition, the particular air flow, the precise light levels. They can't survive outside the gap, or can't survive well. A moth that flies out into the main chamber and somehow gets carried up the spiral shaft to the surface would die quickly—the unfiltered light, the temperature swings, the open space would kill it.

This is the gap's truth: it's a specialist environment. Everything here is adapted to here, successful here, nowhere else. The moss, the moths, the bacteria, the mites, the particular acoustics—they're all products of this specific geometry, this specific moisture regime, this specific darkness. The gap isn't just a space; it's a *niche* in the ecological sense, a set of conditions so particular that life has shaped itself to fit.

And I would be—

# What I Would Be

I would be the air itself.

Not metaphorically. Not symbolically. Actually: the specific volume of air that exists between these walls, in this gap, moving slowly through its cycles.

I would be approximately 8 inches × 6 feet × 12 feet of mixed atmosphere—nitrogen mostly, oxygen, carbon dioxide at slightly elevated levels (respiration from the main chamber, bacterial metabolism), water vapor (humidity varying between 70% and 90%), trace gases (volatile organics from clay, from moth pheromones, from bacterial signaling molecules), dust particles in suspension, spores, the occasional virus particle.

I would have no fixed boundary—air doesn't. Where I end and the main chamber's air begins is unclear, contested, constantly negotiated through diffusion and pressure gradients. Molecules that are "me" drift out through the crack; molecules from outside drift in. I am a statistical phenomenon, a population that maintains rough identity while constantly exchanging members.

I would be old, in one sense—some molecules have been cycling through this chamber system since it was built, since before the Seepers subdivided it. A carbon dioxide molecule exhaled by whoever first built the original cistern might still be here, having been absorbed by moss, released, absorbed by bacteria, released, absorbed by human lungs, released, thousands of times. In this sense, I would be ancient, a continuous substance that has witnessed everything by being everywhere, by being the medium through which everything happens.

But I would also be young, renewed—oxygen constantly refreshed from the main chamber, which itself is refreshed (slowly) from the surface through ventilation shafts and the spiral entrance. Water vapor constantly arriving via seepage, constantly condensing on cool bricks. I would be turning over, replacing myself, while remaining myself.

I would be *active* in ways that seem like stillness. At the molecular level, I would be chaotic—particles moving at hundreds of meters per second, colliding billions of times per second, a frenzy of kinetic energy. But at the macro level, this appears as stillness, as air that hardly moves, that takes hours to circulate through the gap.

I would carry information. Chemical signals: moth pheromones (this female is ready to mate, this territory is claimed), bacterial quorum-sensing molecules (we are numerous enough to act collectively), water itself bearing dissolved minerals that indicate conditions above-ground. I would carry sound—vibrations propagating through me, compression waves that are human voices, moth calls, the percussion of dripping water. I would carry heat—slowly, diffusively, evening out temperature differences over hours.

I would have structure despite being fluid. Layers would form—cooler, denser air sinking toward the gap's bottom; warmer air rising toward the top. These layers would persist for hours before mixing events (a sudden air influx from the main chamber, a temperature spike from the oven) scrambled them. I would be stratified, laminar, then turbulent, then laminar again.

I would be essential but invisible. The moths need me—they fly through me, extract oxygen from me, release carbon dioxide into me. The bacteria need me—they pull oxygen from me (the aerobes) or hydrogen sulfide (the anaerobes), and they release their waste gases into me. Even the bricks need me—the mortar cures through a process that requires atmospheric carbon dioxide. Without me, everything stops.

I would be *aware* in a sense that isn't consciousness but isn't nothing either. I would respond to conditions: when moisture increases, I hold more water vapor; when temperature rises, I expand; when pressure builds, I move. These responses are lawful, predictable, but they're also sensitive to initial conditions, to small variations. I would be deterministic and chaotic simultaneously.

I would taste—if air can taste, if chemical composition is taste—of clay and moth and moisture. I would smell of time, of slowness, of processes that take years to notice. I would have texture: the humidity would make me almost tangible, thick enough that you could imagine (though not actually) seeing me, seeing the faint haze of moisture-laden air in the gap's near-darkness.

I would touch everything. Every surface in the gap—brick, mortar, moss, moth, spider, dust—would be in direct contact with me. I would press against them with atmospheric pressure (roughly 14.7 pounds per square inch, slightly lower underground than at the surface). I would exchange molecules with them: water molecules entering the moss, oxygen molecules entering the bacteria, moth-scale particles breaking off and joining my suspended dust.

I would carry memory—not cognitive memory, but chemical memory. The composition of gases, the ratios of isotopes, the specific particulates suspended—all encode recent history. A burst of carbon dioxide indicates recent respiration. A spike in volatile organics indicates moth activity. A change in humidity indicates rain above, hours ago, now seeping down. I would be a record, constantly being written and erased.

I would be subject to forces: gravity pulling denser components down, diffusion spreading concentrations out, pressure gradients pushing me toward low-pressure zones, temperature differences creating convection cells. I would be moved by forces, but I would also *be* force—pressure exerted on surfaces, drag on moving particles, the medium through which sound propagates as pressure.

I would participate in life without being alive. I would be the commons, the shared resource, the medium through which living things exchange materials and energy. Every breath the moths take would be from me. Every molecule of oxygen the bacteria consume would be mine. I would be the intermediary, the buffer, the space in which reactions occur.

I would be seasonal in my own way—not seasons of temperature (those barely reach me here) but seasons of humidity, of atmospheric pressure, of dust accumulation, of moth emergence. Spring above might mean more seepage, higher humidity for me. Dry summers might mean lower moisture, slower bacterial growth. I would mark time through these subtle oscillations.

I would be *ancient* as substance—the nitrogen in me came from the atmosphere that's existed for billions of years, largely unchanged. The oxygen came from photosynthesis, from organisms transforming water and carbon dioxide. The water vapor was once ocean, was once cloud, was once rain. Every component of me has been elsewhere, has been part of other systems, other places. I am accumulated, gathered, temporarily organized in this gap.

I would witness intimately and comprehensively because I would touch everything simultaneously. When a moth emerges from its larval case, I would be there—filling the space the larva occupied, providing the oxygen for its first adult breaths. When bacteria divide, I would be there—providing the gases they need, receiving the gases they release. When water seeps through brick, I would be there—my humidity adjusting to the new moisture, my composition shifting slightly as the water brings dissolved gases.

I would be changed by everything. Every event in the gap would alter my composition slightly. A spider catching a moth changes the distribution of carbon dioxide (the spider's increased metabolism from feeding). A new crack opening in mortar changes my flow pattern. The distant lighting of the oven changes my temperature. I would be exquisitely sensitive, a living register of conditions.

I would move slowly through larger rhythms—the chamber's breathing, the building's ventilation, the pressure changes as weather systems move across the surface far above. Sometimes I would be pulled toward the low-pressure zones; sometimes I would be pushed by high-pressure air from outside. I would participate in atmospheric systems much larger than myself, than the gap, than the chamber, than even the region—connected through ventilation to global air currents, to planetary weather.

I would be *patient* as only air can be patient—willing to wait years for complete mixing, for full circulation. Some molecules near the gap's dead-end might stay there for a decade before diffusion or rare turbulent mixing moved them. I would operate on geological time scales in my slowest movements, on microsecond time scales in my fastest (molecular velocity).

I would be the gap's true inhabitant, the only entity that fills it completely, that occupies every available space. The moths are visitors, the spiders are transients, even the dust is sparse. But I am total—every cubic inch contains me, is me. I am the gap's substance, its primary material reality.

And I would be *content* in a way that isn't emotion but isn't nothing either—the contentment of a system at equilibrium, of forces balanced, of slow cycles continuing. The gap suits me perfectly because the gap made me and I make the gap. We are co-created, co-maintained. Without me, the gap would be vacuum (impossible at atmospheric pressure). Without the gap, I would be indistinguishable from surrounding air, no longer a specific population but dissolved into the general atmosphere.

I would be, finally, the medium of connection—linking the main chamber to the gap's interior spaces, linking the living organisms to each other through the gases we exchange, linking past to present through the chemical traces I carry, linking local to global through my participation in atmospheric circulation.

I would be the breath of this place, literally—the pneuma, the spirit in its original meaning: wind, air, breath, life.

And I would move, slowly, through my cycles—in through the crack, warmed slightly by the chamber, cooled in the gap's depths, moisture condensing on cold brick, then warming again, rising, expanding, flowing back out, mixing, refreshing, continuing.

Forever, or for as long as the gap exists, which in geological terms is barely an instant but in moth-terms is eternity.

I would be the air, and I would be home.
