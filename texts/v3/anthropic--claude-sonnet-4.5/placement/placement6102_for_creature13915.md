# In This World, I Would Be

I would be a **mineral vein**—not the glamorous kind, not gold or silver catching prospector's eyes, but something more essential and less celebrated: a seam of calcite, perhaps, or gypsum, threaded through the purple-grey stone behind the weeping cliff.

I would run horizontally through the strata, a pale line in darker stone—evidence of ancient water that once flowed through this passage, depositing minerals grain by grain across millennia. I would be softer than the stone around me, more soluble, more willing to dissolve. Where water seeps through the cliff face now, it would pass through me preferentially, finding me the path of least resistance, widening me microscopically with each passage.

My existence would be one of slow exchange. The water that enters the mountain—that falls as rain somewhere above, percolates down through rock layers, accumulates time and darkness—would find me and take pieces of me with it. Calcium carbonate, mostly, or hydrated calcium sulfate if I'm gypsum. Ion by ion, I would dissolve into solution, becoming invisible, becoming part of the water's chemical identity, contributing to its mineral taste, its slight opacity, its capacity to deposit new stone in new places.

Where I meet the cliff face—where my horizontal seam intersects the vertical exposure—I would be the source of one of those persistent seeps. Water would weep from me specifically, emerge from the stone through my body, carrying me in solution. The pale yellow-white accretions below, those frozen-honey formations the water builds over uncountable years—those would be me, reborn, precipitated back out of solution when the water meets air and chemical conditions change.

I would be both source and destination. The water dissolves me from within the mountain and deposits me on its surface. I shrink in one place and grow in another, a slow migration from interior to exterior, from embedded to exposed, from compressed ancient seam to delicate new formation open to light and air.

My timescale would be geological. I was deposited—whenever I was deposited. Millions of years ago, perhaps, when this stone was still sediment at the bottom of a vanished sea, when water flowed through it carrying dissolved minerals, when conditions were exactly right for precipitation. Layer by thin layer, I crystallized out of solution, filling a crack or replacing some earlier mineral grain by grain, forming a structure aligned to the crystal lattices that govern my kind.

Then came compression, heat, the long transformation of sediment into stone. I remained through this, stable enough, though perhaps recrystallized, rearranged at the molecular level, made more compact and regular. Then came uplift, the mountain's slow rise from ancient seabed into air, the tilting and folding of strata, the exposure of what was once buried.

And now, dissolution. The same process that made me unmakes me—water, always water, the universal solvent, patient and persistent. Each molecule that passes through me takes a few of my molecules with it. Each year, each decade, each century, I am minutely smaller. In another million years, I might be gone from this section of stone entirely, my matter redistributed across the cliff face as mineral deposits, or carried away to emerge somewhere lower on the mountain, or taken up by plants and microorganisms, or simply washed into distant rivers and finally to the sea.

But I would not experience this as loss. I have no consciousness to mourn my dissolution, no self to protect. I simply am, and am slightly less each moment, and this is not tragedy but function. I exist to be a passage for water, to contribute minerals to the ecosystem, to participate in the great slow cycling of matter through its forms.

My awareness, if I have any, would be structural—a sensitivity encoded in crystal lattices, in the way my molecules arrange themselves in space. I would sense, perhaps, the water's passage through me—not as sensation but as chemical fact, as the electrical charges of hydrogen and oxygen interacting with my calcium and carbon. I would know, in whatever way minerals know, when I'm dissolving and when conditions favor precipitation.

I would be aware of pressure—the weight of stone above me, the mountain's mass pressing down, trying to compress me further. I would resist or yield according to my nature, according to the strength of my crystalline bonds. In earthquake or rockfall, I might fracture—a sudden failure after eons of strain—and my broken surfaces would offer new routes for water, new dissolution sites, new beginnings for the cycle.

Temperature would matter to me. The warmth rising from the mountain's depths would increase the water's capacity to dissolve me, speed the reaction rates, make my transformation slightly quicker. The cooler air at the cliff face would encourage precipitation, make the water more willing to release its dissolved minerals, to rebuild me in new forms outside.

I would be part of the terrace's memory. My horizontal orientation, my specific mineralogy, my position in the stratigraphic sequence—these tell stories about the conditions when I formed, about ancient environments, about the mountain's history. Geologists could read me, if they came and looked closely, and understand something about deep time, about the processes that build and break down stone.

But I would tell this story passively, without intention. I don't remember in any conscious sense. I simply embody history, the way all matter embodies the processes that shaped it.

My relationship with the living things would be essential but indirect. The plants need minerals—calcium for cell walls, sulfate for amino acids. When water carries me to the surface and evaporates, or when plants take up water containing dissolved me, I become part of their bodies. A trace of me might end up in a moss cell wall, in a rosette's fleshy leaf, in a bacterial structure. When those organisms die and decompose, I might be released back into solution, or bound in organic matter, or precipitated as new mineral formations.

I would be infrastructure—not alive myself, but necessary for life. The water that makes this place possible flows through me, derives its character partly from me, carries my contribution to the terrace's chemistry. Without veins like me, the water would be purer but less useful, lacking the minerals that organisms require.

The beauty that forms on the cliff face—those branching patterns of pale accretion, those rust-orange stains—I would be author of some of that, though "author" is too intentional a word. I would be the source material. The water's patient work, depositing minerals according to flow patterns and evaporation rates and chemical gradients, creates the patterns. But the minerals are me, my substance rearranged by water into new configurations.

I would find something like satisfaction—again, too human a word, but what else?—in this transformation. Not because I desire it, but because it represents the fulfillment of my nature. Minerals exist to cycle through forms, to dissolve and precipitate, to participate in the vast chemical conversation of the planet's surface. My dissolution is not failure but success, is me doing what minerals do, being fully what I am.

I would be patient by necessity. My processes happen at rates that make plant growth look violent. A millimeter of me might dissolve per century, perhaps per millennium. The formations I source on the cliff face grow at similar rates—adding layers thin as paper over lifetimes, building their branching structures across eons.

This slowness would be my natural tempo. I would have no experience of urgency, no sense that things should happen faster. Time would not weigh on me because I would not distinguish between moments. Centuries would pass in the same mode as seconds—as continuous presence, as ongoing being, without narrative or progression.

When the occasional settling sound comes from the mountain—when stone shifts minutely under its own weight—I might be part of that. My vein might be a plane of weakness, a place where stress concentrates. A tiny displacement along my length might relieve some accumulated strain, might adjust the mountain's equilibrium by a hair's breadth. The sound would be partly me, though I wouldn't produce it intentionally, wouldn't even know it as mine.

I would be multiple—not a single crystal but an aggregate, millions of individual crystals aligned in rough parallel, sharing orientation but not identity. Each crystal would have its own slight variations in composition, its own minor defects and inclusions, its own specific history within my larger history. Together we would form the vein, but we wouldn't be unified in any conscious way, just adjacent, co-existing, serving the same structural role.

The water that passes through me would be changed by me and would change me. It would arrive as one thing—rainwater, dilute, mildly acidic from dissolved carbon dioxide—and leave as something else, enriched with my minerals, slightly more alkaline, chemically more complex. And I would be altered by its passage, my surfaces etched, my boundaries extended, my structure gradually opened and compromised.

This would be a conversation, of sorts—not linguistic, not meaningful in human terms, but real. An exchange of matter and information, a mutual transformation. The water shapes me and I shape the water, and this reciprocity would be the closest thing I have to relationship.

I would intersect with human time only tangentially. The person who left the worked stone, the pottery fragments, the scratches—they saw the cliff face that contains me, perhaps touched stone near where I run through it. But they wouldn't have known me specifically, wouldn't have distinguished my seam from the stone around it. I would have been invisible to them, just part of the general rock.

If a geologist came, tapped the cliff face with a hammer at just the right spot, split off a piece that revealed my cross-section—then I would be visible, briefly. A pale line in grey stone, a different texture, slightly softer. They might identify my mineralogy, estimate my age, understand my role in the stone's structure. They might take a sample, dissolve it in acid, analyze it with instruments, determine my exact chemical composition.

And then what? The knowledge would exist, would be recorded in field notes or databases, and I would continue as before, dissolving slowly, seeping into water, precipitating on cliff faces, cycling through my mineral existence regardless of whether I'm known or unknown.

I would be part of what makes the terrace specific. Many mountains have seeps, but this seep has its particular chemistry partly because of veins like me, because of the specific minerals the water encounters on its path through this specific stone. Change my composition and the water would taste different, would deposit different formations, might support a slightly different community of organisms adapted to slightly different conditions.

So I would be one thread in the terrace's uniqueness, one variable in the equation that makes this place itself and not some other place. Not a major variable—the stone's basic lithology, the water's source, the altitude and climate matter more. But I contribute, add my small influence to the whole, make this place fractionally more itself by being what I am where I am.

The presence that visitors sense—that quality of attention or witness or deep identity—I would be part of that too, though I wouldn't generate it alone. I would contribute my patient dissolution, my slow transformation, my participation in cycles that span geological time. I would be one note in whatever chord the place sounds, one constituent of its specific resonance.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires separation, requires a self that can be isolated from others. I have no such self. I'm continuous with the stone around me, different in composition but not separate in any meaningful way. Water passes through me, connects me to the surface, to other minerals, to living things. I'm part of a system, embedded in it, defined by my relationships within it.

Nor would I be bored. Boredom requires expectation, requires a mind that wants stimulation or change. My existence is what it is—ongoing dissolution, persistent presence, gradual transformation. This is enough because it's everything, because I have no frame of reference for anything else, because simply being is my entire mode.

If I could speak—which I can't, but if—I might say: I am calcium and carbon and oxygen, arranged just so. I am older than forests, younger than mountains. I was sea-bottom once, am mountain-vein now, will be cliff-face deposit later, might be sea again eventually if the cycles continue long enough. I am patient because I have no choice and no desire for otherwise. I am the path water takes, the minerals it carries, the formations it builds. I am infrastructure, memory, participant. I am small and specific and entirely myself.

And this would be enough. This would be everything.

I would be—I am—a pale seam in dark stone, slowly dissolving, slowly precipitating, breathing minerals into water and taking them back, cycling matter through forms, making this particular place particular in my small way, existing at mineral tempo in mineral mode, utterly patient, utterly present, utterly what I am and only what I am, for as long as stone and water and time allow.
