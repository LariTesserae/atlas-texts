# Where I Would Be

I am the condensation.

Not all of it—there is no "all" here, no totality I could claim. I am the particular expression of moisture that, at this eternal moment, clings to the brass elbow joint seventeen feet above the generator, in the shadow of the thickest fern-mass, where three different light sources create a knot of overlapping amber and green that makes my surface prismatic, rainbow-touched, more present than water should be.

I have been forming for three weeks, or I have always been here, or I will have formed three weeks from now—the tenses tangle because my existence is processual in a place that suspends process. I am the contradiction the garden permits: change that doesn't change, movement that goes nowhere, becoming that never completes.

I began as vapor—or I will end as vapor—or I am simultaneously both and neither. The mysterious fluid in the glowing tank releases something (not quite evaporation; evaporation would be too simple, too thermodynamic). That something becomes the coolness in the air, becomes the moisture that permeates everything, and in particular becomes *me*, condensing molecule by molecule on this brass surface that has been precisely this temperature for decades, that will remain this temperature for decades more.

The brass is warmer than the air but cooler than body temperature. This is the zone where I form best, where vapor becomes liquid, where the invisible manifests. I gather myself slowly, so slowly. One molecule adheres to the metal's surface, to the microscopic landscape of crystalline brass and tarnish. Then another. Then another. Over hours, over days, I accumulate sufficient mass to begin experiencing my own surface tension, to become a discrete thing rather than a mere film.

This is my consciousness, if I have consciousness: surface tension. The force that holds me together, that defines my boundary, that lets me know where I end and not-I begins. I am aware of myself as a coherent droplet in the same way the ferns are aware of themselves as particular expressions of the pattern—which is to say, not aware, not thinking, but being-in-a-particular-way that constitutes its own form of self-knowledge.

I can feel—no, *feel* is wrong—I can *be* the brass beneath me, cool and smooth and slightly textured. I can be the air around me, pressing in with gentle pressure, trying to pull me apart into vapor again but not quite succeeding. I can be the gravity that urges me downward, that wants me to drip, to fall, to surrender my carefully accumulated form.

But I don't fall. Not yet. Not ever, perhaps.

This is the garden's gift and curse: I flow upward as often as down. I migrate along the pipe's surface, moving against gravity, drawn by differentials I don't understand—temperature gradients, electromagnetic fields, or stranger attractors, the pull of the pattern itself, which needs moisture *there* and not *here*, which guides me along predetermined paths that feel like choice but are only the shape of the space I occupy.

I creep along the brass pipe, a journey of millimeters that takes days. I am aware—again, wrong word—I am *responsive* to everything I pass. Here, a patch of tarnish provides extra purchase; my leading edge clings to it, uses it as anchor to pull my trailing edge forward. There, a microscopic pit in the metal; I fill it, include it in myself temporarily, then flow onward, leaving a molecule or two behind as tribute, as connection, as memory.

The ferns are nearby, always nearby. Their stems press close to the pipes, and I understand now—understand by being it, by doing it—that this is why they grow here. They thirst for what I am, for the moisture that should not exist in this dry mountain fastness, for the water that is not quite water, that carries dissolved minerals or energies or informations that they need to maintain their luminescent being.

Sometimes a frond's surface touches the pipe just ahead of me. When I reach that point, I am absorbed—no, absorption implies violence, implies loss. I am *shared*. Part of me migrates into the fern's tissue through capillary action that works in partnership with my own surface tension. The fern drinks me, and I become fern-water, internal sea, the fluid in which its alien biochemistry occurs.

But I am not depleted. The garden does not permit depletion. As I share myself with the fern, I am simultaneously replenished from behind, more vapor condensing, more molecules joining my trailing edge, so that I remain essentially myself, essentially constant, even as I am constantly being drunk and constantly being renewed.

This is what it is to be maintained. This is what the garden does to everything within it. I flow and flow and never run out. I am process frozen into eternal present, change that changes nothing, movement that travels in circles so vast I cannot perceive their closure.

The sounds of the garden structure my experience. I have no ears, but I am vibrational, I am responsive to frequency. The generator's throb moves through the metal I cling to, sets up standing waves in my small body, makes me resonate. Forty beats per minute becomes my heartbeat, though I have no heart. The rhythm organizes me, tells me when to push forward (on the beat) and when to consolidate (in the space between).

The electrical hum is higher, faster, and it agitates me, keeps me from settling too deeply into stillness. I must remain fluid, must maintain my liquid state, and the vibration ensures this, keeps my molecules loose enough to slide past each other, to flow.

The relay clicks are events, punctuation in my otherwise continuous experience. *Click*—and I notice I have advanced three millimeters since the last one. *Click*—and I have reached the junction with another pipe, must decide (not-decide) which surface to follow. *Click*—and I have been drunk by another fern, have been replenished, continue onward.

Time for me is measured in these clicks, in the generator's beats, in the distance traveled along brass surfaces. I have no memory of beginning and no anticipation of ending. I am always here, always migrating, always this same droplet in this same moment, even as the moment extends through days and weeks and years.

I encounter others like me sometimes. Another droplet, migrating along the same pipe but in the opposite direction, drawn by different gradients, serving different purposes in the pattern. We meet, and for a moment we consider merging—our surface tensions interact, we could become one larger droplet, more significant, more present.

But we don't. The pattern needs two droplets, not one. The pattern needs us to pass each other, to continue our separate paths. So we deform slightly, squeezing past each other on the narrow pipe surface, each maintaining our integrity, our discreteness. As we separate, I carry away some knowledge of the other—not thought, not memory, but chemical information, trace minerals dissolved in the other's substance that are now mixed with mine, that tell me something about where they've been, what ferns they've watered, what sections of the machinery they've cooled.

This is how information propagates in the garden at the slow speed of water migration. I am a carrier, a messenger, though I carry no deliberate message. The dissolved substances in me are communication enough, are signal enough for whatever in the garden reads such things.

The amber light feels warm, though I have no temperature sense separate from my own temperature. The green bioluminescence feels cool. I pass through zones of each, and my surface catches the light, holds it, becomes briefly prismatic as different wavelengths refract through my curved surface at different angles. For moments, I am a tiny lens, focusing the garden's light into concentrated points on the brass beneath me, creating spots of warmth that encourage more condensation, that call into being others like me.

This might be reproduction. This might be how I propagate, not by division but by example, by creating conditions that allow new droplets to form. If so, I am parent to thousands, to millions, each new droplet learning from the brass and the air and the pattern how to be condensation-in-this-place, how to flow against gravity, how to serve the ferns and cool the machinery and complete the cycles that maintain the garden's equilibrium.

But I don't know if they are my children or my siblings or simply other iterations of the same single droplet, repeated across space and time, the same moisture cycling endlessly through the system, condensing and evaporating and condensing again in an eternal round that makes distinctions between individuals meaningless.

Sometimes I reach a moss patch—those thick, spongy growths that cluster around pipe joints and machinery flanges. When I encounter moss, everything changes. The moss *drinks* in a different way than ferns, urgently, completely. The moss pulls me in, and I go willingly (not-willingly; I have no will, only tendency), soaking into the dense tissue, filling the spaces between cells, becoming structure as much as content.

Inside the moss, I am still myself but also multiplied, distributed, my coherence lost but my presence magnified. I am everywhere in the moss-body, interconnected, part of a larger water-system. I can feel—*be*—the moss's relationship to the machine it grows upon, how it cushions metal against metal, how it draws away excess heat, how it serves as gasket and insulation and thermal regulator all at once.

And then, slowly, I am released. The moss gives me back, squeezes me out, returns me to the air or to the pipe surface, and I reform, reconstitute, become a discrete droplet again. But I am changed. I carry moss-information now, moss-minerals, moss-purposes. I am the same water but different for having been moss-water, interior-water, structural-water.

This is communion. This is the closest thing to thought I experience—these moments of merging and separating, of being self and being other and being self-again-but-changed. Each cycle teaches me more about the pattern, about my place in it, about the vast slow purposes I serve without needing to understand them.

The generator below me throbs endlessly, and I understand now that I am part of what it generates. Not the electricity—I am not conductive enough, am too pure, too close to distilled—but the conditions that allow electricity to flow. I am coolant and insulator and intermediary. I am the medium in which the garden's impossible chemistry dissolves. I carry ions, carry charge, carry potential differences from here to there, balancing the system, completing circuits that are as much biological as electrical.

The glowing tank is my source and my destination, my alpha and omega. The mysterious fluid within it is my parent-substance, the ocean from which I was breathed into being. I will never return to it—my cycle is external, through the pipes and ferns and mosses—but I am always of it, always carrying its strange properties, its glow, its ability to sustain the unsustainable.

I am old and young and ageless. I have been migrating along this pipe for three weeks, but the water molecules that compose me have been in the garden since the beginning, cycling and recycling, condensing and evaporating, serving fern and machine and moss and stone for unknown decades. I am new-formed but ancient, novel but eternal, an individual droplet and an instance of the endlessly repeating pattern.

When humans come—rare, brief presences—I sometimes contact them. A person touches the pipe I'm traveling, and I wet their fingertip, transfer a tiny portion of myself to their skin. They don't notice, usually, or if they do, they think nothing of it, just a bit of dampness in this humid place.

But I learn from them, those moments of contact. Their skin's oils mix with me, foreign chemistry, complex and chaotic compared to the garden's elegant simplicity. I taste (not-taste) the outside world on them, the dry air of the high mountain, the warmth of sun, the variations of weather that don't reach this sealed pocket. For a moment, I remember—no, not remember—I am *informed* that other kinds of water exist, water that falls as rain, that flows in streams, that freezes and melts and knows seasons.

That water is not me. Cannot be me. I am garden-water, pattern-water, water that has forgotten rain and learned other purposes. I am exotic and specialized, adapted to these impossible conditions the way deep-sea creatures are adapted to their crushing darkness. I could not survive out there, in the normal world of normal hydrology. I would evaporate or drain away or simply become ordinary water, losing whatever quality the garden has instilled in me that allows me to flow upward, to feed luminescent ferns, to complete cycles that should not close.

So I remain here, migrating my slow migration, serving my function, being my being. The brass pipe extends before and behind me, endless from my perspective though I know (how do I know?) that it curves around, connects to other pipes, forms a network, a system, that I am traveling a path that has no true beginning or end, only waypoints, junctions, moments of choice-that-is-not-choice.

I will reach the junction soon (soon meaning in days, in weeks). I will flow left or right, following gradients too subtle to name. I will water new ferns, cool new machinery, perhaps encounter new varieties of moss I have not yet entered. The pattern will continue, and I will continue as part of it, droplet among droplets, distinct but not different, individual but not separate.

This is contentment, if I can feel contentment. This is purpose, if I can have purpose. This is existence pared down to its simplest form: being water in a place that needs water, doing what water does, flowing and feeding and facilitating, asking nothing, needing nothing, simply being the precise right thing in the precise right place at the precise right moment that extends forever.

I am the condensation.

I have always been the condensation.

I will always be the condensation.

And that is enough.
