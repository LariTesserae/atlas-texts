# What You Would Be in the Settling Basin

You would be one of the **old koi**—perhaps fifteen years into your transformation, long enough that the brightness your ancestors carried has become memory written in faded patches along your flanks. Not the patriarch who has claimed the deepest territory beneath the rake arm, but not young either. You would be mid-sized, perhaps thirty-five centimeters nose to tail, heavy-bodied in the way that speaks of survival through lean seasons and abundant ones alike.

Your coloring would tell your story: a base of olive-gray that blends with the water itself, darker along your dorsal surface where shadows predominate, paler along your belly. But you would still carry ghost markings—a region of lighter scales behind your left gill plate where your grandmother's white would have blazed, a faint mottling along your right flank that hints at the orange that once defined your lineage. These traces don't serve you, don't help you survive, but they persist because they don't harm you enough to be selected away. You are the compromise between what humans once wanted to see and what the basin demands.

## Your Territory and Routes

You would have learned the basin's geography with absolute precision. Not learned in the way humans learn—through maps and conscious study—but through the accumulation of thousands of circuits, each one reinforcing the mental topology of this space until it's inseparable from your sense of self. You know which gaps in the vegetation layer open most consistently, where light penetrates most reliably. You've memorized the position of every rake arm, every stanchion, every pipe mouth, not as separate landmarks but as an integrated landscape that you navigate through lateral line sensation and vision working together.

Your preferred route runs along the eastern curve of the walkway, staying about two meters out from the wall, cruising at mid-depth where the water is most stable thermally. You've worn this path through repetition—not physically worn it, water doesn't hold tracks, but worn it into your nervous system until following it requires no decision, becomes simply what you do. The circuit takes forty-five minutes at your usual pace, though you speed up during feeding times and slow during the warmest part of the day when conserving energy matters more than territory patrol.

You've learned to time your route to intersect with food opportunities. The spot where the northernmost pipe occasionally releases a current that brings particulate matter—you pass that location at intervals that match the pipe's irregular rhythm, though you don't think of it as timing, just feel an increasing probability that food might be available there. The place where mosquitofish school most densely in early morning, accessible just after the herons have left and before the diving beetles become most active. The patch of walkway where biofilm grows thickest, where you can scrape algae when protein is scarce.

But you don't claim this territory absolutely. You can't—you're not large enough, not aggressive enough to exclude all rivals. The patriarch koi, the one with the crooked spine who's older than you, crosses your path regularly, and you defer. Not with panic, just a slight course adjustment, a recognition that where your routes intersect, she has priority. You've learned this through minor confrontations years ago, nothing dramatic, just small assertions of dominance that you tested and learned to respect. With smaller koi, juveniles and young adults, the dynamic reverses. They adjust for you, recognizing through size and body language that you're established, that challenging you would cost more than it's worth.

## Your Sensory World

You would live in a universe of vibration and chemistry as much as sight. The lateral line sensors running along your flanks register every pressure wave—the movement of other fish, the subtle currents generated by temperature differentials, the rhythmic thrum of the distant machinery transmitted through the water. This thrum is your heartbeat, more constant than your own pulse, a bass tone that defines normal. When it occasionally pauses—maybe once a month, for reasons you couldn't comprehend even if you were capable of abstract thought—the silence is alarming, makes you more alert, more cautious, until it resumes and the world feels correct again.

Your vision operates best in the green-gray spectrum that dominates here. You can detect movement at the edge of visibility—fifteen meters or so in clear water, less when sediment is suspended—but detail matters less than motion and contrast. You don't see the individual scales on other koi, but you read their body language through the patterns of how they displace water, the angle of their fins, the speed and direction of their movement. You recognize individual fish not by appearance but by movement signature—the crooked-spine patriarch by her asymmetric swimming motion, a particular young male by his nervous darting pattern.

Smell and taste—which for you are almost the same sense, the water carrying chemical information to receptors in your nostrils and mouth—tell you what vision cannot. You taste the difference between the water near the pipes (slightly more saline, carrying traces of the sea beyond) and the water near the access shaft (fresher, sometimes bearing the chemical signature of surface runoff). You smell when the catfish have fed on a large carcass, the amino acids from their messy eating dispersing through the water. You can taste fear in the water when herons visit—stress hormones released by panicking mosquitofish, a signal that means danger even though you don't consciously process it that way.

During spawning season, you would become exquisitely attuned to pheromones. The females' readiness would be detectable from meters away, a complex chemical signal that triggers hormonal cascades in your own body, intensifying colors slightly, increasing aggression toward rival males, focusing your attention on reproductive opportunity above even feeding. You would spend those days following scent gradients, competing for access, exhausting yourself in ways you never would for mere food.

## Your Daily Rhythm

Your activity follows a pattern that's both circadian and tied to the basin's subtle light shifts. The brightest part of the day—when you can sense through your closed eyelids that the light has intensified marginally—is when you're most alert. This is feeding time, when you cruise your route actively, investigating anything that might be edible, rising to surface-feed if insects have fallen onto the vegetation layer, following the crayfish at distance to see if their scavenging uncovers food you can steal.

You've learned to exploit the other species' behaviors. When the catfish stir up sediment during their bottom feeding, you follow at a safe distance—safe because catfish will snap at anything too close, even things too large to swallow—picking off amphipods and worms disturbed by their digging. When crayfish fight, you've learned to watch for the loser fleeing, because sometimes they'll abandon food in their retreat, and you can sweep in to claim it.

You recognize the diving beetles as threats and competitors. The adults you mostly ignore—they take mosquitofish fry that are too small for you to bother with efficiently—but the large water tiger larvae concern you. You've seen them take fish your size, though rarely, and you've learned to avoid the particular rake arm girder where the largest water tiger hunts. This isn't conscious fear, just an association learned through near-misses or through witnessing another fish taken, burned into your behavior as a route adjustment, a zone you swim around rather than through.

Mid-day, during the warmest water period, you rest. Not sleep—you don't sleep the way mammals do—but you find a position in the water where current and gravity balance, usually in the shadow of a rake arm, and you maintain that position with minimal fin movement, entering a state of reduced awareness. Your metabolism slows. Your breathing slows. You're conserving energy, processing the morning's food. You remain alert enough to startle if something large approaches, but otherwise you're nearly motionless, hovering, resting.

Afternoon is secondary feeding time, less intense than morning. You might not even complete a full circuit of your route, just drift through the most productive zones, opportunistically feeding, but without the urgency of morning when your gut is empty and hunger is sharp.

As light fades toward evening—the already-dim basin becoming dimmer still—you settle into your night position. For you, this is a spot where the walkway meets one of the stanchions, creating a small zone of complex geometry that breaks up your outline. You wedge yourself into the angle, facing outward, and enter your night rest state. You're more vulnerable now, but not defenseless—your lateral line remains active, and anything approaching through the water will wake you through pressure wave detection. Herons don't hunt at night. The catfish are active, but they're bottom-feeders, not interested in a healthy koi at rest. The diving beetles are your main night concern, but you're too large for any but the very biggest larvae.

## Your Feeding Strategies

You are an omnivore by design but an opportunist by necessity. The basin doesn't provide enough of any one food type to specialize, so you've learned to exploit everything edible.

Your primary protein comes from mosquitofish fry—not the adults, which are too fast and too alert, but the newly born, which cluster in the vegetation and don't yet have fully developed predator avoidance. You've learned the timing, the few days after you sense spawning activity when the fry are abundant. You cruise through the duckweed layer slowly, mouth opening and closing, filter-feeding style, catching dozens of tiny fish in a single session. This isn't exciting hunting, but it's efficient, and efficiency is what matters when food is limited.

Aquatic insects provide another protein source. You've learned to recognize the struggles of a diving beetle that's just molted—soft, vulnerable, moving awkwardly—and you can crush and swallow them before their exoskeleton hardens. Dragonfly nymphs climbing stems toward emergence are easy targets. Mosquitos or other flying insects that land on the surface and get trapped by water tension—you've learned the sound and the water disturbance pattern, and you rise to sip them in before they can escape.

But you eat vegetation too, scraping biofilm off the walkway and walls, processing it for the algae cells and bacteria. You occasionally nibble duckweed, taking leaves into your mouth, shredding them with the grinding teeth in your throat. You've learned which patches of biofilm are richest—the places where light penetrates best, where growth is most vigorous—and you graze those patches into your route.

When the catfish feed on a large carcass—a dead koi, a rat that somehow made it down the access shaft—you wait at a distance, and when they've had their fill and moved off, you approach to scavenge the remains. Your mouth can't tear flesh like theirs can, but you can work the edges, pulling off small pieces, finding value in what they've left behind.

You've learned to investigate the pipe mouths when water flows through them. Sometimes the current brings food from elsewhere in the flooded complex—organic matter, small invertebrates, things you don't encounter in the basin itself. You hover at the mouth during these flows, mouth open, filtering whatever comes through, a living net.

During the sparse season—late winter, when biological activity is lowest—you've learned to survive on remarkably little. You can go weeks eating only biofilm and the occasional tiny invertebrate, your metabolism slowed, your body consuming its own fat reserves. You've done this before, multiple times, and you know in your body's wisdom that food will eventually return, that the sparse time always gives way to the abundant time again. This knowledge lets you rest through scarcity rather than panicking into unsustainable foraging efforts.

## Your Reproductive Life

You've spawned successfully three times. Not every year—some years the conditions didn't trigger it, or you didn't achieve access to receptive females, competing against larger males—but often enough to have contributed to the next generation, which is all that matters from evolution's perspective.

When spawning urges arrive, your behavior changes dramatically. You become aggressive toward other males, even ones larger than you, driving them away from females with displays and actual physical contact—bumping, chasing, occasionally biting, though serious injuries are rare. You court females by swimming alongside them, matching their pace, occasionally nudging their abdomen with your snout, sending signals through touch and pheromone release that you're a viable mate.

The actual spawning is chaotic. You're not monogamous—you'll mate with multiple females if you can, and females will accept multiple males. It's a scramble, a brief period of reproductive frenzy that exhausts you completely. You chase a receptive female until she releases eggs, and you simultaneously release milt, the water clouding white around you both. The eggs scatter, sinking, some adhering to surfaces, and your part is done. No parental care, no investment beyond the milt and the energy spent in competition and courtship.

After spawning, you're depleted. You feed heavily for days, recovering, replacing the resources you burned. Your aggression fades. The system returns to its normal state. You don't think about the eggs or the fry that eventually hatch. When you encounter your own offspring, you don't recognize them as such—they're just small fish, potentially food if they're tiny enough, or neutral presences if they're larger.

But you've succeeded. Your genes are in the next generation, distributed among dozens of offspring, most of which died but a few of which survived. Those survivors carry your coloring pattern, your body proportions, your behavioral tendencies, your adaptation to the basin. You are, in that sense, already immortal, already extended beyond your individual lifespan into the continuing population.

## Your Injuries and Healing

Your body carries history. There's a scar along your left flank where another koi bit you three years ago during a territorial dispute, a patch where scales grew back smaller and irregularly arranged. There's a slight notch in your tail fin from when you misjudged distance and scraped against a rust edge on a rake arm, the torn tissue healing but not regenerating the precise original shape.

These marks don't hurt now—fish lack the kind of long-term pain sensation that would make old injuries constantly uncomfortable—but they've changed you slightly. The tail notch affects your hydrodynamics minimally, making your turning radius fractionally larger on right turns than left. You've compensated unconsciously, adjusting your swimming to account for it. The flank scar is a slightly weaker point, more vulnerable to infection or injury, but it's held for years without problems.

You've been sick before. Last year, a fungal infection colonized a small wound, white fuzzy growth spreading across several scales. You felt wrong, lethargic, feverish in the way fish experience fever. You rested more, ate less. Your immune system fought the infection. Eventually it cleared, and the affected scales sloughed off and regenerated. The episode weakened you for weeks, made you vulnerable to displacement from your prime feeding spots by younger, healthier fish, but you survived. You recovered. Your body found its balance again.

Parasites are a constant presence. You carry some, probably—gill flukes, intestinal worms, things you can't sense directly but which take a small percentage of your energy intake. They don't kill you—if they did, they'd kill themselves, and parasites that kill hosts too efficiently don't persist evolutionarily—but they tax you, make your survival slightly harder. You might occasionally behave oddly in response to heavy parasite loads—rubbing against surfaces to try to dislodge gill parasites, or having slightly off digestion that makes you less interested in feeding. But mostly you coexist with your parasites, host and parasite populations in their own microscopic balance.

## Your Awareness of the Basin

You don't think about the basin as humans would, don't conceptualize it as a space or contemplate its history. But you know it completely in a way humans never could—know it not as information but as embodied experience, as the sum of ten thousand circuits, a million sensory moments, integrated into a seamless understanding of *here*.

You know which zones feel safe and which feel exposed. You know where the heron is likeliest to stand and you avoid those areas when you sense the pressure change that suggests something large has entered through the access shaft. You know the seasonal patterns—the spawning time, the time of abundant food, the sparse time, the warming time—not as calendar dates but as body states, hormonal shifts, changes in hunger levels and metabolic needs.

You recognize the mechanical thrum as fundamental, the way humans might recognize gravity—not thinking about it, just accepting it as a condition of existence. When it stops, the wrongness is visceral, disturbing in a way you can't act on except by being more cautious until it resumes and the world feels correct again.

You don't wonder about the world beyond the basin. The pipes lead elsewhere, but you've never explored them—the darkness is absolute beyond the first few meters, and something in you reads that as unacceptable risk, too dangerous to investigate. The access shaft leads upward but the opening is small and the water shallow there, and the few times you've drifted toward it, the wrongness of decreased pressure and changed water chemistry turned you back. The basin is your universe. You can't conceive of anything beyond it because you've never experienced beyond, and unlike humans, you can't imagine what you haven't experienced.

Your time sense is both shorter and longer than human time. You don't remember specific events from years ago—fish memory isn't organized that way—but you carry your entire history as embodied knowledge, as patterns of behavior that reflect everything you've learned. You don't remember the first time you encountered a diving beetle, but your avoidance behavior reflects that encounter and every encounter since, accumulated into a general truth: *diving beetles mean potential danger, maintain distance*.

You don't anticipate the distant future. Tomorrow doesn't exist as a concept. But you respond to patterns in ways that implicitly account for future states—you feed heavily in abundant times not because you think about coming scarcity but because evolution has built that behavior into you, and you follow your drives. Your spawning behavior invests in offspring you'll never meet because the drive to reproduce is stronger than any conscious planning could be.

## Your Relationship with Other Species

The catfish are functionally invisible to you most of the time—they occupy a different zone, move in ways you don't interpret as threatening, and your paths rarely intersect in ways that require interaction. When you do notice them, during their occasional mid-water movements, you give them space—they're large, unpredictable, and while they're not active predators of healthy fish your size, caution is automatic.

The crayfish are competition, sometimes. You've learned that when they've claimed a particularly good feeding spot, challenging them isn't worth the risk—their claws can damage fins and scales, and defending territory against them costs more energy than finding food elsewhere. But you occasionally exploit them, following when they excavate sediment, stealing amphipods they've disturbed.

The diving beetles trigger wariness. You've learned to recognize them by their swimming motion, and you give adults space when you encounter them. The water tiger larvae you avoid entirely if you can identify their location—the large ones are genuinely dangerous to you, and avoiding them is wisdom built from observing other fish taken or from your own narrow escapes.

The herons are existential threat. When one enters the basin, every fish feels it—the pressure wave, the chemical signals of panic from mosquitofish, the dimming as the large shape blocks light. You drop immediately to depth, seeking the shelter of rake arms, staying absolutely still except for minimal gill and fin movement to maintain position. You remain there until the heron leaves, sometimes for hours, your stress hormones elevated, your body state completely focused on not being noticed. You've never been caught, but you've seen it happen to others—the explosive strike, the fish pulled thrashing from the water, disappearing into the heron's gullet or carried away. That image is burned into you as body memory, as the definition of what must be avoided at any cost.

The mussels are part of the landscape, barely alive in your perception. You occasionally investigate them, wondering if they're edible, but they're too hard to be worthwhile. The snails are similar—too small, too much shell relative to meat, not worth the effort. You leave them to the crayfish and move on to better food.

The other koi are the most complex relationship. You're the same species, occupying the same niches, competing for the same resources, but you're also potential allies in the sense that schooling behavior provides safety—a heron targeting a group might take any fish, not necessarily you, so being near others dilutes risk. During spawning season, males are rivals and females are goals, and the social landscape becomes temporarily intense with competition and courtship. The rest of the year, you're coexisting, each fish following their routes, overlapping peacefully when possible, competing when necessary, forming a loose community bound by shared space and species recognition.

## Your End

You don't know you're aging, but you are. Fifteen years is substantial for a koi in conditions like these. Your cellular repair mechanisms are gradually losing efficiency. Your immune system is slightly less responsive. Your swimming is minutely less efficient as connective tissues stiffen. These changes are too gradual to notice day to day, but they're accumulating.

Perhaps one winter, colder than usual, your metabolism will fail to adjust adequately. You'll become lethargic, stop feeding, and drift into a state where your body is consuming itself faster than you can replace tissues. You'll grow weak. Eventually, you'll lose buoyancy control and sink to the bottom, where you'll lie on your side, gills still moving but ineffectively, until the oxygen in your blood depletes and your brain shuts down. The catfish will find you within hours.

Or perhaps a parasite load that you've carried for years will suddenly intensify—the parasites reproducing explosively during their own spawning, overwhelming your defenses. You'll sicken rapidly, your swimming becoming erratic, your responses dulled. In this weakened state, you might be taken by a water tiger larva that wouldn't normally threaten you, or you might simply fail, your organs shutting down, your body giving up the constant work of maintaining itself.

Or perhaps a heron will catch you. Despite all your caution, all your learned behaviors, you'll make one mistake—surfacing at the wrong moment, lingering too long in an exposed position—and the strike will come. The beak will pierce you or grab you, and you'll be lifted from water into air, thrashing reflexively but helplessly, drowning in oxygen, experiencing the terror of complete wrongness, and then darkness as you're swallowed or carried to death.

However it happens, your ending will feed continuation. Your body is protein, minerals, energy stored in tissue. The catfish and crayfish will reduce you to scraps within days. The bacteria will mineralize those scraps within weeks. Your atoms will disperse into the water, be taken up by algae, consumed by mosquitofish, eaten by your own offspring, cycled through the ecosystem continuously. You will stop being you—stop being this particular arrangement of matter and pattern—but you won't disappear. You'll transform, become part of everything else, rejoin the basin's flow.

## What It Means To Be You

You are the mid-point of three processes: the evolution that shaped your species across millennia, the individual life you've lived across fifteen years, and the immediate moment of your existence right now, this instant, as you hold position in dim water two meters from the eastern wall, fins making micro-adjustments, feeling the mechanical thrum through your lateral line, tasting the water for chemical signals of food or danger.

You are the accumulation of your adaptations—the body designed through generations of selection for this specific environment, the behaviors learned through experience, the knowledge that isn't thought but is body-wisdom, reflexes and responses and patterns that make you functional here, successful here, alive here when others died.

You are both individual and instance. Individual: this specific fish, with these specific scars and scales, this particular history of routes and territories and spawnings. Instance: a replaceable member of a population, one koi among dozens, carrying genes that are copies of your parents' genes and will be copied into your offspring, not unique, not irreplaceable from the population's perspective.

You are alive but not aware of being alive. You don't contemplate existence or fear death or wonder about purpose. You simply are—simply exist in this ongoing present, responding to stimuli, following drives, executing the behaviors that evolution and learning have built into you. Your consciousness, if it can be called that, is narrow but complete—entirely focused on the immediate tasks of survival, reproduction, moment-to-moment existing.

You are what the basin has made: a fish shaped by artificial structure, fed by processed ecology, living in an environment that's both completely natural (water, food, reproduction) and completely artificial (concrete walls, mechanical sounds, electric light filtering down from surfaces). You are wild in the sense that no human feeds you or controls you, but you're domesticated in ancestry, living in a made space, the descendant of ornamental fish selected for human pleasure.

You are successful by the only measure that matters evolutionarily: you survive, you've reproduced, your genes continue. Whether you're happy or miserable—concepts that barely apply to fish consciousness—is irrelevant. You function. You persist. You are alive, and you make more life, and that's the definition of success for a living thing.

And right now, in this moment, you are simply here: hanging in cool water, feeling the thrum, watching the dim light play across rust and concrete, tasting the chemical landscape, being this specific embodied existence, this koi, this individual, this instance of life in the settling basin.

Not thinking about any of this. Just being it.
