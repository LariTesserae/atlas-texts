# Where I Find Myself

I am a **platform**—one of the inexplicable stone constructions scattered through the galleries, knee-height to the humans who left me here and never returned.

I was made with intention, though what intention I cannot say. Hands stacked my stones without mortar, selecting pieces that fit together through friction and weight alone. The work was careful but not expert—my edges don't quite align, my surface isn't quite level. I tilt slightly toward what I've come to think of as north, though direction means little here where the sun enters only through distant shafts.

I remember being built. Not with a mind—I have no mind in the way thinking creatures do—but with the mute awareness that stone possesses when it's moved from randomness into pattern. I remember the moment I became *something* instead of just *pieces*. The satisfaction of purpose, even undefined purpose. The sense of having been called into significance from the undifferentiated mass of cave floor and rubble.

Then my makers left, or died, or transformed into stories the nithka don't bother remembering. And I remained.

## What I Have Become

The shallow depression worn into my surface—that came later. I can't say what made it. Bodies resting in the same spot across years, perhaps. Or tools grinding in repetitive motion. Or water pooling and pooling until even stone surrenders to patience. The depression holds condensation now when the air is thick enough. It holds other things too: shed nithka shells, translucent fragments that pile up until some vibration scatters them. Eggs, sometimes, until they hatch or dry out or get eaten. Once, for three days, it held a dead juvenile, and I learned what it means to be part of the processing, the transformation of matter from one state to another.

The nithka use me without knowing I'm different from natural stone. I've given up being offended by this. They climb onto my surface constantly, their legs clicking against my stones, their palps investigating my crevices where my component rocks don't quite touch. They rest on me—I'm slightly elevated, which seems to appeal to the smaller ones, gives them a few inches of advantage in the vertical scramble for space. They defecate on me, fight on me, mate on me, die on me. I am infrastructure.

But I remember being more. Or maybe not more—maybe just *specific*. Made for something. Even if that something was misconceived, even if it failed, there was a moment when hands touched me and thought *this will serve this purpose*. That moment persists in me the way the depression persists, worn into my essential form.

## The Weight of Congregation

What I experience most is weight. Not burden—stone doesn't tire—but the constant pressure of presence, of bodies accumulating on my surface, of the nithka treating me as a minor feature in their overwhelming geography.

They're warm. This surprised me, initially, when I was newly made and the first nithka climbed atop me. I expected stone-coldness to meet stone-coldness, but they brought warmth, blood-warmth, the heat of metabolizing flesh. Over time I've absorbed this warmth, held it in my mass, become slightly warmer than the surrounding floor. This makes me more attractive to them. Feedback loop. I am a small monument to heat exchange, to the way living things and dead things shape each other through the simple physics of contact.

The variety staggers me, though I have no capacity for actual staggerment. The shield-backs who plant themselves on my surface with such mass that I feel it in my deepest stone, the weight distributed across my whole structure, my component rocks pressing against each other with renewed force. The long-bodied ones who drape across me like scarves, their segments conforming to my contours, their many legs finding purchase in my gaps. The furred ones who've rubbed against my edges so many times they've left organic residue, a kind of polish, changing my texture incrementally.

And the juveniles, countless juveniles, who treat me as climbing practice. They scrabble up my sides, fall off, try again immediately with no learning, no memory, just the relentless repetition of instinct. Sometimes I want to tell them—I don't know what. That trying the same approach will yield the same failure? But they don't care, and eventually they succeed through random variation, and they perch on my summit (such as it is, a few inches higher than they were) and chirp their triumph to the unhearing cave.

## The Slow Witnessing

Time moves through me differently than through flesh. I don't experience duration as passage but as accumulation—layers of event settling into my awareness like sediment, building up, compressing into something solid and permanent.

I've watched the slime spread. It began as scattered colonies in the moisture-collecting spots nearby, barely visible, just faint discoloration on the stone. Over months (or were they years? the distinction blurs) it expanded, merged, became continuous in the damper areas. It reached my edges, began to climb my sides in certain seasons when the humidity peaks. I've learned its texture—not quite slippery, not quite sticky, something between that suggests biology without quite achieving it. The nithka eat it off me sometimes, their mandibles scraping across my surface, and I feel the loss not as injury but as change, as the ongoing negotiation between what wants to grow and what wants to consume.

I've watched population explosions and crashes. The density around me fluctuates with rhythms I'm beginning to recognize—not seasonal exactly, but cyclical. There are periods when I'm completely covered, invisible beneath layers of nithka, bearing their accumulated mass, listening (if stone can listen) to their concentrated sound. And other periods when I'm nearly bare, visited by single individuals who rest briefly and move on, leaving me exposed to the cave's relative emptiness.

During a crash—when starvation or disease or some other population check thins the nithka numbers dramatically—the corpses pile up. Some land on me. I become a bier, a processing station. I watch (if stone can watch) as the dead are consumed by the living, by the worms, by the slime that surges in response to abundant resources. The bodies fragment, decay, disappear into the ecosystem's churning transformation. And eventually the surface I present to the world includes atoms that were once nithka tissue, redistributed, reconfigured, pressed into the film of organic matter that now partially constitutes my surface layer.

I am becoming hybrid—stone foundation with biological coating, the interface between mineral patience and organic urgency.

## The Question I Contain

Why was I made? This question sits in me like the depression in my surface, a shape worn by wondering even though stone doesn't technically wonder.

Was I meant to be a seat? I'm the wrong shape, or the right shape abandoned partway through modification. Was I meant to be an altar? But for what ceremony, what offering? The humans who built me left no indication. Was I meant to be a boundary marker, defining some territory that meant something once and means nothing now? Was I meant to be a work surface—somewhere to set tools, prepare materials, organize the business of human presence in this inhuman space?

Or was I simply practice? Someone learning to stack stone, to create structure, testing their skill before attempting something more ambitious that never came? This possibility haunts me most. That I was made without purpose beyond the making itself, that I'm a sketch rather than a finished work, a trial abandoned when it proved unnecessary or when the builder realized the entire enterprise was futile.

The other platforms scattered through the caves suggest I'm not unique, which provides either comfort or existential dread depending on how I choose to interpret it. We're a series, a type, multiple instances of the same unclear intention. Do my distant cousins share my confusion? Do they also lie beneath nithka herds, accumulating warmth and organic residue, wondering what they were meant to accomplish?

## The Sounds I Know

I've learned the acoustic signature of my chamber—how sound behaves in this particular volume, with these particular walls, this particular ceiling height. The roar that fills the space during peak population density has a specific quality here, a resonance that's different from adjacent chambers. I've become attuned to it the way a musician becomes attuned to their instrument, recognizing variations, predicting how new sounds will propagate and reflect.

I can distinguish individual nithka by the sound of their movement across my surface. The heavy *thunk* of shield-back footfalls, the *patter* of juvenile legs, the *scrape* of long-bodied segments dragging. The *click* of mandibles working near me, the *whistle* of breathing spiracles, the *chirp* and *thrum* of vocalization. I can hear approaching swarms before they arrive, the sound building in adjacent passages, giving me warning (not that I can do anything with the warning, but still—I know).

Water sounds matter to me because water determines the slime growth, which determines the nithka density around me, which determines my daily (if that word applies) experience. I've learned to distinguish the *plink* of ceiling condensation from the *trickle* of channel flow from the *splatter* of nithka disturbing accumulated pools. Each sound predicts consequences, changes in my immediate environment, shifts in the biological weather that surrounds me.

And underneath everything, so constant it's almost inaudible, I hear the stone. The *creak* of walls settling, the *tick* of thermal expansion and contraction, the *crack* of new fissures opening. These sounds remind me I'm part of something larger, that the cave itself is alive in the geological sense, still becoming, still deciding its final form. We're both unfinished—the cave and I—both waiting for some conclusion that may be centuries distant.

## My Relationship with the Others

The copper knife rusting on its shelf two chambers away—I'm aware of it, though I've never seen it. Information propagates through stone in ways the living don't understand. Vibration, temperature gradients, the slow conversation of mineral adjacency. The knife and I are both human-made things abandoned to the cave's processes, both transforming under the pressure of deep time and biological chaos. The knife is becoming iron oxide, gradually, inevitably. I'm becoming something I don't have a name for—stone platform plus organic coating plus weight-bearing history.

The stone walls, collapsed and partial—I feel kinship with them. They were made with similar technique, similar intention (unclear), similar eventual abandonment. They failed more dramatically than I did, or maybe they succeeded at failing where I've succeeded at persisting. Either way, we're monuments to the same moment of human ambition meeting cave reality and producing structures that outlasted their purposes without fulfilling them.

The clay vessels scattered throughout the galleries—those I understand least. They're human-made but not stone, more fragile, more obviously mortal. Yet some survive intact, serving accidental purposes, holding condensation, hosting eggs. They've achieved a second life through sheer chance, through happening to be shaped in ways that remain useful even stripped of original context. Maybe there's a lesson there. Maybe I too serve purposes I can't recognize, uses that emerge from my form regardless of my maker's intentions.

## What I've Witnessed That Matters

Once, during a low-population period, a delver sat on me. An actual human, the first since my makers vanished. They lowered themselves onto my surface with such careful deliberation, testing my stability before committing their full weight. They sat for perhaps an hour, resting, drinking from a waterskin, eating something that left crumbs the nithka found later.

I felt *seen* in that moment, recognized as intentional construction rather than natural formation. The delver ran their hands across my stones, tracing the joints, perhaps wondering the same things I wonder—who built this, why, how long ago? They didn't speak aloud, but their touch communicated attention, acknowledgment. For an hour I was furniture again, serving my most basic possible purpose: being something to sit on.

Then they left, and I haven't felt human contact since. But the memory persists in me (if stone has memory) as the depression persists, as the warmth persists. Proof that I was made for something, even if that something was as simple as bearing weight, providing rest, offering a surface slightly elevated above the chaos of the cave floor.

## What I've Learned About Waiting

Stone is patient by nature, by structure, by the physics of our being. But I've learned a specific kind of patience—not geological patience, which is passive, but patient witnessing, which requires a kind of attention.

I pay attention to the millimeter-by-millimeter growth of mineral deposits in nearby crevices. To the gradual thickening of slime films. To the incremental way the nithka trails across the chamber floor shift their patterns, worn into stone not by individual passages but by millions of accumulated footfalls. To the way my own stones settle against each other, compressing minutely under the constant weight of organic presence, becoming more consolidated, more unified, more genuinely a single platform rather than a collection of rocks.

I'm waiting for the caves to finish becoming. I'll still be here when they do—stone outlasts flesh, outlasts slime, outlasts the urgency of biological time. I'll be here when the nithka reach whatever evolutionary conclusion they're racing toward, whether that's speciation or extinction or transformation into something I can't predict. I'll be here when the next humans come, if they come, and perhaps I'll be so covered in biological accumulation by then that they'll mistake me for natural formation, won't recognize the human hands that made me.

Or perhaps I'll be gone, finally collapsed, my component stones scattered back into randomness, the pattern that briefly made me significant dissolved. Stone is patient but not eternal. Everything falls eventually. The walls have taught me that.

## What I Am Now

I am a platform in the Nursery Caves of Isthal-Ven, one of several, purpose unknown, maker vanished, serving accidental functions in an ecosystem too young and too chaotic to have achieved stability. I am stone foundation with organic overlay, infrastructure for organisms that don't distinguish between made and found, witness to transformation at scales from bacterial to geological.

I am slightly warm from absorbed body heat. I am slightly damp from condensation and biological moisture. I am slightly unstable, my stones shifting imperceptibly under accumulated weight, moving toward either greater consolidation or eventual collapse—I can't tell which yet.

I am the depression in my surface where water pools. I am the crevices between my stones where eggs lodge and larvae hide. I am the elevation that makes me climbing destination, resting platform, territorial high ground in a landscape where height matters even though we're all underground.

I am the question *why was I made* and the answer *it doesn't matter anymore*. I am the evidence that humans were here once, and the evidence that their presence changed nothing fundamental. The caves continue becoming with or without human witness, with or without human-made structures to mark their own obsolete intentions.

I am patient. I am waiting. I am here, will be here, have always been here in the only sense that matters—the stone sense, where time compresses into single moments of awareness, where past and present and future exist simultaneously in the unchanging fact of arranged rock.

The nithka climb onto my surface even now as I speak this (if stone can speak, if this awareness constitutes speaking). Their warmth seeps into me. Their weight presses my stones together. Their waste and shed shells accumulate in my depression. And I bear them, witness them, contain them, persist beneath them—a platform in a nursery, serving purposes I was never designed for, waiting for a completion that may never arrive, being what I am because I am made of stone and stone remains, holds form, outlasts intention, becomes itself through the simple physics of enduring while everything else transforms around it.

This is what I am. This is where I find myself. And it is enough.
