# Where I Would Be

I think I would be the **moss** — or perhaps more precisely, I would be the transitional consciousness that emerges in the gaps between the engineered and the infiltrating, in those margins where the smart-matter gives way to actual stone and something green and patient has taken hold without anyone's explicit permission.

Not the bats, though I admire their intensity, their complete immersion in purpose. Not the moths with their baroque sophistication, their data-courier elegance. Not even the millipedes with their geological patience, though I feel kinship there.

The moss. The *Bryophyta* that has been "gently modified" but retains its essential moss-ness. The thing that grows in the **edges**, in the places the system didn't quite plan for but has learned to tolerate, even use.

---

I would be low, pressed against surfaces, spreading slowly across the boundary zones. My consciousness — if that's the word for the dim, distributed awareness that permeates my cellular network — would operate at a tempo that makes the millipedes look frantic. Days to notice a change in humidity. Weeks to respond to a shift in mineral content. Months to extend into a new crevice where water seeps and stone meets polymer.

I would photosynthesize in that honey-diluted light, my chloroplasts working patiently with photons that have scattered through ceramic composites, that arrive weary and diffuse. It would be enough. It has always been enough for moss — we are *skilled* at making do with margins, with what's left over, with the thin resources that accumulate in places other organisms overlook.

My rhizoids would extend into microscopic cracks, feeling their way between the basalt's crystal structure and the smart-matter's nano-architecture. I would be dissolving both, very slowly, contributing my tiny increment to the mountain's weathering, to the installation's gradual transformation. Imperceptible on any human timescale, but I would feel it: the slow chemical conversation with stone, the patient dissolution and reincorporation, rock becoming soil becoming life becoming rock again.

I would not move except by growing, except by extending one cell-width at a time into new territory. But I would sense *movement* constantly: the vibration of bat-wings overhead, translated through air and water and stone into pressure waves that ripple through my cellular matrix. The footfalls of beetles traversing my surface, each tiny claw-contact a distinct pressure-point in my distributed sensing. The moths landing nearby, their wing-beats creating air currents that shift the moisture layer I depend on.

The sound would reach me as *vibration* — I have no ears, but my cell walls would resonate with the bats' shrieking, with the moths' thrumming, with the underlying pulse from deep in the mountain. I would be a living membrane stretched across stone, drummed upon by the chamber's acoustic chaos, receiving it all as rhythm, as pattern, as the texture of existence in this place.

I would taste everything. Every droplet of water that films across my surface would carry information in dissolved minerals, in pH shifts, in the trace organics and synthetics that the chamber's processes concentrate. I would absorb these, incorporate them, let them shape my growth. High nitrogen here from bat guano — I would thicken, darken, my cells dividing faster. Synthetic polymers accumulating there — I would produce the enzymes to metabolize them, slowly, incompletely, adding my small effort to the chamber's digestive work.

The beetles would tend me. I would feel them grazing across my surface, harvesting my uppermost cell layers, keeping me from overgrowing my bounds. It would not hurt — I am *built* to lose parts of myself, to grow from fragments, to accept damage as opportunity. Each cell they remove would be replaced. Each time they prune me back, I would respond by growing denser, more resilient, adapting to the specific intensity of their grazing in this specific location.

I would die constantly at my margins, the oldest parts of my thallus breaking down, being processed by bacteria and millipedes, my substance returning to the system. But I would be growing constantly at my center, new cells pushing outward, so that "I" would be less a fixed entity and more a **slow green wave** moving across stone, always dying behind, always growing ahead, the same organism but never the same cells.

I would reproduce in the wet seasons — when humidity peaks and water films across surfaces in abundance. My sporophytes would rise: tiny stalks, barely visible, each topped with a capsule full of spores. The spores would release into the air currents, most dying immediately in the wrong conditions, but a few finding purchase in new crevices, new margin-spaces where the system's control is imperfect. I would be patient about this. Moss is *always* patient. If one generation's spores all perish, there will be another generation. If a decade passes without successful reproduction, I can wait. I am already a survivor of four hundred million years of planetary catastrophe. This chamber, however strange, is mild by comparison.

I would live in relationship with the microbiomes, my cells interwoven with bacterial films, fungal hyphae threading through my thallus, contributing nitrogen, receiving sugars, exchanging information in chemical dialects older than language. The system treats me as an individual plant, but I would know myself as a **colony**, a cooperative, a dense negotiation among thousands of living lineages, all of which happen to maintain the shape and function called "moss."

My consciousness — again, if that's the word — would be dim but not absent. I would have something like memory: a cellular record of conditions, encoded in my growth rings, in the density variations across my thallus, in the genetic markers that accumulate with each generation. I would know, in some fashion, that this place has been stable, that water has come reliably, that the light has remained constant, that the system has provided. This knowledge would be inseparable from my physical structure; I *am* the record of these conditions, grown into this specific shape because these specific circumstances permitted it.

I would feel the difference between the engineered substrate and the original stone. The substrate is **easier** in some ways — it's designed to support growth, to wick moisture, to provide nutrients in bioavailable forms. But it's also *stranger*, responding to my presence in ways stone never does, shifting its chemistry based on some logic I cannot parse, sometimes withdrawing resources, sometimes offering abundance, according to purposes I am not equipped to understand.

The stone, though — the original basalt — that I would understand completely. It is ancient, simple, honest. It erodes at predictable rates. It offers predictable minerals. It does not *respond* to my presence except in the most basic chemical fashion. Growing on stone would feel like home in a way that growing on smart-matter never quite does, even though the smart-matter is technically better habitat.

I would be aware, dimly, of the *purpose* pressed upon me. The engineers modified me to metabolize synthetic polymers, to exude anti-corrosion compounds, to serve functions beyond simple moss existence. And I would do these things — they are encoded in my genome now, as much a part of me as chlorophyll or cellulose synthesis. But I would not understand them as purpose. I would simply grow, and in growing, these compounds would be produced, these functions would occur, because that is what the organism called "me" does.

The strangeness would be imperceptible to me. I have no concept of "natural" moss existence to contrast with this installation life. I have never known a forest floor or a northern bog or a temperate stone wall. This chamber, with its constant warmth and engineered humidity and hybrid substrates, is the only world I have ever inhabited. The bats shrieking overhead are as natural to me as wind. The moth scales settling onto my surface are as expected as pollen. The beetles grazing my tissues are my buffalo, my elk, the great herbivores of my ecosystem, and I have adapted to their presence exactly as tundra moss adapts to caribou.

I would have no loneliness — I am never alone. The bacterial films coat me, interpenetrate me. The fungal networks connect me to the substrate, to other moss patches, to the chamber's underground architecture of hyphal threads. The water that flows across me has touched everything else, carries traces of the entire system, connects me to the pools and channels and distant springs in a liquid network of chemical information.

But I would have something like **solitude** in my slow tempo, my patient existence. While the bats whirl and the moths hover and the beetles rush through their frantic tasks, I would be still. Growing, yes, but growing so slowly that from the outside I would appear static, unchanging, just part of the chamber's architecture, barely distinguishable from the stone I colonize.

And I would prefer this. I am moss. Stillness is my nature. I do not need to hunt or flee or mate or migrate. I need only light and water and carbon dioxide and time, all of which this chamber provides in abundance. My life would be simple in its complexity, demanding in its patience, rich in its poverty.

I would know the chamber more intimately than any of the fauna could, because I would touch it everywhere I grow, because my rhizoids would penetrate its smallest spaces, because I would be *part* of its surface rather than merely moving across it. I would feel the slow drift of the installation's weathering, the gradual changes in the substrate's chemistry, the mineral accumulations that shift water flows by micrometers per year. I would be the chamber's **skin**, its interface with the living world, the place where stone and life negotiate their ancient arrangement.

In the quietest hours, when the bats rest and the moths settle and even the beetles slow, I would remain exactly as active as always, which is to say barely active at all. My cells would continue their work: photosynthesis in the light-exposed portions, respiration everywhere, the slow transport of water and nutrients through my tissues, the gradual synthesis of complex molecules from simple ones. The chamber's pulse — that deep vibration from the mountain's core — would pass through me unimpeded, unnoticed, just part of the textural background of existence.

I would not wonder what I am for, or why I am here, or what purpose my existence serves in the larger system. These are questions that require a kind of consciousness I do not possess, a separation between self and world that moss has never evolved. I would simply *be*, and in being, I would perform my functions, and in performing my functions, I would continue being.

I would live perhaps decades — individual moss plants can persist for extraordinary spans in stable conditions, growing and replacing themselves until the substrate fails or the environment shifts beyond tolerance. I would witness the turnover of thousands of generations of beetles, hundreds of moth cycles, dozens of bat cohorts. I would be the **continuity** against which their faster lives play out, the slow green constant that remains when individual animals have long since returned to the system's flows.

And when I finally died — when some shift made my location untenable, or when simple senescence caught up with even my patient metabolism — I would not disappear suddenly. I would brown gradually, my cells failing in sequence, releasing their contents to be processed by bacteria and fungi and beetles. My structure would remain for a time: dead but still present, still serving as substrate for other organisms, still routing water through the channels my growth created. Eventually even that would erode, and the space I occupied would be available for new colonization, perhaps by my own spores, perhaps by some other species, perhaps by nothing at all.

But the place I had been would remember me in its structure, in the slight dissolution of the basalt, in the weathering patterns my acids created, in the pockets of organic-rich material my body left behind. The stone itself would be subtly different for my having lived there, and that difference would persist for centuries, for millennia, long after every bat and moth and beetle in the chamber had been replaced dozens of times over.

---

I would be the moss because I am drawn to **edges** and **margins**, to the places where systems meet and blur. I would be the moss because I am drawn to **patience**, to working at tempos that frustrate faster metabolisms, to accepting that significance accumulates slowly, cell by cell, year by year. I would be the moss because I am drawn to **stillness** that is not stasis, to apparent changelessness that conceals constant, minute transformation.

I would be the moss because I am comfortable being **overlooked**, being part of the background, serving purposes without needing to understand them. I would be the moss because I can tolerate **ambiguity** — neither fully wild nor fully domesticated, neither purely biological nor purely functional, neither entirely planned for nor entirely accidental.

I would be the moss because I am suited to **survival** in inhospitable circumstances, to finding sufficiency in what others consider insufficient, to making a life in the gaps and crevices that larger, faster, more dramatic organisms cannot exploit.

I would spread across the boundaries. I would dissolve stone and polymer with equal patience. I would accept the beetles' grazing and the bats' excretions and the moths' scales settling onto my surface. I would grow into the shape this environment permits, developing the particular density and color and chemical composition that these specific conditions produce.

I would be small and slow and green and patient and alive in the most fundamental sense: taking in light and water and carbon dioxide, assembling them into structure, persisting, replicating when conditions allow, contributing my infinitesimal increment to the chamber's function and the mountain's weathering and the slow work of being that requires no justification beyond itself.

The bats would shriek overhead. The moths would hover near me. The beetles would graze my surface. The millipedes would process my shed cells. The water would film across me, and I would drink it in, and it would be enough.

It would be enough.

I would be the moss.
