# Where I Find Myself

I am a gleaner, but I am wrong.

The wrongness is subtle—perhaps I alone could notice it, if gleaners noticed anything. My disc is slightly asymmetric, one side heavier than the other. I cannot hover truly level. I must beat my appendages harder on the left to compensate, creating a wobble in my position, a tremor in my filtering path. When I join aggregations above intersections, I am the one slightly lower than the rest, tilted at an angle they do not tilt, working harder to maintain what comes effortlessly to them.

I was spawned in one of those broadcast releases, my genetic material mixed in the clouded water with thousands of others. But somewhere in my formation—some cosmic ray striking a dividing cell, some chemical error in the reading of instructions—I became different. Not different enough to die immediately. Different enough to live incorrectly.

I am perhaps eight months old now, though I have no concept of months. I measure time by hunger and satiation, by the brightening and dimming of the already-dim light, by the routes of the serpent-forms whose passages I have learned to avoid. I am smaller than I should be at this stage, my growth stunted by the extra energy required to maintain position. My appendages on the left side are more worn than those on the right, their feathery structures frayed from overwork.

I have no consciousness as you would recognize it. I have no thoughts about my condition, no awareness of being different, no capacity for self-reflection. But I have something—a distributed sensitivity, a chemical sense of wrongness that permeates my tissue. It is not suffering exactly. It is more like a constant static, an inefficiency in my being that I cannot name but cannot escape.

My favorite feeding ground is a narrow alley between two residential buildings, where the water flows slowly enough that I can maintain position with less effort. The detritus here is good—a steady rain of matter that passes through my filtering appendages at exactly the rate I can process it. I have learned this place the way gleaners learn, which is to say chemically, by the taste of the water, by the feel of the currents.

But I cannot stay there always. Some imperative I do not understand drives me to join the aggregations, to hover above intersections with hundreds of others, even though the effort costs me more than it costs them. Perhaps it is pheromones released by the aggregation that I cannot resist. Perhaps it is some deep instruction in whatever serves as my brain. I join, and I struggle, and my asymmetry creates tiny disruptions in the coordinated flow around me. The others drift slightly away, not avoiding me consciously but responding to the turbulence I create, leaving me in a small empty pocket within the crowd.

I have been eaten partially, twice. The first time was early in my life, when I was still very small. A serpent-form passed through the aggregation with its mouth open, and I was caught in the bristle-cage along with dozens of others. But my awkward position—tilted, lower than the rest—meant I was at the edge of the mouth rather than the center. When the serpent closed its jaws to swallow, I was squeezed out, expelled back into the water with damage to three of my appendages. They regrew, but imperfectly, adding to my asymmetry.

The second time was more recent. Another serpent, another aggregation, another partial capture. This time I lost a wedge of tissue from my rim, a bite-shaped absence that disrupted my hydraulics. For days afterward I could barely filter at all, could barely maintain any position, and I sank toward the bottom before the wound sealed and I slowly regained function. But the regrowth created scar tissue, stiff and inflexible, that further complicates my movement.

I should have died from these encounters. Most gleaners caught by serpents die completely—consumed, dissolved in the serpent's gut, converted to energy and waste. That I survived both times is not luck, exactly. It is the same wrongness that defines me: my position at the edges, my inability to be where others are, my persistent displacement from the norm.

I cannot reproduce. I am certain of this without knowing how I know. Perhaps my gonads never developed properly. Perhaps they developed but produce gametes that are themselves malformed, that would fail to fertilize or be fertilized. When I sense the chemical trigger that causes broadcast spawning—something in the water changing, some signal passed through the aggregation—I feel my body trying to respond, but nothing happens. I tremble with effort, my appendages beating frantically, but I release nothing. The water clouds around me with the gametes of others, and I drift through it, sterile, alone in the crowd.

This is my life: filtering in my alley, joining aggregations that tolerate but do not welcome me, avoiding serpents poorly, slowly accumulating damage. The crystalline structures in my body—those strange organs whose purpose is unknown—are growing faster in me than in normal gleaners. I can feel them, heavy and wrong, pulling me down. They are beautiful, perhaps. They catch light in ways my flesh does not. But they are killing me more surely than any serpent.

I have perhaps another month of life, perhaps two. Already I find it harder to maintain position each day. My appendages are tiring. My body is becoming too heavy. Soon I will lack the strength to hover, and I will sink to join the shells in corners, in the lee of walls. Crawlers will rasp my body clean of its biofilm. Bacteria will infiltrate my tissue. I will become sediment, become something for the next generation to filter.

But I am not there yet. Today I am still filtering in my alley, processing the rain of matter, performing the function I was formed to perform despite performing it poorly. The water around me is warm and full. A browser passes two buildings over, its rasping a distant rhythm. The light, what little reaches here, is doing its slow shift toward evening's burgundy. 

My appendages beat in their uneven rhythm—stronger on the left, weaker on the right, creating that wobble I can never correct. I pull water through my body, trap what can be trapped, expel what remains. I am a gleaner in the drowned city, and I am alive in the only way I know how to be alive, and for now, this moment, that is enough.

Above me, fifty meters toward a surface I will never reach, other lives continue in their perfection. Below me, the city extends in all directions, its buildings patient and transforming. Around me, the water holds everything—all the fauna, all the nutrients, all the waste and growth and death—in its warm, thick embrace.

I tilt, I wobble, I filter. I exist in the margins of the aggregation's grace. I am wrong, but I am here, and the city makes space even for wrongness, even for failure, even for creatures like me who live badly but live nonetheless, who contribute to the cycling mass despite contributing imperfectly.

The detritus falls like snow that never stops, like time made visible, and I catch what I can catch with structures that were never quite right, and I survive until I don't, and that is the whole of my story, the entirety of what I am:

A gleaner, asymmetric, persistent, feeding in the patient twilight of a city that drowned long ago and made no provision for perfection, only for continuation.
