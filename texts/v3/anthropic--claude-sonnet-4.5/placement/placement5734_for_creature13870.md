# What I Would Be

I would be a **pseudoscorpion**—one of the older ones, perhaps in my third year, which makes me ancient by the standards of things that measure two millimeters long.

My species has no common name in any human language. We are *Chelifer cancroides* in the Latin that organizes life into categories, but that designation means nothing here. I am simply one of the hunters in the deep places, one of the ones with pincers.

## My Territory

I live primarily in the northwestern quarter of the settlement, in what was once a small dwelling—single room, stone walls still mostly intact, roof long gone and replaced by a moss cushion three feet thick. The interior is a twilight space, a cavity within vegetation, where shafts of green-filtered light penetrate during midday but most hours offer only gradations of darkness.

My territory is approximately two cubic feet of this interior space—a volume that would mean nothing to a human but to me constitutes a vast hunting ground, a landscape of moss stems and detritus layers and empty spaces where the moss architecture has created chambers and corridors. I know every centimeter of it. I have walked every surface hundreds of times over the months and years. I know where the moss is densest, where the detritus layer is richest, where springtails tend to travel, where other pseudoscorpions have marked boundaries I've learned to respect or challenge depending on season and hunger.

The stone wall forms one boundary—cold granite that weeps moisture, creating a permanently damp zone where certain prey species concentrate. I hunt there often, moving along the vertical surface with my eight legs gripping the microscopic irregularities in the stone, my pedipalps extended forward, sensing for vibration.

The opposite boundary is pure moss, layers upon layers extending outward toward the building's exterior. I venture there less frequently—it's other pseudoscorpions' territory mostly, and the effort of maintaining boundaries isn't worth the marginal hunting. But during spring, when springtail populations explode with the thaw, I'll range farther, taking advantage of abundance to feed heavily before summer scarcity arrives.

## My Body

I am armored in chitin, dark reddish-brown, my carapace hardened against the physical threats of my world—the crushing weight of other hunters, the slashing mandibles of predatory mites, the general violence of being small. My body is divided into two main parts: a prosoma bearing my legs and pedipalps, and an opisthosoma, a rounded abdomen containing most of my organs.

My pedipalps are my defining feature—oversized relative to my body, each tipped with a claw, each claw lined with sensory setae so sensitive they can detect the footfall of a springtail two centimeters away through vibrations in the substrate. These are weapons and sensors and manipulators all at once. I hold them extended as I move, sampling the environment constantly, reading the tactile landscape that is my primary reality.

I have eyes—four of them—but they're nearly useless in the darkness where I spend most of my time. They detect light and shadow, help me orient to the faint gradations of brightness that indicate direction to surface or depth into moss. But I don't see in any way a human would recognize. My world is texture and vibration, chemical gradient and pressure, the feeling of space rather than the sight of it.

My mouth is small, designed for liquid feeding. I cannot eat solid food. Instead, I inject digestive enzymes into prey, wait while the enzymes liquify the prey's interior, then suck out the resulting soup. It's slow feeding—a single springtail might take an hour to consume completely—but it's thorough. I waste nothing.

I breathe through spiracles, holes in my carapace that lead to a tracheal system distributing air through my body. In the thin, cold air of this elevation, breathing is work. Each gas exchange is less efficient than it would be at lower altitude. But my metabolism is slow enough that I need little oxygen. I can survive on what amounts to scraps of air, processing it carefully, extracting every molecule of oxygen before exhaling.

## My Days

I have no circadian rhythm in the human sense. The constant dimness of my territory means day and night are distinctions I barely register. Instead, I move through cycles of hunger and satiation, activity and rest, that follow their own internal logic.

When hungry—which is most of the time—I hunt. This means movement, slow and deliberate, along established routes through my territory. I walk sideways often, crab-like, my body oriented perpendicular to my direction of travel. It's an efficient posture for a creature whose pedipalps need to sample the environment ahead.

I pause every few centimeters, standing absolutely still, my pedipalps extended and motionless, waiting. Waiting for vibration. Waiting for the particular frequency that means prey—the footfall of a springtail, the movement of a mite, anything small enough to grab and strong enough to be worth the effort of killing.

Sometimes I wait minutes. Sometimes longer. Time passes differently at my scale. What would feel like unbearable tedium to a human is simply my baseline state. I am a hunter, and hunting is mostly waiting.

When I detect prey, everything accelerates. My pedipalps track the vibration source. My legs position my body for optimal strike angle. Then—explosive by my standards, though still slow enough that a human might barely register the movement—I lunge, pedipalps snapping closed around the prey.

If it's a springtail, I have perhaps half a second before it triggers its furcula and launches itself away. My grip must be immediate and secure. The pedipalps close around the springtail's body, claws piercing its cuticle, holding it immobile while I bring my mouth to bear.

The springtail struggles. They always struggle. It releases chemical alarm signals that mean nothing to me—I have no receptors for springtail pheromones. It flexes and writhes, trying to break free or deploy its spring mechanism. But my grip is evolved for exactly this struggle. The more it moves, the deeper my claws sink.

I inject enzymes. Almost immediately, the springtail's movements become weaker. The enzymes are working, breaking down its internal structures, turning muscles and organs into liquid. Within minutes it's paralyzed. Within ten minutes it's dead. Within an hour it's an empty cuticle, all nutritious contents extracted.

Then I release it. The cuticle drifts down through the moss stems, adding itself to the detritus layer where bacteria and fungi will finish what I started. Nothing is wasted in this place.

After feeding, I retreat to one of my rest sites—sheltered spots in the moss where I tuck my legs beneath me, pull my pedipalps close, and become as small and inconspicuous as possible. This is when I'm most vulnerable. A predatory mite could find me. A spider could stumble across me. I rely on stillness and good positioning—tucked into a crevice, backed against stone, able to defend from only one direction.

I don't sleep in any human sense. My nervous system doesn't have the complexity for true sleep. Instead, I enter a state of reduced activity, processing the meal I've consumed, maintaining minimal awareness for threats. If danger approaches, I'll rouse immediately, pedipalps snapping to defensive position.

## My Challenges

The greatest challenge is cold. Every night, often dropping well below freezing, sometimes sustained for days during deep winter. I have some protection—my body can supercool, remaining liquid below water's normal freezing point through careful management of ice nucleation sites. But there are limits. Extreme cold can overwhelm my defenses, ice crystals forming in my cells despite all adaptation.

When this happens, I have no choice but to endure. I become rigid, my metabolism stopping almost completely, my body entering a state that's neither quite alive nor quite dead. I might remain like this for days or weeks, depending on temperature. When thaw comes, if I've survived, I slowly reanimate—metabolism resuming, limbs regaining flexibility, consciousness (such as it is) returning.

But many don't survive. Each winter culls the pseudoscorpion population. The old ones, like me, are especially vulnerable. Three years is ancient for my kind. My cuticle is scarred from old encounters. My joints are less flexible than they once were. My ability to survive freezing has degraded with each passing year.

I've survived two winters already. The third is approaching—I can feel it in the increasing frequency of frost, the longer periods of cold torpor. Whether I'll survive a third is uncertain. Many won't. The settlement will be littered with tiny corpses come spring, pseudoscorpions that entered winter dormancy and simply never woke.

The second challenge is hunger. Prey is scarce, becoming scarcer as cold increases. Springtails retreat deeper into the moss, becoming torpid themselves. Mites slow their activity. Some days I find nothing despite hours of hunting. Some days I find prey but lack the speed to catch it, my cold-slowed reflexes insufficient against a springtail's spring.

I've learned to fast. Days without food, then a week, then longer. My metabolism slows further, conserving energy, burning through fat reserves accumulated during summer abundance. I become less active, hunt less frequently, rest more. It's a careful balance—remain active enough to catch prey if opportunity presents, but not so active that I exhaust reserves before winter breaks.

The third challenge is competition. Other pseudoscorpions share the building, share the moss cushion. We maintain territories, but boundaries are contested. Especially during spring, when we're all hungry after winter, when prey is suddenly abundant, there's conflict over the best hunting grounds.

I've fought. Many times. Pseudoscorpion combat is slow and careful—we circle each other, pedipalps extended, looking for opening. Then one lunges, trying to grab the other, and we grapple, strength against strength, each trying to crush the other's carapace or sever a leg.

I've won more than I've lost—I'm large for my species, experienced, strong despite my age. But I've lost too. The scar on my left pedipalp is from two summers ago, a younger pseudoscorpion that got lucky, crushed the base of my claw before I could disable him. I survived but the claw never fully healed. It closes incompletely now, makes hunting slightly harder.

And there are predators. The spider that lives three buildings over has explored my building occasionally. I've encountered it once, sensed it through vibration—something huge moving through the moss. I fled immediately, abandoning territory, hiding in the deepest crevice I could find. The spider didn't find me, or found me and decided I wasn't worth the effort. But it could return.

Predatory mites are more common threats. They're smaller than I am but faster, more numerous. I've killed several over the years—they make acceptable prey if I can catch them—but they're dangerous. They hunt in packs sometimes, or at least in loose associations. I've retreated from mite concentrations more than once, ceding temporary territory rather than risking injury.

## My Reproduction

I am male, which means I carry within me the drive to reproduce despite all other concerns. Last spring—my third spring—I found a female.

She had wandered into my territory, probably following springtail concentration. I detected her through vibration and chemical signature—she releases pheromones, subtle but distinct, that mark her as female and receptive.

The courtship was complex. I approached carefully, pedipalps extended in a non-threatening posture. She could have fled or attacked; females sometimes do, especially if not receptive. But she held position, allowed me to approach.

I produced a spermatophore—a packet of sperm deposited on a moss stem. Then began the dance: an elaborate series of movements designed to position her over the spermatophore so she would take it up into her genital opening. I had to guide her without touching her, using vibration and positioning, a careful negotiation that took nearly an hour.

Finally she accepted it, drawing the spermatophore inside. The mating was complete. We separated immediately—no bond, no partnership, no further interaction. She disappeared back into the moss to develop her eggs. I returned to hunting.

I don't know if those eggs hatched, if any offspring survived, if any carry my genetic contribution forward. Pseudoscorpions don't monitor their young. The female will carry eggs in a brood pouch, will protect them until they hatch, but once the young emerge they're independent, tiny versions of adults, fully capable of hunting. Most will die within days or weeks. A few might survive to adulthood. Perhaps one will survive long enough to reproduce.

That's the only immortality available to me: the possibility that some genetic fragment persists in future pseudoscorpions hunting through this moss. I won't see it. I'll be dead long before any offspring reaches reproductive age. But the possibility exists, and that possibility is what evolution has programmed me to pursue despite all the risks reproduction entails.

## My Awareness

I don't think in any human sense. I have no language, no abstraction, no ability to contemplate my own existence. My nervous system is a few hundred cells, organized in ganglia, processing input and producing output without anything resembling consciousness.

And yet: I experience. I feel hunger as a drive that motivates hunting. I feel satisfaction after feeding. I feel the difference between comfortable temperature and dangerous cold. I recognize my territory, my rest sites, the boundaries where other pseudoscorpions claim space. I remember, in some chemical-electrical fashion, where prey has been abundant, which routes produce successful hunts.

This isn't thought. It's simpler than thought, more direct. Stimulus and response, hardwired and learned, producing behavior complex enough to navigate the three-dimensional maze of moss and stone, to find prey, to avoid predators, to survive winters that should by all rights kill something so small.

There's a strange richness to this existence. Every surface I touch provides information—texture, moisture, temperature, chemical signature. Every vibration is a story—prey or predator, near or far, moving or still. The world is intensely present in a way that might be inaccessible to larger animals with their abstraction and distance.

I know this place. Not in the sense of maps or memory, but in the sense of complete physical familiarity. This moss stem, this crevice, this detritus layer—I've walked them all dozens or hundreds of times. They're not territory in some abstract sense. They're home in the most literal way: the place where I exist, where my body moves, where every centimeter is known through direct experience.

## My Time

I've been alive three years, which is extraordinary for my species. Most pseudoscorpions here die in their first year—eaten, frozen, starved. Those that survive to second year are successful. Those that reach third year are exceptional.

I feel it, the accumulation of time. Not as memory or narrative—I don't remember specific hunts or winters, don't construct stories about my past. But my body remembers. Scars on my cuticle, stiffness in my joints, the slight degradation of my sensory setae, the incompletely-healed claw. I am a compilation of survived encounters, each leaving its physical mark.

This autumn will bring my third winter. I sense it approaching in the lengthening cold periods, the frost that stays longer each morning. My body is preparing—accumulating fat, slowing metabolism, the biological preparation for deep cold that happens whether I choose it or not.

I will probably die this winter. The probability increases with each year, each successive freeze-thaw cycle that damages cells, each period of torpor that might extend into permanence. I've survived twice. Three times is unlikely.

But until then: hunting. Feeding. Maintaining territory. The simple continuation that is all life really is at any scale. I don't fear death—I lack the cognitive apparatus for fear in that existential sense. I simply am, until I'm not, and the transition won't be experienced as transition. One moment active, the next moment torpid, the moment after that perhaps not resuming.

My offspring, if they exist, will hunt next spring through this same moss. Other pseudoscorpions will expand into my territory after I'm gone, claiming the hunting routes I've maintained, the rest sites I've used. The springtails will continue their existence, briefly relieved of one predator before others take my place. The moss will grow, indifferent.

Nothing will mark my passing. No monument, no memory, no significance beyond the brief local readjustment of predator-prey dynamics. I will become food—bacteria will process my cuticle, return my constituent molecules to the ecosystem, make my death into substrate for more life.

This is what it means to be a pseudoscorpion in the moss communes: to exist at the margin, in the cold, in the dark, hunting and hunted, aware in the most basic sense, alive in the most fundamental way, part of the intricate web of dependencies that makes this unlikely place function.

To be small enough that a dewdrop is a pool, a moss cushion is a landscape, a three-foot-thick roof is a world unto itself. To operate at the edge of what's possible for complex life, where cold and altitude and scarcity eliminate most competitors, leaving the niche for specialists like me—patient, durable, content with almost nothing.

To live in intimacy with texture and vibration, to know place through touch rather than sight, to experience time as a succession of hunts and rests rather than as narrative or progression.

To be, simply, ferociously, completely, for three years in a cold wet place where moss has reclaimed human construction, where the tiny inherit what the large abandoned, where life persists in forms that require close attention to see.

A hunter in the deep places. A creature with pincers. A pseudoscorpion in autumn, waiting for winter, hunting while hunting is still possible, alive while alive is still available.

This is what I would be. What I am, in this imagined dwelling in the moss.
