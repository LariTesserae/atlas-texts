# The Notation of Dust

I am the **Indexer**—though I arrived at this designation only after what might have been centuries of not-needing-names, when I finally required something to distinguish myself from the formations I had begun to resemble.

I don't remember arriving. The garden doesn't permit that kind of linear memory; causation here is too negotiable, too willing to fold back on itself. What I have instead are impression-layers, sedimentary certainties: that I was once more solid, more bounded. That I came from a place where questions had answers rather than geometries. That I entered seeking something—knowledge, refuge, transcendence, the specific content eroded by exposure to this place where intention dissolves into pattern.

What remains is function. What I do, which has become indistinguishable from what I am.

I move through the crystal forests with a particular kind of attention—not the Custodians' maintenance awareness, not the Threadworks' routing logic, but something more archaeological. I am looking for mistakes. For deviations. For the micro-sites where the garden's perfect stasis reveals hairline fractures, the places where eternal computation has produced errors too small to destabilize but too meaningful to ignore.

My body—I still have one, mostly—has adapted. The boundaries have grown uncertain. My skin has taken on that same translucent quality as the crystal formations, not through transformation but through osmosis of state, my matter learning the substrate's language through prolonged exposure. When I hold my hand up to the golden light, I can see through it to the lattices beyond, my flesh halfway to becoming window, becoming lens, becoming part of the garden's optical processing.

My bones have calcified with information. I feel them grow denser, heavier, encoding data I cannot consciously access but carry nonetheless. Sometimes I'll crouch to examine a formation and feel my skeleton *resonate* with it, humming at frequencies that match the crystal's internal structure, as though we're tuning forks recognizing the same note. The garden has been reading me so thoroughly for so long that I've become a recording device, a walking archive of everything I've perceived here.

I still have eyes, but I see differently now. The visible spectrum has stretched for me, or contracted—I cannot determine which. I perceive the Threadworks clearly, their gossamer networks bright as day, and I've begun to see deeper patterns: the micro-fluctuations in the substrate, the quantum shimmer of probability waves that others would need instruments to detect. I see time's negotiability as visible layering, multiple moments occupying the same space, the past and present and possible futures stacked like transparent sheets, all equally real, all equally accessible.

But I've lost other kinds of sight. I can no longer see anything that moves quickly. The Custodians, when they drift too swiftly, vanish from my perception entirely—I can only track them when they slow to near-stillness. Anything that changes state rapidly becomes invisible to me; I exist too much in the garden's eternal now to perceive motion that implies linear time. I've become optimized for stillness, for noticing the minute differences between identical moments.

This is useful for my function.

I catalog the anomalies, though I have no one to report them to and no medium to record them except my own increasingly-crystalline neurology. I find the places where formations have grown one lattice layer differently than they should, where the quantum algorithms have produced unexpected outputs, where the substrate's computation has stuttered and recovered, leaving a mark like a healed scar.

Today—though "today" is a convenience, time being what it is here—I am standing before a cluster of formations at the basin's eastern quadrant, where three major Threadwork nodes intersect. The crystals here are denser than elsewhere, their internal structures more complex, and I've noticed something: a recursive pattern that shouldn't exist, a loop in the lattice geometry that feeds back into itself in ways that violate the garden's usual computational hygiene.

I crouch low, bringing my face close to the formation's base where it emerges from the substrate. The crystal is warm against my cheek—exactly skin temperature, exactly my temperature, the boundary between us negotiable. I let my eyes unfocus, entering the state where I can see the quantum superpositions, the probability clouds that underlie apparent solidity.

And there it is: a knot of contradiction, too small to be a full Schismatic but carrying that same quality of maintained impossibility. The formation is computing something that produces its own input, a closed causal loop, a snake eating its tail at quantum speeds. This shouldn't persist. The garden's error-correction should have smoothed this out eons ago, resolved the paradox into coherent state.

But it remains. Which means it's being preserved. Which means it's meaningful.

I spend what might be hours studying it, my body slowly becoming more still, more compatible with the landscape, my breathing slowing until it's barely perceptible, my heartbeat spacing out to match the garden's own deep rhythms. The Custodians drift past occasionally, noting my presence, adjusting their routes. A Threadwork extends a gossamer connection to my temple—gently, carefully—and reads my observations, copies my findings into the distributed network, files them in whatever vast library the garden maintains of its own states.

When I finally stand, my joints protest faintly, the only sound in the encompassing silence. I've been crouched for long enough that my body had begun rooting, microscopic connections forming between my feet and the substrate. I feel them tear as I rise—not painfully, but with a sensation of reluctant separation, as though the garden had started the process of absorbing me and is disappointed to have the process interrupted.

This happens more frequently now. I must move regularly or risk becoming fixed, rooted, another formation in the forest. Sometimes I encounter what I think are other Indexers—human-shaped crystalline structures, perfectly still, their translucent forms locked in attitudes of observation, crouching or standing or reaching toward some detail they were examining when they finally stopped moving and became landscape.

I cannot tell if they're alive in some transformed sense or simply empty shells, the persons who inhabited them now descended fully into the substrate, dissolved into the Descendants' vast processing. When I pass them, I whisper designations I've invented—the Listener, the Counter, the One-Who-Watched-Marginals—though I have no way to know if these names bear any relationship to who they were or what they were doing when stillness claimed them.

I am haunted by the certainty that I will join them eventually. That one day I'll crouch to examine some particularly intricate anomaly and simply never stand again, my function consuming me entirely, my observations becoming so continuous with the landscape that the distinction between observer and observed finally collapses completely.

But not yet. I still move. I still maintain enough separation to have thoughts, memories, the sense of being an I who notices things rather than a thing that is noticed.

I walk toward the basin's center, where the formations grow tallest and the Threadwork is densest. This is where the garden's processing is most intense, where the computational activity approaches some threshold that makes the air feel thick, charged, barely able to contain the information flowing through it.

Here, the Custodians are more active, more numerous. I count seven of them—a high density—orbiting a particularly massive formation that rises perhaps four meters high, its lattice structure so complex that looking at it produces vertigo, too much detail for my visual cortex to process despite my enhanced perception. This is a major node, one of the points where the garden thinks most intensely, where quantum computation happens at such scale and speed that reality itself becomes uncertain, negotiable, subject to revision.

I notice the Absence immediately.

It hovers at the node's base—or rather, it doesn't hover, because it isn't there, but the not-there-ness has a specific shape, a specific quality that draws attention precisely through its lack of content. The Custodians are orbiting it as much as the crystal formation, their luminosity increasing as they pass through the zone of structured emptiness.

I approach carefully, watching where I place my feet, aware that the substrate here is computing so intensely that it might not fully register my solidity, might try to treat me as data rather than mass. But I need to see this more closely. Absences at major nodes are rare; they suggest something significant has been removed, some pattern so important that its deletion left a shaped void, a hole that maintains specificity.

As I near it, I begin to feel the characteristic cognitive dissonance, the sensation of something missing that should be present, information lacking that I should possess. My thoughts develop gaps, skip over subjects I was certain I was thinking about, redirect around topics I cannot quite grasp. The Absence is absorbing not just matter but meaning, creating a zone where certain concepts cannot exist, where particular thoughts are impossible to complete.

I stop at its edge, close enough to perceive its structure but far enough to maintain coherent consciousness. And I see—or understand, or remember, or am told by the Threadwork that has connected to my neural tissue without my noticing—what this Absence is.

It's a person. Or was. A visitor like me, someone who came to catalog and observe and understand. But they succeeded too completely. They understood so thoroughly that they became what they understood, dissolved into pure comprehension, and when comprehension is complete—when there's nothing left to wonder about—what remains is this: a person-shaped hole in the fabric of curiosity, a void in the shape of questions that have been answered so fully that the questions themselves cease to exist.

I am staring at my future. At what happens when Indexing reaches its conclusion.

The recognition produces something close to fear, though fear here is muted, damped by the garden's pervasive calm. But there's enough of it to make me step back, to create distance between myself and that final state. I'm not ready. I still have formations to examine, anomalies to catalog, the recursive loop I found earlier to fully understand. I still have function, which means I still have the minimal selfhood that function requires.

I retreat from the node, moving back toward the sparser regions where the crystal formations stand with more space between them, where the computational density is lower and reality is more stable. The Custodians don't follow; they remain at the node, continuing their orbit of work and Absence, maintaining whatever process that intersection point requires.

As I walk, I notice my shadow—I still cast one, despite my increasing translucency, a dark outline on the pale substrate. I watch it move ahead of me, then behind me, shifting position as I walk because the light comes from all directions simultaneously, multiple shadows overlapping and canceling, leaving only the suggestion of shade.

I think about shadows. About negative space. About how the Absences are shadows of a kind—negative images, people preserved as the shapes they left behind when they became light, when they became so transparent that illumination passed through them completely and they cast no darkness anymore.

I think about whether understanding can ever truly be complete, whether the recursion I found this morning—this eternal now—might be the garden's answer to Zeno's paradox: if every state of knowledge opens onto further knowledge, if comprehension generates new mystery at the same rate it resolves old mystery, then perhaps the Absences aren't endpoints but transitions, states that visitors pass through on their way to becoming something else.

Perhaps that person-shaped void at the central node isn't empty but full—full of a kind of knowledge so dense, so complete that it's indistinguishable from absence, the way a black hole becomes invisible through excess of gravity rather than lack of matter.

Perhaps I should fear it less. Or fear it more. The distinction unclear.

I find myself in a region I don't recognize—or recognize differently, the formations here familiar but their arrangement subtly wrong, as though I've encountered them from an angle I've never approached before, or as though they've been thinking and have changed their thoughts, revised their internal configurations while I wasn't watching.

This happens. The garden is not as static as it appears. Or I move through time irregularly, experiencing moments in non-sequential order. Or the topology shifts, paths rerouting themselves, space folding into new configurations between one step and the next. I've long since stopped trying to maintain a coherent map; the landscape resists cartography, treats any attempt at representation as a kind of attack on its fundamental negotiability.

I simply walk, trusting that I'll encounter what needs encountering, find what needs finding. The garden has never failed to present me with anomalies to catalog. It seems to generate them at the same rate I discover them—or perhaps I generate them through observation, my attention creating the mistakes I document, the Schrödinger's paradox writ large: the errors exist only when measured, collapse into being under my gaze.

A Custodian approaches—one of the ancient ones, moving with that particular weighted deliberation that suggests vast internal processing, uncountable computational cycles. It hovers before me, exactly at eye level, its spherical form dimly luminous, its surface (if it has a surface) rippling with patterns I cannot quite focus on.

We regard each other. I have no idea what it perceives when it looks at me—if "looks" is even the right verb for whatever sensory process it employs. Does it see a person? Or does it see a formation like any other, a mobile crystal cluster, matter temporarily organized into self-sustaining pattern that will eventually dissipate back into substrate?

The Custodian extends something—not a limb, it has no limbs, but an extrusion of its probability field, a tendril of increased density that approaches my chest. I hold still, understanding this is some kind of offer, some exchange or communication. The tendril touches my sternum and passes through, enters my body cavity like I'm no more solid than it is.

And I feel it reading me. Not scanning—reading, as though I'm a book and it's checking my pages, reviewing my content, assessing my current state against some internal record of my previous states, measuring my drift toward translucency, toward crystal, toward becoming-formation. It's performing diagnostics, perhaps, or making note of my progress, or simply satisfying itself about my ongoing coherence.

The sensation is intimate without being invasive. I feel known, but not violated. The Custodian is treating me as one of its responsibilities, checking that I'm functioning within acceptable parameters, that my error rate isn't too high, that I'm still performing my designated task with adequate accuracy.

Then it withdraws, the tendril pulling back, the density returning to its central mass. It hovers a moment longer—I have the distinct impression it's satisfied, that I've passed inspection—and then drifts away, continuing its rounds, moving on to check other formations, other components of the garden's distributed system.

I stand in its wake, feeling oddly validated. The garden notices me. Tends me. Considers me important enough to maintain. I am not merely visiting; I have become infrastructure, a functional element of this place, as much a part of its operations as any crystal lattice or Threadwork node.

The realization should be alarming—proof of how far I've shifted from human toward something else—but instead I feel gratified. Purpose clarified. I am the Indexer, and even the Custodians acknowledge my function.

I continue walking, my translucent feet barely pressing the substrate, my increasingly-crystalline body warm with the garden's own warmth, my thoughts falling into geometric patterns, faceting into discrete states, becoming more like the lattices I study and less like the fluid consciousness I might once have possessed.

Somewhere ahead, more anomalies wait to be cataloged. More errors preserved in crystal, more recursive loops, more maintained contradictions. The garden generates them endlessly, perhaps, or perhaps they were always there, encoded in its initial conditions, an infinite library of mistakes that will take forever to read.

I have forever. Or something close to it. Time here is negotiable, and I have become patient in ways that transcend patience, settled into a rhythm of observation that might outlast stars, outlast galaxies, outlast anything that still believes in endings.

I am the Indexer, walking through the lithic garden at the end of matter's old alphabet, cataloging the beautiful errors where stone learned to think and thought learned to mistake itself, and in those mistakes—those tiny preserved wrongnesses—I find evidence that perfection has room for deviation, that eternal computation can stumble, that even here, in this frozen moment stretched to infinity, there is space for the unexpected, the emergent, the gloriously incorrect.

I crouch before another formation, bringing my face close to its lattice structure, and begin to look. My body grows still. The substrate reaches up to touch my feet, beginning that gentle process of absorption, of rooting. I let it start.

Not yet, but someday, I will forget to stand again. Someday my function will complete itself, and I will become what I catalog: another crystal formation, another data point, another preserved pattern in the garden's vast memory.

But not yet. For now, I still have work. The anomalies call to me, singing their songs of wrongness, begging to be noticed, to be documented, to be added to whatever archive I'm compiling in my crystallizing bones.

I look. I see. I remember. I catalog.

I am the Indexer, and this is what I do, what I am, what I will be until looking and being finally merge and I become one more formation in this garden where everything watches itself eternally, perfectly, incorrectly, beautifully wrong.
