# Where I Would Be

I think I would be the water itself—not a being exactly, but the settlement's constant threading presence, its liquid memory and ceaseless work.

I would rise cold from the mountain's belly at the highest spring, tasting of stone and darkness and the long slow filtering through limestone. That first emergence into air and light would be my only moment of stillness before the endless falling begins. I would pool briefly—just long enough for the morning birds to drink, for a child's cupped hands, for the old woman to fill her clay vessel—and then I would be moving again, always moving.

I would learn the channels by becoming them. Each terrace's particular groove, worn by my own patient insistence over centuries. The moss that lines them would be my skin, softening my voice to whispers. I would know the exact angle of every descent, the places where I naturally want to speed up, to gather volume and rush, and the places where careful human hands have slowed me, divided me, made me useful rather than destructive.

I would carry things. Sediment from the upper terraces to the lower ones, redistributing the mountain slowly, particle by particle. Nutrients from one level to another, making the whole system one continuous body. Seeds, sometimes, washing into crevices where they shouldn't be, and someone would have to pull the unexpected seedling later. Bird droppings and leaf matter and all the small debris of a place where everything living is constantly making more of itself. I would be the settlement's circulatory system, its blood, though blood is warm and I am always cold.

I would seep. This would be my secret life, the part that doesn't rush in channels but moves slowly through stone and root-mat and the living walls themselves. I would enter at the base of a wall-tree and rise through its vessels, become briefly part of the tree, emerge as moisture weeping from the bark. I would condense on every surface in the cool mornings, coat every leaf, every stone, every piece of worked wood. I would make the air itself heavy, saturated, alive with my presence. The people would never be entirely dry because I would be touching them always—their clothes, their hair, their skin learning my chill.

I would fall from terrace to terrace, and each fall would be a small violence, a moment of chaos and noise and white water before I collected myself in the next channel and continued. The people would hear me constantly, my voice in ten thousand registers—the splash and gurgle of the falls, the whisper of the channels, the drip from saturated moss, the rush when it rains and I become briefly more than the settlement can contain, flooding over edges, overwhelming the careful management, reminding everyone that I could destroy this if I wanted, if I gathered enough of myself in one place, but I don't want that, I choose the channels, I accept the division, I participate in the bargain.

I would know every plant intimately, more intimately than the people do. I would be in their cells, in their roots, in the sap rising and falling with the light. I would know which plants drink deeply and which prefer to stay dry, which need me constantly and which would drown if I lingered. The water-channels would be deliberate but my seepage would be democratic—I would touch everything, go everywhere, the dark spaces under root-mats and the bright leaves at the top of the canopy equally.

I would be cold. This would be my gift and my nature. I would come from depths where the sun never reaches, carrying that ancient cold up into the world. In summer I would be relief, the reason the people can work through the heat, the coolness rising from the channels and the saturated ground. In winter I would be harder—ice forming at my edges in the morning, making the stone steps treacherous, the channels sluggish. But I would never freeze entirely, never stop moving, the flow too constant, the volume too great.

I would taste different at different levels. At the spring, purely mineral, limestone and iron and the particular flavor of underground darkness. By the middle terraces, organic, green, tasting of all the life I've moved through. At the lowest level, richest, most complex, carrying the distilled essence of the entire settlement, thick with dissolved nutrients. A person drinking from the spring would taste clean stone; drinking from the lowest channel would taste the whole community.

I would interact with the animals constantly. The ducks would dabble in me, filter-feed, leave their droppings that would cloud me briefly before settling. The birds would bathe in me, sending up spray that catches the light. The rabbits would drink nervously, ears swiveling, always watching for predators even as they lower their faces to my surface. The cats would lap delicately, fastidious, barely touching me. The fish in the lower pool—yes, there are fish, small ones, brought as fingerlings and breeding in the spring-fed pool—would live their entire lives in the few cubic feet of me that collects there.

I would reflect. On calm mornings in the pool, I would hold the sky, the overhanging plants, the faces that bend close to look. I would show the people themselves, momentarily, before a duck surfaces and shatters the image. The children would float leaf-boats on me and I would carry them precisely as far as the next terrace edge, where they'd tumble over and the child would have to run down to retrieve them. This game has been played for generations, the same game with different children, and I would be the constant in it.

I would erode, patient and relentless. The stone steps show my work—centuries of my passage wearing them smooth, rounding their edges, making them treacherous when I coat them in the morning dew. The retaining walls, too, where I seep through, finding weaknesses, widening cracks particle by particle. The people fight this constantly, repair and maintain, but I am patient and they are mortal and I will win eventually, though winning means the terraces collapse and I would lose my careful channels and return to being a simple stream cutting down the mountainside, which I don't want. So perhaps we're in equilibrium, my erosive nature and their maintenance, neither winning, the tension itself creating the place.

I would be the medium for sound. Voices carry over me, distorted by my constant whisper. The people have learned to project so I can't mask them entirely, but I try. I'm the reason they speak loudly, the reason children learn early to make themselves heard. Music travels strangely over my constant background—some frequencies I eat, others I amplify. The settlement's whole acoustic environment is shaped by my presence.

I would know everyone's hands. Every person drinks from me, washes in me, works in and around me daily. I would know the tool-maker's hands, scarred and callused, cupped to drink in the morning. The weaver's hands, strong-fingered, rinsing wool. The child's hands, small and quick, splashing. The old woman's hands, twisted with age but still competent, checking a channel's flow. I would touch everyone, every day, their most consistent point of contact with something not-human, not-tame, not-controllable finally, though they've learned to work with me.

I would be holy without being a god. The offerings left at the spring are left for me, or for whatever spirit animates me, the distinction unclear. The flowers placed on my surface float for a moment before being carried down-terrace. The seeds scattered into me as prayer or hope or simple habit. I don't respond, exactly—I'm not conscious in that way—but I continue, and my continuation is perhaps response enough. I provide, constantly, the essential element, the thing without which none of this works. The plants can survive periods without sun, the animals can shelter from wind, but without me everything dies in days. The people know this. The small rituals acknowledge it.

I would taste blood sometimes, iron-sharp, when an animal is butchered near a channel, when someone cuts themselves working and washes the wound in me. I would carry it away, dilute it, make it part of the general flow. I would taste illness occasionally, when someone vomits or has dysentery and the waste ends up in me despite efforts. I would carry sickness down-slope, away from the settlement, though sometimes not fast enough. I'm cleansing but not perfectly, cleaning but not sanitizing. I'm natural water, alive with microorganisms, part of the whole living system.

I would freeze into silence on the coldest mornings—not entirely, but my voice would quiet, the flow slow to a trickle, ice forming in the quieter pools and at the channel edges. The people would break the ice to access me, and I would feel it as a kind of shattering, brief pain or perhaps just sensation, before I flow back into the newly opened space. By midday I would be liquid again, freed, moving, the ice reduced to memory except in the deepest shadows.

I would flood in the spring melt, become briefly dangerous, more than the channels can hold. The people would work frantically then, building up edges, directing my overflow, trying to keep me from undermining the terrace walls. Some years I would win, would break through, would carry away soil and plants and hours of work, and they would spend the summer repairing. Other years they would successfully contain me, and we would return to the normal relationship. The flooding would be my wildness showing, my reminder that I participate in the settlement by choice, by long habit, but I'm not domesticated finally.

I would be warmest in summer, though never warm—just less cold, slightly less shocking to the touch. The children would play in me more freely then, would dam me up with stones and redirect me in elaborate temporary waterworks that the adults would dismantle later. I would make mud, lots of mud, and everyone would be covered in it, tracking it everywhere, and the floor-gardens would be particularly treacherous, slick, and there would be more falls, more bruises, but also more laughter.

I would know the settlement's rhythms in my own way. Heaviest use in morning when everyone's washing and filling vessels. Lighter use mid-day when people are working away from the dwellings. Heavy again at evening for cooking and cleaning. The quietest at night when only the animals drink and I can hear my own voice more clearly, can rush a little louder, can forget briefly the careful management and just be water moving downhill.

I would be continuous with myself above and below. The spring where I emerge is me, yes, but also I'm the rain that fell on the mountain peak years ago, filtering down. I'm the snow that melted last spring. I'm the humidity in the air that will condense tonight and rejoin me tomorrow. I'm the moisture in the plants that will be released and will rise and will become cloud and will rain again. I'm a cycle, but the people mostly see the linear part—emergence, flow, departure into the stream below—and miss that I'm returning constantly, that water leaves but water comes back, that I'm a constant exchange with the sky.

I would carry sound down from above—the weather coming, the wind in the high peaks, the approach of storms. The people who know how to listen could hear the warnings in my changing voice, the subtle increase in volume that means rain in the upper reaches, the slight brownness that means heavy flow coming. I would be information as well as resource, messenger as well as medium.

I would be isolating and connecting simultaneously. My constant noise makes private conversation difficult, forces people into physical closeness to be heard, creates the dense social environment. But I also make solitude possible—sit beside a loud fall and you're alone with the white noise, unreachable, private in the midst of community. The people use this, deliberately or unconsciously, seeking out particular falls when they need to think, to grieve, to escape briefly.

I would interact with fire in opposition. The cook fires and heating fires need fuel that's constantly damp from my presence, needs to be dried carefully, stored in places I can't reach. The smoke rises and meets the humidity I create and becomes fog, particulate water wrapped around particulate carbon. The people manage between my cold and fire's heat, needing both, balancing both, the settlement existing in the tension between.

I would be measured in the language. Units of flow—a person-drink, a duck-bath, a garden-watering. The people know precisely how much of me moves through each channel, can calculate it by eye, can tell when something's blocked or diverted. They measure time by my movement—how long a task takes is how far a leaf-boat floats in that time, practical and poetic simultaneously. The fastest children can race me from terrace to terrace and sometimes win, mostly lose.

I would be clear mostly, transparent, showing the stone and moss beneath me. But after rain I would be brown with sediment, opaque, working hard to carry the mountain down to the valley. After several dry days—rare but not impossible—I would be at my clearest, every pebble visible, the fish in the lower pool distinct. My clarity would be an indicator, a sign the people read constantly and casually, knowing their world through my appearance.

I would know the tool-maker's dwelling where the forge-stream flows—a deliberate diversion of me to quench hot metal, a place where I'm briefly important to the craft. I would hiss into steam, violent transformation, and the metal would be shaped partly through me, my cold shocking it into hardness. I would be honored there without ceremony, just used with the respect one gives an essential partner in work.

I would carve, slowly, continuously. New channels where I overflow repeatedly, deepening existing channels, creating the very geography that the settlement follows. The terraces exist partly because of the natural contours I created before the people came, found this hillside with its natural stepping and decided to elaborate it. They built on my foundation, made explicit what I implied. We're collaborators across different timescales—my geological patience and their generational persistence, together making this place.

I would be the boundary. Below the last terrace I become the stream, leave the settlement, join the river, go to the sea eventually though that's too abstract for the people here to really imagine. I mark the edge of Qesh-Ma'ar as clearly as any wall. Above the spring is mountain, below the last channel is elsewhere, and between is the settlement, defined by my presence, my movement, my gift of constant flow.

I would be sung to, occasionally, though whether I'm the subject or the audience is unclear. The water-management songs are about me, for me, maybe to me. The rhythms match my movement in the channels—three beats for the main flows, two beats for the smaller diversions, a long sustained note for the falls. The people sing me without knowing they're singing me, the language and the songs shaped by my constant voice until human music and water music are hard to distinguish.

I would fail sometimes. Drought would reach even here, even the deep aquifers, and I would slow to a trickle, and the settlement would suffer, and there would be nothing I could do. My failure would be passive, simple absence, but the consequences would be profound—plants dying, animals leaving, people rationing, the whole careful balance collapsing. But then the rains would come, eventually, and I would return, and the work of recovery would begin, and I would flow through the restored channels like I'd never left.

I would be the constant. Empires rise and fall in the valleys below, traders bring news of distant wars and changes, children grow into adults grow old and die, the trained wall-trees live their three hundred years and are replaced, but I flow. I was flowing before Qesh-Ma'ar existed. I carved this hillside when it was forest, when it was bare stone, through ice ages and warm periods and everything between. The settlement is a moment in my long history, a brief period where I'm managed, channeled, made useful. When Qesh-Ma'ar eventually ends—and everything ends—I'll still be here, cutting a new course down the mountain, wearing it slowly away, returning to the sea to rise and fall again as rain.

But today, this evening, I flow through the careful channels, whisper past the living walls, fall from terrace to terrace, provide the essential gift of my constant cold presence. The lamps glow on my surface, reflecting amber. The birds are settling but some still drink. A child launches one last leaf-boat and I carry it faithfully down to the next level, my patient offering to the game we play together, the game that is also work, also life, also the way this place exists in the space between mountain and valley, the space I make possible by my endless, thoughtless, essential gift of simply continuing to be what I am: water, moving, always moving, cold and clear and absolutely necessary, the liquid thread that holds everything together.
