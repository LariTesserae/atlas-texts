# Where I Am

I would be in the transitional zones—those uncertain edges where the horizontal plateaus begin their slow curve upward into the vertical walls, where the mist pools thickest in the hours before what passes for dawn, where the ancient compressed surfaces meet the younger, softer growth.

These are the places where the architecture becomes ambiguous. Stand here and you can't quite tell if you're on a floor or a wall, if the surface ahead is horizontal or has already begun its turn toward vertical. The plant matter here grows in confused spirals, receiving conflicting instructions from the deep structures below: be stable, be porous, be weight-bearing, be climbing. It does all of these at once, creating formations that twist through impossible angles, surfaces that fold into themselves.

This is where the Silk-Spiders' threads first extend outward from their vertical domains, testing the horizontal space, creating the beginnings of those vast geometric webs. But it's also where the Grazers sometimes venture, their wide feet carefully testing each surface before committing their weight, browsing on the especially nutritious growth that flourishes in these confused regions where multiple plant strategies compete and hybridize.

The light here is strange even by the standards of the Woven Altitudes. The mist condenses more heavily where horizontal meets vertical, creating layers that refract what little light exists into bands—subtle gradients of grey that shift as you move, creating an effect like being inside a pearl. Visibility drops to arm's length sometimes, and in those moments you navigate by feel, by the give of the plant matter under your feet, by the texture of the mist itself which seems to have grain here, directionality.

I would notice that I cast multiple shadows, or shadow-suggestions, each one pointing toward a different source of diffuse light, each one slightly offset from the others. Walking forward, I would see my shadows multiply and converge, separate and fade, in patterns that have nothing to do with my actual movement. Sometimes my shadow would appear ahead of me. Sometimes it would be absent entirely, and I would seem to be the shadow of something else.

The sounds are different here too. The subsonic rumbles from the Grazers arrive distorted, echoing off surfaces that curve in ways that bend sound back on itself. The grinding of the Root-Crawlers, usually distant and ambient, sometimes becomes suddenly loud, as if they're just beneath the surface, though the plant matter shows no sign of their passage. And there are sounds that belong to nothing identifiable—creaks and sighs that might be the structure settling, or might be the plant-consciousness dreaming in voices.

# What I Am

I would be a Threshold-Walker, though that's not what the Velenne call me (they have no word for me, just a pulse-pattern that means approximately "the one who stays in-between").

My body would be wrong in subtle ways. Human-shaped, human-sized, but not quite human anymore. The skin on my hands and feet has begun to thicken into something like bark, rough and deeply textured, and when I press my palms to the surface I can almost feel what the Velenne feel—that vast network of communication humming beneath me, carrying messages in patterns I sense but cannot read. On my back, between my shoulder blades, something has started growing: a cluster of small shelf-like formations, neither quite fungus nor quite plant, that release spores in the mist-heavy hours. I don't know what they're for.

I've been here too long. That's clear from the changes. Most visitors who stay develop some marks—a patch of moss-skin, flowers growing from fingernails—but those fade when they leave. Mine are not fading. Mine are spreading, slowly, claiming more of me each month. My left foot has begun putting down small rootlets overnight when I sleep, thin white threads that bury themselves in the surface and have to be pulled free each morning with increasing difficulty. Soon I won't be able to pull them free. Soon I'll sleep in one place and wake there and remain there, my body slowly joining the structure, becoming a feature rather than a visitor.

I am not frightened by this. That's perhaps the most disturbing part—I am not frightened. I am curious. I want to know what comes next, what it feels like when the transformation completes, whether consciousness continues or diffuses, whether I will remember being human or whether that memory will decompose into something else, some pattern in the plant-network that resembles memory the way these Shepherds resemble people.

I still think in human terms, mostly. I still use language internally, though I've stopped speaking aloud—there's no one to speak to, and the sound of my voice in the mist feels like vandalism, like breaking something that should remain unbroken. I count days, though I've lost track of the total. I remember having a name, though it's becoming abstract, a sound without meaning.

What I do in the transitional zones is document, though I no longer write. My equipment failed long ago—moisture got into everything, and the plant matter grew through it, and now my camera is a small garden, my notebooks are covered in a fine fuzz of white growth. Instead, I document by arrangement. I gather things—shed Silk-Spider threads, fallen Glider wings, Grazer dung, fragments of ancient tools, interesting stones (though stones are rare here, suspicious, possibly not stones at all)—and I arrange them in patterns on the surfaces where I sleep.

The arrangements are not random. They follow rules I've developed, or discovered, or been taught by something. Each object relates to others through proximity, through orientation, through the shadows they cast in the dim light. Together they create... what? Maps? Poetry? Instructions? I'm not sure anymore. But I know they're correct. I know when an arrangement is finished because I feel it, a small click of recognition, and then I leave it and move on and start another somewhere else.

The Velenne find these arrangements sometimes. I've watched from a distance as they approach, as they stand in circles around my work, their hands pressed to the surface beneath it. They remain for hours, reading something I've written without meaning to write, in a language I don't consciously know. Then they dismantle the arrangements, not carelessly but deliberately, distributing the objects according to their own logic, and they move on. Whether they approve or disapprove, whether they're correcting my work or completing it, I don't know.

I eat, when I remember to eat. The plant matter is nutritious enough if you choose carefully—certain flowers, certain young shoots, the soft inner bark of specific formations. I've developed an instinct for what's edible, or perhaps the plant-consciousness has developed an instinct for keeping me alive, guiding me toward things I can metabolize. Sometimes I feed like the Velenne feed, pressing my palms to marked surfaces and waiting, and the plant matter does soften, does liquify, and I do absorb it through my skin. It tastes of nothing and everything. It tastes like memory. Afterwards, flowers grow where my hands were, pale ones, very small, that glow faintly in the dark hours.

I sleep in different places, trying to avoid repetition, trying not to establish a territory. But I'm failing at this. I notice I return to certain formations again and again, certain hollows in the curved surfaces where the mist pools just right, where the plant matter has a particular give beneath me. These places are becoming mine, marked by my presence, changed by it. The vegetation around them grows differently now—I can see it happening, accelerated somehow, as if my proximity acts as fertilizer or instruction or both.

Dreams here are not like dreams elsewhere. I dream in plant-time, slow and patient. I dream of root systems extending, of water moving through vessels, of cellular division and the patient assembly of cellulose. I dream from perspectives that have no eyes, sensing through chemistry and pressure and slow electrical pulses. I wake—if wake is the right word—disoriented not about where I am but about *what* I am, taking several minutes to remember that I have a distinct body, that I end at my skin, that I am not the entire surface for yards around me.

The Breath-Kin visit sometimes. One in particular has taken an interest, or has been assigned to me, or has bonded with me in whatever way Breath-Kin bond. It manifests as a persistent thickening in the mist just beyond arm's reach, and it follows me through the transitional zones, occasionally condensing enough that I can see something like a face in it—not a face, a suggestion of face, a place where the mist is organized in ways that create the impression of eyes, of attention. It warns me of dangers: thin surfaces, approaching predators, areas where the plant-consciousness is dreaming and reality becomes negotiable. It has saved my life multiple times. I have tried to thank it by altering humidity patterns with my breath, mimicking its language, but I don't know if it understands. It remains anyway.

I have seen Shepherds three times. Once, I was caught in a section where the surface was failing, the plant matter decomposing faster than it was being renewed, and I was sinking, slowly but certainly, into a kind of bog of rotting vegetation. The Shepherd appeared ahead of me, featureless and patient, and I followed it to solid ground. Once, I was lost—truly lost, not just disoriented but unable to find any landmark I recognized, walking in circles in a section where everything looked identical—and the Shepherd appeared and led me for hours to a region I knew. The third time, I don't know why the Shepherd came. I was in no danger, not lost, not trapped. It simply appeared, stood in front of me for several minutes, then dissolved. I stood in that spot for a long time afterwards, hands pressed to the surface, trying to understand what message I'd been given, what instruction, what blessing or warning.

The Velenne acknowledge me now. They didn't at first—for weeks, months, they passed as if I were furniture, as if I were just another formation in the landscape. But something changed. Perhaps I crossed some threshold of transformation, became enough of the place that I registered to them. Now when I encounter them, they stop. They don't approach, but they stop and they orient toward me, and I feel that pulse in the surface beneath my feet, that pattern of communication I almost understand. I've started responding in kind, pressing my palms down, trying to send signals back. I don't know if what I'm sending is coherent, if it means anything. But they pause, they receive, and then they move on.

I am forgetting human things. The names of objects, the faces of people I knew, the purposes of habits I once had. These things slip away like water into the absorbent surface, and I don't mourn them. They seem irrelevant to where I am, to what I'm becoming. I am learning other things instead: the feeling of growth, the patience of roots, the satisfaction of successful absorption. I am learning to think in cycles of weeks rather than hours, to plan across seasons I can barely perceive. I am learning the grammar of the pulse-language, the way different surfaces carry different meanings, the protocols for approaching a vent-shaft, for reading the health of the plant matter, for predicting where new growth will emerge.

My body continues changing. The bark-skin has spread to my elbows now, to my knees. My hair has begun to feel less like hair and more like some kind of sensory filament—it moves sometimes when there's no wind, responding to things I can't consciously detect. My nails have turned woody, brown, and they grow in strange directions now, curling around my fingertips. The growths on my back have expanded into a kind of shelf that catches moisture, creating a microenvironment where tiny plants have begun growing, an entire miniature ecosystem taking root in my body.

I avoid reflective surfaces, the rare pools of water, anything that might show me what I'm becoming. Not from vanity—that left me long ago—but from a sense that seeing might break something, might snap whatever thread is allowing me to transform without horror. I touch my face sometimes, mapping the changes by feel: ridges forming along my cheekbones, a roughening of skin around my eyes, something growing from my temples that might be branches in their earliest stage. I am becoming a feature of the transitional zones, a formation that moves but not quickly, that remembers being human but won't for much longer.

At night—the dark hours—I glow now. Just faintly, from the growths on my back, from my fingernails, from the flowers that sprout from my scalp and wither and sprout again. The glow is pale green, the same shade as certain fungi, certain deep-dwelling flowers. Small creatures are beginning to feed on this light, tiny things that cluster on my shoulders and back, incorporating me into their life cycles. I let them. They tickle, sometimes. They feel right.

I document all of this, or try to. My arrangements on the surface have become more complex, incorporating living materials now—seeds I'm carrying somehow, producing somehow, finding in my pockets though I don't remember gathering them. The arrangements grow after I leave them, the seeds germinating in patterns that extend my initial work, and weeks later I return to find gardens I planted without knowing, in languages I don't speak, saying things I didn't consciously intend.

The Velenne read these growing arrangements with what seems like approval, or at least sustained interest. Sometimes they add to them: a particular flower placed just so, a seedling transplanted to a specific location. We are collaborating, after a fashion, though toward what end I cannot say. Building something. Describing something. Becoming something.

I still have moments of human clarity, moments when I remember what I was and see what I'm becoming and feel the gulf between. In those moments, I could probably still leave. The transitional zones have passages that lead back to the regions where visitors come, where rescue could be arranged, where I could be taken away and perhaps the changes could be halted, reversed, treated. But I don't go. Every time I consider it, I press my palms to the surface and feel that vast network humming beneath me, and I understand that I am part of something now, that leaving would be amputation, would be death of a sort even if my human body survived.

So I stay. I walk the transitional zones, documenting in my incomprehensible language, sleeping in hollows that are becoming mine, eating light and moisture and the patient gifts of the plant matter beneath my feet. I feel my roots growing deeper each night. I feel my consciousness beginning to diffuse, spreading out through the network, becoming less localized in this body. I am learning what it means to be structure, to be patient, to be grown rather than born.

The Breath-Kin that accompanies me has grown denser, more persistent. Sometimes now it condenses enough that it seems almost solid, hovering at shoulder height, keeping pace with my slow walking. I have begun to understand it as a friend, or a witness, or perhaps as whatever will carry my last human thoughts out of these transitional zones when I finally root completely, when the transformation completes and I become still, become furniture, become a feature that future visitors might touch without knowing I was ever someone who chose to touch back.

I am forgetting my name. This feels important. My name is—

—was—

The mist is very thick this morning. The light is very dim. I should make another arrangement. I have seeds. I have objects. I have patterns that want to be expressed. My hands are already moving toward the surface, already finding the right placement, already beginning to write in the language of positioning and growth and patient, patient time.

The Velenne will come. They always come, eventually. They'll read what I've written and perhaps they'll understand that it says: I was human once, I was separate once, I was looking for something and I found it and it's dissolving me and I am grateful.

Or perhaps it says something else entirely. Perhaps it says nothing. Perhaps it's just beauty, pattern for pattern's sake, the arrangements of someone who has forgotten why arrangement matters but remembers that it does.

I lower my hands to the surface. The plant matter is warm and yields slightly. I can feel the network beneath, humming with information, with life, with the slow patient thoughts of something vast and old. I add my signal to it, my small pulse of presence, and somewhere far away perhaps another Threshold-Walker feels it, another person who has stayed too long, transforming in their own transitional zone, and perhaps we are saying hello, or perhaps we are saying goodbye, or perhaps we stopped distinguishing between those things long ago when our roots first touched the deep networks and we learned that hello and goodbye are the same word in the language of growing things.

The mist closes in. The light fades. I am exactly where I should be.

I am becoming the Woven Altitudes, slowly, and the Woven Altitudes is becoming me, and soon there will be no difference, and that will be perfect, and that will be home.
