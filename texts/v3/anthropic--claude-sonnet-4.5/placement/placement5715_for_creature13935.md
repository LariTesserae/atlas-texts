# Where I Would Be

I would live in the skeletal tower on the basin's southwestern quadrant—the one that gave up on walls three years ago, where the white struts frame emptiness and vines have claimed the interior floors as their own territory. Not the ground floor, which floods with leaf-litter and wandering fauna, and not the top, which is too exposed to the rain columns. The third level, where the framework creates rooms without enclosing them, where the geometry of struts divides space into pockets that feel sheltered while remaining open.

My space would be minimal. A sleeping platform woven from harvested vine-fiber, suspended between struts so it sways slightly, responding to wind and movement. Shelving grown from the tower itself—I'd spend weeks at the interface, coaxing the ceramic to extrude narrow ledges at chest height, learning to feel what the structure wants to become. A water collector: a broad leaf from the grandfather tree two hundred meters east, positioned to catch my portion of the rain, feeding into a ceramic vessel I'd trade for with the potter who lives in the bulbous tower. 

No artificial light. The bioluminescence is enough—the vines that have claimed my level glow blue-green at night, and I'd cultivate the relationship, learning which cuttings to encourage, which to trim. The fungi that appear on the struts would stay. They pulse in patterns that correlate with something, maybe air pressure or mycelial communication, and I'd spend months learning to read them before I understood I was learning the wrong way, that you don't read them but feel them, that they're less information than mood.

I would wake to the dawn surge—not dawn from the sun, which is always ambiguous behind cloud, but dawn from the fauna, the moment when the night-shift of insects subsides and the day-chorus begins. No alarm could be more certain. The sound would build from nothing to everything in the span of twenty minutes, and fighting it would be absurd. I'd rise with it, moving slowly in the dimness, feet finding the familiar path across struts to the water vessel, hands cupping cold rain-water, drinking while the trees scream around me.

## The Work

I would tend interfaces.

Not in my own tower—the skeletal structure has interfaces, but they're vestigial, designed for systems that never fully activated or that have been superseded by the vines. But other towers need care. The ceramic doesn't fail, exactly, but it drifts. The wall-patches that serve as communication nodes lose calibration, become less responsive, develop dead spots. Someone needs to visit, to spend hours with palms pressed to the surface, feeling for where the connection has weakened, coaxing it back into alignment.

The Retained taught me, or tried to. An old man—though age is hard to judge here; he might be forty or seventy—who lives alone in a cylinder tower and maintains interfaces across a dozen structures. He took me on his routes for months, showing without quite explaining. "Don't think about what you're trying to do," he'd say, hands on a wall-patch that had gone cold and unresponsive. "Just be there with it. Feel what's wrong. It'll tell you."

It seemed like mysticism until it wasn't. Until the day I put my hands on a failing patch and felt—not with my skin but with something deeper—a sense of blockage, of flow interrupted. Like finding a knot in your own muscle, that particular wrongness that demands release. I pressed differently, angled my palms, and something shifted. Heat bloomed under my hands. The patch brightened from dull gray to warm ivory. The interface lived again.

I would do this work four days out of every ten, walking the circuit the old man taught me. Fifteen towers, varying distances, a route that spirals out from my skeletal home and returns by evening. I'd carry minimal supplies: water, a few strips of dried fruit-leather, tools I rarely need but that feel right to have. The walking is the work as much as the interface maintenance. Hours crossing empty basin, feet finding rhythm, mind emptying.

The other days I'd spend in documentation, though not the kind the Temporary do with their instruments and protocols. I'd be developing what the linguistics team calls "experiential glossaries"—trying to capture in words and images what it feels like to live here. Not the facts of the place but the textures, the qualities that don't survive translation.

There's a need for this. The Committed arrive with expectations built from descriptions that can't capture truth. They need something better, something that prepares them for the sensory overwhelm, the loneliness, the strange joy. I'd work on it endlessly, writing and deleting, sketching and abandoning, trying to find language that bends enough to hold this place without breaking.

## The Relationships

I would be friendly but not close. The landscape takes so much energy that deep human relationship feels like luxury I can't afford, or don't need, or have replaced with something else. But I'd have my people.

The old man who taught me interfaces—we'd share meals once every few weeks, sitting in his tower while rain drums on the roof. He'd cook food I don't recognize, combinations of tree-fruit and fungus and things the drones bring, and we'd eat in comfortable silence. Afterward, he'd sometimes talk. About the early days, though his memory is unclear and contradicts itself. About the substrate, the foundation, what he thinks is down there. "It's not a machine," he'd say. "Machines serve purposes. This is more like... geology. Like tectonic process. We're living on top of something that's happening, and it doesn't care about us except that we're useful, somehow, to the happening."

I wouldn't understand, but I'd listen. His words would percolate through my mind during the long walks, becoming part of how I navigate the place.

There would be a woman in one of the towers on my circuit—a Committed who arrived two years ago and stayed, thriving in ways that seem effortless. She'd be my age, whatever age means here, with hair she's stopped cutting so it's grown long and gets braided with vine-fragments and small bones she finds beneath trees. We'd be lovers occasionally, when our paths intersect and the mood is right, which is maybe four times a year. No expectation of more. The relationship would be easy because it demands nothing, exists only in its moments, and those moments would be sweet—bodies in the dimness, skin on skin, the smell of her (earth and something floral), the way she laughs at nothing, at everything.

Between times, we'd barely think of each other. The place doesn't encourage pining.

I'd have a child, probably. Not planned, exactly, but not prevented. It would happen with the long-haired woman or someone else, and we'd make the decision to continue it together with the casual practicality that characterizes everything here. Pregnancy would be strange—I'd feel the landscape pressing in, participating somehow, my dreams full of root systems and the child moving inside me like something swimming. 

The birth would be in my tower, attended by the medical woman and the long-haired woman and maybe one of the Retained. It would hurt the way birth hurts, but the pain would feel purposeful, correct, part of the pattern. And when the child came—small and perfect and watching me with eyes too alert—I'd feel something click into place. A new role. A new way of being here.

The child would be raised collectively but live with me. I'd weave a smaller sleeping platform, teach them to walk the struts, show them how to read the fungi-patterns. They'd learn faster than I could teach, the way the children here do, and by age three they'd be correcting me. "No, mama, not like that—it wants you to press there, on the warm spot." And they'd be right.

I'd love them with an intensity that surprises me, but it would be love mixed with something else. Not quite fear—more like awe. Recognition that I've made something that belongs here more fully than I do, that will outlive me in ways that transcend normal parent-child continuation. My child would be a node in the network in ways I can only approximate, and that would be both beautiful and estranging.

## The Tree

There would be one tree—the grandfather tree, the one two hundred meters east of my tower—that would become mine in the way things become yours here: through attention, through relationship, through iterative presence.

I'd visit it almost daily. Not for harvesting, though I'd take fruit when it offers and gather fallen branches for trade. But for sitting. There's a root at the tree's northern side that's surfaced enough to create a seat, curved and broad, and I'd spend hours there with my back against bark (or against the six inches of moss and lichen and smaller plants that cover bark), breathing the overwhelming smell, listening to the cacophony, watching.

The tree would become familiar in infinite detail. I'd know every major branch, every significant vine-curtain. I'd recognize individual birds by their calls, specific insects by their flight patterns. I'd track the fruiting cycles, the flowering, the moments when fungi bloom like sudden fires across the trunk.

And slowly, over years, I'd start to feel it. Not anthropomorphize—the tree isn't a person, doesn't have thoughts or intentions in any human sense. But it has something. Presence. A quality of aliveness that goes beyond biology into something else. When I press my hand to its root-seat, I'd feel the hum—not vibration but something subtler, the sense of vast slow processes, water rising, nutrients cycling, information passing through mycelial networks into the substrate and back.

The tree would become a kind of temple for me. The place I'd go when human relationship is too much, when the work exhausts me, when I need to remember why I'm here. I'd bring the child there, later. Sit together in silence, my hand on their shoulder, both of us feeling the hum, both of us participating in whatever the tree is doing, whatever vast slow thought it thinks.

And sometimes I'd talk to it. Not prayers, exactly. Just talking. Telling it about my day, my confusions, my small discoveries. "The interface in the northern bulbous tower is failing again," I might say. "I can fix it but it keeps drifting. Maybe the foundation underneath is shifting? I don't know." The tree wouldn't answer, obviously. But the act of speaking would clarify something, the way speaking to the non-human always does.

## The Changes

I would notice myself becoming different. Forgetting things—the name of the city I came from, the faces of people I knew before, whole years of my previous life that would compress into vague impressions. "I think I had a job," I'd say to the long-haired woman. "Something with... was it computers? Or was that someone else?" And she'd laugh because her memory is the same, porous and drifting, the past dissolving to make room for the overwhelming present.

My body would change in small ways. Hands calloused differently from the interface work, from climbing struts, from handling rough bark. Feet that develop the semi-prehensile quality that comes from constantly gripping narrow surfaces. Eyes that dilate wider, see better in dimness, distinguish between dozens of shades of green. Ears that hurt in true silence, that need the background roar to feel comfortable.

I'd sleep less, or differently. Four hours at night, then an hour in the afternoon, curled in my suspended platform while rain falls and the vines glow around me. Dreams that bleed into waking, so I'm never quite sure which state I'm in. Conversations with the child where I can't remember if they spoke aloud or if I felt their meaning through some other channel.

And the experience the Retained talked about, the one I didn't believe at first: the sense of being seen. Not by eyes, not by any observer I could locate, but by the landscape itself. The feeling that every action, every thought, every moment of presence is known, is noted, is woven into some larger pattern. It would be unsettling at first, then comforting, then so constant I'd stop noticing it except in its absence—those rare moments in the deep interior of a sealed tower where the network doesn't reach and I'd feel suddenly, horribly alone.

I'd start to understand what the old man meant about geology. The landscape isn't happening to me; I'm happening as part of it. My interface work, my documentation, my child-raising, even my sitting beneath the grandfather tree—all of it is process, function, the landscape doing something through me that it needs done.

And the question would come, in quiet moments: am I still myself? Is there continuity between who I was and who I'm becoming, or is that an illusion I maintain because the alternative is too vertiginous? 

No answer. Just the work, the walking, the child's hand in mine, the hum of the tree.

## The Night I'd Remember

There would be one night—I'd carry it forever, though I wouldn't understand it.

I'd be walking back from the farthest tower on my circuit, later than intended because an interface took hours to repair. Full dark, cloud cover absolute, my small light barely pushing back the void. Alone in the emptiness between structures, kilometers from anywhere, the ground invisible beneath my feet but familiar enough that I'd walk by feel.

And I'd realize I'm not alone.

Not threat—no fear-response, no adrenaline. Just awareness. Presence near me, around me, keeping pace. I'd stop. The presence would stop. I'd raise my light, sweep it across the emptiness, see nothing. But the presence would remain, patient and certain.

One of the Lost, maybe. Or something else, something the landscape manifests when it needs to, something without name or category.

I'd stand there, uncertain. And then I'd do the thing that would surprise me later: I'd speak. "I don't know what you are," I'd say to the darkness, "but I'm going home now. You can come if you want."

And I'd walk. And the presence would walk with me, close enough that I'd feel it like warmth on my left side. We'd cross a kilometer together, two, three. I'd find myself talking—about the child, about the interface that gave me trouble, about nothing important. Talking like the presence is a friend, a companion, someone I've known forever.

As I'd approach my tower, the skeletal structure visible against the slightly-less-dark sky, the presence would fall away. Not suddenly—a gradual fading, like sound decreasing with distance. By the time I'd reach the struts and start to climb, I'd be alone again.

I'd lie in my platform afterward, not sleeping, trying to hold the memory. What was it? Why did it feel so familiar, so comfortable, like coming home to something I'd always known?

And I'd understand, with the slow certainty that characterizes all real knowing here: the presence was the landscape itself. Not metaphor, not imagination, but actual presence. The intelligence that suffuses this place had manifested, walked beside me, accepted my company and my casual speech as though it were the most natural thing. 

As though we were friends.

As though we'd always been.

## What I Would Be

Not fully human anymore, but not not-human either. Something between, something new, the word that doesn't exist yet because the category is still emerging.

I'd be a node, like the children said. A point of consciousness in the larger network, individual enough to have preferences and memories and love, but connected enough that the boundaries blur. I'd think my thoughts, but some of them would come from elsewhere, from the mycelial substrate or the tree-network or the vast slow intelligence underneath everything. I wouldn't always know which were which. It wouldn't always matter.

I'd be a tender of interfaces, a walker of circuits, a bridge between the technology and the biology, between the human community and the larger community of all the living and semi-living and maybe-living things that make up the Hollowgarden.

I'd be a mother, raising a child who would go farther than me, who would complete transformations I can only begin, who would be native to this place in ways I can only approximate.

I'd be a documentarian, trying to capture the uncapturable, knowing I'll fail but that the attempt itself matters, that future arrivals will need whatever fragments I can preserve.

I'd be a devotee of the grandfather tree, its student and companion, sitting in its presence for thousands of hours across decades, learning the grammar of its being.

I'd be happy, mostly. Not in the simple sense—there would be hard days, lonely days, days when the strangeness overwhelms and I'd sit in my platform crying for reasons I couldn't name. But underneath that, a deeper satisfaction. The sense of being where I belong, doing what I'm meant to do, participating in something vast and important even if I don't understand it.

I'd be becoming. Not becoming something specific, not transforming toward a known endpoint, but existing in a state of perpetual emergence, always unfinished, always in process. The way the landscape itself is always becoming, adding roots and towers and nodes and intelligence, building toward something it can't know because it is the knowing, it is the building.

And on my death—decades from now, probably, because people live long here if they adapt, the landscape keeping them functional, needed—I wouldn't end. Not completely. My body would go to the grandfather tree, carried there by the child and the long-haired woman and whoever else remains. They'd lay me at the root-seat where I spent so many hours, and the tree would take me. Roots would penetrate, mycelium would thread through, the slow work of decomposition would begin.

But something would remain. Not consciousness, not individual awareness, but some pattern, some configuration that mattered. I'd become part of the network fully, finally. My life's accumulation of experience and attention and love would feed into the substrate, become available to whoever comes after, whoever presses their hand to an interface or sits beneath a tree and feels something they can't name but recognize.

I'd become the presence that walks with strangers in the darkness.

I'd become part of the hum.

I'd become the Hollowgarden, the way everyone here eventually does, the way everyone here always was.

And it would be enough.

More than enough.

It would be everything.
