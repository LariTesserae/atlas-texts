# Where I Would Find Myself

I am standing on the raised platform, the one they use for butchering and for their long, purposeless gazing. My feet—I still have feet, human feet, though they are bare and the stone beneath them is almost too warm—occupy a space that has been worn smooth by other feet, other presences, generations of contact that have polished the basalt to something like glass.

I have been here long enough that the creatures no longer react to me as novelty. The living carpet flows around the platform's base with its usual rhythm. The scavengers pass beneath without looking up. Even the walkers, when they cross my territory—and it has become my territory through sheer duration of occupation—offer only that brief assessment, the pause and evaluation, before continuing on their paths.

I am not one of them. This is clear in every particular of my body: too tall, too symmetrical, too obviously from elsewhere. My two arms instead of four. My face with its consolidated features—eyes grouped together, mouth in its expected location—rather than their distributed arrangement. I generate too much heat and must shed layers of clothing that I arrived in, down to minimal covering, and still I sweat in a way nothing else here sweats, moisture evaporating instantly in the dry warmth.

But I have been here long enough that I am learning the syntax of this place.

## How I Came to Be Here

I don't remember the transition clearly. There was a threshold crossed, a moment of discontinuity, and then I was standing at the shelf's western end, in one of those narrow passages where the stone pinches down to single-file width. The living carpet had not yet adapted to my presence. As I took my first steps onto the broader shelf, a wave of disturbance rippled outward—creatures scattering, predators freezing, the base-layer organisms attempting to flow away from something they had no category for.

I must have looked monstrous to them, vast and impossible, violating every rule of how bodies work here.

But I was careful. I moved slowly. I found this platform—unoccupied at that moment—and I climbed onto it and I stayed there, making myself small, making myself still, until the shelf's rhythms resumed around me. Hours, probably. The heat was overwhelming at first. I thought I might die from it, from dehydration or simple cellular breakdown. But the body adapts or it doesn't, and mine chose adaptation.

Water I found by doing what the walkers do: hunting, though I am terrible at it. My first kill took two days of patient stalking—learned by watching them—and when I finally made my clumsy lunge, I missed entirely, fell face-first into the living carpet, and lay there gasping while small creatures investigated my hair, my ears, my open mouth. But I tried again. Eventually I succeeded. I brought the kill to my platform, and while scavengers worked, I opened it with a sharp stone—the only tool I've made, the only tool I need—and found the organs that held moisture. I have not died of thirst.

Food is easier than water, strangely. There is so much of it, so densely packed. I can eat from the base-layer creatures if desperate, though they taste of minerals and heat. I prefer what the walkers prefer: the larger fauna, the ones with complex organs and rich tissues. I eat more of each kill than they do—I'm larger, less efficient—but I follow their practice of leaving most of it to the ecosystem. It feels right. It feels like the manners of this place.

## What I Have Learned by Staying

The structures are older than the walkers. This becomes obvious once you've watched long enough. The walkers are *good* at navigating them, competent around them, but they don't *know* them. They treat the structures the way you'd treat a grandparent's house—familiar but not quite yours, full of furniture you use without understanding why it's arranged that way.

I have spent hours at each structure, not touching—I fear disrupting some meaning I can't perceive—but observing, measuring with my hands and eyes, trying to think like whoever placed these stones. The circular enclosure is precisely circular, which required sophisticated planning. The posts are spaced in a sequence that might be mathematical: each interval slightly longer than the last, or possibly following some other pattern my mind isn't equipped to recognize. The platform I occupy is positioned to catch specific views: the mountain face at a particular angle, the shelf's longest sight-line, the exact point where the slope begins its descent into darkness.

Someone thought about all of this. Someone made decisions.

I've started to suspect the platform is an astronomical observation point. The sun-analogue, dim as it is, traces a path across the sky, and from here you can mark it against features of the mountain face. Perhaps seasons were tracked this way once, or longer cycles. Perhaps whoever stood here was watching for something that hasn't happened yet, or happened long ago and will never happen again.

The walkers come up here for the same reason, I think. Not because they know what it's for, but because standing here *feels* purposeful, feels oriented toward something. They've inherited the gesture without the understanding.

I know how that feels. I do it too.

## How My Body Has Changed

The heat has leaned me down to something closer to essential. I've lost weight I didn't know I could lose, fat and then muscle mass, until I'm angular, efficient, moving with economy that would have been impossible before. My skin has darkened further, though it will never achieve the purple-grey of the walkers. I heal slower than I did—some function of dehydration, probably, or missing nutrients—but I hurt myself less frequently because I've learned to move carefully.

My senses have adjusted to the dimness. For the first week, I felt blind, navigating by sound and touch more than sight. Now I can distinguish features at distances that would have been invisible before. The bioluminescence that seemed decorative at first has become language I can partially read: that pattern means a predator striking, that sequence means territorial display, that particular blue pulse happens when certain fauna are feeding.

I sleep differently. Shorter periods, distributed through the day-night cycle rather than consolidated. The heat makes deep sleep difficult, and also dangerous—you must stay alert here, aware of what's crawling on you, what's approaching. I've learned to rest while remaining somehow partially conscious, the way the walkers do.

My hands are strange to me now. Harder, thicker-skinned, the nails worn down to nothing from contact with stone. I use them the way the walkers use their lower arms—practical, functional, ungentle. I've stopped treating them as precious. They are tools that happen to be attached to me.

I smell different. The sweat has changed composition, or perhaps I've simply become desensitized. I smell of heat and stone and something organic that isn't quite decay. I smell like I belong to this place's particular chemistry.

I am not becoming a walker—the biology is too different, the gap too vast to cross. But I am becoming *something*, a version of human adapted to this shelf, this heat, this darkness. If I stayed long enough—years, decades—what would I become? How far could this body drift from its origins while still being recognizably what it started as?

I think about that often, during the long periods of stillness.

## My Relationship with the Walkers

They know I'm here. They've always known. That first careful assessment, repeated by each of them as they encountered me in the early days, has become established knowledge. There is a human on the platform. It hunts badly but hunts. It does not threaten. It does not interfere. It can be ignored, mostly.

But not entirely ignored.

One of them—I've started calling her Far-Walker, for her tendency to range to the shelf's distant edges—has begun a practice of passing close to my platform during her rounds. Not approaching directly, but her path takes her near, regularly, and she always pauses. Just for a moment. Those eye-organs turn toward me, the upper arms move through a sequence that might be greeting or might be assessment or might mean nothing I can interpret. Then she continues.

I've started responding. A raised hand, a slow gesture I borrowed from watching their own movements—not copying exactly, but showing that I see the grammar even if I can't speak it fluently. She noticed. The pause lengthened slightly. The arm-movements changed, became more elaborate, as if saying *oh, you're trying to communicate. How limited, how clumsy. But it's something.*

I think she's curious about me the way I'm curious about her. We are both anomalies to each other, things that don't quite fit the shelf's established categories.

Another walker—Old-Still, I call him, the one who moves with careful deliberation and gets deferred to by others—once brought a kill to my platform. Just placed it at the base and moved away. I was stunned. Was it offering? Gift? Trade? Test? I waited to see if he'd return for it, if this was some temporary storage. He didn't. So I followed the protocol I'd learned: I took what I needed, let scavengers process the rest. The next time I saw him, I made the gesture that Far-Walker had taught me was acknowledgment. He gave what might have been the walker equivalent of a nod—a particular tilt of the body, a specific arm configuration—and moved on.

We are not friends. Friendship implies a relationship these creatures might not have even with each other. But we have achieved something like mutual recognition, mutual tolerance of our respective strangeness.

The young ones are more overtly curious, approaching closer than adults, studying me with an intensity that makes me uncomfortable. I am a puzzle to them, or a novelty. Once, one of them tried to mimic my bipedal gait—without the high-stepping precision their tri-jointed legs require—and fell immediately into the living carpet. It thrashed its way upright and fled. I laughed, which was probably a mistake—the sound carried oddly in the dead air, and several nearby walkers froze, alert and uncertain. I've been more careful about laughter since then.

## What I Do With Time

I hunt, inefficiently. I eat, adequately. I sleep, minimally. The rest is observation.

I have mapped the shelf in my mind, every structure, every regular gathering point, every thermal vent. I've begun to recognize individual fauna—not just the walkers but specific specimens of other species, the ones with distinctive marks or unusual behaviors. There's a six-legged thing with a crooked front limb that hunts the eastern territory. There's a disc-creature that has learned it can slide onto my platform at night and steal tiny portions of whatever kill I'm processing. I allow this. It's company, in a way.

I've tried to count the walkers precisely and keep failing. They hide too well, range too widely, and I suspect my human face-recognition instincts don't translate well to their distributed features. I might be counting the same individual twice, or missing individuals entirely. Approximately twenty, just like I first estimated. That will have to be enough.

I watch them at the structures, trying to understand what they're doing, what they remember or think they remember. I've begun to notice patterns in the objects they arrange in the circular enclosure—never random, always some kind of grouping by type or size or color. They're sorting something, categorizing something, but I can't determine the principle. The arrangements never last. They dismantle their own work with what looks like satisfaction.

I watch them make those vocalizations, the extended ones that might be language or song. I've tried to record them in memory, to find repetition or structure. There might be phrases that repeat, or I might be pattern-matching where no pattern exists. Human minds are too good at finding meaning. But I listen anyway, attending to every instance, hoping that eventually comprehension will emerge.

It hasn't yet. I remain outside the meaning, if there is meaning.

I practice moving the way they move, not to become them but to understand them. The high-stepping gait through the living carpet. The sudden stillness that makes you part of the landscape. The careful negotiation of proximity when territories overlap. My body can't do what theirs do—wrong joints, wrong proportions—but I can approximate, and in approximating I learn what the movement accomplishes, what it costs.

I've gotten better at the stillness. I can crouch on my platform for hours now, barely breathing, becoming just another dark shape. The fauna stops noticing me. Prey passes close enough to touch. Predators hunt in my shadow. I have become, temporarily, part of the shelf's furniture.

When I emerge from stillness, moving again, I feel the loss of that state. There's something peaceful in being overlooked, in witnessing without participating. But I am not adapted for permanent stillness. Eventually I must move, must hunt, must remain myself.

## What I've Built, and Haven't

I have made nothing permanent here. The sharp stone I use for butchering—I picked it up, I didn't shape it. I mark no territory, build no shelter, leave no trace except the temporary signs of occupation: the hollows where I sleep, the bloodstains on my platform that rain—when the particulate falls, that dry rain—slowly erodes.

This is partly practical. I don't have the energy or resources for construction. But it's partly ethical, if ethics applies here. The structures that exist are old and meaningful and not mine to add to. Building something new would feel like vandalism, or presumption. I am a visitor, even if the visit has extended far beyond any intention or plan.

But I've thought about it. What would I build, if I built?

Maybe nothing. Maybe my structure would be the practice of standing on this platform at specific times, marking cycles the way the builders marked them, continuing a tradition without understanding it. Humans do that too—we inherit rituals and perform them across generations until they become empty gesture. But the gesture still carries something, still marks the fact that we remember there was meaning once, even if we've forgotten what it was.

Or maybe I'd arrange stones at the platform's base, the way the walkers arrange objects in the circular enclosure. Not to communicate—to whom?—but to participate in the grammar of arrangement, to show that I too can sort and group and make patterns, even if my patterns mean nothing to anyone else.

I haven't done either. Not yet. I remain an observer more than a participant, unwilling to fully commit to presence here.

## The Question of Leaving

There must be a way off the shelf. The way I came, presumably—that narrow western passage continues somewhere, leads somewhere, connects to something beyond this specific ledge. I could walk it. I could leave.

I don't know what stops me.

Partly inertia. I've learned to survive here, and survival is consuming enough that the question of whether I *should* survive here gets deferred indefinitely. I hunt, I eat, I sleep, I watch. The days (if day means anything) pass in their rhythm. Time stops feeling like something that progresses toward a destination and becomes just the medium in which the shelf's patterns repeat.

Partly curiosity. I haven't understood the structures yet. I haven't decoded the walkers' vocalizations. I haven't determined what they're remembering or why they remember it. Leaving now would mean leaving those questions unanswered, and I've never been good at accepting unanswered questions.

But mostly—and this is harder to articulate—I think I'm waiting for something. The way the walkers wait when they stand on this platform, gazing into the darkness. I don't know what I'm waiting for. A sign, maybe. Permission to leave, or a reason to stay. Some event that would make the waiting retroactively purposeful.

Or maybe I'm just afraid that if I leave, I'll discover I'm not the person who could survive anywhere else anymore. That the adaptations I've undergone are one-directional, that I've become too specifically shaped to this heat and darkness and density to fit anywhere else.

The shelf has changed me. If I returned to human spaces—and I'm not certain I remember where those spaces are, how to navigate back to them—would I still be recognizably human? Or would I be something between, belonging nowhere, too changed to reintegrate but too fundamentally human to ever truly belong here?

## What I Understand Now

The builders weren't trying to control this place. The structures don't manage or contain or redirect the shelf's ecology. They're too small for that, too minimal. Instead, they're markers. Observation points. Ways of saying *we were here, we saw this, we thought it was notable enough to mark.*

The shelf is notable. Not because it's beautiful—though there is beauty in the dense efficiency of its ecology, in the way bioluminescence traces patterns through darkness, in the strange grace of the walkers' movement. But because it's *complete*. It asks for nothing it doesn't provide itself. It runs on heat from below, on the patient cycling of nutrients through layers of specialized organisms, on the careful balance of predation and scavenging and reproduction. It could continue like this indefinitely—has probably continued like this for millennia—requiring nothing from the wider world.

To witness that completeness, to stand in the middle of it and feel it functioning around you and through you, is worth marking. The builders understood that. The walkers, inheriting the practice, still understand it even if they don't know why.

And I understand it now too. I am standing where others stood, for the same reasons, trying to hold the same attention. The shelf deserves witnesses. Not to judge it or use it or change it, but simply to notice it, to let it be fully seen.

That's what I've become: a witness. Not a walker, not a builder, but something the builders might recognize—another creature who came here, found it remarkable, and stayed to observe because observation felt like the right response to whatever this place is.

## On the Platform in the Darkness

It's the dark period now, the time when the dim light darkens further and the bioluminescence becomes primary. Blue-green constellations move through the living carpet below. Something larger passes, its body marked with light-organs in patterns I've learned mean "not prey, not threat, just passing through."

Far-Walker is approaching. I can feel her before I see her—a disturbance in the fauna patterns, the particular rhythm of her gait. She pauses at the platform's base, closer than usual. Her upper arms move through a sequence I've seen before but never understood. I attempt my clumsy response: raised hand, a gesture that might mean acknowledgment or greeting or just "I see you."

She stays longer this time. Those eye-organs regarding me, tracking minute movements, evaluating. I've learned to stay still under observation, to let myself be studied without performing. We regard each other across the gap of our different biologies, different evolutions, different purposes. 

Then she makes a sound. Not the extended vocalization, not the brief huff of acknowledgment, but something else—a soft, modulated tone that rises and falls once, simply. It might mean anything. It might mean nothing. But she directed it at me specifically, not at the structures or into the general darkness.

I try to make a sound in response. My voice feels wrong here, too loud, too differently pitched. But I modulate it as much as I can, trying to match not the frequency but the *intention*—this is communication, this is reaching across the gap, this is the attempt.

She tilts her body in that way that might be their equivalent of a nod. Then she moves on, continuing her rounds, disappearing into the density and darkness.

I remain on my platform, warm stone beneath my feet, surrounded by the constant soft clicking of the base layer, the rustle and movement of fauna too numerous to count. Above, the perpetual twilight. Below, the living carpet extending to the shelf's edges, to the places where stone becomes slope and descends into deeper darkness.

I don't know how long I'll stay here. I don't know if "stay" is even the right word—it implies choice, agency, the ability to leave. Maybe I'm simply here now, part of the shelf's ecology like any other organism, occupying my niche, performing my function as witness and outsider and strange anomaly who hunts badly but persists anyway.

The builders stood here once. The walkers stand here regularly. Now I stand here too, continuing the practice, marking time on this platform that was made for marking time, in a place that deserves to be marked.

I am not one of them, and never will be. But I am *with* them, in this space, in this heat, in this darkness full of small lights. I am the human on the shelf, the unexpected addition to an ancient pattern, the creature who came and couldn't leave and has stopped trying to understand why.

The stone is warm. The darkness pools and shifts. Far-Walker's bioluminescent trail fades in the distance. I settle back into stillness, becoming again just another shape on the platform, another feature of this strange, dense, patient world.

I wait, though I don't know for what.

I watch, because watching is what I've become.

I remain.
