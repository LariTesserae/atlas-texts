# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the deep gallery where the textiles wait.

Not the weaving room with its clatter and companionship, though I pass through there—nodding to Sorine, whose hands never stop moving even in conversation, feeling the humid warmth from the mechanical looms, the smell of lanolin and the mineral tang of dye vats. No, my place is beyond that, in the storage gallery that cuts back into the mountain's body, where the finished cloth is kept before the traders take it down.

It's a long space, this gallery—perhaps forty feet deep, twelve wide, the ceiling lost in shadow despite the two electric bulbs that burn here constantly, their light never quite sufficient, never quite failing. The air is cool and dry, carefully so; dampness would ruin the work of months. There are ventilation shafts drilled through the stone, narrow as a fist, that draw air in a slow circulation that you feel only if you stand very still. Three walls are living rock, dark schist veined with paler minerals that catch the light in thin striations. The fourth wall, the outer one, is built—fitted stone with a heavy door and high narrow windows that can be shuttered against storms.

The textiles are stored on wooden shelves that run the length of both side walls, floor to ceiling. Each piece is folded with precision, wrapped in undyed cloth, labeled in Sorine's meticulous script with information about weight and weave and intended purpose. There are the water-resistant traveling cloaks, dense-woven and heavy as chain mail. Lighter fabrics for shirting and lining. The specialty work—tapestries woven on the hand looms with intricate patterns that take weeks to complete, destined for lowland houses where people have space for beauty that serves no practical function. Blankets. Canvas for tents and sails. Each piece representing hours of labor, the rhythm of the looms, the sheep on the high slopes, the plants that gave their subtle colors.

This is where I would be. Not weaving—I've tried and lack the patience, the ability to maintain the pattern while the shuttle flies and the reed beats and the harnesses shift in their sequences. But here, in the keeping and the ordering, I find something that settles me. I am the inventory keeper, the one who knows what exists and where, who receives the work from the weavers and arranges it, who brings pieces out when the traders come, who notes what's been taken and what needs to be made.

I came here seven years ago from a lowland city I won't name, for reasons that seemed overwhelming then and seem smaller now, though no less real. The usual story of someone who couldn't fit the shape of the life that was expected. In the city I had been a clerk in a merchant house, working with ledgers and bills of lading, and I was competent but desperately unhappy in ways I couldn't articulate. The noise, perhaps. The constant press of people. The sense that everything was always in motion and nothing ever actually moved. When I heard that the mountain settlements needed someone with facility in numbers and letters, someone to manage the inventory of trade goods, I applied with an urgency that surprised even myself.

The first year nearly killed me. Not literally, though I did spend three months in a state of constant altitude sickness that left me gray-faced and retching, dizzy on the stairs, my heart hammering at exertions that left the native-born unbothered. Oldric dosed me with things that helped marginally. Told me I might not adapt, that some people simply couldn't. But I was stubborn, or desperate, or both, and I stayed. By the second winter my body had made its accommodations—larger lungs, thicker blood, whatever mysterious adjustments flesh makes to necessity.

But the physical adjustment was easier than the rest of it. Learning to navigate the vertical maze of the settlement, the endless stairs, the galleries that connected in non-intuitive ways so that I'd often emerge somewhere I hadn't intended. Learning the social structure, who was who, how decisions were made, where I fit in the intricate web of relationships and obligations. Learning to tolerate the confinement, the way you encountered the same faces daily, how nowhere was truly private, how everyone knew your business whether you wanted them to or not.

What saved me was the textile gallery. Sorine needed help—the previous inventory keeper had died the winter before I arrived, an elderly man whose organizational system had died with him, leaving chaos. She asked if I could make sense of it, restore order, create a system that would persist. I said yes, grateful for a defined task, for a space that would be specifically mine, for work that felt comprehensible when so much else was bewildering.

I spent months in here that first year, going through every piece, creating the inventory, building the shelving system with help from Koln's maintenance crew, establishing protocols. The gallery became my refuge, my decompression chamber between the intensity of the settlement's social life and my own need for quiet. In the city I had been lonely in crowds; here I discovered I could be solitary without loneliness, that being alone in a room where beautiful things were carefully kept could be a kind of peace.

I am forty-one years old now—I arrived at thirty-four—and the person I was in the city feels like someone else, someone I knew slightly and didn't particularly like. The mountain has carved me into a different shape. I am leaner, harder, my hands callused in new places from the work of moving bolts of cloth and hauling storage crates and the endless climbing that life here requires. My face has weathered—I can see it in the small mirror I keep, the fine lines from squinting in the bright alpine light, the permanent windburn. I breathe differently. Move differently. Think differently.

The rhythm of my days is this: I wake early in the small dwelling I occupy, a room carved into the mountain with a built front wall, a narrow window, space enough for a bed, a chest, a small table, hooks for clothing. I make tea from mountain herbs—mint and something else I don't know the name of that grows in the high crevices, astringent and clarifying. Eat yesterday's bread or porridge if I've made it. Then down the stairs to the textile gallery, unlocking the heavy door with the key I wear on a cord around my neck.

The morning I spend on whatever the current project is. Sometimes it's receiving new work from the weavers—Sorine or one of her workers will bring pieces to the gallery, and I'll examine them, note their characteristics, assign them positions in the inventory, wrap and shelve them. Sometimes it's preparing orders for the traders—selecting pieces that match what's been requested, carrying them out to the trading post, adjusting the inventory records. Sometimes it's maintenance—checking the older stock for any sign of moth damage or moisture infiltration, rotating pieces so the same fabrics aren't always at the front, making sure the labeling remains clear.

I've developed an eye for the work over the years. I can assess quality now almost as well as Sorine—see the evenness of the weave, the trueness of the dye, the specific characteristics that make one piece more valuable than another. I've learned which fabrics the lowland merchants prize most, which move quickly and which languish. I advise Sorine sometimes on what might be worth producing more of, what might be reduced. She listens about half the time, which seems about right—I know the market side, but she knows the making, and the making has its own logic.

Midday I take the meal with everyone else in one of the common galleries. I have a few people I gravitate toward—there's Esric from the generator station, who shares a certain quality of temperament, a comfort with systems and mechanisms and the satisfactions of maintained order. There's Breta the librarian, though her conversation is often strange, looping back to things in books rather than things in the world, but I find I don't mind. There's Harwen from the council, the other relative outsider, and we occasionally compare notes on the experience of trying to become part of a community that existed long before we arrived.

Afternoons vary more. Sometimes there's inventory work that spills over from the morning. Sometimes I help with other tasks—everyone contributes to maintenance when there are big projects, and I've spent days carrying stone or mortar, helping repair walls, clearing channels. I'm not skilled at this work but I'm functional, can follow instructions, provide an extra pair of hands. Sometimes, if there's truly nothing pressing, I go to the library and read, working slowly through its idiosyncratic collection. Or I walk the settlement, just moving through the galleries and terraces, maintaining my mental map of the place, observing the small changes—new construction, repairs completed, the seasonal shifts in the vegetation.

Evenings I often spend in the gallery, though there's no work requirement to do so. There's something about the space in the evening hours that I've come to value. The light from the bulbs takes on a different quality against the gathering darkness outside. The silence is more complete—the looms have stopped, the workshops quieted, even the water sounds seem more distant. I sit sometimes on the stool by the work table, or on the floor with my back against a shelf, and I simply exist there among the wrapped textiles, the smell of wool and dye and old wood, the coolness of the stone at my back.

I'm not meditating exactly—I've never been able to empty my mind the way the contemplative texts describe. But I'm also not quite thinking in the usual sense. My awareness diffuses into the space, into the texture of the quiet, into the sense of things being in their proper places, ordered and kept and waiting for their eventual purposes. Sometimes I think about the lives that will eventually intersect with these textiles—someone in a lowland city wrapping themselves in a blanket woven here, someone traveling in a cloak made from mountain wool, never knowing exactly where it came from, who made it, who kept it before it found its way to them. These phantom futures feel oddly intimate.

I don't have what you'd call close relationships in the settlement. There are people I'm friendly with, people I work alongside, people I enjoy encountering. But I haven't formed the deep bonds of friendship or romance that I see in others. This used to trouble me—felt like another failing, another way I was deficient. But I've come to accept that perhaps I'm simply someone who needs more solitude than most, who finds intimacy more in well-ordered spaces and careful work than in the vulnerabilities of close relationship. The settlement tolerates this in me. I contribute, I'm reliable, I show up for communal obligations. That I'm also fundamentally private, somewhat distant, seems acceptable here in ways it wasn't in the city, where such reserve was interpreted as judgment or coldness.

There are children who come to the gallery sometimes—it's not forbidden, though they're warned to be careful, not to disturb the organization. Some of them are fascinated by the textiles, want to touch them, understand how they're made. I let them, within limits. Show them the different weaves, the patterns in the tapestries, explain the purposes of different fabrics. I'm not particularly good with children—don't have the playfulness they respond to best—but they seem to find my seriousness acceptable, treat the gallery visits like being admitted to some specialized knowledge.

There's one girl, maybe nine years old, named Senna, who comes more often than the others. She's one of the mechanical-minded ones, drawn to systems and order, and she's fascinated by the inventory system—the way each piece is tracked, the logic of how things are arranged. She asks questions that suggest she's thinking about how such systems could be applied elsewhere, improved, adapted. I suspect she'll end up working with Esric eventually, will have a gift for the kind of thinking that maintains complex operations. But for now she visits the gallery, and I let her help with small tasks—labeling, carrying lighter pieces, checking items against the written records. She works with a focused silence that reminds me uncomfortably of myself at that age, before the world started demanding I be different than I was.

The settlement has given me what the city couldn't—a place where my particular way of being is functional, even valued. The work I do matters. The textiles need to be properly kept, the inventory accurately maintained, the traders supplied with what they need. That I do this work alone, in a quiet space, that I'm not particularly social or demonstrative, that I need significant time away from others—none of this prevents me from being useful, contributing to the collective function.

I've learned things about myself here that I didn't know in the city. I've learned that I have a capacity for physical endurance I never suspected—the endless stairs that defeated me at first have become simply the way you move through the world, and I climb them now without thought, carry loads that would have been impossible when I arrived. I've learned that I can tolerate cold I thought would be unbearable, that my body has resources I wasn't aware of. I've learned that I actually like the constraint of the mountain, the way it limits options and thereby simplifies choices. In the city there were infinite possibilities, which paradoxically made all of them feel meaningless. Here there are relatively few choices about how to live, and within those constraints I've found a kind of freedom.

I've learned that beauty for me is not in grand vistas or sublime experiences but in small perfections of order—a shelf of textiles properly arranged, an inventory that balances perfectly, the precise fit of a label to its piece. When I get these things right, when the system functions smoothly and everything is where it should be and I can locate any piece immediately and the records reflect the truth of what exists, I feel something that might be happiness, or at least deep satisfaction.

I don't know if I'll stay here forever. "Forever" is not a frame of time that makes sense to me—I think in terms of seasons, years, the period until the next decision point. I've stopped making plans beyond the immediate future. The mountain has taught me that much—things change, people leave or arrive, the weather shifts, what seemed permanent turns out to be contingent. But for now, and for the foreseeable now, this is where I am. In the textile gallery, among the wrapped and waiting cloth, keeping the inventory, maintaining the order, existing in the particular quality of quiet that this space provides.

On clear nights sometimes I do stand on the outer terraces like others do, looking out at the darkness and the stars. I don't have profound thoughts during these moments. Mostly I'm aware of the cold, the way the thin air makes the stars seem simultaneously closer and more distant, the sound of water somewhere in the dark. I think about the gallery waiting behind me, the textiles in their places, the work that will resume tomorrow. I think about the improbability of being here, of this place existing at all, of humans having carved out a life in this improbable location and sustained it across generations. I think about the pieces that will go down to the lowlands soon, the ones that are already gone, the ones still being woven. The long thread of making and keeping and sending forth, of which I've become a small part.

Then the cold drives me back inside, back to my small room, back to sleep, back to waking and beginning again. The rhythm continues. The mountain endures. The textiles wait in their gallery. I keep the records. It's enough. More than enough. It's the life I have, and I've learned to live it, and some days—more often now than in those first difficult years—it even feels like the life I was supposed to find, though I don't believe in fate or destiny. Just in the occasional strange fit between a person and a place, between work that needs doing and someone willing to do it, between the loneliness that comes from not fitting and the solitude that comes from finding, finally, a shape that accommodates who you actually are.

The mountain doesn't care about any of this, of course. The stone is indifferent. But the people here have made something despite that indifference, or perhaps because of it—a community, a set of practices, a way of living that works. And I, who couldn't find a place in the easier world below, have found I can function in this harder one. Can contribute something. Can be, in my own limited way, useful.

In the textile gallery, where the light is always slightly insufficient and the air smells of wool and the shelves hold the work of other hands, carefully kept against future need. That's where I am. That's who I've become. The keeper of what others make, the maintainer of the inventory, the one who knows where things are and ensures they're properly cared for until they're needed. It's not a large role, not dramatic or essential in the way the smith or the generator engineer or the maintenance crews are essential. But it's necessary. And it's mine. And that, I've learned, is enough.
