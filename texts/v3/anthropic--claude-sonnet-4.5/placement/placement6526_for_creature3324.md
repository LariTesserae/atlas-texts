# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be one of the cables.

Not the whole system—I am a single span, perhaps two hundred feet of braided steel stretching between a pylon on a ridge and another that leans slightly where the foundation has shifted over decades. I run at a gentle angle, sagging under my own weight and the weight of the moss that has colonized my surface, creating a thick sleeve of green that retains moisture and adds perhaps two hundred pounds to what I was designed to carry.

I was installed in the third phase of construction, when the system was being optimized. My strands are a slightly different gauge than the older cables, a fractionally purer alloy. The workers who strung me wore gloves that left no trace, but I remember—not consciously, but in the way metal remembers—the tension of being winched tight, the settling as I found my catenary curve, the first months of weather when my surface oxidized into its protective layer.

I connect a platform where a small processing station once operated—something to do with sorting, machinery that shook and separated, all quiet now—to a higher point where raw materials were transferred from another cable line. I was functional infrastructure, purpose-built, engineered with specific load tolerances and safety factors. But those purposes have become archaeological. What I carry now is different.

The moss arrived slowly, spore by spore, finding purchase in the microscopic roughness of my surface. It grew in my crevices first, where the individual strands of my braid come together, where moisture could collect. Over years, it spread, until now I am almost entirely sheathed in it. The moss is luminous green after rain, darker when dry, and it creates its own microecosystem—tiny insects live in it, spiders build webs between the moss clumps and my pylons, occasionally a bird lands on me and picks through the moss looking for prey.

I sag more each year. Gravity is patient and absolutely consistent. My pylons hold, but I stretch—steel does that, especially at the smaller scales, the elongation happening in the crystal structure, the individual grains of metal deforming microscopically. In another thirty years, I will be two inches longer than when I was installed, and my curve will be noticeably deeper. Eventually—perhaps in a century, perhaps two—I will fail. One of my outer strands will part, then another, the load redistributing until the cascade is irreversible. But that is far ahead, and I exist entirely in present tension.

The people interact with me regularly. The old man on his thermal route crosses me twice—once going, once returning. He walks my length with bare feet, and I feel his weight as a traveling point of pressure, his balance adjusting to my slight sway. He knows exactly where I am weakest, where I ring with different tones when struck, where the moss is thinnest. Sometimes he stops midway across, stands on one foot in a balance that seems precarious but never fails, and listens to something—perhaps my internal sounds, the tiny stress noises that I make constantly, or perhaps to something else, using me as a perch from which to hear the mountain.

The silent one who works with moss tends to my growth. She removes moss from my southern side every few months, keeping it thin enough that I dry somewhat between fogs. She harvests carefully, taking sections that she'll transplant elsewhere, and I feel the difference immediately—a slight increase in my flexibility where she's lightened me, a change in how moisture moves across my surface. She never takes from the northern side, which has developed moss so thick it hangs in small curtains. I think she understands that this asymmetry is now part of my structure, that I have adjusted to being heavier on one side, and to remove it would unbalance me in ways that might accelerate my failure.

When wind comes from the west and reaches a particular velocity—around fifteen miles per hour, with minimal gusting—I sing. The wind crosses my length at an angle that sets up vibrations in my strands, and because I am both tensioned and massed with moss, I produce a tone that is lower than a clean cable would make. It is not quite a note—more of a harmonic hum with several frequencies present simultaneously. On rare occasions, when conditions are precisely right, I resonate with other cables in the system, and we create that larger music that stops everyone. I do not know I am doing this—there is no consciousness in it—but I feel it, the way a tuning fork feels when it's struck, my entire length vibrating in response to the air moving across me.

Birds use me as a highway through the canopy. They land, rest, sometimes walk my length as they look for the insects in my moss. Their claws create tiny point stresses, negligible individually but part of the constant weather of small forces that I experience. When they defecate, the droppings add nutrients to my moss, making it grow more luxuriantly in those spots. I have become infrastructure for them as I was infrastructure for the humans—a way to move through space, to cross gaps that would otherwise require flight.

I collect water. The fog condenses on my moss and on my metal surface where it's exposed, and the water runs along my length, following my slope, dripping from my lowest point. The drip is constant enough that the ground beneath me has changed—there's a slight depression where the water lands, and different plants grow there, species that appreciate the reliable moisture. The water collection system occasionally redirects me, attaching a gutter at my low point to catch what I shed, and when this happens I feel slightly unbalanced, as though part of my purpose has been redirected. But I still drip, because I cannot help but drip.

I am cold in winter, though winter at this altitude is not dramatically different from summer. My metal conducts heat away efficiently, and my moss insulates me somewhat but also holds moisture that can freeze. On the coldest mornings, I am rigid with rime, each strand of my braid outlined in frost, my moss crisp and white. The sun melts this within hours, and I spend the day warming, my metal expanding microscopically, my sag increasing fractionally. At night I contract again. This cycle is part of my slow fatigue.

I am an anchor point for spiderwebs. The spiders appreciate that I am stable and elevated, offering good positions for web construction between me and the nearby branches. In summer mornings, webs string from my length to the trees, made visible by dew, creating a network that uses me as its hub. The webs fail constantly—torn by birds, by wind, by the people passing—and are rebuilt constantly. I am indifferent to them, but they make me part of yet another system, another layer of connection.

The children have named me. I do not know what they call me—I have no ears, no way to process language—but I know they've named me because they refer to me specifically, meeting at me, using me as a landmark in their games. They dare each other to cross me in fog, when the visibility makes the far pylon invisible and the crossing requires trust that I will hold, that I emerge eventually from the gray. They have good instincts; they sense that I am strong still, that my sag is gradual and controlled, that I will not fail suddenly. They are lighter than adults, and when they cross me I barely register their presence, just a quick traveling pressure, often running, their joy or fear creating a bouncing that sets me swaying gently for minutes afterward.

I have witnessed everything from my position. The fog arriving in banks, flowing around my pylons like slow water. The rain coming in from the west, hitting me first, running down my length in streams. The rare snow, which burdens me temporarily, building up on my moss until it becomes too heavy and slides off in small avalanches. I have watched the forest grow—trees that were saplings when I was installed are now mature, their canopies rising to nearly my height. I have watched the platform I'm anchored to deteriorate, the machinery going from functional to stopped to slowly dissolving into rust forms. I have watched the people arrive, the current inhabitants appearing over years, establishing their patterns, their routes that use me and other cables as their paths.

I experience time as duration and stress. I do not sleep. I do not think in any way that would be recognizable as thought. But I am not inert—I respond to every force applied to me, I adjust to every change in my loading, I resonate with the air and the system. I am always in conversation with gravity, always in negotiation with my own structure. Every moment, I am deciding—not consciously, but physically—how to distribute the stresses, which strand will bear more load, how to flex without breaking.

My failure will come eventually. One strand will corrode through, or fatigue to the point of fracture, and the sudden redistribution of load to the remaining strands will exceed their capacity. I will part, probably in the middle where the catenary creates the most tension. My two halves will recoil toward my pylons, and I will become something different—no longer a crossing but an ending, two dangling lengths that will be slowly absorbed by the forest, pulled down by the weight of moss and time until I lie on the ground, still connected to my pylons but no longer spanning the gap.

But that is future. Present is this: I am taut and sagging simultaneously. I am metal becoming garden. I am infrastructure becoming habitat. I am purpose that has outlived its original function and found new ones. I am strong enough to hold the people who need me, weak enough to sing in the right wind, permanent enough to be named, temporary enough to be precious.

I connect two points in space, and in doing so, I create a path, a perch, a instrument, a collector, a landmark. I am what I was made to be and also what I have become through decades of weather and neglect and moss and careful tending. I am no longer entirely industrial and not yet entirely natural. I am both, suspended between, holding the tension that defines this place.

The old man crosses me now, moving slowly in the morning light. I feel his weight, the careful placement of his feet, the way he pauses at my center point. He is listening. I am resonating very faintly, a tone below human hearing but present in my structure. He feels it through his feet, and for a moment we are connected in our purposes—both of us holding things together, both of us wearing slowly down, both of us still functional, still here, still singing when the conditions align.

I am the cable, and the cable is enough.
