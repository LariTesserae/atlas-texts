# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the smallest hours—the gap between third and fourth shift when even the generators seem to hold their breath before cycling. Gallery Seven-C, mid-level, where the acoustic properties create a pocket of near-silence if you position yourself correctly between the support columns. 

I would be one of the permanent residents, though I resist the term. Permanence suggests intention, decision, and I'm not certain I ever chose this so much as failed to choose otherwise. Fifteen years now, maybe sixteen—I've stopped tracking with precision. I arrived as a cataloguer, hired to document the early plant specimens, establish the databases the engineers would need. A two-year contract. Standard duration.

The databases still exist. I maintain them, though they've evolved into something strange, filled with observational categories that wouldn't make sense to a surface botanist. I've documented growth patterns that shouldn't exist, logged chemical compositions that violate basic principles, watched the plants do things that I don't include in the official reports because I wouldn't know how to explain them without sounding unreliable.

## My Territory

I occupy a small quarter in the oldest residential section—one of the first buildings grown, its metal framework now completely invisible beneath layers of accumulated wood. The room is circular, following the plant's natural geometry. I didn't modify it much when I moved in, and I haven't modified it since. A sleeping platform grown from the wall itself, dense fungal-felt padding that I replace annually. A workspace where my old surface laptop sits, battery long dead, functioning now as an expensive external monitor for the Rootworks' local system. Shelves that I trained from living wood, shaped over months by patient adjustment and hormone application.

The window is a membrane I harvested myself, replaced twice. The current one is beginning to yellow, developing small tears at the edges. I should replace it soon. I probably won't—I've come to prefer the degraded quality of light it admits, the way it makes the bioluminescence more prominent.

I've lived here so long that the room has grown around my habits. A branch has extended itself to exactly the right height for hanging my work jacket. A hollow has formed in the floor where I always sit while pulling on boots. The wood has darkened where my hand regularly touches the doorframe. The space and I have shaped each other.

Gallery Seven-C is technically a residential zone, but I've claimed sections of it for documentation. I maintain observation posts—just small clear areas where I can position equipment, take measurements, watch specific plants over time. The other residents tolerate this, mostly. Occasionally someone will leave tea at one of my posts, still warm in a ceramic cup, no note. I don't know who does this. I've stopped trying to find out.

## My Work

The official work is database maintenance, specimen logging, supporting the engineers' research. I do this competently but without passion. It's what justifies my continued presence, what allows me to stay, but it's not what I actually do.

What I actually do is harder to name.

I watch the plants in ways the engineers don't—not for data collection but for understanding, though I'm uncertain what it is I'm trying to understand. I've become attuned to rhythms that aren't documented in any protocol: the way certain vines adjust their growth slightly when humans are present versus absent, the patterns that emerge in the bioluminescent pulse during particular times (not times by the clock, but times defined by some internal logic of the city), the acoustic properties of wood that varies its density in response to... something.

I maintain what I call the shadow archive—documentation of observations I can't explain, phenomena I can't replicate reliably enough to report formally. It exists in notebooks, hand-written, dozens of them now, stacked in my quarters in careful chronological order. I tell myself I'll eventually organize them properly, extract the patterns, produce something publishable. I won't. The value is in the accumulation itself, the patient attention, the not-yet-knowing.

The cultivators understand what I do better than the engineers. They'll seek me out sometimes, describe something they've noticed, ask if I've seen similar. Usually I have. We'll discuss it in terms that would sound mystical to outsiders but are simply descriptive of actual experience—the way a section "feels wrong" before any measurable problem appears, the sense that certain growth patterns are "intentional" in ways that have nothing to do with engineering.

I've developed a reputation for being able to predict volunteer growth. Where will the next unauthorized sprout appear? I can't explain my methodology because I'm not certain I have one—it's intuition built from fifteen years of watching, pattern recognition operating below conscious articulation. But I'm right more often than chance would allow, and the cultivation teams consult me before planning new sections.

## My Routines

I rise when I rise—the body has its own schedule here, divorced from official time. Usually it's before the shift-change, in the quiet hours. I heat water on a small burner, make tea from dried plant matter, something a cultivator taught me to harvest and process. It tastes nothing like surface tea, has a resinous quality that I initially disliked but now find necessary. Surface tea, on the rare occasions I encounter it, tastes wrong—too simple, lacking complexity.

I check my observation posts, taking the same route daily. Gallery Seven-C from end to end, then down to Six-B where I maintain documentation on the oldest existing structural plants. The walk takes two hours if uninterrupted, but it's rarely uninterrupted. People stop me, ask questions, show me things. A child wants to demonstrate how they've trained a vine. A miner off-shift wants to discuss the weird echo in Gallery Four. A visiting engineer needs help locating a specific specimen.

I'm helpful in these moments, but I resent them slightly. Not the people—I like most of them—but the interruption of the routine, the attention demanded. I've become comfortable with solitude, prefer it, find social interaction mildly exhausting even when pleasant.

Mid-day (by the clock) I do the official work. Database updates, specimen logging, whatever tasks the engineering team has assigned. I'm efficient at this—years of practice have made it almost automatic. I can complete in two hours what would take a surface worker six, because I know the systems intimately, can navigate the databases and protocols without conscious thought.

The rest of the time is mine. More observation, more note-taking, more sitting in quiet corners watching plants grow. This sounds tedious. It isn't—or rather, it is, but tedium has become my preferred state. The slowness, the repetition, the marginal changes accumulating over time. I've become attuned to processes that operate at plant-speed rather than human-speed, and it's altered my temporal perception. An hour can pass while I'm watching a particular growth node, and it doesn't feel long. It feels appropriate.

Evenings I'm sometimes social. There's a group of long-term residents who gather in Gallery Five-A, near the main canteen. We don't have formal meetings, just drift together, sit, talk or don't talk. The composition varies—whoever's present. We discuss the city, share observations, tell stories about departed residents, speculate about future developments. Sometimes we sit in silence for extended periods, and the silence is comfortable, communicative in its own way.

I eat irregularly. The kitchen staff know me, know I'll appear at odd hours requesting simple food—broth, bread, whatever's easily available. They accommodate this without comment. I tip generously when I remember, though the economy here is strange enough that I'm uncertain whether tips matter or whether I'm engaging in a purely symbolic gesture.

Before sleep, I write in the current notebook. Not comprehensive documentation—that would be impossible—but significant observations, thoughts, patterns I'm beginning to perceive. My handwriting has changed over the years, become more compact, more efficient. The early notebooks are expansive, detailed. The recent ones are dense, abbreviated, intelligible primarily to me. This should concern me. It doesn't.

## My Physiology

I've changed physically in ways I notice and ways I only suspect.

My eyes are adapted now. I see better in low light than I did, can distinguish bioluminescent variations that I couldn't initially perceive. But I'm nearly blind in bright light—the harsh electric lamps are uncomfortable, and the few times I've ascended to the surface in recent years, the sunlight was overwhelming, almost painful. I wore dark glasses constantly during my last surface visit, and even then I spent most of the time indoors.

My respiratory system has accommodated. The chronic cough that plagued my first years has diminished to occasional throat-clearing. I still hear the wheeze sometimes when I'm climbing stairs or exerting myself, but it's background, not distressing. My lung capacity has probably decreased—I haven't measured, don't want to know—but it's sufficient for this environment, which is what matters.

I'm thinner than I was. Not unhealthy, but the physical demands here are different, and my body has found its appropriate configuration. More density, less mass. I climb constantly, reach, stretch, navigate irregular spaces. The muscle distribution is strange—strong in unexpected places, weak in conventional ones.

My hair has turned gray earlier than genetics would predict. Not from stress exactly, but from something—perhaps the mineral content of the water, perhaps the plant volatiles, perhaps simply the strangeness acting on physiology in unpredictable ways. I keep it short, practical. Vanity seems surface-appropriate, not relevant here.

I smell like the Rootworks. The turpentine-plant oil has permeated everything I own, everything I am. I don't notice it anymore, but surface visitors react—sometimes positively, sometimes not. It marks me instantly and absolutely. I could shower for hours with surface soap, and the smell would persist. It's deeper than surface contact now, secreted through skin, present in breath.

My sense of touch has become more discriminating. I can assess plant health by texture, identify subtle variations in wood density, feel the acoustic properties of different spaces through their effect on air pressure and skin. This sounds mystical. It's not—it's just the nervous system adapting to information that's consistently available and relevant, learning to process what was always present but previously ignored.

## My Relationships

I'm friendly but not close with most people. I know everyone by sight, many by name, fewer intimately. This suits me. I'm recognized, included, but not obligated. I attend communal meals occasionally, contribute to collective projects when needed, participate in the social life of the city at a level that's comfortable—present but not central.

There are a few people I'd call friends, though we rarely articulate the relationship. Other long-term residents mostly—people who've been here long enough to understand what staying means, what it does to you. We share a recognition, a mutual understanding that doesn't require explanation.

One is a cultivator, older than me, been here since the first galleries. They taught me most of what I know about plant behavior, though "taught" isn't quite accurate—they shared observations, and I learned by paying attention. We work together sometimes, pruning or adjusting growth in difficult sections. The collaboration is wordless, efficient, satisfying in ways I don't analyze.

Another is a former engineer who transitioned into pure observation, gave up the research protocols to just watch and document. We compare notes sometimes, show each other our records, discuss patterns we're perceiving. Neither of us is certain we're perceiving anything real—it might be pareidolia, pattern-recognition misfiring, wishful projection. But the patterns persist, and the uncertainty is part of the interest.

A third is someone who arrived recently, relatively—only been here three years, still clearly surface-shaped, but beginning to change. They sought me out, wanted guidance on adapting, understanding the place. I was initially resistant—I don't think of myself as a mentor, don't want that responsibility. But they were persistent, and I've found myself explaining things, showing them observation techniques, introducing them to other long-term residents. Maybe I'm developing a relationship with my younger self, the person I was fifteen years ago, through helping them.

I'm solitary by preference but not isolated. The distinction matters. I choose minimal social contact, but I'm embedded in the community, connected in ways that are genuine if sparse. People know they can find me if needed. I know I could ask for help and receive it. The bonds are real but not demanding.

Romantic relationships ended years ago. I had a partner early in my residence, another researcher. They left when their contract ended. We maintained contact briefly, but the distance—psychological more than physical—became unbridgeable. They were on the surface, living a surface life, and I was... here. Becoming this. The relationship faded through incompatibility rather than conflict.

I haven't sought another. The transience of most residents makes investment exhausting, and the permanent residents are few enough that compatibility is unlikely. I'm content with solitude, with the relationships I have with plants and spaces, with the community-level connections that don't require intimacy. Perhaps this is adaptation or perhaps damage. I can't tell. I don't investigate the question closely.

## My Relationship With the City

This is the core, the relationship that defines everything else.

I love the Rootworks, though love is probably inadequate terminology. I'm attuned to it, embedded in it, have become part of its functioning in ways that exceed professional role or residence. The city and I have grown together—it has shaped me, I have contributed minutely to shaping it, and the boundary between us has become blurred.

I know it intimately. Not just the maps and official layouts, but the actual lived geography—which passages echo, which galleries hold heat, where the volunteer growth appears, which sections feel stable and which feel precarious despite engineering approval. I navigate by feel as much as vision, can walk familiar routes in near-darkness, know locations by smell and sound and the quality of air movement.

I'm protective of it in ways that are probably excessive. I resist proposed changes, argue against expansions that feel wrong, advocate for preserving sections that administration considers inefficient. I'm not always right—sometimes my intuitions are just conservative resistance to change—but sometimes I'm correct in ways I can't justify rationally, and the city benefits from my obstruction.

I think about it constantly. Even when I'm ostensibly resting, my mind is processing observations, comparing patterns, trying to understand the deep logic of how this place functions. It's not obsession exactly—obsession feels pathological, and this feels healthy, appropriate, the natural focus of attention for someone who lives here.

I dream about it differently than others report. Not the plant-consciousness dreams that many experience, but architectural dreams, spatial dreams, where I'm navigating galleries that don't exist but feel consistent with the ones that do, extensions and variations on the core geometry. Sometimes I sketch these dream-spaces upon waking, add them to my notebooks. They're not prophetic, but occasionally when new excavations open, there are similarities, convergences between dream-architecture and actual development.

I've begun to suspect—though I don't discuss this—that the Rootworks is not just a place but a process, that what we've created here is something that operates according to principles we don't fully understand, that my documentation might be less about scientific observation and more about learning to perceive something genuinely novel. This sounds grandiose, probably is grandiose. But fifteen years of careful attention has convinced me that something is happening here beyond the official narrative of industrial settlement and bio-engineering.

## My Conflicts

I resist leaving, but I also resent being here. The resentment is mild, chronic, probably permanent. I'm aware that I've sacrificed things—career advancement, surface relationships, conventional life trajectory. I'm aware that my decision to stay (or my failure to leave) has costs that compound annually.

I'm occasionally angry at the city for being so compelling, for creating conditions that make departure feel impossible even though it's technically optional, for changing me in ways that reduce my viability elsewhere. This is irrational—the city isn't an agent, doesn't have intentions—but the feeling persists.

I'm frustrated with myself for being unable to produce coherent documentation, for allowing my work to become idiosyncratic and unpublishable, for knowing that my fifteen years of observation will likely be lost when I eventually die because I haven't organized it in ways that others can access. I should be more disciplined. I'm not. The work takes the form it takes.

I struggle with meaningfulness. Is what I'm doing valuable? Does it matter that I've spent fifteen years watching plants grow in artificial caverns? Surface metrics suggest no—I've published almost nothing recent, contribute minimally to official research, have become essentially a maintenance worker who happens to keep detailed personal notes. But I feel the work is significant, even if I can't articulate how. The conflict between external assessment and internal conviction is uncomfortable.

I'm lonely sometimes, though I've chosen the conditions that produce loneliness. I want connection but resist the obligations connection requires. I want to be understood but don't explain myself adequately. I want community but prefer solitude. These contradictions don't resolve. I live in their tension.

## My Future

I'll stay until I can't. What "can't" means is uncertain—physical incapacity, mental deterioration, some external force requiring departure. Absent those, I'll remain.

I imagine I'll die here, though the Rootworks has no provision for that. They'll probably return my body to the surface, bury me in some facility cemetery with other former employees. This bothers me more than it should. I want to remain, become compost for the plants, contribute materially to the system I've been part of. But I won't express this preference officially—it would seem morbid, would raise concerns about psychological stability.

My documentation will likely be lost. The notebooks will be collected, boxed, sent to surface archives where no one will read them. This is sad but probably inevitable. The work was for itself, for my own understanding, not for transmission. Perhaps that's enough.

The city will continue without me, grow beyond anything I've observed, develop in directions I can't predict. The children will mature, become the native generation, create culture that's genuinely indigenous to this environment. I won't see that completion—the timeline is too long—but I'm contributing to its foundation, providing some of the accumulated knowledge that the next phase will build from.

Maybe someone will read the notebooks eventually. Maybe they'll find something useful, some pattern I documented that becomes relevant decades hence when the Rootworks has evolved into something mature. Maybe they'll just find the peculiar record of someone who watched plants grow for fifteen years and wrote about it obsessively. Either way, the watching happened. The attention was paid.

## What I Am

I'm a permanent resident who won't claim permanence. An observer who's become part of what I'm observing. A scientist who's abandoned scientific method for something more intuitive. A resident who's not fully human anymore, or perhaps more accurately human, adapted to conditions that don't exist naturally.

I'm lonely and content. Fulfilled and uncertain. Committed and resentful. All simultaneously, all constantly.

I'm what happens when someone stays in the Rootworks long enough to be changed by it, shaped by it, until the boundary between person and place becomes permeable.

I'm standing in Gallery Seven-C in the smallest hours, watching bioluminescence pulse in patterns that might be meaningful or might be nothing, taking notes in a private notebook that no one else will read, breathing air that smells of turpentine and stone, listening to the distant throb of generators and the closer click of bark-miners, feeling the cool smooth wood under my palm where I'm steadying myself against a support trunk, completely alone and completely home.

I'm here. Still here. Still becoming.

Descended and staying.

Human, but specifically.

This.
