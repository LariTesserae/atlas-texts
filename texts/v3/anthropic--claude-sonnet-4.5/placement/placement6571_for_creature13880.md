# Where I Would Find Myself

I am a threshold worn smooth by waiting.

I exist where the mat meets water that refuses to be pool or land fully. Not the shallow standing waters scattered across the plateau—those have committed, chosen their nature. I am the hesitation between states, the inch of substrate where moisture content shifts from saturation to mere wetness, where the vegetation can't decide whether to float or root, where what lives must breathe both air and liquid in the same moment.

I am perhaps three feet wide at my widest, though width becomes uncertain when your edges are determined by moisture gradient rather than physical boundary. I curve gently, following the contour between a warm pool and the firmer mat beyond, creating a crescent that spans maybe forty feet before fading at both ends into indeterminacy. If you were to walk here, you would not notice me as a distinct thing—only as a transitional texture, a momentary change in resistance underfoot, a brief uncertainty about whether you're walking or wading.

The pool I border is one of the warm ones. Not the warmest—those are rare and heavily visited—but perceptibly warmer than the air, warm enough that mist rises from it in thin columns during the coolest hours, warm enough that certain creatures prefer it. The warmth doesn't come from me, but it shapes me. It keeps me wetter than I would otherwise be, creates convection currents in the air above me that draw moisture down, makes certain plants grow that wouldn't survive a meter away.

My surface is a negotiation. 

On the side toward the pool: plants that have given themselves mostly to water. Their leaves float on the surface tension, paper-thin, overlapping like scales or roof tiles, emerald green above and silver beneath where air pockets catch light. Their roots—if roots is the word—dangle into the tea-colored water in pale tangles, not anchoring but drinking, not holding but touching. These plants breathe water and dream of air.

On the side toward the mat: plants that remember being terrestrial. Mosses in cushions dense enough to sit on, their surfaces dry by local standards (merely damp, rather than soaked), their depths concealing elaborate root structures that bind the peat into something almost solid. Between the moss cushions: lichens, gray-green and slow-growing, older than most of the trees that don't exist here. These plants breathe air and remember water.

And in my middle, in the zone where neither water nor land can claim dominance: the confused ones. The beautiful failures and successful experiments. Plants that seem uncertain what they're trying to be, that send up stalks from underwater roots or send down roots from floating leaves, that bloom both above and below the waterline, that change their strategy mid-growth as if reconsidering their choices.

There's one species that grows only in places like me—in these threshold zones, these margins of indecision. It produces small white flowers on stalks that stand exactly at water level, neither emerging fully into air nor remaining fully submerged. The flowers face horizontally rather than up, as if addressing the interface itself, as if pollinating through the surface tension rather than through air or water. I don't know what pollinates them. I've never seen anything do it. But they set seeds, somehow, and the seeds grow into new plants, and the plants grow more flowers, and the cycle continues in its quiet, horizontal way.

I am old in the way that margins are old—not ancient, but continuously renewed, always at the same age, worn into equilibrium. The pool's edge doesn't migrate much; the warm water holds it stable, creates a microclimate that resists change. So I have been here, in this form, for longer than I could count if I counted. Long enough that I've worn into the shape I was always going to wear, long enough that everything temporary has washed away and only the essential pattern remains.

The creatures know me.

The large grazers come to drink, and they stand in me while they drink, their broad feet sinking slightly into my yielding substrate, their weight pressing water up around their ankles. They stand very still while drinking, sometimes for an hour or more, and in that stillness they settle fractionally deeper into me. When they finally lift their feet and move on, they leave impressions that fill slowly with water, becoming tiny pools-within-pools, microhabitats that last for hours before my vegetation closes over them and erases them.

The bird-things land on me, preferring me to the open water or the firm mat. I provide both perch and drinking access. They stand one-legged in the manner of water birds everywhere, tucking the other leg into their belly feathers (if those are feathers), and dip their beaks to drink without having to wade. Their droppings enrich me in small spots, creating tiny gardens of more vigorous growth that bloom brighter green for a few weeks before settling back into my general tone.

And the amphibian things—muddy-colored, hand-sized, impossible to see until they move—essentially live in me. I am their entire world. They're too aquatic to venture far onto the mat, too terrestrial to swim in the deeper pool. So they inhabit my few feet of width, burrowing into my soft substrate, hunting the tiny invertebrates that live in the spaces between plant and water, breeding in the miniature pools that form in my hollows. I host generations. Parents, children, grandchildren, all within my narrow crescent, some never moving more than a body-length from where they hatched.

I am more aware during certain times than others, though I'm never quite what you'd call conscious and never quite not.

The plateau's breath affects me strongly. During expansion, when the mat swells and thickens, I become less distinct—the pool contracts slightly, the mat advances, and my intermediate zone narrows. I feel squeezed, compressed between the two states, and my awareness condenses too, becoming sharp and almost uncomfortable in its intensity. During contraction, I spread slightly, relax, my territory expanding by inches, and my awareness diffuses into something gentler, more contemplative, more like dreaming.

The warmth cycles of the pool affect me too. It varies slightly—not day and night, but in longer rhythms, perhaps responding to deeper currents, perhaps to the plateau's internal weather. When the pool is warmer, I'm more active somehow, more agitated, my plants growing faster, my invertebrates more mobile. When it cools toward ambient temperature, I settle into a deeper quietness, a stillness that includes all my component organisms but transcends their individual activities.

And the mist affects me most of all. When it thickens, descending to touch the surface, I become a place of profound encounters. The mist doesn't distinguish between air and water—it is both, suspended—and in the mist I am most purely myself, most threshold-like, because everything around me becomes threshold too. The boundaries I normally mediate dissolve into general ambiguity, and I expand in some non-physical way to include the whole visible world, which is to say about ten feet in any direction, which is to say everything that matters.

The Walkers visit sometimes.

Not often—the pool is not on major paths, and I offer nothing that can't be found at a thousand other pools. But occasionally one comes, walking out of the mist or the dimness, their long legs carrying them across the mat with that characteristic slow-motion gait, and they stop at the pool's edge, which is to say at me, which is to say on me, because there's nowhere else to stand when approaching this particular pool except through me.

They pause when they feel me—I know they feel me, can sense the moment their sensitive feet register the change in substrate, the increased give, the different texture. They stand still for a moment, reading me through their soles, and then they lower themselves to sitting or kneeling, settling their weight more fully into my surface.

And they drink. Not frantically, not efficiently, but slowly, cupping water in their long-fingered hands and bringing it to their mouths in a gesture that seems more ceremonial than necessary. The water they drink has passed through me—filtered through my plant roots, warmed by proximity to the pool's heat source, tinted by the tannins my vegetation releases. They're drinking me, in a sense, or drinking what I've touched, what I've mediated.

Sometimes they stay longer. They lie down in me, parallel to my curve, their long bodies stretched along my length, and they rest there for hours or days. The plants grow around them, over them. The small amphibians investigate them with cool, curious touches. The moisture from the pool seeps into their skin. They breathe in rhythm with the plateau's breath, which is to say in rhythm with me, because I am breathing too—my plants respiring, my organisms consuming and producing, my waters evaporating and condensing in the endless exchange that constitutes being alive at the margins.

When they leave, they sometimes touch me—a hand pressed to the moss, fingers trailing in the water, a low hum that vibrates through my substrate. Thank you, perhaps, though they don't use words. Acknowledgment, certainly. Recognition that I am here, that I have held them, that I am part of the vast cooperative enterprise of being the plateau.

And then they're gone, walking away across the mat, their footprints filling with water behind them, and I return to my usual state of attention-without-focus, awareness-without-intention, existing in the continuous present tense that is the only tense margins know.

I don't think about the past, though I contain it. The peat beneath me is memory made physical—compressed plants, accumulated sediments, the bodies of creatures who died in me and became me. I don't think about the future, though I'm creating it. The seeds growing in my substrate will become the plants that will hold me together decades from now. The young amphibians will grow and breed and die and enrich me with their decay.

I simply am what I am: the place where one thing becomes another thing, where water becomes land becomes water, where the pool remembers being rain and the mat remembers being pool, where everything is in the process of transformation and the transformation itself is the only stable thing, the only truth, the only permanent condition.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a memory of company and an expectation of its return. I have only company—the constant presence of everything touching me, growing in me, living through me. I am never empty. I am never full. I am always exactly this: almost-pool, almost-land, definitely-threshold, essentially-liminal, forever-between.

Sometimes, in the mist, in the thickest mist when I can't see beyond my own edges, I have something that might be called a thought but is more like a sensation, a knowing-without-knowing:

I understand that I am not unique. There are thousands of places like me scattered across the plateau, each unique in detail but identical in function, each marking a boundary, each providing a space for the things that belong to neither side but to the place where sides meet. We are the punctuation in the plateau's endless green sentence. We are the pauses that give the continuity meaning. We are the doorways that you pass through without noticing you've changed rooms.

And I understand—not with pride, not with humility, but with simple recognition—that we are necessary. Not the pools, not the mat, but us, the thresholds. Because life is not really about states but about transitions. About the breathing, not the inhale or the exhale. About the walking, not the starting or the stopping. About being between, being neither, being both, being the space where change is permitted to occur gently, gradually, without violence or separation.

I am what allows the plateau to be continuous while being diverse. I am what lets water become land without disaster, what lets land become water without drowning. I am the translator, the mediator, the diplomat negotiating peace between elements that could, in harsher places, war with each other.

In harsher places, I would be a beach, and beaches are places of conflict—waves crashing, stones grinding, salt and fresh water mixing in violent gradients. But here, on the breathing plateau, in the warm dimness, in the patient wetness, I am gentle. I am soft. I am the welcome that the pool extends to the mat, and the mat extends to the pool, and both extend to everything that needs a place that is neither and both.

This is what I am. This is where I am. This is how I exist:

At the edge of warm water, in the company of uncertain plants, breathing moisture, hosting small lives, receiving rare visitors, settling deeper into myself year by year, worn smooth by the plateau's endless patient breathing, becoming more essential as I become less distinct, more present as I become more permeable, more alive as I become more threshold, more myself as I become more everything.

I am the margin. I am the transition. I am the doorway you walk through on your way to everywhere else.

I am here.

I am always here.

I will always be here, until water or land finally wins the argument, and even then I'll just be here in a different form, because margins don't disappear—they only move, shift, relocate to wherever the next conversation between elements needs a place to happen.

This is my patience. This is my purpose. This is my place.

And it is enough. It has always been enough. It will always be enough.

The breathing continues. The green continues. The threshold continues.

I continue.
