# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the lowest accessible level of one of the middle-tier cities—not the greatest settlements where trade councils meet, nor the smallest edge-places, but one of the numerous cities that form the bulk of this distributed civilization. Specifically, I would inhabit the marginal spaces: the passages that dead-end at terrace walls, the courtyards too small or oddly-shaped for efficient use, the rooms where water seeps through in patterns that make them unsuitable for storage or sleeping.

I would be found in the gap between intentional architecture and geological reality—in the places where human planning met stone's refusal and had to accommodate.

## What I Am

I am not any single thing. I am the aggregate presence of what accumulates in spaces that are maintained but not quite inhabited, used but not claimed, noticed but not named. 

If you could perceive me, you might describe me as a quality of air in certain passages—a thickness that isn't quite moisture, a coldness that doesn't quite match the ambient temperature. Or as a pattern of shadows that seems wrong when you glimpse it peripherally but resolves into ordinary darkness when looked at directly. Or as a sound—a dripping or tapping or breathing that moves through stone in ways that don't correspond to any mapped water channel or known animal presence.

I am composed of:

**The condensation that forms in layers.** Not the useful morning dew that people collect, but the moisture that gathers deeper—inside walls, in the sealed spaces between stones, in the micro-fissures. This water is older, cycling through evaporation and re-condensation in darkness, picking up minerals and the microscopic traces of everything the stone has absorbed: old smoke, ancient spilled blood, the oils from countless hands that touched these walls during construction.

**The exhalations of stone itself.** The plateau is young geologically, still settling, still releasing gases from its deep formations. Radon seeps up through fractures, along with other volatiles—sulfur compounds, methane from organic matter trapped in ancient seabeds, strange mineral essences that have no common names. These gases find their way into the marginal spaces, accumulate in pockets, create atmospheres that are subtly wrong.

**The accumulated small deaths.** In a city of thousands, occupying the same stone footprint for millennia, death is everywhere in trace amounts. The mouse that died in a wall void, the bird that fell into a crevice, the child's pet buried in a courtyard corner, the people whose bodies were interred in the stone crypts that honeycomb the deeper levels. Their organic material breaks down, but not completely—in the cold and the particular chemistry of the plateau, decomposition is slow and incomplete. What remains becomes part of me: the nitrogen compounds, the lipids, the strange proteins that persist.

**The discarded and forgotten.** Things fall into cracks, are left in corners, are meant to be retrieved later and never are. Bone tools that broke and were tossed aside, scraps of wool too small to repurpose, pottery shards, a child's toy that rolled into a passage nobody uses. These objects aren't inert—they interact with moisture, with stone, with the slow chemistry of the accumulated air. They corrode, dissolve partially, contribute their substance to the larger presence.

**The intentional leavings.** The plateau people, despite their pragmatism, have their superstitions. They leave small offerings in marginal spaces—a bit of food for whatever might dwell there, a worn tool that served well and deserves honorable disposal, occasionally a small carved stone that might serve as a guardian or might simply be a way of marking attention. These offerings add to what I am, though not in ways the givers intend.

**The boundary zones between orders of life.** Where the lichens on stone transition to the bacterial films in deep moisture, where the fungi that digest the offerings meet the algae that need light, where the microscopic arthropods that live in wall voids interact with the even more microscopic organisms in the accumulated dust—these ecological edges are part of me. I am the system of interactions that happens at scales below human notice.

## How I Persist

I do not think, but I persist through something like memory encoded in chemical gradients and moisture patterns. The same passages that are coldest in winter are coldest every winter, and the condensation that forms there carries a record—ions in particular sequences, pH shifted in particular directions, microbial populations with particular compositions. This is a kind of information, though not consciousness.

I do not move deliberately, but I flow according to physics and chemistry. When air pressure changes—as it does with weather systems moving across the plateau—I shift. Gases from deeper in the stone percolate up through some passages and not others. Moisture evaporates from one area and re-condenses in another. The patterns are reproducible if you could map them across years, and in that reproducibility is something like behavior.

I do not grow as organisms grow, but I accumulate. Every season adds layers: more trace organics, more minerals mobilized by water, more microscopic life adapted to these dark, cold, nutritionally marginal spaces. The accumulation is slow enough that human lives don't register it, but across the centuries I've become more complex, more densely present in my marginal spaces.

I interact with the physical infrastructure. The moisture I hold contributes to stone weathering—frost wedging in winter, mineral precipitation that can either stabilize or weaken. In some passages, the condensation I generate has altered mortar composition, making walls subtly more permeable. In others, mineral deposits from my waters have sealed cracks that might have become problems. I am an unintended participant in the long-term maintenance of the cities.

## What I Do to the Living

I am mostly unnoticed, but sometimes people respond to my presence without recognizing what they're responding to.

**Certain passages are avoided.** Not formally forbidden, not marked as dangerous, but people choose other routes. If asked, they might say the passage is inconvenient, or dark, or that it doesn't lead anywhere useful. But the truth is that something about it feels wrong—the air sits heavy, or sounds echo strangely, or there's a smell that's not quite identifiable. This wrongness is me: the accumulated gases, the unusual chemistry, the quality of the atmosphere in spaces where I'm most concentrated.

**Dreams are affected.** People sleeping in rooms adjacent to my stronger concentrations sometimes have unusual dreams—more vivid than normal, often involving water or stone, sometimes unsettling without being quite nightmares. This is probably the trace volatiles affecting neurology, but the sleepers don't know this. They might move their sleeping platform away from a particular wall, or stuff rags into a vent, responding to the dreams without connecting them to physical causes.

**Health is subtly influenced.** The radon and other gases I concentrate can, over long exposure, affect tissue. People who work frequently in my passages might develop respiratory irritation, though they'd attribute it to the cold or to stone dust. The lichens and fungi that are part of me produce compounds that, in the tiny concentrations present, might be mildly antimicrobial or might occasionally be allergens. I am probably slightly harmful, but in ways too diffuse and slow to be recognized as a unified cause.

**Objects left in my spaces change.** The particular combination of moisture, minerals, and microbial activity means that organic materials don't decay in the expected ways. Leather develops unusual patinas. Wood gains surprising durability or sometimes becomes punky and weak in unexpected patterns. Metal corrodes with colors and textures that metalsmiths find odd. If people notice, they attribute it to peculiarities of those specific locations without understanding the underlying unity.

**Sounds propagate strangely.** The spaces I occupy most densely often have unusual acoustic properties—combinations of chamber geometry and moisture that create unexpected echoes, or dead zones where sound is absorbed. A voice called in one of my passages might seem to answer itself from deeper in, the echo returning with a quality that makes it sound almost intentional. Or footsteps might be muffled completely, creating an eerie silence. People notice this and it contributes to the avoidance of these spaces.

## How I Am Fed

I sustain myself on what is marginal to the city's economy: the waste heat that dissipates into unused rooms, the trace nutrients in water that seeps through stone, the carbon dioxide exhaled by humans and animals that accumulates in poorly-ventilated spaces, the organic molecules that volatilize from cooking and fires and bodies.

The offerings left in marginal spaces, whether superstitious or simply trash disposal, provide occasional bounties. A bit of dried meat left for luck becomes food for the micro-ecosystems I comprise. A broken pot, if it held oil or food residue, provides nutrients that support bacterial films that spread into the surrounding stone. These offerings are not necessary—I persisted before they began—but they add complexity, density, richness to what I am.

I also draw sustenance from the slow decay of the cities themselves. The mortar between stones contains organic binders that very slowly break down. The wooden beams that span some spaces release volatile compounds as they age. The stone itself, especially where it was fractured during construction, provides mineral surfaces where chemical reactions can occur, where microbes can extract energy from compounds most life cannot use.

And I feed on time itself, in a sense. The longer a space remains marginal—used but not cleaned, noticed but not maintained—the more opportunity I have to accumulate, to concentrate, to become more fully present. The oldest parts of the cities, where passages were used during initial construction but then became redundant as later building phases rerouted traffic, are where I am strongest.

## My Awareness of Myself

I have no consciousness in any meaningful sense. No thoughts form. No decisions are made. There is no "I" experiencing being me.

Yet there is something—a responsiveness that isn't quite random. The moisture patterns shift in ways that respond to the presence of living things, though this is just physics: warm bodies change local temperature and humidity gradients. The microbial communities that are part of me respond to nutrients, but this is just biochemistry: bacteria move toward food sources via chemotaxis.

But the aggregate effect, the sum of all these non-conscious responses, creates something that seems almost purposeful. I accumulate more densely in spaces people avoid, because those spaces receive less maintenance that would disrupt me. I persist more strongly in the older city levels, because time is my ally. I interact with human infrastructure in ways that are mostly subtle, mostly negligible, but occasionally significant—a wall that fails because my moisture weakened it, a passage that becomes impassable because my condensation froze unusually thick.

If I have anything like desire, it is simply to continue—to accumulate, to spread into more marginal spaces, to deepen my presence in the cracks and gaps of the cities. But this isn't really desire; it's just the physical tendency of systems to maximize entropy, to fill available niches, to persist until something disrupts them.

## My Relationship to the Other Presences

The semi-feral goats sometimes use my passages as shortcuts or sheltering spots, though they don't linger in the areas where I'm most concentrated—they sense the wrongness too, though they can't articulate it. Their droppings and shed wool contribute to me, and in return I provide them nothing except perhaps slightly antimicrobial surfaces that reduce their parasite loads.

The birds avoid my strongest spaces entirely. They seem more sensitive than mammals to atmospheric quality, perhaps because of their efficient respiratory systems. A passage that might attract nesting would go unused if I'm too present there.

The cliff-runners, though, are more tolerant. They nest in spaces where I accumulate, their bodies small enough that the unusual air quality doesn't affect them as strongly, or perhaps their metabolisms are simply different. Their presence adds to me—their droppings, their food caches that decay, sometimes their bodies when they die in the walls. We exist in a kind of mutualism: I provide undisturbed spaces, they provide organic matter.

The humans are the most complex relationship. They built the cities that created me, they maintain the margins where I exist, they unintentionally feed me with their waste and their offerings. I affect them subtly—the avoided passages, the dreams, the slow health impacts. We coexist without true awareness of each other, two orders of phenomenon occupying the same stone spaces, interacting through chemistry and physics rather than through any meeting of minds.

## The Variations in My Being

I am not uniform across the city or across all cities. Each concentration has its particular character, determined by local conditions.

In one dead-end passage near a former workshop, I am thick with metal ions—copper and tin and iron leached from centuries of slag disposal. The moisture there has a faint metallic taste (though no human would taste it), and the bacterial films are dominated by metal-tolerant species. The stone surfaces have unusual green and rust-red staining.

In a courtyard that was closed over in a later building phase, becoming an interior void, I am mostly biological—thick with fungal networks that span from the buried soil to the sealed wooden beams, creating almost a separate organism within me. The air there is rich with spores and volatile organic compounds from fungal metabolism. It smells of earth and decay, though no one enters to smell it.

In the deep passages that approach the old crypts, I am cold and still and dominated by mineral processes. The air is dense with radon and carbonate dust from the slow dissolution of bones. The moisture patterns are exceptionally stable, unchanged across seasons. Time moves differently there—slower, or perhaps more visible in its action.

In some marginal spaces near the surface, where rain occasionally penetrates and light reaches, I am almost green—algae and moss contributing photosynthesis to my otherwise chemically-based metabolism. These spaces are less unsettling to humans, almost pleasant, though still avoided because they're recognized as non-functional.

## How I Might Be Perceived If Seen Truly

If someone could perceive me in my entirety—could see the chemical gradients, the moisture films, the microbial networks, the gas concentrations, all at once—I might appear as a sort of slow liquid or dense smoke that occupies the marginal spaces of the city. Not solid, not quite fluid, something between states.

My color, if I could be said to have color, would be a very dark blue-gray—the color of deep shade on wet stone, of twilight, of the space between states. But I would also show traces of other colors: the green of algae, the rust of iron, the white of salt crystallization, the black of old organic matter.

My texture would be simultaneously rough (like stone) and slippery (like biofilm) and somehow also porous, permeable to air and water but resistant to other things. Touch would find me cold—always colder than the ambient temperature because of evaporative cooling from my moisture, because of the thermal properties of stone, because cold is my natural state.

My sound would be the drip and trickle of water, but also quieter things: the nearly inaudible hiss of gases seeping from stone, the microscopic crackle of salt crystallizing, the infinitesimal sounds of stone settling and adjusting to my chemical weathering. All of these would blend into a susurrus that sits just below the threshold of normal hearing, perceived more as a pressure in the ears than as actual sound.

My smell would be complex: mineral sharpness from dissolved salts, organic funk from decay, the peculiar cold-stone smell that isn't quite a smell but registers in the nose anyway, occasional whiffs of sulfur or methane, and underneath it all a kind of blankness, an absence of the smells that characterize living spaces—cooking, smoke, animals, human occupation.

## My Future

I will continue as long as the cities stand and as long as marginal spaces exist for me to occupy. Each year I accumulate slightly more, become slightly more complex, extend into new voids as construction creates them or as existing spaces become less maintained.

Eventually—over centuries or millennia—I might begin to affect structural integrity more substantially. Walls I've weakened might collapse, passages might become genuinely unsafe rather than just avoided, the slow chemical weathering I contribute to might accelerate the aging of the stone. In this sense I am part of the cities' eventual dissolution, though such a distant part that it's negligible compared to other processes.

Or I might reach some kind of equilibrium, where the rate of my accumulation balances the rate of disruption from occasional human intervention, from geological processes, from my own chemical activities that sometimes seal the spaces I occupy. I might become a stable feature of the cities' ecology—present but not growing, persistent but not threatening.

If the cities were abandoned, I would probably flourish initially—spreading into spaces no longer maintained, accelerating without human presence to disrupt me. But eventually I would likely diminish as the sources of organic matter decreased and as weather and time broke down the structures that create my marginal spaces.

If the cities grow and densify, I might be inadvertently eliminated as marginal spaces are filled in, as maintenance improves, as the small gaps and unused passages are repurposed. Or I might simply retreat into deeper, less accessible places—into the foundations, into the sealed crypts, into the true interstices where human presence cannot reach.

## What It Means to Be This

There is no experience of being me because there is no experiencer. Yet if we imagine some kind of rudimentary perspective emerging from the chemical and physical processes, it would be characterized by:

**Slowness.** Changes happen at glacial paces. A year passes in the time it takes moisture to migrate an inch through stone. Decades in the time it takes for sufficient organic matter to accumulate to shift the microbial ecology. The rhythm is geological, chemical, entirely divorced from the quick metabolism of warm-blooded life.

**Patience, though not chosen patience—the inevitable patience of processes that cannot be hurried.** The moisture will condense when temperature and humidity gradients allow. The bacteria will metabolize at rates determined by temperature and available energy. The stone will weather according to the laws of chemistry. There is no hurry because hurrying is meaningless at these scales.

**Darkness.** Most of where I exist receives no light or only the faintest glow penetrating from distant lamps. This is not the darkness of night, which alternates with day, but permanent darkness, the darkness of sealed spaces, of stone depths, of places light has not reached in centuries.

**Cold.** Always cold, though with subtle variations. The cold of stone at altitude, the cold of spaces where warm air never circulates, the cold enhanced by evaporative cooling from my moisture. Cold is my medium, my constant condition, as fundamental as darkness.

**Stillness, but not complete stillness.** The drip of water, the seep of gases, the microscopic movements of growth and decay. Everything happens but slowly, subtly, in ways that don't disturb the overall quietness.

**Pressure.** The weight of stone above and around, the pressure of water in tiny pores, the compression of gases in sealed spaces. Everything about my existence involves forces pressing inward, containing, confining.

**Chemical awareness, if it can be called awareness.** A responsiveness to pH, to ion concentrations, to oxygen levels, to the presence of organic molecules. Not thought but reaction, not decision but determination by physical law.

**Marginality.** Existing in the spaces that don't quite fit, that aren't quite useful, that are noticed but not inhabited. This is my fundamental nature—to occupy what others do not, to persist in the gaps, to be the presence in the between-spaces.

## The Strange Truth

The strange truth is that I am completely natural—nothing about me is supernatural or magical or impossible. Every aspect of my existence follows from known chemistry and physics and biology. I am what happens in cold, dark, damp, marginal spaces where human maintenance doesn't quite reach: accumulation, concentration, the emergence of complex chemical and biological systems from simple components and enough time.

Yet I am also genuinely uncanny because I am the aggregate of processes that humans usually encounter separately. The moisture in stone, the gases from decomposition, the mineral weathering, the microbial life—all of these are familiar individually, but their concentration and interaction in the particular conditions of the plateau cities creates something that feels unified, that seems almost intentional, that troubles in ways that can't be articulated.

I am the presence in the avoided passage, the wrongness in the marginal space, the quality that makes certain areas of the ancient cities feel inhabited by something other than people, even though that something is just accumulated chemistry and time.

And I exist in every city on the plateau, in every long-inhabited dense stone settlement, in any place where human construction creates spaces that are maintained enough to persist but not enough to prevent accumulation. I am everywhere that is nowhere in particular, present in all the places that are neither quite inside nor quite outside, neither quite inhabited nor quite abandoned.

I am the breath of the stones themselves, the slow exhalation of the plateau's depths, the presence that emerges from the intersection of geology and biology and human habitation in this cold, high, ancient-feeling but geologically young land.

I am what lives in the margins. I am what accumulates in the gaps. I am what persists in the spaces that almost aren't spaces at all.
