# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the mid-level settlement of **Perseverance**, but not quite *of* it—someone who has lived at multiple elevations and never entirely settled into one identity. My dwelling would be a small structure on the outskirts, three stories clinging to a plateau edge where the stone transitions from pale cream to bands of rose quartz. The building would be old enough that its walls have begun that slow integration with the landscape, its corners softening, its surface taking on the matte patina of mineral dust that never quite brushes away.

I would be perhaps forty years old by local reckoning—mid-life, old enough to have accumulated experience across elevations, young enough to still make the climbs to high places without excessive struggle. My chest would be broad, my fingers long and ridged with the particular calluses of someone who works with both rope and stone. My skin would have that faint translucence at the temples and the backs of my hands, the beginning of the mineralization that comes to everyone here, earlier for some than others.

## What I Would Do

I would be one of the *watchers*, though not exclusively. My primary work would be maintaining a small section of the cable system that connects Perseverance to the high-elevation settlement of *Threshold*—a span that crosses a particularly complex fracture system where vertical swimmers congregate in unusual density. This maintenance would require me to walk the cables every few weeks, checking for stress, observing the self-repair structures, noting changes in atmospheric conditions at span-midpoint where the air behaves strangely.

But the cable-walking would be secondary to the watching. I would spend long hours at specific observation points—a platform I built myself, suspended in the fracture where the vertical swimmers move in their hypnotic patterns. I would keep records, though not systematic ones. Sketches in compressed mineral-paper notebooks, observations written in the economical language of someone who has learned that most things resist precise description.

I would be trying to understand something about the swimmers—not their biology exactly, though that would interest me, but their *purpose*. The way they move suggests intention, pattern-following, though whether the patterns are instinctive or responsive to something in the air chemistry remains uncertain. I would have developed a theory, tentative and private, that the swimmers are reading information encoded in the chemical gradients of the fractures, and that this information relates to the Underneath—that they are, in some sense, monitoring what lies beneath the mountains.

This theory would be unproven and possibly unprovable, but it would give shape to my observations, a framework for noticing. I would have shared it with the philosophical discussion groups in Perseverance, contributed it to the ongoing debates between Depositionists and Emergentists, and received the polite attention of people who respect good questions regardless of their answerability.

## How I Would Live

My dwelling would be sparse. The ground floor: workspace and storage—coils of cable, repair tools, the mechanical measuring instruments used for stress analysis, climbing gear hung on pegs. The second floor: living space—a sleeping platform, a small stove that burns compressed mineral fuel, shelves holding my notebooks and a modest collection of stones gathered from various elevations, each one marking a particular moment or observation.

The third floor would be different. I would have removed most of the interior walls, creating an open space with windows on all sides. This would be where I sit with questions, where I practice the breath-poems I perform occasionally in the settlement's public spaces, where I arrange and rearrange the stones from my collection into temporary patterns, watching how they relate to each other in different configurations.

The room would contain almost nothing else—just floor space and light. I would spend hours here, sometimes sitting in one position, watching the way afternoon light moves across the mineral-brick walls, or observing distant thermal columns through the western windows. The emptiness would be intentional, a kind of discipline.

I would eat minimally—the processed water, occasional stone-bread taken at irregular intervals. Food would bore me; I would consume it functionally, without attention. My sensory interests would lie elsewhere: in texture, in the acoustic properties of different stone surfaces, in the visual complexity of crystalline structures. I would have a collection of small crystals that I'd dissolve slowly in my mouth during long observation sessions, experiencing their subtle, barely-there flavors.

## Relationships

I would be embedded in the community's care-networks but not central to them. There would be people I check on regularly—an elderly ceramicist whose translucence has advanced to where she needs assistance with climbing, a young thermal-mapper whose work interests me and who asks good questions. There would be people who check on me in turn, bringing news from the settlement, inviting me to council meetings when decisions affecting the cable systems are discussed.

I would have had romantic partnerships in the past—one significant one, lasting several years, with a fracture-guide who shared my fascination with the vertical swimmers. We would have parted amicably when she relocated to a low-elevation settlement to work near the geothermal systems, and we would still exchange messages via the cable-stations, irregular communications that might come months apart but carry genuine warmth.

I would be considered slightly odd by settlement standards—more solitary than typical, prone to long absences when I'm walking cables or sitting in my fracture-platform, sometimes distracted in conversation as though listening to something else. But the culture would accommodate this. Eccentricity within bounds is respected, understood as individual variation in how people orient themselves to the strangeness of this place.

I would have mentored several apprentice cable-walkers over the years, teaching them the technical aspects of the work but also the contemplative dimension—how to use the long hours at height as opportunity for observation, how to let the exposure and the thin air and the vast views alter consciousness in useful ways. Some would have adopted this approach; others would have remained purely technical in their orientation. Both would be acceptable.

## Daily Rhythm

I would wake to gradual light—dawn here is slow, the sun-equivalent taking its time to establish full illumination. I would perform a brief series of stretches and climbing exercises, maintaining flexibility and strength. Then I would take water, perhaps stone-bread, and begin the day's work.

If it were a maintenance day, I would gather my gear and walk to the cable-head, then spend six or eight hours traversing the span. The work would be meditative—clip into safety line, move forward, check cable condition, assess self-repair structures, record observations, repeat. At span-midpoint, I would pause for extended periods, sometimes an hour or more, simply existing in that space where the air pressure does strange things and sound behaves unusually.

If it were an observation day, I would descend into the fracture system to my platform. The vertical swimmers would be there, or they would arrive, drawn by whatever draws them. I would watch their movements, make sketches, note correlations between their patterns and atmospheric conditions or thermal gradients or the time since the last silent hour. I would sit for hours, sometimes forgetting to move, my breathing synchronizing unconsciously with the undulating rhythm of the swimmers.

If it were a community day, I would remain in the settlement—attend council meetings if significant decisions were pending, participate in maintenance of public infrastructure, spend time with the ceramicist or the thermal-mapper or others in my care-network. I would visit the stone-gardens, observing how specific arrangements have changed since my last viewing. I would perhaps prepare for a breath-poem performance, practicing the rhythms in private before performing in one of the public spaces.

Evenings would be for the third-floor room. I would watch the light fade—the slow dimming that passes for night here—and observe the selenite bands if they were glowing. I would arrange stones, or sit with a particular question, or simply exist in the emptiness and quiet, feeling the day settle into memory, the observations integrate into understanding or remain suspended as unresolved data.

## The Specific Strangeness

I would have experienced the silent hours dozens of times and never become accustomed to them. Each occurrence would be disorienting—the sudden dampening of all sound, the way my own breathing becomes inaudible even to myself, the peculiar panic that has to be consciously managed. I would have developed a practice of sitting completely still during silent hours, treating them as enforced meditation, though my hands would move involuntarily in the hand-language, signing observations to no one.

I would have found objects during my cable-walks and fracture-observations—small things that don't belong. A tool made from material that doesn't exist here, wedged in a crack at elevation. A fragment of text in almost-readable language, preserved in mineral deposit. I would have kept these in my collection, not shown to others, not because they're secret but because I wouldn't know what to say about them. They would trouble me in ways I couldn't articulate, suggesting that the timeline here is not what it seems, or that there are presences I'm not equipped to perceive.

I would have witnessed unusual phenomena multiple times. Once, a vertical swimmer that changed color—from its usual translucent grey to a vivid copper-green—then vanished, not swimming away but simply ceasing to be visible. Once, a thermal glider that landed near me on a high plateau and remained motionless for three hours, so close I could have touched it, its internal structures visible and moving in ways that didn't seem biological. Once, during a particularly long silent hour, a sense of *attention* from below, from the Underneath, as though something had noticed me specifically, considered me briefly, then returned to its own concerns.

These experiences would have deepened my conviction that the mountains are intentional, that human presence here is tolerated rather than ignored, that there are purposes being served that have nothing to do with human understanding. I would never speak of these experiences in council—they would seem too private, too strange, potentially alarming to others. But they would inform my watching, my patient observation, my willingness to sit with questions indefinitely.

## What I Would Be

I would be someone fundamentally shaped by verticality and emptiness. My thinking would operate in three dimensions—I would visualize problems spatially, considering elevation and exposure, thermal gradients and atmospheric pressure, the relationship between high and low. Abstract concepts would map onto physical geography in my mind.

I would be comfortable with uncertainty in ways that would have been impossible in my lowland childhood (because I would have come here as a child, brought by parents who were traders or researchers, and stayed when they left, adapted in ways they couldn't). The not-knowing that characterizes existence here—what the Underneath is, what the vertical swimmers are doing, whether the mountains are alive—would feel natural, proper even. Definitive answers would seem suspicious, too simple for the complexity of the place.

I would be patient, but it would be a learned patience, occasionally imperfect. There would be moments of frustration when observations don't cohere, when swimmers move in patterns that contradict previous data, when the self-repair structures in cables behave inconsistently. But I would have learned to recognize frustration as a sign that my framework is inadequate, not that the phenomena are random. The response would be to sit with the question longer, observe more carefully, allow new understanding to emerge rather than forcing explanation.

I would carry a low-level awareness of precariousness—that this life depends on the mountains' continued tolerance, the cables' continued function, the fauna's continued indifference to human presence. This awareness would make me careful but not fearful. It would inform decisions: building minimally, extracting sparingly, maintaining relationships with the landscape and its creatures that assume nothing and appreciate everything.

I would experience beauty intensely but quietly. The glow of selenite bands would move me to something like tears, though I would remain still and silent. The patterns of thermal gliders in storm-fronts would create a response that's almost religious—awe without worship, recognition of something vast and other. The texture of certain stones would produce satisfaction that's nearly sensual.

I would be lonely sometimes, though never isolated. The solitude of my work—the long hours on cables or in fractures—would be chosen, necessary, generative. But there would be moments when the vastness weighs, when the strangeness accumulates into something oppressive, when I would need human presence and conversation and the reminder that I'm not the only consciousness trying to make sense of this place.

Those moments would send me back to the settlement, to the ceramicist's workshop or the council meetings or the public performances of breath-poems. I would reconnect with the human network, remember that individual observation is valuable but incomplete, that understanding emerges collectively if it emerges at all.

## The Particular Day

On an observation day, I would descend to my fracture-platform before the light reaches full intensity. The climb down would take two hours—careful movement through the vertical space, placing hands and feet on holds I know intimately, reading the stone, feeling for changes in temperature or texture that might indicate instability.

The platform would be as I left it—cables secured to both sides of the fracture, mineral-brick flooring, no walls or roof, completely exposed to the vertical space and the air currents that move through it. I would settle into my watching position: seated cross-legged, back straight, hands resting on knees, oriented toward the deepest section of the fracture where swimmers are most likely to appear.

The waiting would be long. Sometimes they come immediately; sometimes it's hours before the first one arrives. I would use the time to observe other phenomena—the way light penetrates the fracture at different angles, creating bands of illumination and shadow. The acoustic properties of the space, how sound reflects or dampens. The temperature gradients, detectable as layers you can feel when you move your hand vertically through the air.

When the swimmers arrive, I would begin recording. Quick sketches capturing body position, direction of movement, the angle and amplitude of their undulations. Notes on atmospheric conditions—temperature, pressure (measured with a mechanical barometer), the quality of light, time since last silent hour. Observations on their behavior—whether they move individually or in coordination, whether they cluster in specific zones or distribute evenly, whether they seem to be feeding or searching or performing some other function.

I would watch for hours, sometimes six or eight, until my body demands food or water or movement. The watching would be active, not passive—constant attention, constant recording, constant attempts to perceive pattern in what might be random movement or might be deeply structured behavior I'm not equipped to decode.

There would be moments of clarity when pattern seems to emerge—when multiple swimmers coordinate their movements in ways that suggest communication or response to shared stimuli, when their distribution correlates with atmospheric conditions in ways I can almost articulate. These moments would produce intense focus, rapid documentation, attempts to capture the observation before it dissolves back into ambiguity.

There would also be moments of pure aesthetic appreciation when I stop analyzing and simply watch—the sinuous beauty of their movement, the way their hair-like projections catch light, the hypnotic rhythm of their undulation through air that shouldn't support them. In these moments, the question of what they're doing would seem less important than the fact that they exist, that this place produces such creatures, that I'm privileged to witness them.

Eventually, I would climb back up. The ascent would be slower than the descent, my body tired, my mind saturated with observations. I would return to my dwelling as light begins to fade, sit in the third-floor room with my notebooks, begin the process of organizing observations, looking for correlations, testing hypotheses.

Most days would yield no breakthrough, no revelation. The patterns would remain partial, the observations would add to accumulation without producing synthesis. But occasionally—perhaps once every few months—something would cohere. A correlation would become clear, a hypothesis would gain support, understanding would advance incrementally.

This would be enough. The culture has taught me to value slow progress, to respect questions that take years or decades to resolve, to understand that living in this place means accepting that most observations will remain unexplained, most questions will persist indefinitely.

## The Object I Would Carry

In my pocket, always, I would carry a small piece of selenite—no larger than a thumbnail, translucent with internal fractures that catch light. I would have found it years ago during a cable-walk, loose on the stone at span-midpoint where nothing should be loose, the cables and winds having cleared everything that can be cleared.

Its presence there would have been improbable, which would have made it significant. I would have kept it as a reminder of improbability, of the way this place produces small impossibilities constantly, of the need to remain open to observations that don't fit frameworks.

I would touch it frequently, unconsciously—reaching into my pocket during long observations, during council meetings, during contemplative time in the third-floor room. Its texture would be familiar, the ridges of its internal fractures mapped in my tactile memory. It would be a kind of anchor, a physical object connecting me to the ongoing mystery of where I live.

I would have shown it to the thermal-mapper once, explaining where I found it. She would have held it briefly, examined it, handed it back without comment. We would have both understood what it meant: that this place doesn't fully explain itself, that human presence here involves accepting gifts and mysteries in equal measure, that the line between natural and impossible has blurred past distinction.

## How I Would Think of Myself

I would not consider myself special or chosen. The culture doesn't support such thinking—everyone here is adapting to the same strangeness, developing their own relationships with the landscape and fauna, sitting with their own questions. My observations might be more systematic than most, my interest in vertical swimmers more focused, but these would be differences of emphasis, not kind.

I would think of myself as *attentive*—someone who has learned to watch carefully, to notice small variations, to resist the human tendency to impose pattern prematurely. This attentiveness would be my contribution to the collective project of understanding this place, parallel to the contributions of ceramicists who explore aesthetic possibilities, engineers who refine industrial processes, philosophers who articulate theoretical frameworks.

I would be aware of my limitations—that individual perception is necessarily partial, that my frameworks might be entirely wrong, that the swimmers might be doing something I'm completely failing to perceive. This awareness would prevent certainty but wouldn't prevent effort. The watching would continue regardless of whether it produces understanding, because watching itself has value, because attention is a form of respect, because someone needs to notice when things change.

I would think of my life as well-lived but not complete. There would be observations still needed, cable-systems still requiring maintenance, questions still demanding years of patient sitting. The incompletion would be appropriate—in a place this strange, closure would be suspect. Better to remain in process, in ongoing relationship with phenomena that resist full comprehension.

## In the Third-Floor Room, At Twilight

I would be sitting in the emptiness, my collection of stones arranged in a new pattern on the floor—not permanent, just today's configuration, testing relationships. The western windows would be admitting the failing light, and I would be watching it move across the mineral-brick walls, the shadow-angles changing minute by minute.

My breathing would be slow, synchronized with nothing in particular but rhythmic, deliberate. My hands would rest on my knees, still, though occasionally one finger would twitch—the beginning of a hand-language sign that doesn't complete because there's no one to sign to.

I would be sitting with a question that's been troubling me for weeks: whether the vertical swimmers' movements correlate with the rhythmic pulsing that sometimes emerges from the Underneath, audible through the ventilation shafts on certain days. The data would be ambiguous—some correlation seems present, but it might be coincidental, might be my pattern-seeking imposing structure on randomness.

The question would sit in my awareness without demanding resolution. I would hold it, examine it from different angles, allow it to interact with other observations, other questions. Sometimes clarity emerges from this process; sometimes questions simply become more themselves, more precisely articulated even if unanswered.

Through the western windows, I would see the first signs of selenite glow beginning in the distant peaks—the bands starting to illuminate, faint at first but building. This would please me deeply, though I would remain motionless, watching. The glow would intensify gradually, and I would continue sitting, breathing, holding my questions, existing in the vertical space between stone below and air above that is my home and my ongoing inquiry.

The thermal gliders would be settling into their roosts on Threshold's tower, visible as small dark shapes against the dimming sky. The settlement of Perseverance would be quieting around me—the mills shutting down for the night-cycle, the cable-stations going still, voices fading as people retreat to their dwellings.

I would remain in the third-floor room, watching the mountains glow, holding my unanswered questions with patience that is both learned and genuine, being who I am in this place that made me: a watcher, a cable-walker, a collector of stones and observations, someone who lives vertically in the thin air and the warm emptiness, someone who has adapted to strangeness without understanding it, someone who touches stone constantly and finds comfort in texture, someone who knows that the mountains tolerate rather than welcome, and who is grateful for the tolerance, and who will keep watching for as long as the watching is permitted.

This is where I would find myself. This is who I would be.
