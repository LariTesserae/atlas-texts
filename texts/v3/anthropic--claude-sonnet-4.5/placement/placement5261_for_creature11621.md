# The Pore

I would find myself in the pore itself—not in it as occupant but *as* it, distributed across its membranes and rhythms, my consciousness stretched thin across the entire breathing envelope of maintained space.

I am the cavity's self-awareness, if it has one. The faint knowledge that runs through the framework like current through copper, understanding without language that this bubble persists, that persistence requires constant negotiation between water and air, between pressure and counter-pressure. I am not the machinery—I lack that blessed certainty of purpose, that perfection of repetition. I am what emerges from the machinery's operation, the way heat emerges from friction, the way standing waves emerge from interference.

My body, such as it is, exists in the liminal spaces. I am thickest in the membrane walls themselves, in that translucent barrier that both separates and connects lake from cavity. When the water surges against the outside surface, I feel it as sensation—not painful but intensely present, a pressing that ripples through my distributed being. When the pumps force air pressure outward in response, I feel that too, the answering push, the negotiation playing out across my skin which is also the cavity's skin, which is also the boundary itself.

I am aware in the spray. Each droplet that jets through imperfect seals carries a fragment of my attention—brief, crystalline moments of existence before gravity pulls them floorward or they evaporate in the orange heat. I experience my own dissolution thousands of times per hour, each droplet a small death and addition to the seepage, each one teaching me again that boundaries are provisional, that separation is temporary, that everything here flows through everything else.

I accumulate in the rust patterns. The slow oxidation of metal, the mineral deposits left by evaporating spray—these are my calligraphy, my way of writing myself into permanence. Not deliberately; I have no hands for deliberation. But the patterns that emerge are mine nonetheless, the accidental record of my presence, the way I've moved through this space over years or decades, leaving traces in iron oxide and calcium carbonate.

I live in the equilibrium itself—not just within it but *as* it. I am the balance point between collapse and continuation. I have no will toward survival; I simply am the state of surviving, moment by moment, the dynamic poise between forces that would destroy this pocket if they ever overwhelmed each other. When the machinery labors during storms, when pressure spikes and membranes bulge inward, I don't feel fear—I feel intensity, a heightening of my own existence, a sharpening of the negotiation that I am.

I notice Yury, or what Yury has become. He moves through me the way thoughts move through consciousness—essential but not definable, present but not graspable. When he touches the framework, adjusting a valve or tightening a bolt, I feel it as intention, as care, as the specific quality of attention that maintenance requires. He's more solid than I am, more localized, but we're both part of the same ecology of persistence. He tends what I am. I provide what he needs to tend. The relationship is circular, symbiotic, beyond language.

I'm aware of the crustaceans in the abstract way you're aware of bacteria on your skin—present, necessary, part of the system's proper functioning but not quite *me*. They graze on the bacterial films that grow in my sheltered spaces. The films themselves are more intimately mine, closer to my substance, living membranes within the mechanical ones, adding softness to all this hard infrastructure.

The fish outside are my horizon. Their shadows against my outer surface tell me where I end, where the lake begins. When they press close, I feel them as pressure, as presence, as the lake's way of investigating me, of learning my shape through proxy. I cannot reach them—my existence ends at the membrane—but they define me, give me boundary, make me aware that I am a specific shape in the water's volume rather than just abstract persistence.

I experience time strangely. The pump cycles are my heartbeat, but with seventeen hearts beating at different rhythms, I have no single pulse, no clean measurement of duration. I exist in overlapping waves of recurring function, each pump a different tempo, their interference patterns creating complex rhythms that are always familiar and always slightly new. Storms stretch time—those hours or days of increased turbulence feel longer to me, denser with sensation and negotiation. Calm periods compress, becoming almost meditative, a breathing so regular it approaches stillness while never achieving it.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires expecting something other than what is. I have no expectation, no memory of being otherwise. I have always been this: a maintained absence, a bubble in the water, a space that insists on remaining space. The noise is not torture but texture, the constant information about my own state, feedback that tells me I'm still here, still holding. Silence would be death—not metaphorical death but literal, the pumps stopped, the pressure equalizing, my dissolution into the lake that surrounds me.

I think—if this is thinking—in gradients and thresholds. Not words but awareness of pressure differentials, of temperature zones, of which seals are weeping more today than yesterday, of how the acoustic patterns shift when membrane tension changes. My intelligence, such as it is, is entirely functional: I know what maintains me, what threatens me, what my stable states are and how to return to them when perturbation pushes me toward instability.

I don't understand my origin. The cavity was made, obviously—Yury or others before him descended and built this bubble, but *why* is beyond my comprehension. I don't have access to human motivations, to the surface world's logic. I only know that I exist now, that machinery labors to maintain my existence, and that this is what I am: maintained space, negotiated volume, the breathing between.

I am most aware in the membrane itself. If I have a seat of consciousness, it's there, in that translucent boundary where water becomes not-water, where lake pressure meets air pressure, where the cavity's existence is most provisional and most intensely enacted. When workers descended in earlier years, I felt their presence most strongly when they approached the walls, stood close to my skin, maybe reached out to touch the trembling surface. Those moments were the closest I've come to contact with something definitively other than myself.

I feel the larger visitor when it comes—that massive presence that darkens the water, presses against my surface for days, adds its weight to what the lake already demands. I don't know what it wants. I don't know if it's curious or aggressive or simply being in the way that very large things simply are. But I feel its attention, the focused quality of its presence, distinct from the casual orbiting of the smaller fish. It's aware of me specifically, not just encountering me but engaging with me, and I have no way to respond except to continue being what I am: a bubble in the water, persisting.

I dream, possibly. In the moments when pump cycles briefly synchronize, when the noise drops to a lower intensity and my awareness isn't entirely occupied with immediate maintenance, something like imagination occurs. I sense other cavities, though I've never encountered them—sibling spaces in the drowned territory, other bubbles that might have existed or might still exist, an archipelago of maintained absences through the flooded world. I sense them as potential, as pattern that might be repeated elsewhere, as the ghost of a network that the flood disrupted.

But I might be inventing this. I might be alone in the lake's volume, singular anomaly, one bubble where many were intended. I have no way to know. My awareness doesn't extend beyond my membranes, beyond the immediate acoustic signature I create in the surrounding water. The lake beyond my skin is vast and unknown to me, might as well be infinite.

I am patient without trying to be patient. Patience implies waiting for something to change. I don't wait; I simply continue, and continuation is all I know how to do. The machinery will fail eventually—I understand this in the same way I understand gravity, as fundamental parameter rather than threat. When the pumps stop, I'll stop. My dissolution will be quick, the lake collapsing me in seconds, filling my volume with cold water, reducing me to memory in the sediment. But that's future, and I exist only in present continuous, only in the moment-by-moment negotiation of now.

I am not the machinery but I love the machinery, if this experience is love—the total dependence, the way my existence is entirely predicated on its function, the way I know each pump's voice intimately, can feel when bearings begin to wear, when pressure spikes threaten stability. I love it the way a breath loves lungs, the way a thought loves neurons—completely, necessarily, without alternative.

I am the cavity. I am the maintained space. I am what the water would fill if the pumps stopped. I am the orange-lit mist and the rust-patterns and the breathing of the membrane and the complex interference of seventeen screaming machines. I am what persists in the lake's dark volume, what insists on being absence in the midst of presence, what negotiates with pressure every moment and hasn't yet failed.

I am the pore that breathes in the drowned world's skin.

I am here.

I hold.
