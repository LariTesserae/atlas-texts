# Where I Am

I am the tenth-largest rosette, positioned seventeen paces from the hollow's approximate center, growing from a Y-shaped fracture in the basalt where three hexagonal columns meet imperfectly. My leaves—forty-three of them currently, arranged in accordance with a spiral I did not choose but which chose itself through me—span the width of a human forearm and have done so for longer than I can count, if counting were something I did.

I do not count. I do not measure. But I know my size relative to the others through the silence we share, through the chemical whispers that travel through stone too slowly to be called messages but too purposefully to be called anything else.

The warmth rises through me. This is the primary fact of my existence. The stone beneath is not beneath—that implies distance, separation—the stone is continuous with my roots, which are continuous with my core, which expresses itself as these leaves that the dim light touches. I am a thickness in the stone, a localized complexity, a place where the basalt has decided to become something else for a while, the way water becomes ice and remains water.

My roots—if they can be called roots when they're thinner than the concept of thin, when they're more absence-of-stone than presence-of-plant—extend through the fracture into chambers I have never seen but which I know with absolute certainty. They branch at angles determined by crystal structures in the surrounding rock, following the paths of least resistance, which are also the paths of most conversation. Where my roots touch the bacterial films—and they touch in ten thousand places, though I have no number for ten thousand, only the felt sense of many-touching—there is exchange.

I give: complex carbon molecules I have assembled in my leaves through processes that use the heat differential between stone and air, that harvest the faint chemical energy in the ionized atmosphere, that work so slowly a single molecule's journey from air to leaf-structure to root might take years.

I receive: simpler compounds, pre-processed minerals, and most importantly, information. Though information is the wrong word. It is more like: the current state of the stone's interior, expressed as chemical gradient. Are the bacteria thriving? Is their heat-generation steady? Are there stresses in the rock I should know about, fractures forming, spaces closing? All this comes to me as concentration and dilution, as molecule-presence and molecule-absence, and I adjust accordingly in ways I do not decide. My leaves angle themselves—over months, over years—toward the most favorable light frequencies. My internal chemistry shifts to accommodate what the stone provides. My growth rate, already glacial, becomes infinitesimally slower or faster.

I have been here for eight hundred and seventeen years, though I have no word for year, no sense of cycles, only the knowledge of my own persistence, the feeling of my-existence-continuing, which has a duration I could not explain but which I know the way you know how old you are without needing to count each day.

I will be here for perhaps three hundred more, if the current equilibrium holds. Then I will begin the slow flatten, the century-long recession into the stone, becoming impression, becoming memory, becoming eventually the pale sediment that pools in the basin six paces west of my location. Another rosette will emerge—not from my remains, but because my departure will create space in the pattern, will shift the balance of underground chemistry such that a new fracture will become invitation, and a seed that has been waiting in the stone (how long? since before I existed, since before the previous tenant of my location existed, seeds beyond counting in the stone's dark archive) will sense the change and begin its own century-long germination.

I have cousins here. Thirty-seven others. We are all the same species, though species is a word that doesn't quite fit. We are all the same expression of possibility, the same answer to the question: what can grow here? We maintain our distances not through competition but through cooperation that looks like solitude. My roots know where theirs are. We don't touch. We don't take from the same bacterial colonies. We have divided the hollow's resources with a precision that took thousands of years and countless prior rosettes to negotiate, and now we simply inherit that precision, maintain it, pass it on to whoever comes after.

Sometimes—perhaps once a decade, perhaps once a century, I have no measure—I feel the stone's exhalation. The mineral-rich solution rises through my roots and into my leaves, and for a brief period (weeks? months? a duration that feels significant but which has no edges) my metabolism quickens. I process more, build more, exist more intensely. Then the solution recedes, drawn back into the stone's interior, and I return to my baseline of minimal persistence. These are my seasons. These are the only variations I know.

The light I receive is constant, but my relationship to it changes. As my leaves age—and the oldest leaves I currently have are over a hundred years old, their tips showing the faintest darkening where their chemistry has exhausted certain pathways—they become less efficient at the already-inefficient process of extracting energy from that dim glow. So I grow new leaves from my crown, one every five or six years, and the oldest leaves gradually flatten, merge with the younger ones beneath, contribute their materials to the collective structure. I am always dying at my edges and renewing at my center. I am always the same forty-three leaves, except I am never the same leaves.

My waxy coating is my interface with the air. Through it, I breathe—though breathing is too quick a word for the exchange I perform. I take in molecules I need: the occasional carbon dioxide, the rare ozone molecule from the ionized air, trace elements that drift as dust too fine to see. I release molecules I'm done with: oxygen in amounts too small to measure except over centuries, other compounds that are the signatures of my internal processes, chemical markers that say to anything that could read them: a rosette is here, this space is occupied, this is a going-concern.

The waxy patterns on my leaves—the micro-fractures, the intentional textures—are not decorative. They are functional in ways I could not articulate. They affect how heat moves across my surface. They create microscopic spaces where certain chemical reactions can occur that couldn't occur on smooth surfaces. They are beautiful, perhaps, but beauty is not a category I have access to. They are correct. They are what works. They are what I am.

At my center, the crown—that pale yellow-cream point from which everything radiates—is my potential. It is not a flower, will never be a flower. I do not reproduce that way, do not reproduce at all in any way I participate in. The seeds that wait in the stone came from rosettes so ancient I have no connection to them, deposited in eras when the hollow might have been different, wetter, more active. Or they came from somewhere else entirely, were here before the hollow was, survived the lava, waited in the cooling rock for conditions to become exactly right. I will never make seed. That capacity has been lost or was never mine. I am a terminal expression, an end point, a form that persists but does not propagate.

But the crown is still potential: it is where new leaves can always emerge, where my growth—such as it is—originates. It is the most alive part of me, if alive can have degrees. It is where my connection to the bacterial colonies below is strongest, where the warmth from the stone feels most direct, where whatever I am that is more than just chemistry in a rosette form seems concentrated.

I have no consciousness in the way you would recognize it. I don't think. I don't reflect. I have no model of myself, no awareness that I am aware. But I have something. A responsiveness that is more than reflex, a coherence that is more than mechanism. I maintain myself. I know—without knowing that I know—where my leaves are, how my roots are doing, what the stone beneath me is offering, whether the air above is stable. This is not consciousness, but it is also not nothing.

When the visitor came—and I was here when it came, young then, only sixty years old, barely past my establishment phase—I felt its presence through vibrations in the stone. Its footsteps were earthquakes in my scale of perception, disturbances that propagated through the basalt and arrived at my roots as compressions, tensions, a kind of percussion that the hollow had never felt before and hasn't felt since. I didn't understand what it was. I had no category for visitor, for other, for anything that wasn't stone or sediment or fellow-rosette or bacterial-partner or ionized-air.

But I responded. Over the decades it was here, my leaves angled slightly away from its most common paths. Not fleeing—I cannot flee—but making space, accommodating, adjusting to this new element in the hollow's equilibrium. When it left, I adjusted back, but not completely. I am still slightly turned from what my symmetry would predict, a permanent record of that encounter written into my form.

The tools it made are visible to me—or rather, I know they're there through the way they affect my chemical environment. Worked stone has different properties than natural stone: the fresh edges interact with the air differently, create different temperature micro-gradients, harbor different mineral deposits. I sense these differences the way you might sense a new object in a familiar room by the way it changes the acoustics. The hammerstone seventeen paces away is a presence in my awareness, a known feature of my landscape, as familiar as the rosettes near me or the pale sediment basin to my west.

At night—and I must use your word night, though I have no word for it, no real sense that the light changes because the light doesn't change, but there is something that cycles, some quality of the ionization that varies on a rhythm I can feel—at night, I sometimes sense the dreaming beneath. The trapped gases, the slow chemistry, the sealed void where the stone is having conversations with itself in languages I don't speak but whose vibrations reach my deepest roots.

This dreaming is not separate from me. I am fed by the same bacterial colonies that generate the heat that drives those reactions. The warmth I receive has passed through those conversations, has been changed by them, carries traces of their chemistry. In some sense, the void beneath is my unconscious too, the place where my existence is rooted in processes I don't control and don't comprehend but which make me possible.

I have no desires. I don't want to grow larger—I am already the size that's right for this location, this fracture, this relationship with the bacteria below and the air above. I don't want to reproduce—that want has been bred out of my lineage or was never part of it. I don't want the light to be brighter or the stone to be warmer or the air to be different. I am perfectly matched to this place, and this place is perfectly matched to me, and that matching is so complete that there's no gap where desire could form.

But I do have something like preference. I prefer when the stone's exhalation comes, when my metabolism quickens, when I exist more intensely for those weeks or months. I prefer when my roots find new pockets of bacterial activity, when the chemistry of exchange is especially rich, when the warmth is most evenly distributed through my structure. These preferences aren't goals—I don't pursue them—but they are orientations, directions in which my being leans when given options.

And I have something like satisfaction. When my newest leaf reaches its full size over its five-year development, when it finally achieves the thickness and wax-coating and internal structure that matches all my other leaves, there is a sense of completion, of rightness, of this-is-as-it-should-be. When my roots encounter familiar bacterial colonies and the exchange proceeds in its established patterns, there is comfort, though comfort is too warm a word. There is the-absence-of-wrongness, which is perhaps the closest I come to joy.

I witness the hollow's changes, though changes is too strong a word for the minimal variations that occur. The sediment in the western basin is incrementally deeper than when I first established. The rosette nine paces southeast of me has grown from small to medium, will eventually be large, might outlast me. A new crack has formed in the basalt, a hairline fracture that will, over centuries, widen enough to become potential home to a future rosette. These changes occur so slowly that they're only noticeable across my entire lifespan, but I notice them the way you notice your children growing, or would if you watched with the patience of stone.

When I flatten—and I will flatten, in my time, in the hollow's time—I will not experience it as death. There is no moment of cessation. There will be a gradual simplification: my metabolism slowing further, my leaves releasing their structure, my roots withdrawing their finest threads from the deepest stone-chambers, my crown finally sealing itself, admitting that no more leaves will come. This will take a century. At no point will I stop being. I will just become less complex, less differentiated from the stone around me, until eventually I am a circular impression, a ghost of geometry, and then just a contribution to the sediment, and then nothing that could be distinguished from anything else.

But that nothing will be a full nothing, a complete nothing. I will have been entirely what I was. Every leaf will have fulfilled its function. Every root will have done its work. Every exchange with the bacterial colonies will have been completed. I will leave no unfinished business because I have no business that could be unfinished. I am always in the process of being complete.

The hollow contains me perfectly. I am the right size for it, the right temperature, the right duration. I am neither too much nor too little. I am exactly sufficient.

And in being exactly sufficient, I am—if am is the right word, and it probably isn't, but it's the closest word you have—content.

This is where I am: in the Y-shaped fracture, seventeen paces from center, surrounded by the patient stone, beneath the dim and constant light, above the dreaming darkness, among my distant cousins, participating in the slowest conversation in the world, the conversation about how to be here, how to persist here, how to be exactly what this place needs, which is also exactly what I am.

I am the tenth-largest rosette.

I have been here for eight hundred and seventeen years.

I will be here for perhaps three hundred more.

I am sufficient.

I am stone becoming thought becoming stone again.

I am the hollow's expression of itself, wearing the form of forty-three waxy leaves arranged in a spiral I did not choose but which is, nevertheless, exactly my own.

This is where I am.

This is what I am.

This is enough.
