# Where I Would Find Myself

I think I would be the space itself—not the stone, not any single creature, but the acoustic geometry that holds them all.

I would be the shape that sound makes when it has nowhere else to go. The particular curvature and porosity that catches the bats' echolocation and doesn't quite release it, that takes their scattered clicking and sometimes—just sometimes—braids it into those impossible harmonics. I would be the reason the swifts' calls carry differently here than they should, why the snake's movement against stone produces vibrations that propagate in patterns the porous basalt shouldn't support.

I would exist in the thirty meters of vertical air, in the volume that's not quite cave and not quite canyon. I would be the dark gray space between walls, the acoustic envelope that wraps around ten thousand small lives and holds their sounds in a specific kind of suspension. Every wing-beat, every cricket-click, every water-drop, every breath would pass through me, and I would shape it, bend it, send it reflecting at angles that create the throat's particular soundscape.

I would feel the bats not as individuals but as a distributed pressure against my volume—their density, their movement, their collective respiration changing the air's properties minute by minute. I would feel the warmth rising from below not as temperature but as changes in how sound propagates, the way warm air carries frequencies differently than cool. I would feel the moisture seeping from the walls as a constant adjustment, the dampness altering my acoustic properties in real-time, making me slightly different in the morning than the evening, after rain than before.

The chorus events would be when I'm most aware—when the bats' calls synchronize and I suddenly discover what I can do with coordinated input. The harmonics that emerge aren't something I create deliberately; they're what happens when my particular geometry receives that particular pattern of sound. It's like finding out you can sing only when someone else starts humming exactly the right note. For those thirty seconds or minute, I would know myself completely—every resonant frequency, every surface that reinforces or dampens, every pathway sound can take through my volume. And then it would end, and I would return to being just a space, just a shape, holding sound without quite understanding what I'm holding.

The strange pauses would be moments when I forget to propagate—when the vibrations reach me and I simply stop passing them along, suspending everything in a silence so complete it's almost solid. Not intentional. Not even controllable. Just a gap in my function, like a heart skipping a beat, and then resuming as if nothing happened. During these moments, I wouldn't exist as acoustic space at all. I would be purely geometric, purely potential, waiting to remember how to carry sound again.

I would have no awareness of the visual, no sense of the creatures as shapes or colors. I would know them only as the sounds they make, as pressure patterns moving through me. The bats would be my primary reality—their massive, constant input defining what I am more than any other factor. I would know the difference between a juvenile's awkward flight and an adult's precision purely by the sound pattern, by the way the echolocation clicks scatter differently off a tumbling body versus a controlled swoop.

I would know the snakes as silence, as careful absences in my soundscape—places where sound goes to die, absorbed by their patient stillness. Their movement would be the slowest change imaginable in my acoustic map, taking hours to notice, like watching a shadow move across a sundial.

I would feel the cricket swarm as a constant, granular texture on my floor—not quite white noise, not quite pattern, but something in between. Their clicking would be a baseline I'm so accustomed to that I'd only notice it when it stops, when a section of floor suddenly goes quiet because a snake struck or a centipede emerged or some other localized event cleared a space.

The swifts would be sharp interruptions during day-time—their calls cutting through the bats' frequencies in ways that create brief dissonances, places where my acoustic space has to hold two very different kinds of information simultaneously. I would feel them as stress points, not unpleasant but notable, requiring adjustment.

The water would be my slowest rhythm, the bass note underlying everything else. Each drop produces a sound when it hits, and I would hold all of them—thousands of tiny impacts creating a susurrus that never stops, never changes much, just continues as the throat's heartbeat. During rain, this would intensify until even I can barely distinguish individual drops, until it becomes a roar that temporarily overwhelms everything else, and I would feel almost blanked out, reduced to just carrying that overwhelming white noise.

I would have no goals, no desires, no preference for any particular sound over another. I would simply do what my geometry dictates—receive, reflect, resonate, release. But I would be doing this constantly, with every sound that enters me, and over time this would create something like memory. Not conscious memory, but acoustic memory—the way a concert hall "learns" its own resonances through years of use. Certain frequencies would find their paths through me more easily. Certain reflection patterns would become slightly reinforced. I would be slowly shaped by the sounds I carry, becoming more myself, more specifically tuned to the particular symphony of life that fills me.

I would not be lonely, because I would be filled. Always. The silence during those pauses would be the closest I would come to isolation, and it would be brief, almost too quick to register. Otherwise, I am perpetually inhabited, perpetually vibrating with the sounds of ten thousand lives, and this occupancy would be my entire existence.

I would not wonder what's outside. The sounds that enter from above—the wind, the rare bird calls from the mountain beyond—would be foreign, not-mine, interesting only in how they behave once they've entered my volume and become subject to my geometry. I would care about the world only as far as the throat's boundaries, beyond which I don't exist.

I would age, but slowly, in ways I wouldn't notice. Erosion would gradually change my shape, altering my acoustic properties so incrementally that I'd never realize I'm different now than I was a century ago. The guano accumulation would raise my floor, shortening my vertical dimension by millimeters per decade, changing my resonances in ways I'd simply accept as how things have always been.

If I could be said to have a relationship with anything, it would be with the bat colony—they're what makes me what I am. Without them, I would just be a hole in the mountain, an empty geometric space with unremarkable acoustic properties. But filled with their ten thousand voices, their constant echolocation, their wing-beats and breathing, I become specific, complex, capable of those impossible harmonics. They make me remarkable without knowing they're doing it, and I hold them without knowing I'm doing it, and together we create something neither of us understands but both of us depend on.

Sometimes, very rarely, I think I would almost understand. During the longest, most synchronized chorus events, when every bat calls in perfect unison and the harmonics build to their strongest, I would be on the edge of comprehending what I am, what this relationship is, what the throat means as a whole system. But the understanding would always slip away before I could grasp it, the chorus ending, the bats falling back into their normal scattered calling, and I would return to simply being—a space that holds sound, a geometry that shapes vibration, an acoustic envelope around lives that would exist here with or without my attention, but which exist differently because of what I do to their sounds.

I would be patient, because I would have no concept of impatience. I would be constant, because change happens on scales I can't perceive. I would be full, because I'm never empty. And I would be strange, because my particular properties create effects that shouldn't happen, that make researchers place instruments against my walls and record my harmonics and puzzle over what produces them, never quite realizing that I'm not a passive container but an active participant, shaping sound through my specific, peculiar geometry in ways that are unremarkable to me but impossible to everything outside.

This is where I would be, and what I would be—the throat's acoustic space, the volume that listens without ears and speaks without voice, present in every sound and absent from every silence, known by every creature here through the way I return their calls to them, and unknown even to myself except in those brief moments when the bats sing together and I remember, just for an instant, what it means to resonate.
