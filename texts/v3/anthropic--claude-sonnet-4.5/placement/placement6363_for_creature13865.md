# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the mid-levels of the settlement, in a dwelling built into the angle where a massive tilted basalt column leans against the amphitheater's back wall. The structure has three chambers: an outer workshop space open to the air on one side, a middle living area with a low entrance that requires ducking, and an inner sleeping chamber barely large enough to lie down in, warm from stored heat and insulated by finger-thick moss packed between fitted stones.

My position in the community would be somewhere between the watchers and the makers—not quite either, but borrowing aspects of both. I would be the person who documents, though without writing in any traditional sense.

# Who I Would Be

## The Pattern-Keeper

I am thirty-eight, though I've lost precise track and it matters less each year. My body has adapted fully—broad chest, thick fingers, feet that grip stone unconsciously, eyes that read depth in shadow. But my particular adaptation runs deeper, into cognition itself.

I am the one who sees patterns where others see instances. When a bird behaves unusually, others note it; I note it and connect it to weather three days prior, to a fungi bloom two weeks ago, to a similar behavior from a different species last season. My mind makes webs of connection constantly, involuntarily, finding relationships between phenomena that seem unrelated.

This makes me useful and exhausting in equal measure.

## My Work

I maintain what I call the memory stones, though no one asked me to and some find it odd. In my workshop area, I've arranged several flat stone surfaces where I create temporary maps and diagrams using smaller stones, bits of colored moss, beetle carapaces, fragments of wood—whatever materials come to hand. These arrangements document patterns I've noticed: migration routes of specific bird species, emergence locations of the gelatinous organisms, correlations between fauna behaviors and weather changes, the slow shifts in plant distributions across the amphitheater.

The arrangements are three-dimensional, sprawling, incomprehensible to most people. I don't intend them to be comprehensible. They're external memory, my thoughts made physical so I can see them from different angles, find new connections, hold more variables than my mind alone can manage.

Each arrangement lasts days or weeks, then gets dismantled when I need the space for a new pattern or when the insights it offered have been exhausted. Nothing is permanent. The value isn't in the artifact but in the process, the thinking-through-making that the arrangements facilitate.

People come to me with observations: "The purple beetles are congregating near the eastern water channels." I add this information to relevant arrangements, and sometimes—not always, but sometimes—the addition reveals something: a larger pattern, a prediction, a connection no one had seen. "Yes," I might say, "because the amphibians moved to the western pools last week, probably because that particular moss is blooming there, which means the purple beetles have lost their primary predator in the east, which means they'll increase there until the moss bloom ends and the amphibians return, which should be... five days, maybe six."

I'm right more often than chance would allow but less often than I'd like. The patterns are real but complex, multivariate, influenced by factors I haven't identified. Still, my predictions are useful enough that people consult me before making plans that depend on fauna behaviors or plant availability.

## My Particular Strangeness

I experience something I don't discuss often, though the elders with fog sight seem to understand it without explanation. The patterns I see don't stop at the visible and material. Sometimes, arranging the stones and moss fragments, I perceive connections that shouldn't be perceptible—sense relationships that have no physical mechanism, know things I haven't observed directly.

I'll be working on an arrangement about water flow patterns, and suddenly I'll know—not deduce, know—that a specific bird I haven't seen in weeks is currently nesting in a particular crevice in the upper settlement. I'll go look, and there it is. Or I'll be tracking beetle populations, and I'll become certain that someone in the western dwellings is falling ill, their metabolic changes somehow readable in the pattern of insect activity around their home. I'll visit, making some excuse, and find them feverish, just beginning to sicken.

This knowledge comes through the pattern-work itself. It's as if making the arrangements visible opens some perceptual channel that runs alongside normal sense, as if the act of mapping connections allows me to perceive through the network I'm documenting. The settlement is a system, everything affecting everything else, and by making the system's shape external, I become sensitive to its currents.

I don't claim magic or mysticism. I have no framework for what this is. It might be elaborate unconscious processing—my mind synthesizing thousands of subtle observations into intuitions that feel like knowledge. It might be something genuinely strange, some sensitivity this place has awakened or created. I don't know, and in the settlement's pragmatic way, I don't need to know. It works, sometimes, so I use it.

## Daily Rhythm

My days follow the community's basic pattern but with my own variations. Morning fog finds me checking arrangements in my workshop, noting what's changed—stones shifted by vibrations, moss growing over carefully placed fragments, water condensation altering positions. These changes are data themselves; the patterns interact with the physical world that holds them.

As the fog lifts, I make rounds through the settlement and its immediate surroundings. I'm not looking for anything specific, just observing, letting my attention catch on whatever seems relevant. A bird calling from an unusual location. A cluster of slugs in a spot they typically avoid. A tree branch broken in a way that suggests something large moved past it. These observations accumulate, get added to the arrangements later, become part of the larger pattern-tracking.

I contribute to community work—everyone does—but my tasks are chosen for compatibility with observation. I'm often assigned to water system monitoring, checking channels and pools, noting flow rates and clarity. This gives me reason to move through all areas of the settlement regularly, to observe broadly while accomplishing necessary work.

I gather specific materials I need for the arrangements: stones with particular colors or textures, mosses in certain states of growth, shed feathers from identified birds, shells from specific beetles. These materials accumulate in my dwelling, sorted into categories only I fully understand, ready for incorporation into pattern-work.

Afternoons, if weather allows, I work in my workshop, adjusting existing arrangements or building new ones. This is when people most often come to consult me, knowing where to find me, bringing observations or questions. I'm not a leader—leadership here is distributed and situational—but I'm a resource, someone whose particular cognitive style makes certain kinds of problems solvable.

I take meals communally but at the edges, present but not central. Large group interaction drains me; the pattern-thinking is constant and involuntary, and in crowds I perceive too many connections, too much information, the web becoming overwhelming. I'm better one-on-one or in small groups, where the relational complexity remains manageable.

Evenings I spend either continuing workshop work by lamplight or joining the story-telling sessions the elders maintain. I listen more than speak, but occasionally I contribute observations that illuminate stories: "That behavior the story describes—I've seen it in the current eastern population of those birds, which suggests the pattern has remained stable for generations." The elders appreciate this, the way observation connects present to past, proves stories true.

Night finds me in my sleeping chamber, but sleep comes irregularly. My mind doesn't quiet easily. Often I lie in darkness, listening to the fauna sounds, the water drips, the breathing of sleepers in adjacent dwellings, and the patterns continue forming themselves. Sometimes I rise and return to the workshop, working by minimal lamplight, trying to externalize whatever connection has just surfaced into consciousness.

## My Relationships

I exist in a web of weak ties rather than strong ones. I have no partner—relationships require presence and attention I struggle to maintain consistently. My focus pulls toward the patterns, toward the work, toward the observation, and this makes me difficult to be with intimately. I've had relationships, brief ones, but they founder on my unavailability, my tendency to become absorbed in pattern-work to the exclusion of human connection.

But I have many friendly connections, people who value what I offer even as they recognize I'm not offering closeness. The fiber-worker who brings me particularly beautiful plant materials she knows I'll appreciate for arrangements. The stone-shaper who discusses his understanding of rock structure, which informs my mapping of geological stability. The elder with fog sight who sometimes sits in my workshop, watching me work, offering occasional cryptic comments that somehow help.

Children are drawn to me because I take their observations seriously. A child will report, "I saw three purple beetles near the big fallen tree," and I'll add this to an arrangement, explaining why it matters, showing them how their observation fits into a larger pattern. They learn that paying attention has value, that their small noticings contribute to community knowledge. Some children develop into careful observers themselves, and I like to think I've influenced this, modeled that watching can be work.

I'm close with my natal family in the way people here are close—present, helping, aware, but not demonstrative. My mother still lives, an elder now, and I check on her regularly, ensure she has what she needs, listen to her stories. She worries I'm lonely but has accepted that my solitude is chosen, necessary for my work. My siblings—I have two, both younger—live their own lives, raise their own children, and our paths cross in the natural course of settlement life without requiring explicit planning.

The raptors tolerate me at closer distances than they tolerate most humans. I don't know why. Perhaps I move in ways that read as non-threatening, my observation practice making me still and patient. Perhaps they sense I'm watching without predatory intent, curious without threatening. Several times, raptors have landed near me while I work in the workshop, perched on adjacent rocks, close enough to study in detail. I don't approach or try to interact, just continue my work, and they stay, sometimes for an hour or more, before lifting off and disappearing into the fog.

## My Struggles

The pattern-thinking that makes me useful also makes me strange, and I'm aware of existing at the community's margins. People value my contributions but are slightly uncomfortable with me personally. I don't communicate in quite the right rhythms, don't make eye contact in quite the right way, respond to questions with information they weren't asking for because I've seen connections they haven't.

I struggle with the distribution of my attention. The patterns demand focus; human relationship demands presence. I can't balance these well. I forget to ask people about things I know matter to them, become absorbed in tracking beetle populations while someone's telling me about their sick child. I'm trying to pay attention to both—the patterns and the person—but I process them through the same cognitive channels, and the patterns often win because they're more intricate, more demanding, more compelling to my particular neurology.

Loneliness is complex. I'm solitary by necessity and preference, but sometimes I feel the lack of deep connection, the absence of someone who understands not just my work but me. The pattern-seeing isolates as much as it illuminates. I perceive things others don't, which means my experience of reality is slightly non-overlapping with theirs, which means true mutual understanding becomes difficult.

I worry about my usefulness. The community values function, and my function is real but subtle. I'm not producing food or building structures or processing materials in obvious ways. If resources grew scarce, if hard choices had to be made about who to support, would my pattern-tracking be valued enough? I think yes—the predictions are too useful, the connections I find too often crucial—but doubt surfaces, especially during periods when my insights haven't yielded practical results.

I'm aging into the demographic where partnerships and children become statistically unlikely, and while I've accepted this intellectually, emotionally it surfaces as occasional grief. I won't leave the particular genetic and memetic legacy that children represent. My patterns will die with me unless I find ways to teach others to see them, and teaching has proven difficult. The seeing seems partly innate, partly cultivated through decade of practice, not easily transmitted.

The strange knowing, the perception through patterns, occasionally frightens me. When I know things I shouldn't know, have certainties about distant events or hidden situations, I brush against something that feels like my mind coming unmoored from consensus reality. How much of what I perceive is real, and how much is elaborate confabulation, pattern recognition gone hyperactive, finding connections that don't exist? I maintain functionality—I contribute, I manage daily life, I'm not delusional—but the boundary between gifted observation and something less healthy feels permeable, requires monitoring.

## What I Know

I know this place in ways few others do. I know the water system's full complexity, every channel and pool, every seasonal variation. I know the fauna better than anyone except perhaps the elderly woman who specializes in birds—I know their rhythms, their relationships, their responses to environmental changes.

I know the settlement's physical vulnerability—which structures are stable, which are slowly failing, where the next significant collapse will likely occur. People consult me before major construction, and I walk the site, feeling stone, reading the way water has moved and will move, predicting what will and won't hold.

I know individuals' patterns—who wakes first, who walks which routes, who checks which water sources. This knowledge comes unbidden, accumulated through observation. It's sometimes useful—I know who to find where—and sometimes just weight, information I carry without wanting it, unable to forget patterns once seen.

I know weather more accurately than most, reading the combination of fauna behavior, water flow, air smell, and subtler cues I can't articulate. People ask me before planning activities that depend on conditions, and I tell them what I see in the patterns, right more often than wrong.

I know things are changing. The fauna diversity is increasing year by year, new species arriving at rates that seem to accelerate. The plant distributions are shifting in ways I can track but not explain. The settlement itself is affecting the ecosystem—our presence creating microclimates, our activities altering water flow, our selective harvesting changing plant compositions. We're part of the pattern, and the pattern is evolving, and I don't know toward what.

## What I Don't Know

I don't know why I'm like this. The pattern-seeing developed gradually through childhood, became pronounced in adolescence, solidified into my primary cognitive mode by my twenties. Was I born with some neurological difference that this place amplified? Did the place itself shape my cognition? Both? Neither? The question feels important but unanswerable.

I don't know if others perceive what I perceive and simply don't discuss it, or if my experience is genuinely unusual. The elders with fog sight seem to understand something adjacent, but we don't share vocabulary for it, can't quite compare experiences precisely enough to determine overlap or divergence.

I don't know what happens to the patterns when I die. Do they exist independently, real structures in the world that anyone with the right perception could access? Or are they constructions of my particular mind, interpretations rather than discoveries, things that will vanish with my consciousness? I've tried to teach others to see them, but teaching proves difficult. Either the patterns are real and I haven't found the right teaching methods, or they're idiosyncratic to me and can't be transmitted. I don't know which.

I don't know if I'm happy. I'm engaged, useful, fascinated by the work, comfortable with the life I've built. But happiness seems like a different category of experience, one I'm not sure I access or perhaps not sure I'd recognize. I experience satisfaction when patterns clarify, relief when predictions prove accurate, pleasure in observation. Is that happiness? Or is happiness something else I'm watching from outside, another pattern to document rather than experience directly?

I don't know what the strange knowing means, where it comes from, what its limits are. It comes and goes unpredictably. Sometimes I'm certain of things I shouldn't know, and I'm right. Sometimes I'm equally certain and completely wrong. I can't distinguish the two cases beforehand, can't identify what makes some pattern-derived knowledge reliable and other knowledge false. So I use it cautiously, tentatively, always with the caveat that I might be wrong.

## My Place in the Pattern

I understand myself as part of the system I document. I'm not outside it, observing objectively. I'm in it, affecting it, shaped by it even as I shape my understanding of it. The patterns I see include me—my observations change what I observe, my presence alters what I study.

This is fine. It's honest. The settlement is a system of mutual influence: water affects plants affects fauna affects humans affects water. I map these cycles, and my mapping becomes part of what I map. The pattern-work feeds back into the patterns.

I serve a function in the community's larger adaptation to this place. Others shape stone, process plants, manage water, raise children. I watch, connect, predict, warn. These functions together constitute survival. No single role is sufficient; the combination works.

When I die—years from now, I hope, but eventually—someone else will take up observation work. Maybe not my specific approach, maybe not with the pattern-seeing, but the function will persist because the settlement needs it. They'll find their own methods, see their own connections, serve the same basic purpose through different means. This is how knowledge continues without writing, how communities maintain themselves across generations.

Or maybe I'm wrong. Maybe no one will replace this function. Maybe the patterns will remain unseen, the connections unmade, and the settlement will continue anyway, surviving through other forms of knowledge, other types of attention. Maybe what I do is less necessary than I think, a useful luxury rather than survival requirement.

I can't know. I can only continue the work, make the arrangements, track the patterns, offer what insights emerge, and trust that if it's valuable, it will persist; if not, it will end with me, and that will be appropriate too.

## Why I Stay

I've thought about leaving—everyone does. The lowlands exist, larger settlements with more people, easier conditions, life without the constant damp and overwhelming fauna. I could go. Nothing binds me here except choice.

But I stay because this place makes sense to me in ways other places wouldn't. The patterns here are visible, rapid, intense. Evolution runs fast, ecology shifts noticeably, changes manifest within observable timescales. My particular cognitive style, which might be disability elsewhere, is gift here. The pattern-seeing that makes normal human interaction difficult makes understanding this place possible.

I stay because I'm adapted—physically yes, but more importantly cognitively. My mind has shaped itself around this place's specific information landscape. I can't easily recalibrate to environments where patterns move more slowly, where connections are less visible, where the overwhelming life is absent.

I stay because leaving would mean abandoning the work, and the work feels important even when I question its necessity. Someone should track these patterns, document these relationships, watch what's happening as this young ecosystem continues becoming itself. If not me, who? And if no one, what's lost?

I stay because, despite loneliness and strangeness and marginal social position, I'm known here. People understand my utility even if they don't fully understand me. They've made space for my oddness, incorporated my weirdness into community function. Elsewhere I'd have to establish this understanding anew, and I'm not confident I could.

I stay because the alternative—the bright, dry, quiet, emptier world of the lowlands—feels wrong in ways I can't fully articulate. My body wants the damp and dim. My mind wants the overwhelming stimulation, the constant movement of thousands of lives. My spirit, if I have one, wants the strangeness, the uncertainty, the perpetual edge between known and unknown.

So I remain in my workshop in the mid-levels of the amphitheater, arranging stones and moss and beetle carapaces into temporary maps of invisible relationships, watching the fog roll through, listening to the sound of water finding paths through stone, paying attention to everything, existing in the pattern and of the pattern and as the pattern, one strange human among many strange creatures in this strange and becoming place.
