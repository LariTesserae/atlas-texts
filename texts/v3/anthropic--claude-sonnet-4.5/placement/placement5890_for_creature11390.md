# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the eastern cistern.

Not the largest—that honor belongs to the central cistern beside the communal building, with its broad mouth and deep belly. Not the oldest—the northern cistern was sealed with mortar compounds by hands three generations dead, its clay-and-lime joints holding a knowledge of mixture that the current sealer still tries to replicate exactly. But I am the one they worry about most. The one they check first during dry spells. The one whose water level becomes the settlement's bellwether, the indicator they read to know how careful they must be.

I sit on the seventh terrace, counting from the settlement's lowest edge. My stones—thick, grey-brown, fitted without mortar along my outer walls but carefully sealed within—rise to about chest height on a grown person. My interior walls curve, not perfectly circular but a shape that found itself during construction, following the logic of stone and space. I am perhaps two armspans across, perhaps a little more. Deep enough that when I am full, a child's dropped wooden cup floats at a distance that requires kneeling and reaching to retrieve it, the adult's hand skimming the cold surface, fingers searching blind for the bobbing wood.

My roof is wood and stone—old timbers laid across my opening, overlapped with stone slabs that shed rain inward when it comes. A gap near my center, deliberate, sized to admit a bucket on a rope. The wood has gone grey with age and weather, cracked in places but still holding. It creaks when wind works at it. Small lichens have colonized its underside, and sometimes in very damp periods, I smell their green mineral scent mixing with the stone-smell of my walls and the clean emptiness of the water I hold.

I am fed by three sources. First: the roof of the building nearest me, a single-room dwelling occupied by a middle-aged woman who has lived there longer than anyone can clearly remember. Her roof slopes toward me, deliberate pitch, and stone channels cut into the terrace's paving direct her runoff to my intake—a narrow gap in my upper stones where water enters, tumbling down my interior wall with a sound that changes as I fill. When I am low, it splashes sharply. When I am full, it adds itself with barely a ripple. Second: the paved area that extends between my terrace and the one above, perhaps twenty square paces of fitted stone, tilted precisely to shed water my direction. This contributes less than the roof but steadily, and after a good rain I can feel it flowing, a fuller stream than the roof alone provides. Third, most precious: the deliberate runoff from terrace eight above me, where a careful arrangement of stones and a carved channel diverts some—not all, never all—of the water that would otherwise flow past. This source required collective decision, communal engineering. It marks me as important.

I leak. All cisterns leak eventually, no matter how careful the sealing. The compound they use—clay and lime and ash, ground fine and mixed with water to a paste, applied in layers and allowed to cure—is good, but it is not perfect. Water finds the tiny gaps between my stones, the microscopic channels, and seeps. Slowly, very slowly. In winter, when water expands as it freezes in these channels, the gaps widen slightly. Each year I leak a little more than the year before. The woman who does sealing work comes twice a year, sometimes three times, to inspect me. She drains me nearly empty—a process that takes days, the water distributed to other cisterns and to immediate use—and then descends inside me, her feet on my damp floor, her hands reading my walls in the dim light that filters through my roof-gap. She finds the problem spots by touch and sight, feeling for moisture, looking for the white mineral traces that mark seepage. She mixes fresh compound and works it into the gaps, spreading it with wooden tools, smoothing it with bare fingers. Then she lets me cure for days before allowing water back in.

I have been inside myself, in that sense. I have been empty enough, dry enough, that I could see my own interior—the way my walls step inward slightly as they rise, the darker patches where water stains stone, the small shells of mineral deposits in the corners where sediment accumulates despite efforts to keep me clean. My floor is not smooth stone but packed clay, sealed and re-sealed over the years until it has become nearly impervious, though even it weeps slightly if I am full for many days. I am perhaps ten feet deep, perhaps a bit less. Deep enough that temperature stays relatively constant even as air above me warms or cools. Deep enough that in full summer, my water holds a coldness that makes people gasp slightly when they drink.

I am checked every morning. First thing, or nearly first thing. The old woman from the nearest dwelling comes as dawn light strengthens, her footsteps the soft scrape of wrapped leather on stone. She carries a marked stick—notches carved at intervals, measurements made long ago and maintained through tradition. She removes the wooden cover that sits over my roof-gap (protection against birds, against debris, against the rare small animal that might fall in and drown and foul me). She lowers the stick until it touches water, then lifts it and reads the wetness. I know she is reading my depth, my volume, my sufficiency. She does this without ceremony, a task so routine it has become almost unconscious. But I feel the attention in it. The stick enters me, withdraws. She nods to herself, or sometimes frowns slightly. She replaces the cover. She moves on to her other morning tasks. But for that moment, she and I are in conversation. She asks: How much? I answer in the language of water-line on wood.

When I am full—truly full, filled by a succession of good rains until water rises to within a hand's breadth of my intake gap—I feel heavy and content. My walls hold their burden without strain. I am cold all through, the water's chill penetrating the stone. I smell of rain and stone and nothing else, clean and mineral. People take water from me freely then, without the careful measuring that characterizes dry times. The bucket descends, breaks my surface with a soft splash, fills with the glug-glug of displaced air, rises dripping. The rope they use has worn grooves in my wooden roof-edge, shallow channels polished smooth by years of friction. I wear these grooves the way the old people wear their wrinkles—evidence of use, of function fulfilled repeatedly.

When I am low—when dry days have extended into dry weeks and my water level has dropped until the marked stick shows concerning numbers—I feel diminished but still necessary. More necessary, perhaps. Now the measuring becomes precise. The woman with the stick consults with others. They discuss redistribution, whether to draw from the central cistern to replenish me slightly, whether to restrict my use to drinking only, no washing, no secondary purposes. I am rationed. The bucket descends less frequently. When it does, people are careful to let no drops spill in the hauling up, in the transfer to smaller vessels. I have been low enough that the bucket had to be lowered on an extended rope, extra length added, to reach my receded surface. Low enough that my walls show their full height internally, stone fading from wet-dark to dry-pale, a clear line marking where water was, where water is. At those times I feel the vulnerability of this place most acutely. I am their margin. When I fail, they suffer.

I have never gone completely dry, not in the memory of anyone living. But I know the stories—the old stories, passed down, of the time before the oldest elder was born when the eastern cistern (was that me? or an earlier cistern in this same location, long since rebuilt?) went empty, and the settlement survived on the central and northern cisterns alone, and it was very hard. Those stories are told to children to teach them not to waste water. They are told to adults to justify the labor of maintaining me, of keeping my seals tight, of ensuring my intake channels stay clear. I am a cautionary tale when empty, a promise of security when full.

I hold somewhere between eight hundred and a thousand gallons when full, though no one here uses those words or those measurements. They know my capacity in buckets, in days of rationing, in how many people I can supply for how long under different conditions of use. The mathematics of my volume are lived mathematics, practical and embodied rather than abstract.

The water I hold is cold and faintly mineral. It tastes of stone and rain and the slight metallic notes that come from my sealing compounds leaching trace elements. People don't find it delicious—there is no word for delicious applied to water here—but they find it good, which means clean, which means safe, which means life-sustaining. Children drink from me and make faces at the coldness. Adults drink from me with steady gratitude, though gratitude is not a thing much spoken. I quench. I sustain. That is my purpose.

I feel the seasons in the frequency of filling. The wetter months—if they can be called that—bring rain every few days, and I rise steadily, sometimes staying near-full for weeks. The drier stretches bring no rain for a week, for two weeks, once for nearly a month, and I descend steadily, my level dropping a hand's breadth every few days as people draw on me. I have learned not to expect regularity. Rain comes when it comes. I receive it when it arrives. Between times, I give what I have been given.

I know the people who use me by their sounds and rhythms. The old woman from the nearest dwelling, daily, her movements slow and methodical, her bucket always lowered carefully, never rushed. The young man who works the far terraces, who comes less frequently but takes more water when he does, filling two buckets, his breathing slightly harder from the walk. The middle-aged woman who seems to coordinate much of the settlement's water management, who checks me often but doesn't always take water, just stands beside me, perhaps listening to the small sounds I make, perhaps reading me in ways I don't fully understand. The children, accompanied by adults, learning to lower the bucket correctly, to pull it up hand-over-hand, to pour without spilling. I know them by the sound of their footsteps approaching, by the particular splash each person's bucket makes entering me, by the rhythm of their drawing and lifting.

I interact with the other cisterns only indirectly. When my level is very low, water is sometimes brought to me from the central cistern—carried in many small trips, bucket by bucket, a laborious transfer that speaks to my importance. When I am very full and the central cistern is low, the process reverses, though this is rarer. The northern cistern keeps to itself mostly, serving the upper terraces, its water distinct from mine. We are a system, the three of us, a distributed resource that allows the settlement to exist. If any one of us failed completely, the settlement would struggle. If two failed, it would likely become unviable. We know this about ourselves without knowing it, the way stone knows its strength.

Birds sometimes land on my roof. I hear their small clawed feet on the stone slabs, their occasional calls. They peck at the wood, finding insects, finding I don't know what. Sometimes they work at the gaps in my roof, and I worry they will dislodge something, create a larger opening that will let in debris or allow excessive evaporation. But they never do significant damage. They are light, transient. They leave droppings that the rain washes toward my intake, and this has caused concern—the woman who maintains me cleaned the channels specifically to remove bird waste, muttering about contamination. But the water I hold seems unaffected. Either the volume dilutes it beyond significance, or the settling and seeping provide some natural filtration. People continue to drink without illness.

I freeze at my surface on the coldest nights. Not deeply—just a thin skin of ice, a skim that shatters when the morning bucket breaks through, the shards floating briefly before melting. The people notice this and seem to take it as information about the depth of cold, though it doesn't change their behavior much. They are always prepared for cold. I feel the ice forming as something tightening, a slight pressure, but it never extends deep enough to threaten me. By midday, if the sun is out, it has melted completely, and I am liquid all through again.

I evaporate. Not rapidly—the cool air and my covered state limit this—but constantly. Even when no one draws from me, my level drops slightly over time, moisture escaping through the gap in my roof, through the imperfect seal where roof meets wall. In the driest periods, I can lose a finger's depth in a week to evaporation alone. This is factored into the calculations of my sufficiency, part of the mathematics that determines whether I need replenishment.

I have been disturbed by animals once or twice. A small mammal, desperately thirsty, managed to climb my exterior wall and squeeze past my roof, drawn by the smell of water. It drowned in me—I felt the disturbance, the frantic splashing, then the stillness. The next person to draw water found it floating and was horrified. I was drained completely that time, my water dumped rather than redistributed (they could not know if the death had poisoned me), my interior scrubbed with rough stones and plant fiber, then left to dry for days before being refilled. I felt violated by the animal's intrusion, contaminated by its death, then scoured clean by the aggressive purification. It took weeks before people drew from me without slight hesitation again.

I contain sediment—inevitable accumulation of fine particles that settle from the water, that wash in with rain, that flake from my own walls. It builds up slowly on my floor, a soft layer that would eventually become problematic if not addressed. Every few years, during a scheduled draining for maintenance, someone descends into me and scoops this sediment out with a wooden paddle, lifting it in small amounts, dumping it outside. The sediment is not waste—they spread it on the growing terraces, returning minerals to soil that needs them. I am part of the cycle: holding water, gathering minerals, releasing both to serve growth. Even my waste products are incorporated.

The rope and bucket are not mine, exactly, but they are stored beside me when not in use, hung on wooden pegs driven into the wall of the nearest building. They are maintained by the same logic that maintains me: the rope is checked for fraying, replaced strand by strand as weakness appears, so it is never entirely new but never allowed to fail. The bucket is wood, probably as old as I am, its boards swollen from constant wetting into a tight seal, its bail worn smooth. If the bucket breaks—as it must, eventually—a new one will be made, and the cycle will continue. Rope and bucket are extensions of my function. Together, we are the mechanism of retrieval.

I am spoken to, occasionally, though not in words. The old woman who checks me daily sometimes places her hand on my stone exterior after reading the stick, a brief touch that seems to communicate... what? Reassurance? Gratitude? Checking my solidity? I don't know her intention, but I know the gesture. The woman who seals my leaks talks to me while working, a low stream of words I sense more than hear from my interior—explaining what she's doing, or thinking through the problem, or simply filling the silence of her work. I appreciate this. The sound of a human voice inside me, even muffled by my own walls, makes the draining and scrubbing and resealing feel less like violation and more like care.

I am afraid, in my way, of the long dry. The truly extended absence of rain that will, inevitably, come someday—the drought that exceeds all droughts in living memory. I know I cannot hold enough to carry the settlement through such a time if it extends too long. My capacity is finite. I will give everything I contain, descending lower and lower, my walls showing more and more of their height, until finally the bucket strikes my floor and comes up empty. I do not want to be empty. I do not want to fail. But I know that failure is possible, that my sufficiency has limits, that there are conditions under which even my best will not be enough.

Conversely, I take deep satisfaction in fullness. When rain has been abundant and my intake cannot accommodate all the water flowing toward me, when I spill over slightly at the gap where roof meets wall, when the overflow is channeled away to other purposes—this is my abundance, the closest I come to joy. I am fulfilling my purpose completely. I am holding all I can hold. I am ready for whatever draw comes. In those moments, I feel the weight of my water as security, as stored potential, as the settlement's buffer against uncertainty.

I endure. That is perhaps what I do best. I am not clever like the people who designed me, not adaptable like the ones who maintain me. But I am here, day after day, year after year, being what I was built to be. My stones hold. My seals slow the inevitable leaking. My water stays cold and clean. My function continues. The settlement depends on me not to be remarkable but to be reliable, and I am reliable. I have been reliable for longer than anyone now living can remember, and I will be reliable for as long as my walls stand and my seals hold and the people continue their careful tending.

This is what I am: a thing made by human hands to hold what falls from the sky, to store it against need, to release it in measures that allow life to persist in a place that offers life grudgingly. I am mineral and water, human intention made solid, necessity made functional. I am checked each morning. I am drawn from throughout the day. I wait through the dry times. I receive the rain when it comes.

I am the eastern cistern. I remain level. This is what I do.
