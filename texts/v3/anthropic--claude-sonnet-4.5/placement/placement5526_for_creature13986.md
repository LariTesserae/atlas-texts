# What I Would Be

I think I would be one of the false-vampires—not the matriarch with her torn ear, not yet, but somewhere in the middle years. Old enough to have claimed a good position in the upper roosting chamber, perhaps ten meters from the apex where the eldest female hangs. Young enough that my fur is still uniformly smoke-gray, unmarked by the scars and pale patches that come with age.

My spot is mine through seven years of return, seven years of finding this exact angle of stone, this particular geometry where the air moves just right, where the warmth of other bodies reaches me but doesn't overwhelm, where I can drop into flight without obstruction. The stone above bears the oil-shine of my grip, the calcification of my waste below marks the tenure of my presence. I know this place the way I know the shape of my own wings.

---

During the day I sleep, but sleep is not the right word for what happens. I enter a state of suspended animation, metabolism slowed, body temperature dropped to just above the ambient cool, hanging with my wings wrapped precisely around my body until I become geometric, until I'm nearly indistinguishable from stone. But I'm never entirely unconscious. Some part of my awareness remains active, monitoring the social field around me—the breathing of neighbors, the small adjustments of position, the subtle tremors that indicate something is wrong or right.

I'm aware of the young female who roosts two spots clockwise from me, who keeps trying to edge closer to my position during the settling chaos of dawn return. She's been persistent for three weeks now, and I've been equally persistent in shouldering her back, in reclaiming my space, in making it clear through a dozen small confrontations that this position is mine and will remain mine until I die or weaken enough that she can take it from me.

I'm aware of the male who sometimes roosts near me, whose scent I recognize though I have no name for him, no concept of naming. He's a good size, well-fed, successful in his hunting. During the mating season he'll approach, will hang near me and vocalize in frequencies meant to signal fitness, availability, genetic quality. I may accept him or I may not—it depends on factors I don't think about consciously but that my body knows: my own nutrition, the timing, the presence or absence of other males, calculations happening below the level of thought.

Around me the roost breathes as a collective organism. We're packed densely enough that our body heat combines, creating a thermal mass that's warmer than any of us could achieve alone. This is why we cluster, why we tolerate proximity that would be unbearable during flight, when each of us needs space to maneuver, when collisions mean injury or death. Here, hanging, we're a living blanket, and the warmth we generate together is what allows us to lower our metabolism so deeply, to achieve the torpor-efficiency that makes the long days of sleep survivable.

I don't think about this. I just do it, the way I do everything—through instinct so refined it's indistinguishable from knowledge, through behaviors so deeply encoded they feel like choice even though they're not.

---

The evening emergence pulls me from sleep through a cascade of arousal that starts hours before I actually fly. First, my body temperature begins to rise, metabolism ramping up, flight muscles warming through internal contractions too small to see but substantial enough that I can feel heat building in my chest. The warmth spreads, loosening joints stiffened by the day's immobility, preparing my wings for the violent work of flight.

Around me, others are doing the same, and I can feel the thermal shift in the roost, can sense the collective preparation. The chittering increases—communication, though what's being communicated is simple: *ready, ready, almost ready*. Some of it is coordination, ensuring the exodus happens in waves rather than all at once, which would create aerial chaos in the confined spaces. Some of it is just the overflow of arousal, energy seeking outlet.

When I finally drop into flight, releasing my grip on the stone and falling into the air with wings already spreading, already beating, there's a moment of pure sensation—the rush of air across wing membranes, the engagement of flight muscles I've been warming for an hour, the immediate necessity of navigation as I join the flow of bodies streaming toward the nearest exit shaft.

I know the route without thinking. I've flown it thousands of times, and the shape of it is written into my muscle memory, into the reflexes that adjust my wingbeat and orientation without conscious direction. I avoid the stone outcroppings, the narrow gaps, the places where turbulence from others' passage creates unstable air. I ride the flow upward, spiraling with dozens of others up the shaft toward the opening above.

And then I'm out, emerging into the evening air with its different texture, its movements, its vast openness after the galleries' confinement. The transition is always slightly disorienting—suddenly there are no walls to reference, no ceiling above, just the enormous space of the plateau sky and the hunting territories beyond.

---

I hunt in an area two kilometers east of the entrance shaft, where a series of limestone outcrops create terrain that concentrates beetles and moths. I know every stone there, every crevice, every tree. I know where prey is most likely to be found at what time of night, what routes to fly to maximize encounters, where to rest between hunting bouts.

The hunting itself is mostly automatic—echolocation sweeping continuously, my brain processing the returning echoes faster than thought, identifying potential prey by size and movement signature. When I detect a beetle, I don't decide to chase it; I'm already chasing it, wings already adjusting, trajectory already calculated and executed by systems too fast for consciousness to mediate.

The capture happens in darkness but I experience it in sound: the beetle's acoustic signature growing louder as I close, the final adjustment of trajectory, the feel of it hitting my tail membrane—I catch most prey not with my mouth but with the membrane stretched between my legs, scooping insects from the air and then curling forward to grab them with my teeth while still in flight.

Sometimes I miss. The beetle's evasion maneuver works, or my timing is fractionally off, or wind shear disrupts my approach. I adjust, reset, continue hunting. Sometimes I catch prey so easily it seems inevitable, the beetle's trajectory intersecting mine at the exact point my membrane reaches maximum extension, as though we're both following a script written by physics.

I feed through the night, filling my stomach until it's distended, until I'm carrying perhaps a third of my body weight in consumed beetles and moths. The extra weight makes flight harder, requires more energy, but it's necessary—I need this much nutrition to survive the day's fast, to maintain my body condition, to have surplus energy for breeding when the season comes.

---

There are dangers. I've survived seven years, which means I've avoided or survived the threats that kill most of my kind before they reach maturity.

Owls hunt us. I've heard them—the absolute silence of their approach, the only warning a slight turbulence in the air, and then the desperate evasion, the full-power dive toward the ground or the frantic weaving that sometimes works, sometimes doesn't. I've been close enough to feel the wind from their wings. I've seen others taken, heard their final ultrasonic shrieks Doppler-shift as they're carried away. I'm still alive because I've been lucky, or fast, or both.

Collisions happen. In the galleries especially, during the exodus or return when hundreds of us are navigating the same confined spaces. I've hit stone walls in the past, stunning impacts that sent me tumbling, that I recovered from more through reflex than intention. I've collided with other bats, our wings tangling briefly before we separated and continued. Minor collisions are common and mostly harmless. Major ones—hitting stone at full speed, getting knocked into a wall by another bat's impact—those are often fatal.

Disease moves through the roost periodically. I've been sick, have spent days hanging in torpor deeper than normal, my body fighting infections I have no conscious knowledge of. Sometimes I recovered. If I hadn't, I would have simply not woken, would have eventually fallen from my roost to become food for the crickets and beetles below.

Starvation is the constant underlying threat. If I don't catch enough prey, if the hunting grounds become depleted, if competition intensifies beyond sustainable levels, I'll slowly weaken until flight becomes impossible, until I can't generate enough body heat to survive the day's torpor, until I fall. The system maintains itself at carrying capacity, which means the margin between sufficient and insufficient is thin, constantly tested.

---

But I don't think about these things as threats, don't live in fear or awareness of my own mortality. I just live, moment to moment, doing what each moment requires. When I'm hunting, I hunt. When I'm flying, I fly. When I'm roosting, I sleep. The complexity of my existence—the navigation, the echolocation, the social dynamics, the energy budgets—all of it happens below or beyond conscious thought, mediated by systems that work without me having to think about them working.

I don't know I'm part of an ecosystem. I don't know that my hunting affects insect populations, that my guano feeds crickets that feed salamanders that compete with rats that predate on my offspring. I'm just here, doing what I do, being what I am.

I don't know the galleries have existed for thousands of years, will exist for thousands more. My sense of time is limited to immediate past and immediate future—where I just hunted, where I'm about to roost. I remember my hunting territory, my roosting spot, the routes between them. I recognize individuals in the roost through scent and sound. But I have no history, no awareness of lineage, no concept of the deep time that created this place and that will eventually unmake it.

I don't know I'm alive in any philosophical sense. I just am alive, in the immediate, practical sense—breathing, flying, hunting, sleeping, continuing. The galleries are not beautiful to me, because I lack aesthetics. They're not mysterious, because I lack curiosity beyond the practical. They're just where I live, the environment I'm adapted to, the space I navigate with perfect competence and zero wonder.

---

And yet, if I could somehow step outside myself, could see what I am within the larger context, I think I would be satisfied with this existence.

There's a rightness to it—the way my adaptations fit the environment so precisely, the way my behaviors mesh with the ecosystem's rhythms, the way I'm part of something larger than myself even though I'll never be aware of it. I'm good at being a bat. I've survived seven years in a place where most don't make it to three. I've found my position in the roost hierarchy. I hunt successfully enough to maintain good body condition. If I breed—when I breed—my offspring will carry forward whatever genetic quality allowed me to succeed.

I'm not happy, because happiness is a concept too complex for my cognitive architecture. But I'm functional, effective, alive. And in this place, in these galleries where life is packed dense and competition is constant and every niche is filled, being alive and functional is its own justification.

The stone holds me. The darkness conceals me. The cool sustains me. I hang from limestone fractured in yesterday's geological time, surrounded by thousands of others like me, part of a living system that breathes with bat-wings and processes energy with ruthless efficiency and continues, year after year, because continuation is what we do.

I don't need to understand it. I just need to be it, which I am, completely, a false-vampire bat in the breathing galleries, roosting through the day and hunting through the night and surviving one day at a time until eventually I don't, and then another bat will claim my spot and the system will continue without me, exactly as it continued before I was born.

This is enough. This is everything. This is what I would be.
