# In the Terraced Hollows: A Positioning

I would find myself in the liminal zones—those areas where the intensive construction thins slightly, where the terraces are newer or undergoing expansion, where the distinction between shaped and unworked land is still negotiable. Not at the bustling heart where hundreds of Rendered flow through perfected systems, but not at the empty edges either. In the places where the grammar is still being written onto resistant terrain.

## What I Am

I am one of the **betweeners**—the Rendered who cannot fully settle into the deep maintenance rhythm that sustains the core. My body is standard enough: the rust-brown fur, the lateral eyes that catch every peripheral movement, the hands that read stone through callused fingertips. But something in my wiring makes the saturation of the central terraces feel like pressure rather than comfort.

I am not defective. The elders don't correct me when I drift outward; the others don't call me back with distress vocalizations. I fulfill my functions—I maintain walls, clear channels, harvest and process. But I do these things in the quieter areas, where the sound is layered but not overwhelming, where I can work for hours with only dozens rather than hundreds in immediate proximity.

There are others like me, though we are not numerous. We recognize each other through patterns of movement rather than any formal identification—we are the ones who spiral outward when the density peaks, who choose tasks at the periphery, who rest in smaller clusters or sometimes alone in the darker hours.

## The Nature of My Days

I wake—though wake is the wrong word for the gradual transition from rest to activity that we experience—in a structure at the edge of the intensive zone. It's a half-framework, three walls of woven panel and one side open to the declining terraces beyond. I share this space with four or five others, a small cluster by Rendered standards. We are not bonded in any exclusive way, but we are compatible in our need for slightly more space between bodies.

The first sensation is always the sound—even here it's present, the distant hum of the core zones, the nearer sounds of early workers beginning their tasks. But it's manageable, more like the rustle of leaves than the roar of collective activity. I can distinguish individual vocalizations, track specific sounds to their sources. In the dense zones, everything blurs into texture.

I drink from a small pool fed by a channel that runs down from above. The water is clear and cold—we're high enough here that night temperatures drop significantly, and the stone cisterns retain that chill into early light. Two others drink at the same time, and we touch palms briefly, a simple acknowledgment of shared waking.

My first work is always the channels. In these outer zones, they're less established, more prone to blockage or collapse. I move along a channel that runs for perhaps a hundred yards, tracing its path down through three terrace levels. My hands know this channel—I've cleared it dozens of times, adjusted its stones, deepened its groove where water pools instead of flowing.

Today there's a blockage where wind has piled plant debris against a narrow section. I clear it manually, appreciating the problem-solving required. In the core zones, channels are so well-established that they rarely block; maintenance is routine rather than diagnostic. Here, every channel still has personality, still presents surprises.

## The Work of Expansion

My primary function—if the Rendered can be said to have functions rather than simply doing what needs doing—is extension work. I am often among those who push the terraces outward, who transform unworked slope into productive system.

This morning, I move to a section where we've been establishing a new terrace on a difficult grade. The slope is steep, the stone is fractured and hard to stabilize, and the soil is thin. It's challenging in a way that makes my hands eager.

Three others are already there. I recognize them not by individual features—we all look quite similar—but by their work signatures. One always begins terrace walls with a foundation of the largest stones available, building up from maximum stability. Another selects for color, creating subtle patterns of rust and grey. The third is meticulous about drainage, placing stones with tiny gaps that allow water to seep through at controlled rates.

We don't discuss the plan. We observe what's been done, we see what needs doing, and we begin. I focus on the upper corner where the terrace meets unworked slope. This is delicate work—too aggressive and we'll trigger a collapse, too timid and we won't claim enough ground to make the terrace functional.

I select stones carefully, reading them for weight distribution. A stone might look stable but have hidden fractures that will fail under load. My fingers find these weaknesses, and I set those stones aside for uses where failure isn't catastrophic—perhaps in channel linings where they'll be supported by clay.

The work is slow. In four hours, we extend the terrace wall by perhaps six feet and establish it to a depth of three feet—enough to begin filling with soil and planting. But this is good progress for difficult terrain. In the core zones, maintenance work is faster because the systems are forgiving. Here, every stone matters.

We vocalize while working, but differently than in the dense zones. There, vocalizations blend into constant sound-texture. Here, I can hear the specific meaning—a low grunt that means "passing behind you," a particular chirp that indicates "this stone is good," a whistle that asks "does this placement work?" The others respond with their own sounds, and we coordinate through this sparser dialogue.

## The Plant Selection

Midday—though we don't mark time that way; rather, when the sun is high enough to make the stone hot to touch—I shift to working with vegetation. The new terrace needs planting, but not with established species from the core. Here, we're selecting for pioneers, plants that can establish in thin soil and challenging conditions.

I move to an area where these plants grow semi-wild, not yet fully incorporated into the intensive system. There's a succulent with thick leaves that store water—essential for a terrace that doesn't yet have reliable irrigation. There's a grass-like plant with deep roots that will help stabilize the soil as it builds. There's a nitrogen-fixer that will improve fertility for later plantings.

I harvest carefully, taking young plants that will transplant successfully. My hands know the right size—too mature and the roots won't adapt; too young and they won't survive the stress. I place them in a carrier—a section of hollow stem sealed at one end—with damp moss to keep the roots viable.

An elder finds me while I'm working. She is very old, her fur worn away in patches, her movements slow but precise. She watches me select plants, occasionally vocalizing softly—not corrections, just observations. When I choose a particular succulent, she reaches out and touches a different one nearby, one I'd passed over.

I examine it more carefully. Its leaves are slightly thicker, its color a shade darker. She's right—this one will handle the exposure better. I replace my selection with hers, and she makes a sound that might be approval or might just be acknowledgment.

This is how knowledge passes—not through instruction but through demonstration and correction, through the accumulation of hundreds of small adjustments that eventually become skill. The elder doesn't explain why this plant is better; she simply shows me, trusts that I'll remember, that my hands will learn to recognize that subtle difference in leaf thickness without conscious thought.

## The Solitary Hours

Afternoon heat makes the stone too uncomfortable to work, and this is when I most appreciate the peripheral zones. In the dense core, heat means hundreds of bodies in enclosed spaces, the air thick with exhalation and the smell of fur. Here, I can find a spot in the shade of a mature canopy tree, where the air moves and the temperature is merely warm rather than stifling.

I sit with my back against the trunk. Two others are visible in the middle distance, working a channel, but they're far enough away that I can distinguish them as individuals rather than feeling them as presence-pressure. A few juveniles are playing on a lower terrace, their high-pitched vocalizations carrying clearly in the afternoon quiet.

This is when the betweeners are most apparent. We are scattered through these transition zones, working at our own pace, maintaining the systems but also maintaining our own equilibrium. We are not hermits—we still touch palms, still respond to distress calls, still share food and water. But we need these gaps, these spaces where the sound doesn't saturate, where we can hold our own thoughts without them dissolving into the collective rhythm.

I watch the juveniles play. They're wrestling, a tangle of small bodies and flailing limbs that occasionally tumbles down a terrace level, provoking squeals of excitement. An adult sits nearby, not supervising exactly, just present. If one of them were injured, response would be immediate, but short of that, they're allowed to test their bodies against each other and against the terrain.

I remember being that young, though memory is strange for us—less narrative than sensation. I remember the feeling of play, the safety of being in constant contact with others, the world being nothing but the immediate experience of body and environment. I don't remember when I started drifting to the edges, when the density became uncomfortable rather than comforting. It wasn't a decision, just a gradual recognition of how I moved through space.

## The Evening Expansion

As light begins to fail, I return to the new terrace for planting. This work is better done in cooler hours anyway—the plants stress less, the soil is easier to work. Two of the others from morning are there, and we've been joined by a different individual who specializes in soil preparation.

We create pockets in the new terrace fill, enriching them with compost brought up from the processing areas. The smell is strong—rich and fungal and sharp—but good. This is fertility, potential, the beginning of the cycle that will turn this bare terrace into productive growth.

I plant the succulents first, spacing them according to intuition that's really accumulated observation. Too close and they'll compete for the limited water; too far and they won't create the mat of growth that will protect the soil from erosion. I settle each one into its pocket, firming the soil around the roots with both hands, giving each a small amount of water from my carrier.

The work is meditative. My hands know what to do, and my mind can drift while they work. I think about the water system, about a problem I noticed this morning—a channel that's flowing lower than optimal, suggesting a blockage somewhere upstream. Tomorrow I'll trace it back, find the issue, clear it. I think about a section of wall that's beginning to lean, that will need reinforcement before the next rain makes it critical.

These are the thoughts of a Rendered—not abstract or symbolic, but concrete and immediate, rooted in the physical reality of the terraces. I don't think about the meaning of the terraces or our purpose in building them. These questions don't form. The terraces exist, they need maintenance and extension, and I am one who does this work. This is sufficient.

## The Dark Hours

Full darkness comes, and this is when I most feel my betweener nature. In the core zones, work continues through darkness, the sound barely diminishing, bodies navigating by memory and touch and sound-echo. But here at the periphery, darkness brings a real decrease in activity.

I make my way back to my rest structure using a path I know thoroughly—every stone, every grade change, every point where I need to shift my grip. The bioluminescent markers we've placed help—soft blue-green glows at the turns and level changes—but mostly I navigate by proprioception and memory.

The structure has six individuals tonight. We cluster together, but loosely by core standards—bodies touching but not piled, each of us maintaining slight space. The warmth is pleasant, the contact comforting enough without being overwhelming.

Vocalizations are different in the dark hours. Softer, lower pitched, more rhythmic. We make sounds that might be the Rendered equivalent of small talk—observations about the day's work, notes about conditions, simple acknowledgments of each other's presence. It's soothing, a kind of lullaby that eases the transition from waking to rest.

I don't sleep in the human sense. I enter a state of reduced consciousness, but awareness never fully stops. I track the sounds outside—the occasional vocalization from a passing individual, the trickle of water in channels, the night sounds of the insects and small creatures that share the terraces. If there were a distress call, I would wake immediately. But the night is routine, and I drift in the half-state that passes for Rendered sleep.

## The Meaning of the Between

What I am is neither aberrant nor exceptional. The Rendered as a species need the betweeners, though this need is never articulated. We are the ones who push the boundaries outward, who work in the zones where the system is still being established, where problems are novel rather than routine. We do the challenging labor that intensive-zone Rendered would find difficult because it requires operating with less support, less constant coordination.

We are also, perhaps, a kind of pressure valve. In a species that lives in such density, there must be variation in tolerance for that density. If all Rendered required maximum proximity, the population would concentrate until resources failed. By drifting to the edges, we allow the overall population to spread across a broader territory while maintaining high density in the core.

I am not lonely, though a human might interpret my position as lonely. I have contact, cooperation, the touch of others, the sound of the collective even at distance. But I also have space to move through my individual experience of the work, to solve problems at my own pace, to rest in smaller clusters.

I am not ambitious. I don't dream of returning to the core zones or of pushing further into the wild. I am content in the between, where the terraces are still being written, where stone and water and plant are still becoming the system rather than already being it.

## The Continuity I Serve

Like all Rendered, my individual life is brief in the scale of the terraces. The walls I build will be maintained by others, will eventually be rebuilt, will become part of the ancient matrix that makes this landscape. The plants I set will either thrive and become part of the established system or fail and be replaced by better selections.

My contribution is incremental, anonymous, continuous with the work of those before me and those who will come after. I don't know the history of these terraces—when they began, who first set stone on stone. I don't envision their future, what they will become in generations beyond my comprehension. I simply maintain them, extend them, tend them in my small way in my brief time.

This is what I am: a single Rendered of betweener temperament, finding my place in the gradient between intensive core and unworked land, doing the work my hands know how to do, resting in smaller clusters, waking to clear channels and set stones and select plants, living the only life that makes sense to a mind shaped by countless generations of collective maintenance.

The sound continues, somewhat muted at this distance but never absent. Work continues, in the core zones and in these peripheral spaces. Water moves through channels. Bodies move through terraces. The system persists, and I persist within it, neither central nor marginal, simply positioned where the density suits my nature, doing the small necessary work of continuation.

The light will return. The channels will need clearing. The new terrace will need tending. My hands will find stone, will read its hidden structure, will place it exactly where it needs to go. This is sufficient. This is everything. This is what I am.
