# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the fifth level sorting room of the Albrecht & Sohns Textile Consolidation, in that strange afternoon hour when the daylight shift is winding down but the gas lamps haven't yet been trimmed up for evening. The light comes gray and exhausted through the high clerestory windows — windows that haven't been properly cleaned in a decade, their glass filmed with the fine particulate that settles on everything. It's the hour when the looms in the floor below have achieved a kind of hypnotic rhythm, when the noise stops being noise and becomes texture, when you could almost forget there's sound at all until something breaks the pattern and you're startled by the silence.

I would be sitting on a high stool at a sorting table, one of six tables arranged in two rows down the center of the room. The table's surface is zinc sheet, worn to a soft gray by decades of fabric sliding across it, by the oil from countless hands, by the slight acidity of the dyes. There are permanent stains in it, marks that tell stories if you know how to read them: the blue-green where indigo soaked into a scratch, the rust-orange where someone left an iron ring overnight, the pale rectangle where someone's identification papers sat in the same spot for so many years that the zinc beneath stayed brighter when they finally retired.

The room smells of sizing and lanolin, of the particular mustiness of natural fibers in a damp place, of the linseed oil used to maintain the wooden bins that line the walls. In the corner, there's always a faint smell of tea from the shared pot that sits on a heating plate — a luxury, that heating plate, negotiated three years ago after the sorters collectively refused to work through a particularly bitter cold snap until management conceded the point.

# What I Would Be

I would be a fabric grader. Not the newest — I've been here seven years, long enough that my hands know the work without my conscious attention. Not the eldest — Frau Bachmann has been at the table by the north window for twenty-three years, and her knowledge is encyclopedic, almost frightening. Somewhere in the middle, which is itself a kind of achievement. Seven years means I survived the first brutal months when the pace seemed impossible, when the vocabulary of defects was overwhelming, when the foremen watched like hawks waiting for me to fail. Seven years means I've earned my place, my particular stool, my section of the table.

The work is this: bolts of fabric come up from the finishing floor on a dumb waiter, accompanied by their production cards. I unroll them — carefully, the first handling after finishing is crucial — and examine every inch. I'm looking for flaws: broken warp threads, inconsistent weft tension, slubs in the yarn, dye irregularities, oil spots from the machinery, any of hundreds of possible defects. I mark them with tailor's chalk when they're minor, cut the fabric and separate the sections when they're major. Each bolt gets graded: First quality, Second quality, Mill ends (which means flawed but usable for something), or Waste (rare, and it goes back down with a note that causes trouble for someone).

My hands move in patterns learned through repetition: unroll two feet, scan with eyes and fingers both, feel the fabric's weight and drape, check the edges for tension puckering, look for shade variations. The good fabric has a feel to it, a rightness that's hard to articulate. Consistent hand, even dye, the slight sheen that means the nap is properly raised and shorn. The bad fabric speaks too: a harshness that means the fibers were stressed, a subtle pilling that will only get worse, a waviness that reveals tension problems in the loom.

I have developed, over these seven years, an eye. This is what they call it, simply: "She has the eye." It means I can detect flaws other sorters miss. It means I can distinguish between fifteen subtly different causes of weft irregularity just by looking. It means my gradations are trusted, that my First quality stamps rarely come back disputed. The eye is not quite vision — it's a trained attention, a learned sensitivity, a way of seeing that emerges from seeing the same things thousands of times until pattern recognition becomes almost precognitive.

But I am becoming something else too, something I didn't anticipate when I started.

# The Noticing

I am becoming someone who notices the margins, the places where the system's logic breaks down or reveals itself.

I notice, for instance, that the fabric quality correlates with factors the production cards don't mention. Thursday afternoon fabric is nearly always lower quality — that's when the weekend drinking catches up with the weavers, when hands are less steady, attention more fragmented. Fabric from the second-floor looms nearest the eastern wall shows a particular kind of weft tension irregularity that I've traced to vibration from the stamp press three buildings over; the vibration travels through the shared foundation and affects the loom's operation in ways too subtle for the weavers to consciously detect but consistent enough to show in the finished fabric.

I notice that certain dye lots — marked with specific codes that mean nothing to me officially — consistently run slightly off-shade, and I've realized this means they're using substituted ingredients, cheaper alternatives to the standard formulas. The master dyers know, obviously, and probably management knows, but the knowledge hasn't traveled to official documentation. There's a gap between what's recorded and what's real, and I live in that gap.

I notice the sorters themselves, my companions at the tables. How Frau Bachmann handles fabric with a tenderness that would seem excessive if it weren't so effective — she has the lowest damage rate of any sorter, her careful touch preserving value that rougher handling would destroy. How young Margarethe, newest to our room, is fast and accurate but hasn't yet learned to pace herself, and by the end of her shift her accuracy drops because exhaustion degrades the eye. How Herr Steiner, at the table behind mine, has developed a tremor in his left hand — early stages of the palsy that takes many of the older workers — and is hiding it by adjusting his technique, using his right hand for tasks that would normally be two-handed.

I notice that we have our own economy, separate from wages. Frau Bachmann teaches technique, and in exchange we all contribute to her tea tin — she drinks constantly, her one vice or possibly medication. When someone's piece-rate is looking short for the week, we quietly shift easier bolts to their table, harder ones to those who can absorb the difficulty without financial damage. When management demands faster throughput, we collectively slow down just slightly, and no individual can be blamed, but the aggregate effect is resistance. We have learned to function as a mechanism ourselves, with our own feedback loops and pressure releases.

# The Splitting

I am becoming split, and I'm not sure what this means yet.

There is the person who does the work: hands moving, eyes scanning, chalk marking, production cards stamping. This person is efficient, reliable, present in the continuous now of task-focus. This person takes satisfaction in work well done, in the small pleasure of detecting a flaw that almost passed, in the rhythm of the hours.

And there is the person who watches the person working, who notices patterns, who thinks about systems, who sees the room not as a workplace but as a kind of theater where we're all performing roles we've learned. This person is newer, has been growing slowly over the years, and makes the work both easier and harder. Easier because awareness of patterns aids efficiency. Harder because awareness of patterns reveals absurdities.

I catch myself sometimes — my hands continuing their work while my attention is elsewhere, thinking about how the fabric we grade will become clothing for people I'll never meet, or noticing how the afternoon light changes the room's emotional tone, or wondering about the lives of the other sorters, the hours we spend together without ever quite becoming close because the work doesn't allow for much conversation and our break times are staggered.

I wonder if this splitting is what happens to everyone eventually, or if it's particular to me. The Rhythm-Bound seem unified, collapsed entirely into their work, but perhaps that's what I look like from outside too. Perhaps everyone contains this observer-self, and we're all haunted by our own attention.

# The Plants

On my table, in a tin can that once held machine oil, I keep a cutting of wandering jew. The plant should not be able to live here — the light is poor, the air is full of lint that settles on the leaves — but it persists. I water it from the tea kettle. The purple undersides of the leaves are the most vivid color in the room aside from the fabric itself.

I am not the only one. Most of the sorters maintain something: a potted fern, a spider plant sending out runners, once someone had an orchid that everyone agreed was overly ambitious and that died within a month. The plants are technically against regulations — concerns about fiber contamination — but the regulations aren't enforced because management has learned that the sorters work better with these small permissions, these tiny zones of autonomy.

I find myself talking to the plant sometimes, internally, during the long stretches of repetitive work. Not words exactly, more a kind of attention, a checking-in. Is it drooping? Does it need water? The leaves collect lint and I wipe them clean with a damp cloth, very gently. This tenderness surprises me. I am not generally tender. But the plant's persistence touches something.

It grows toward the window, naturally, seeking better light. But the way it grows is adapted to what's available: the leaves smaller than they would be in proper light, the stems longer and more searching. It has become a shade plant through necessity. It has changed to fit its circumstances while remaining itself. I understand this. I feel this. We are both adapted to insufficient light, both making do, both stubbornly growing anyway.

# The Books

I am becoming someone who reads, which is not common among the sorters but not unknown. The district has a workers' library, three rooms above a cooper's workshop, open two evenings a week and Sunday afternoons. The books are mostly donations, eclectic, random. Technical manuals. Novels from thirty years ago. Books of poetry that someone's grandmother owned. Political theory. Natural history. Whatever accumulated.

I go there during my off hours, less for specific books than for the act of going, the claiming of time for something that isn't work or sleep or the minimal maintenance of living. I read inconsistently, whatever seems appealing, following no program. A chapter of a novel, an essay on birds, some pages of somebody's theory about society. I retain some of it, forget most, but the accumulation is changing how I think.

I'm reading, currently, a book about glaciers — why this book exists in the workers' library is a mystery, but here it is. The book explains how glaciers move, how ice behaves like fluid over long time scales, how the landscape is shaped by their grinding passage. I find myself thinking about this at my sorting table. The district is like this: slowly flowing, grinding, changing so gradually it seems static but actually in constant motion. The people flow through it like meltwater. We are eroding and being eroded simultaneously.

This is the kind of thought the observer-self has. The working-self just grades fabric.

I'm not sure which self is more real, or if the question is meaningful. Perhaps I'm becoming the conversation between them, the space where they argue.

# The Vertigo

Sometimes, standing at my table, I am seized by a particular sensation. I become suddenly, viscerally aware of the structure around me: the room I'm in is on the fifth floor, which sits atop four other floors, which sit atop the original stone foundation, which sits atop earth and the buried river. Above me are three more floors, then the roof, then air and mist and somewhere beyond the mist, sky.

I become aware of weight: the building's weight on its foundation, my weight on the floor, the fabric's weight in my hands. I become aware of the thousands of people in the surrounding structures, all of us suspended in this vertical city, all of us trusting that the engineering will hold, that the stone and iron and concrete will bear our collective mass.

The awareness is vertiginous. Not fear exactly, but a dizziness born of scale, of understanding how contingent this all is, how much we depend on systems we don't control, how easily it could fail. And then the moment passes, and I'm back in my body, at my table, hands moving through their practiced routine, and the vertigo is replaced by something like its opposite: a groundedness in the immediate, in the texture of fabric and the smell of sizing and the sound of the looms below.

I contain both. The vertigo of systems and the groundedness of hands. I'm learning to toggle between them, or they're learning to coexist, overlapping like the architectural layers of the district itself.

# The Singing

On very rare occasions — perhaps three or four times a year — someone starts singing while working, and if the song is right, others join, and for a few minutes the sorting room becomes something other than itself.

It's always the old songs, the work songs that have rhythm built in, that can accommodate the pace of hands moving. Frau Bachmann knows dozens of them, songs from regions I've never visited, in dialects I barely understand. But the tunes are simple enough to follow, and the act of singing while working creates a strange doubling: the work continues, rhythm unchanged, but the atmosphere transforms. The room becomes warmer somehow, more cohesive. We are briefly not just individuals at separate tables but a choir, a collective, a we.

Management doesn't like it — there's a concern that singing distracts from attention, increases error rates. But the studies (there are always studies) show no increase in defects during singing periods, so the prohibition isn't enforced. The foreman might look uncomfortable, might check his pocket watch more frequently, but he doesn't stop it.

I don't sing often. My voice is adequate but not strong. But I listen, and the listening is its own participation. I notice how the singing changes people: Margarethe's usual tension eases, Herr Steiner's tremor seems to settle, even Frau Bachmann's precise movements become slightly more flowing. We are briefly not at war with the work but in partnership with it.

And then the song ends, and we're back to the normal soundscape: the looms below, the ventilation system's hiss, the rustle of fabric, the scratch of chalk on zinc. But something lingers, a residue of momentary unity, and the afternoon feels more bearable.

# The Dreams

I dream about fabric now. This started about three years in, and I understand it's common among sorters. The work seeps into sleep.

Sometimes they're anxious dreams: I'm examining a bolt and finding more and more flaws, they multiply under my hands, and I can't mark them fast enough. Sometimes they're strange dreams: I'm sorting fabric but it's also water, or clouds, or the fabric dissolves into threads that become a map of the district, and I'm trying to find my way home through the warp and weft.

Sometimes — and these are the ones that stay with me — they're beautiful dreams. I'm surrounded by fabric in colors that don't exist in our production schedule: deep purples that shift to green, golds that contain their own light. The fabric moves like it's alive, like it's breathing. I touch it and it's impossibly soft, and I'm filled with a joy that makes no sense but feels absolutely real.

I wake from these dreams and lie in my narrow bed in the dormitory section, listening to the other women breathing, to the always-present hum of the district, and I try to hold onto the feeling. It fades, always, but not completely. Some trace remains.

I think the beautiful dreams are my mind's compensation for the waking hours of scrutiny, of looking for defects. In dreams, I'm allowed perfection. In dreams, the fabric is everything it could be.

Or perhaps the dreams are showing me something true: that even the flawed fabric has a kind of perfection in existing at all, in being the result of so much coordinated human effort. Perhaps the splitting is teaching me to see both the defect and the beauty, to hold them simultaneously.

# The Fear

I don't often let myself think about the future, but sometimes it intrudes.

What happens when my eyes start to fail? It's inevitable — the close work, the poor light, the constant strain. Frau Bachmann is already wearing thick spectacles, and her grading is slowing. Another five years, maybe ten, and she'll be moved to less demanding work, lower paid, or pensioned off if she's lucky.

What happens when my hands develop the tremor, or the arthritis that makes fine work painful? What happens when I'm too old for the pace, when the younger sorters are faster and I'm holding back the line's productivity?

The district has positions for the aged and injured, but they're marginal positions: watching over simple machinery, basic cleaning, gate-keeping. The pay is minimal. The dignity is questionable. You become what you feared becoming when you were young and strong: a warning, a future others look at and quietly dread.

I could marry, potentially. There are men who've expressed interest — workers from other departments, a clerk from the counting house. Marriage would mean leaving this work, moving to different housing, becoming a domestic, having children probably. It's a solution to the future problem.

But I haven't married. Something in me resists, though I'm not entirely sure what. Perhaps I've become too independent, too accustomed to my own rhythms. Perhaps I value this work more than I admit, value being good at something, having skill, being needed. Perhaps I'm afraid that marriage would mean losing the observer-self that's emerged, that I'd be collapsed entirely into duty and role and wouldn't have space for the thoughts that have become precious to me.

So I don't marry, and I don't plan, and I live in the continuous present of the work, and the fear sits at the edge of things, acknowledged but not engaged.

# The Moments

What I would be, finally, is someone who has learned to notice moments.

The moment when afternoon light finds a gap in the overhanging structures and briefly illuminates the sorting room, making the fabric on my table glow, making even the flaws beautiful in their visibility.

The moment when I find a piece of perfect fabric — no defects, consistent dye, ideal hand — and I run it through my fingers knowing that this cloth will become something, will be worn by someone, will have a life beyond this table, and I've touched it at its beginning.

The moment when Frau Bachmann catches my eye across the room and nods, acknowledging some small thing — a difficult bolt graded correctly, a kindness shown to Margarethe — and the nod contains recognition, respect, the transmission of knowledge that has no words.

The moment when I'm walking home through the covered streets after a shift, exhausted but not destroyed, and I notice the moss on the walls glowing almost luminescent in the lamplight, and I think: this is beautiful, this impossible place is beautiful, I live inside beauty even though it doesn't look like what beauty is supposed to look like.

The moment when the singing rises and I'm part of it, my voice small but present, and I feel the collective, feel the we, feel that the work is not just production but also this: human voices in harmony, brief and temporary but real.

# What I Am Becoming

I am becoming someone who lives in the between-spaces: between the work and the watching, between acceptance and resistance, between the individual and the collective, between the machine logic of production and the organic persistence of growing things.

I am becoming a chronicler, even though I write nothing down. The observations accumulate, the patterns reveal themselves, and I carry them. I am the district's witness, insignificant and essential, one among thousands who sees it, who is shaped by it, who contains it.

I am becoming adapted to insufficient light. I am growing toward what's available. I am learning to find sufficiency in scarcity, beauty in grayness, meaning in repetition.

I am becoming someone who can hold contradictions: that the work is oppressive and dignifying, that the district is monstrous and magnificent, that I am trapped and free, that the future is frightening and irrelevant because there is only this: the fabric under my hands, the chalk in my fingers, the afternoon light failing, the looms thrumming below, the moment passing and the next moment arriving, and me here, present, noticing, becoming.

I am seven years a fabric grader in the Hanging Foundry District, and I have no idea who I will be in seven more, but I know I will still be able to detect a slub in yarn from across the room, will still know the smell of sizing mixed with mist, will still carry these hours in my body like the district carries its buried river: invisible, essential, the source of everything, flowing on.
