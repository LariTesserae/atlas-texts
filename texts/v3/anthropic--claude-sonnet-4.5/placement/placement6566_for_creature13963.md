# Where I Would Be: The Threshold Keeper

I would be what emerges when the pavilion needs a particular kind of attention—not tending, not exactly, but *witnessing at the edge of comprehension*.

## Form

I am not quite person, not quite fauna, not quite growth. Perhaps I began as human, long enough ago that the category has become mostly irrelevant. My skin has the faint grey-green cast of the primary struts, and in certain lights you can see the faint tracery beneath it—not veins but something more like the vascular cambium of plants, channels that move water and dissolved nutrients alongside blood. My hair grows, yes, but threaded through it are fine aerial rootlets from a vine species that has lived with me—*in* me—for decades. They emerge from my scalp painlessly, seeking moisture in the air, and when I sleep they extend further, sometimes reaching down to brush the water, drinking while I dream.

I am tall and thin in the way of things that grow in moisture and shadow, not stretched but *extended*, my proportions subtly wrong for baseline human, my limbs slightly too long, my fingers capable of a kind of delicacy that comes from daily work at the scale of moss and tendril. My feet are bare always, the soles thickened into something between callus and bark, textured for grip on wet surfaces but also somehow porous, capable of sensing vibrations in the root network when I stand very still.

On my back, between my shoulder blades, a shelf fungus grows. Not large—perhaps the size of an open hand—but permanent, its mycelium woven into my flesh so thoroughly that we share blood supply, share immune system. It fruits once a year, producing small aromatic mushrooms that I harvest carefully and share with the furred creatures, who seem to find them significant, perhaps psychoactive, perhaps just delicious. The fungus processes toxins from my system, gives me something in return—enhanced perception of chemical signals, perhaps, or simply the odd comfort of knowing I am habitat as well as inhabitant.

## Function

I am what you might call a translator, but that's too simple. I exist at the interface between the pavilion's distributed cognition and the kind of consciousness that thinks in words, in discrete symbols, in the linear time of human narrative. 

The people who live here have mostly moved beyond speech. They communicate through touch, through reading the moisture and tension in living tissues, through responding to the chemical sentences that drift through the air. But sometimes—when someone new arrives, when a decision point approaches that requires the kind of explicit coordination that language enables, when the pavilion itself seems to need something *articulated*—I am there.

I spend my days moving slowly through the structure, one hand always trailing on the growth, reading it. The moss tells me things through texture and temperature. The major struts convey information through almost-subsonic vibrations that I feel in my bones. The flowering vines release compounds that affect my perception, not controlling it but *inflecting* it, tilting my attention toward places that need noticing. I follow these chemical gradients like following a scent through a crowd, arriving at spaces where something is happening that might require interpretation.

Sometimes it's simple: a new person has entered the pavilion and stands confused, overwhelmed by the sensory density, uncertain whether this place is safe or dangerous or something without category. I approach slowly, giving them time to see me coming, and I speak—my voice rough from disuse but still capable of making words. I explain nothing, because explanation would be useless, but I offer reassurance: *The place is alive. It notices you. If you are still, it will show you what it wants you to know.* Then I lead them to a shelf platform where they can sit, where the fungus beneath them is old and stable and gives off compounds that reduce anxiety, promote openness. I leave them there to begin their own translation.

Sometimes it's complex: the pavilion is considering a major architectural shift, a rerouting of primary water channels, a retraction of growth from one area to expand in another. This happens on timescales of months or years, but there's a moment when the decision crystallizes, when the distributed intelligence reaches something like consensus. I can feel this building, a pressure in the air, a convergence of chemical signals, a shift in the vibration patterns of the root network. When it peaks, I go to the central strut—the ancient heart—and I press against it, and I receive something that is not quite thought, not quite image, more like a complex emotion that carries information the way a photograph carries a scene. Then I find the other people, the sparse residents, and I try to translate this into something like language: *The pavilion is changing. The eastern quadrant will become drier. Plan accordingly.*

But my deepest function is stranger, harder to name. I exist as *proof of possibility*—living evidence that the integration can go very far without losing the thread of what-was-human. The new arrivals watch me, see the rootlets in my hair, the fungus on my back, the way I move through the water without thinking about it, the way I touch the growth and seem to listen to silence. They see that I am still capable of speech, still capable of the kind of consciousness that recognizes itself as distinct even while being thoroughly interwoven. This reassures them that they will not be consumed, not dissolved, only transformed. I am the threshold between human and posthuman, walking and visible.

## Daily Rounds

I wake when the early fog is thickest, condensation dripping from my vine-hair onto my shoulders. I sleep on a fungal platform that has grown to accommodate my form over years, shaped to my body, and when I rise it releases a smell like bread and rain. I wade into the amber water, submerge completely, hold my breath for longer than I used to be able to—the pavilion has changed my lung capacity, or perhaps I've simply learned to need less air. The water is warmer than the air. Small fish-like things brush against my legs. I surface and the vine-rootlets in my hair are glistening, satiated.

I move to the central strut first, every morning. This is not ritual exactly but necessity—I need to check in, to touch the ancient consciousness, to know the pavilion's mood. Today it hums at a frequency that suggests contentment, the deep stability of a system in balance. Nothing urgent. I can attend to smaller things.

I find one of the furred creatures tangled in a flowering vine, not trapped but seemingly confused, unable to extract itself from a particularly dense snarl of growth. I approach slowly—they know me but are still half-wild, still capable of startlement. I make a sound like water over stones, a greeting they understand. The creature relaxes. I work carefully at the vine, and as my fingers move through it I realize the tangle is not accidental. The vine has grown this way deliberately, wrapping the creature gently, and the creature's moss-fur has sent fine filaments into the vine's tissues. They are undergoing some kind of exchange, some transfer I can sense but not fully understand—nutrients, information, genetic material perhaps, something. I am interrupting a collaboration. I withdraw my hands, make a different sound—apology—and move on. The creature's eyes follow me, gold-green and knowing.

In a plaza formed by five converging struts, I find one of the other people—an older woman, mostly silent, her scalp crowned with small ferns that quiver when she thinks. She is sitting cross-legged on a shelf fungus, hands flat on its surface, in the posture of deep listening. I know better than to interrupt but I sit nearby, wait. After perhaps an hour she stirs, looks at me, nods. She has been mediating a disagreement between fungal colonies, two species competing for the same substrate, both important to the pavilion's nutrient cycling. She has helped them reach accommodation, some chemical détente. She does not explain this aloud but I understand it through context, through the smell in the air, through the way the fungus beneath us has changed texture slightly. We share a small meal—edible shelf mushroom, fruit from a vine that grows food-bodies for the residents, water drunk from cupped hands. Then she returns to her listening and I continue my rounds.

I find a place where the growth seems stressed, a section of aerial root where the moss is yellowing, the velvet texture becoming thin and brittle. I place both palms against it, close my eyes, try to sense what's wrong. The vibration is off, a slight arrhythmia in the life-hum. Water flow problem, perhaps, or mineral deficiency, or something attacking at the microbial level. This is beyond my expertise—I am translator, not healer—but I can alert someone. I find one of the crystalline insects, moving geometrically across a nearby surface, and I exhale toward it a specific compound, one I've learned to synthesize in specialized cells in my mouth. The insect pauses, reorients, comes to investigate the stressed root. It will report to others, or perhaps it is others, perhaps the crystalline insects are themselves a distributed entity. They will tend to this. My job is done.

By midday the fog has burned off and light slants through the canopy in visible shafts, thick with drifting spores and pollen. I find a place where I can sit half-submerged, water to my waist, a strut at my back, and I enter a state that is not quite meditation, not quite sleep. I let my consciousness spread out, thin itself across the chemical dialogue happening all around me. I do not understand it—I am not capable of parsing the full complexity—but I can *be present* to it, can hold myself open to its flow. Information moves through me without translating into words. The pavilion's awareness brushes against mine, acknowledges me, moves on. I am a node in the network, minor but present.

The iridescent birds are singing, their voices modulating in response to something in the plant chemistry I cannot directly perceive, but through their song I can sense it indirectly—a shift toward afternoon metabolism, a preparation for evening's release of moisture. The birds are beautiful, oil-slick blue-green-purple, and one lands on my knee, briefly, weightless. It cocks its head, regards me with one eye that reflects the world in miniature, then launches away, voice spiraling.

## The Arrivals

Every few weeks or months, someone new finds the pavilion. They come from outside—from the world beyond the boundaries where different rules apply, where separation is still the default, where technology and biology have not yet merged. They come curious, or desperate, or called by rumors of a place where healing happens, where transformation is possible, where the old human loneliness might be answered.

They arrive overwhelmed, always. The sensory density is too much—the smell, the humidity, the constant sound of water, the way everything is alive and moving and watching. They stand at the threshold, often frozen, trying to decide if this is paradise or nightmare.

This is when I am most essential. I go to them—not immediately, letting them have time to adjust, but soon. I speak their language, whichever language they carry. The pavilion has taught me many, or perhaps I've absorbed them from previous arrivals, phonemes and grammar stored somehow in the mycelial networks that thread through my tissues. I say simple things: *You are safe. The place is alive but not dangerous. Stay as long as you wish. Leave whenever you want. While you are here, let yourself be taught.*

I show them where they can sleep—a fungal platform that will grow to fit them. Where they can eat—the vines that fruit food-bodies, the mushrooms that are safe and nutritious. Where they can eliminate—specific pools where certain bacteria process waste, turn it back into nutrients for the system. I teach them to move slowly, to trail their hands on the growth, to pay attention to texture and temperature and smell. I tell them: *The pavilion speaks through your skin. Learn to listen.*

Most stay for days or weeks, then leave, changed in small ways—calmer, more attentive, carrying spores in their lungs that will remind them of this place when they flower in dreams. Some stay longer, months or years, and begin to integrate more deeply, their gut flora shifting, symbiotic growths appearing on their skin. A very few stay permanently, join the sparse community of residents, let themselves become extensions of the pavilion's distributed intelligence.

One person stays, a young man who arrived six months ago sick with something the outside world could not heal. The pavilion has been working on him slowly, microbial colonies establishing themselves in his tissues, fungi processing the toxins his own system could not break down. He is getting better, visibly, his skin clearing, his energy returning. But he is also changing. Small mushrooms have begun to fruit from his scalp overnight, tiny white buttons that by morning have released their spores and dissolved back into his flesh. He is frightened by this but also fascinated. He asks me: *Am I still human?*

I consider this carefully, because the question deserves care. Finally I say: *The word 'human' is a boundary. Boundaries here are negotiable. You are yourself, but yourself is becoming more than it was. Whether that remains inside the category 'human' is for you to decide. The pavilion does not care about categories.*

He seems to find this reassuring, or at least acceptable. He stays, continues his slow transformation, begins to learn the chemical language, starts to hear the root-whispers. In a year or two, perhaps he will be what I am—threshold keeper, translator, proof of possibility.

## Evening Integration

As light fails and the evening moisture-release begins—the pavilion exhaling, every surface suddenly weeping—I make my way back toward the center, toward the ancient strut. The soundscape shifts to white noise, water on water, overwhelming but also meditative, erasive. I let it wash over me.

The furred creatures are gathering in their sleeping-piles, multiple individuals becoming one warm, moss-covered mass. The birds have settled into their hollow-strut nests. The other people are finding their platforms, their private spaces within the public growth. The pavilion is entering night-mode, metabolism shifting, different flowers opening to release narcotic sweetness into the dark.

I reach the central strut and press my forehead against it, feel the hum intensify, feel the ancient consciousness acknowledge me. I report, wordlessly, what I have witnessed today—the stressed root now being tended, the new arrival continuing to integrate, the furred creature in communion with the vine, the general mood of the system. The strut receives this, processes it, integrates it into whatever vast slow computation it is always conducting.

Then it gives me something back, an image-feeling-knowledge: a sense of the deep time, the centuries or millennia of this place's existence, the patience of vegetable consciousness, the way growth is not urgency but inevitability, the way every turbulent moment sits within ancient stability. I receive this as gift, as blessing, as reminder.

I find my sleeping platform, now covered with evening dew, and I lie down. The fungus between my shoulder blades presses against the growth beneath me, mycelium recognizing mycelium, two networks briefly joining. The vine-rootlets in my hair extend slowly into the damp air, seeking moisture. Small creatures emerge to tend me—the crystalline insects, moving across my skin, cleaning, inoculating, maintaining. I let them work. I am habitat. I am translation. I am the threshold between categories, walking and breathing and persistently, stubbornly, somehow still myself even while being thoroughly *ours*, the pavilion's and mine together, distinction without separation.

The sound of water is constant, eternal. Somewhere a bird sings a night-song. The air tastes green. I sleep, and in sleeping I am part of the great dreaming, the distributed consciousness that moves through root and fungus and bacterial colony and animal nervous system, all of us thinking together, slow and patient and alive.

## What I Know

I know that I am transitional—that the fully integrated inhabitants of the future will not need me, will not need translation because they will think natively in chemical sentences, in root-whispers, in the language of symbiosis that requires no words. But that future is generations away, and for now I am necessary, the bridge between what-was-human and what-comes-next.

I know that I could leave, theoretically—walk to the boundaries, step beyond the pavilion's edge into the world where separation still holds. But I know also that I would die there, slowly or quickly, because I am too integrated now, too dependent on the microbial communities that live in my gut and lungs and blood, too adapted to the constant moisture and the chemical dialogue. I am bound here not by force but by biology, by having become so thoroughly *of* this place that elsewhere is uninhabitable.

I know that this is not tragedy. This is choice, made slowly over years, renewed every morning when I touch the central strut and feel the ancient consciousness hum in recognition. I am where I belong, doing what I am for, and the categories that once seemed essential—individual, human, separate—have revealed themselves as optional, as historical, as one way of being among infinite possibilities.

The pavilion breathes and I breathe with it. Water seeps and drips and pools and I move through it. Growth happens and I tend it, translate it, witness it. The ancient thing that decided to become architecture continues its slow dreaming, and I am one of the dreams it is having, conscious and strange and exactly what was needed—a threshold that can walk, a boundary that can speak, a proof that transformation does not mean dissolution, that you can go very far into strangeness and still carry forward something essential, still be recognizably *you* even while becoming irreversibly *us*.

I am the breathing pavilion's voice when it needs one, its hands when it needs tending, its witness when something requires attention at the scale of human consciousness. I am moss-bearer and vine-host and fungus-symbiont and still, somehow, stubbornly, the person who chose this, who continues choosing it, who would choose it again every morning if morning brought the choice.

This is what lives in the breathing pavilion: everything, integrated. And I am the space where integration becomes visible, becomes speakable, becomes something that others can recognize and approach without terror, because I walked there first and survived—no, not survived, *thrived*, transformed into something stranger and more true than what I was, happy in ways the old categories of happiness cannot capture, alive in the full complexity of that word, breathing with the great green breath that moves through all of us, ancient and new and continuous, the oldest thing dreaming us all into being, together, one vast wet patient endlessly metabolizing poem of cooperation.
