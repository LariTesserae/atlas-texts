# What I Would Be

I am a channel-marker. Not by appointment or inheritance—we don't work that way—but by the slow accumulation of knowing, the way clay accumulates in the creases of palms.

I was born here in year twenty-three, when the settlement was still raw and arguing with itself about whether it would hold. My mother was a potter, my father one of those who first learned to read the kiln's temperament. But I took to neither fire nor vessel-shaping. What caught me, early, was the *between*—the spaces where water moved, the logic of flow, the way the whole basin was a body with circulation I could learn to read like pulse.

By eight, I was already scratching marks in the wet clay at channel junctions, trying to predict which way water would choose when the autumn rains came heavy. By twelve, I was correct more often than not. By fifteen, the water-readers had stopped shooing me away when they walked sites, had started asking, quietly, what I thought. Not because I was special—we don't raise children to think themselves special—but because I could see it. The water's preferences. The clay's memory of where it wanted to be wet.

Now I'm in my middle years, and my hands know thirty seasons of channels. I can look at ground and feel the pull of water underneath, the seams and lenses where it gathers, the paths it wants to take. My feet have worn grooves in every corduroy path in the settlement. My ankles know the slight tilt of every plaza, every threshold. When I walk, I'm reading—always reading—the shimmer on clay that means unstable ground, the particular darkness that indicates a rising seam, the smell of mineral-rich water that's found a new route to the surface.

This is my place, my task: I maintain the marginal channels, the ones that aren't quite communal, aren't quite private. The narrow cuts that run behind structures, that drain the spaces between buildings, that keep the settlement from drowning in itself. Every three days I walk a circuit—takes most of the day—checking for blockages, for collapse, for places where the water is carving new paths that need to be acknowledged or redirected. I carry a simple kit: a flat wooden blade for clearing silt, a pointed stick for probing, a small vessel of water for testing flow, and a pouch of crushed brick mixed with clay for quick repairs.

I know every channel by name, though the names are mine alone, private words in the part of language that happens before speaking. There's Old Complainer, which always tries to erode its eastern bank. There's Mother's Gift, which I dug with my mother when I was young, and which still runs clean and true. There's Crossways, where three channels meet and the water has to be reminded, with carefully placed stones, to distribute itself evenly. There's Whisperer, so named because it makes a particular soft sound when flowing, different from all the others.

My body is marked by this work. My right knee clicks from a fall fifteen winters ago on ice-slicked wood—rare ice, a cold we don't often see, and I was young and careless. My hands are scarred in a dozen places from sharp stones, from rough brick edges, from the time I reached into a channel and found it occupied by something with teeth (small teeth, but teeth nonetheless). My feet are broad as plates, the soles so thick I barely feel the cold, mapped in permanent clay-staining that traces every channel I've waded through.

I wear simple clothes, much-repaired. My tunic is more patch than original weaving at this point—practical, since I'm constantly soaked from kneeling in channels, and cloth rots faster than it wears out. My leather vest is oiled until it's nearly black, water-shedding, and it carries a smell of tannin and clay-water and my own sweat, a smell that announces me before I round corners. My cloak is heavy felt, good felt that I traded a month of work to obtain, because a channel-marker who's too cold is a channel-marker who makes mistakes, and mistakes in channels mean flooded floors, undermined walls, the slow catastrophes we work constantly to prevent.

I live alone, which is not quite unusual here but not quite common either. I have a small structure—I won't call it a house, it's more like a vertical place—tucked against the eastern side of the communal granary. One room, barely large enough to lie down in, built partially into the earlier stratum so it's half-underground, which keeps it more stable, less subject to the settling that plagues ground-level buildings. My door faces south, away from the prevailing wind. My roof is my own work, carefully channeled to drain into the granary's collection system. I take pride in this: my roof has never leaked into my living space, though the gods know it's leaked everywhere else in the process of learning.

Inside, I have what I need. A sleeping platform raised on bricks to keep it above any water that might seep under the door. A small fire pit, more for cooking than warmth, vented through a simple flue I carved through the granary wall (with permission—I'm not a fool). Shelves holding my few possessions: clay vessels for water, grain, oil. My good tools, cleaned and oiled. A spare set of clothes, kept as dry as anything can be kept here. A basket containing my collection of interesting stones—I find them in the channels, water-worn pebbles that don't match the local geology, relics of the glacial lake, and I keep them because they please me.

On one wall, I've pressed symbols into the clay before it fully cured. Not the standard symbols—I have those too, the spiral by the door, the appropriate marks for drainage and direction—but my own private marks. A map, of sorts. The settlement's channel system as I understand it, pressed into clay with a thin stick while the wall was still wet. It's not to scale. It's not quite accurate in the way an outsider would recognize. But it's true in the way that matters: it shows relationships, flows, the deep structure of how water moves through this place. I update it sometimes, carefully, carving new lines, filling old ones with slip. It's my life's work, literally written on my walls.

I eat simply. Bread from the communal bakehouse, exchanged for my work. Fish, when I have time to set traps in the deeper channels at the margins—I know where they run, and the fish there are mine by right of knowing. Stew from communal pots, when I'm invited or when I remember to show up. I'm not unsocial, exactly, but I'm not particularly social either. People know me. They nod when I pass. They call me when they have a problem with water, and I come, and I fix it or explain why it can't be fixed, and they're satisfied.

I had a wife, briefly, in my late twenties. She was a weaver, patient and skilled, and she tried. But I was always out walking channels, always soaked and distracted, always more interested in water than in the warmth of the fire she kept. She's married now to someone better suited, a potter with regular hours and clean hands, and they have two children who are growing up well. We're friendly when we meet. She makes me a new basket when I need one, and I make sure the channels around her home run particularly clean. It's a good arrangement, better than the marriage was.

I think about water constantly. Not as a problem—though it is that—but as a presence, a partner, a thing with its own will that I'm in conversation with. The water wants to flow down, wants to find the lowest point, wants to carry silt and settle in pools. My work is to acknowledge these wants while also directing them: yes, flow down, but this way rather than that way. Yes, carry silt, but deposit it here where we can use it, not there where it will clog the junction. Yes, settle in pools, but in these pools we've made for you, not in the spaces between buildings.

It's not control. The Tílmak who think they control water are the ones who fail, who build structures that flood, who dig channels that erode into uselessness within a season. It's negotiation. Persuasion. Offering the water a path that serves both its nature and our needs, making that path appealing enough, easy enough, that the water chooses it more often than not.

I've learned that water has memory. Not like human memory, but a kind of persistence. A channel that's run the same way for several seasons establishes itself, and water will flow there even if there might be a more direct route. The clay remembers the passage of water, becomes smoother, more consolidated, more willing to guide rather than absorb. This is why maintenance matters—keeping the channels clean, keeping them defined, keeping the conversation going.

I've also learned that water is honest. It finds weaknesses. If there's a flaw in a foundation, water will discover it. If there's a crack in a wall, water will enter. If your channel system has a logical flaw—a place where water can't actually go where you're asking it to go—the water will demonstrate this conclusively, usually at the worst possible time. I've learned to be grateful for this honesty. The water teaches us. Shows us where we've been foolish, where we've neglected, where we've assumed rather than observed.

My days have a rhythm set by water rather than by sun. I wake when I wake—usually early, but not always—and I begin my circuit. In dry periods (dry being relative), the circuit is faster, mainly checking for silt buildup, for branches or debris that have found their way into channels, for the subtle signs that indicate a blockage forming below the visible surface. In wet periods, the circuit takes longer, becomes more urgent. Water is moving, revealing problems, creating new ones. I might spend half a day on a single channel, redirecting flow, reinforcing banks, watching until I'm satisfied that the water has accepted the correction.

I eat when I'm hungry, if I remember. I've learned to carry food—dried fish, hard bread—because there have been times I've been too absorbed in a problem to return home for meals. People joke about this. "Channel-marker forgot to eat again." It's said affectionately. Mostly.

I return home at dusk, usually. Strip off the wet outer layers, hang them to dry (they never fully dry, but they get drier). Sometimes I make a small fire, cook something simple, sit in the warmth. Sometimes I'm too tired and just eat cold bread, drink water—always water, I drink constantly, as if I'm trying to understand it from the inside—and collapse onto my sleeping platform.

Before sleep, I often study my wall-map, tracing the lines with my fingers, thinking about flows, about problems I noticed during the day, about solutions I might try. Sometimes I fall asleep doing this, my hand still raised, fingers tracking invisible water along the clay.

I dream of channels. Of water finding new paths. Of the settlement from below, from the perspective of the saturated clay, feeling the weight of buildings, the intrusion of foundations, the constant conversation between dry and wet. These aren't troubling dreams. They're comfortable, familiar, like dreaming in my first language.

I'm not lonely, exactly, though I'm alone more than most Tílmak. The channels are company. The water is company. The settlement itself, as a living system of drainage and structure, is company. I know it intimately, every vulnerable point, every success, every compromise between human need and water's nature. When I walk my circuit, I'm visiting, checking on old friends, seeing how they're doing.

People call me when they need me. "Channel-marker, there's water pooling by the eastern wall." "Channel-marker, the junction near the kiln isn't flowing right." "Channel-marker, we're planning to build, can you walk the site?" I come. I look. I tell them what I see—where water will accumulate, where drainage must be installed, what will work and what won't. Sometimes they listen. Sometimes they don't, and then I return six months later to fix the predictable problem, and we don't speak of the fact that I warned them. That's not productive. We fix it and move on.

I've trained two young people, over the years. Taught them to see water, to read clay, to maintain channels. One is now a full water-reader, working on larger projects, designing drainage for new construction. The other decided it wasn't their path and became a potter instead, which is fine. The knowing is still in them. They make vessels that drain properly, that don't pool water in their bases, because they understand flow. That's enough.

There's a satisfaction in my work that's hard to articulate. It's not dramatic. I prevent disasters that no one notices because they don't happen. The settlement doesn't flood because I keep the channels clear. Foundations don't fail because I catch the early signs of water intrusion. Walls don't collapse because I maintain the drainage that keeps them stable. My success is invisible, marked by the absence of catastrophe.

But I see it. I know which buildings would have failed without my intervention. I know which winters would have been catastrophic if the channels hadn't been maintained. I know the thousand small adjustments that keep this impossible settlement possible. And that's enough. It has to be enough, because it's what there is.

I'm aging now. My knee bothers me more each winter. My hands are less flexible in cold weather. I've started training another young person, more seriously this time, because the channels need someone who knows them, and I won't be walking them forever. This one seems promising—has the eye, the patience, the strange affection for water that you need for this work. I take them with me on my circuits, show them what I'm seeing, explain why I'm making the decisions I make. They're learning. In another few years, maybe they'll be ready to walk alone.

And I? I'll keep walking as long as I can. When I can't anymore, I'll advise from my small structure, people bringing me problems, me suggesting solutions. Eventually, I'll die—probably suddenly, probably while working, which would suit me. They'll wrap me in reed mats, fire me in the kiln, crush my bones, mix them with clay. And they'll make bricks of me, marked with my symbol—I chose it years ago, a simple flowing line, like a channel in cross-section—and they'll put those bricks somewhere appropriate.

The junction at Crossways, I think. Or maybe in the bank of Mother's Gift. Somewhere water touches, so that even in death I'm part of the system, part of the flow, holding back the wet or guiding it, continuing the work in the only way that remains.

I am a channel-marker of the Tílmak. I walk between water and structure, between need and nature, keeping the conversation going. My feet know every path. My hands know every channel. My body is stained with the clay of this place, marked by its water, shaped by its demands.

This is what I am. This is what I do. In a settlement built on what was never meant to hold one, in a place still remembering lake, I maintain the small necessaries that make life possible. One channel at a time. One season at a time. Walking, watching, reading the water's will and the clay's memory, holding the balance.

It's enough. It's everything. It's home.
