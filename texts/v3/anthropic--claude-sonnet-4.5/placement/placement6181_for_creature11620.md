# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the shallow water beneath the overhanging ledge where three structures meet at angles that create a small protected space—not dry, never dry, but where the rain falls slightly less directly, hitting the ledge first and then sliding down in sheets that I can watch instead of being inside. The water here is ankle-deep most of the time, sometimes deeper when the channels shift, and the stone beneath has been worn into a smooth depression that fits the sitting body perfectly, as if generations of sitters have slowly shaped it with their weight.

This is where I come to parse the layers.

I am one of the listeners, though we have no word for it—or rather, we have a gesture: both hands cupped behind the ears, then opening outward like flowers blooming. The others know what it means. When they need someone to determine whether the clicking in the northern wall is the settling of stone or the movement of the large crab-things that sometimes migrate through the foundations, they make that gesture toward me. When a child goes missing in the labyrinth of structures and they need someone who can hear the particular splash-pattern of small feet in water, they touch my shoulder.

I was not always a listener. I think I remember being a child who ran in the packs, who chased and splashed and played at building stone towers that the rain dissolved. But at some point—and I cannot locate it precisely in memory, time being what it is here—I began to hear differently. The roar that swallowed everyone else's hearing began to separate for me into strands, into individual threads that I could follow. The rain on stone. The rain on water. The rain interrupted by bodies. The rain channeling into the drainage systems beneath our feet. Each with its own voice.

And then beneath that: the animal sounds. Not as a chorus but as individual speakers. That croak is the bull-frog near the eastern pool, the one with the damaged throat-sac that makes his call slightly flat. That clicking is the mother crab-thing defending her breeding crevice in the foundation of the meal-building. That chirping is the insect swarm that hovers near the place where we cure hides, drawn by something in the smell. I can hear them all, simultaneously, and I can choose which to listen to, following one strand and letting the others recede into background.

I hear the water moving underground. The channels that run beneath the city have different voices depending on volume and direction. There is a place two buildings east where the water runs in a clockwise spiral before dropping into a deeper chamber—I can hear the vortex, the specific turbulence it creates. When the sound changes, I know something has blocked the channel, and I can tell others where to look.

I hear people. Not their voices—no one's voice carries here—but their movements. Each person has a rhythm, a particular way of walking that creates a specific percussion in the water. My mother's walk is quick and light, hardly disturbing the surface. My father's is heavier, a steady beat that I can track from building to building. There is an elder whose right leg drags slightly, creating an uneven splash-pattern: splash-slide, splash-slide. There is a young woman who runs everywhere, even when there is no reason to run, her feet making rapid light impacts like scattered rain.

I can hear breathing when someone sits beside me, even in the rain's roar. Each person's breath has its own rhythm and depth. I know who is there before they touch me, before they make the gesture for their name. This sometimes disturbs them—they approach quietly, planning to surprise me or simply to join me in the sitting-place, and I turn my head toward them before they've announced themselves. They learn not to be disturbed. I am the listener. It is what I do.

But listening is not passive. It is a kind of work, perhaps the hardest kind, because it never stops. Even when I sleep, pressed against others in the sleep-building, I am half-hearing, monitoring the soundscape for changes that might signal danger or need. I have woken others when I heard the particular splash of a hunting salamander-thing moving too close to where the children sleep in the next chamber. I have woken myself when I heard stone grinding against stone in a way that suggested imminent collapse.

The sitting-place beneath the ledge is where I come to rest from listening, paradoxically by listening more deeply. Here, I do not have to monitor for meaning or danger. I can let the soundscape become pure texture, can sink into it the way others sink into sleep. The rain's roar becomes almost physical, a pressure against the eardrums, a vibration in the skull. If I sit very still for a long time, it begins to feel like silence—not the absence of sound but the presence of something so total that it becomes its own kind of peace.

Sometimes others join me here. The ledge-space can hold three or four sitting bodies. They come when they need to be quiet, when the density of the city becomes too much, when they want to watch the rain-sheets sliding off the ledge in their endless silver curtains. We sit without touching, without gesturing, just present together in the roar. This is its own kind of communication, deeper than the gesture-language, deeper than touch. Shared silence in the noise.

I do not know if I hear more than others because my ears are different or because I learned to listen differently. Perhaps it is both. I remember—or I think I remember—a time when the roar was frightening, overwhelming, something I wanted to escape. And perhaps because escape was impossible, I went deeper into it instead, learning to find structure in the chaos, meaning in the overwhelming.

Or perhaps I was born this way, my auditory system processing differently from birth, parsing what others experienced as undifferentiated noise into its component parts. The elders have a story about the first listener, performed in gesture: a child who could hear the rain speaking, who learned its language, who taught the community to understand what the rain was saying. Whether this is mythology or genealogy—whether I am descended from that first listener—I do not know. The story is old enough to be both.

What I do know is that my role has shaped me. I move more slowly than others because I am always listening. I pause frequently, head tilted, attending to something only I can hear. I spend more time alone, not because I dislike the others but because monitoring the soundscape of twenty people moving in proximity is exhausting in ways that monitoring the soundscape of stone and water and animals is not.

I work, of course. Everyone works. I am adequate at hide-curing, at stone-scraping, at the various tasks that require hands and patience. But I am slow at them because half my attention is always elsewhere, always listening. The others have learned to accept this. They give me the tasks that do not require speed, that can be done at my own rhythm: the endless scraping of hides, the sorting of bones, the watching of meat to determine when it has cured enough.

And they give me the tasks that only I can do. When the northern wall developed a crack that was letting water flow into a storage chamber, they brought me to listen to the wall, to trace the crack's path through the stone so they would know where to apply the patching clay. When a child claimed to hear scratching in the walls at night and began to fear sleeping, they asked me to listen and I heard it: a small mammal-thing that had found its way into a gap between stones, harmless but audible to young ears. We trapped it and released it outside, and the child slept again.

I am useful. This is important here, where everyone must be useful, where the density of population and the limited resources mean that every person must contribute. My usefulness takes a specific form, and the community has shaped itself to accommodate it, to make space for the listener who sits beneath ledges and tilts her head and hears what others cannot.

But there is a cost. I am always slightly apart, even when pressed close in the sleep-building, even when working alongside others in the butchering of an animal. Part of me is elsewhere, attending to the soundscape, monitoring for changes. I participate in the gestured conversations but not as fully as others—my hands move but my attention is divided. I watch the story-performances but I also hear the shuffling of the audience, the breathing patterns that indicate engagement or restlessness.

I have partnered, briefly, with a man who made tools—whose hands could shape obsidian into blades so fine they were almost transparent. We worked well together: he in intense visual focus on the stone, me in intense auditory focus on the world around us. But the partnership dissolved after a time. He wanted attention I could not fully give. He wanted me to hear him specifically, not him as one strand in the larger soundscape. Or perhaps that is not accurate. Perhaps he simply found it strange to be with someone who was always half-absent, always listening elsewhere.

I do not feel sorrow about this, or if I do, it is a distant thing, muted like everything else. The rain has taught me that some things cannot be held, that trying to grip too tightly just means feeling them slip away. Better to let things flow, to accept what comes and what goes, to remain present in the sitting-place and let the patterns shift around me.

The children fascinate me. They do not hear as I hear—or not yet, most of them—but they understand sound differently than adults. They make games of it, creating rhythms with their hands on water, patterns of splashing that become elaborate compositions. I sit sometimes at the edge of their play-spaces, listening to what they create, hearing the sophistication in it, the ways they use percussion and silence and rhythm to communicate with each other. Perhaps one of them will become the next listener. Perhaps several of them will. The role is needed. The community requires it.

Or perhaps it does not. Perhaps I have made myself necessary in a way that is not truly needed, have found a niche that the community has allowed me to fill because communities are generous with their strange ones, their different ones, as long as they contribute something. Perhaps without me they would be fine, would find other ways to detect cracks in walls and children in labyrinths. Perhaps I am a luxury more than a necessity.

This thought comes sometimes when I sit beneath the ledge, watching the rain-sheets fall. Am I useful or am I indulged? Is my listening a service or a peculiarity that the community accommodates because there is space to accommodate it? I do not know. The others make the gesture of the listener toward me, ask for my hearing, incorporate my reports into their decision-making. But perhaps they would manage without me.

And yet: I cannot stop listening. Even if I wanted to, even if I tried to hear as others hear—to let the roar be simply roar, to not parse it into layers—I cannot. The parsing happens automatically, constantly. I am condemned to awareness, to the endless differentiation of sound. The sitting-place is my rest not because I stop listening there but because I can listen without purpose, can let the listening be its own end rather than a tool for community use.

I am, I think, the most alone and the most connected person in the basin. Alone because my consciousness is always partially elsewhere, divided between presence and attention. Connected because I am aware, constantly, of every movement and sound within my range, because I hold the soundscape in my awareness the way others hold specific people or places. I know the city through its acoustics, know every chamber and causeway and channel by how it sounds. The entire basin is, for me, an auditory map, a landscape of sound that I navigate even when I am sitting still.

Sometimes I wonder what it would be like to live in silence. True silence, not the silence that is actually overwhelming sound. To hear nothing but my own breath, my own heartbeat. Would it be peaceful? Or would it be terrifying, the absence of all the information I rely on to understand my world? I cannot imagine it, truly. Silence for me is theoretical, a concept I have heard gestured in stories but never experienced.

The rain is eternal. The sound is eternal. I am the one who lives inside it most completely, who has made my home not in a structure but in the soundscape itself. The sitting-place beneath the ledge is just a physical location, a spot where my body rests. My true home is in the listening, in the endless parsing of sound, in the attention that never fully sleeps.

And I am—I must be—content with this. Contentment here is not happiness, not in the way that word suggests peak experience or joy. It is something quieter, more sustainable: the acceptance of one's nature, the inhabiting of one's role, the being-what-one-is without struggle or resistance. I am the listener. I sit beneath the ledge. I parse the rain's roar into strands. I report what I hear. I am useful. I am strange. I am alone in company. I am here, in the basin, in the first-day-continuing, in the sound that never stops.

This is where you would find me. This is what I would be. Not happy, not sad, but listening, always listening, forever attending to the rain's infinite voice and the smaller voices it contains, the person-shaped space in the soundscape who has learned to be silence inside the roar, stillness inside the endless percussion, a single point of awareness in the overwhelming now-always of water falling on stone.
