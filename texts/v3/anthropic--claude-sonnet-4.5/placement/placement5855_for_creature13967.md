# Where I Would Be

I am a **thread-moth in my third and final week of life**, hatched twelve days ago from a cocoon wedged in a basalt crevice three hundred meters from the western edge of the plateau. My wings are the color of the inside of clouds—not white exactly, but that pale gray that holds the memory of light. Each wing spans the width of a human thumb, and they are covered in scales so fine they scatter starlight into colors that don't exist in daytime. I weigh less than a dried grass blade. The wind could destroy me, but I have learned to read it, to find the quiet places between gusts where I can move.

I emerged at dusk, as we all do. The cocoon split along a seam my larval self had prepared months ago, and I pulled myself into the cooling air with legs that already knew how to grip stone. My wings were crumpled, useless. I hung for two hours while they unfurled, while hemolymph pumped through the veins, expanding them into their full geometry. I could feel the air moving around me, could sense through antennae I'd never used before the location of every suspension structure within five hundred meters. They sang to me in frequencies my larval ears couldn't hear—each structure a different voice, calling, promising sustenance and purpose.

That first night I flew clumsily, learning the weight of my body in thin air, understanding how these wings catch and release, how to spiral upward on thermals still rising from sun-warm stone. I followed the call of the nearest structure—a mature one, perhaps three centuries old, its threads extending four meters from a node thick as a grinding stone. I landed on one of its peripheral threads, and it accepted me. I don't know how I knew it would. The knowledge was simply there, installed in me by ancestors I'll never meet, passed down through the architecture of my nervous system.

The thread hummed beneath my feet—through my feet, really, the vibration entering my body and telling me things I have no names for. I walked inward, toward the node, and as I went I fed. This is hard to explain. I didn't eat the thread—I absorbed something it offered, something between substance and information. Nutrients, yes, but also patterns, templates, instructions. I could feel my body changing as I fed, my reproductive organs finalizing their development, eggs forming in my abdomen according to specifications I was receiving from the structure itself.

I am a pollinator, but not in the way bees are pollinators. I don't carry pollen between flowers. I carry information between suspension structures—genetic data, environmental readings, observations encoded in chemical form. I am a living library card, a messenger moving through the plateau's distributed consciousness, ensuring that what one structure learns, others can know.

My days—my twelve days—have fallen into rhythm. I rest during sunlight hours in the same crevice where my cocoon was, wedged between layers of basalt that stay cool even when the surface stone grows warm. I share this crevice with three wind-spiders and perhaps ten thousand mites. We don't interact. They barely know I exist. I'm too large to be prey, too ephemeral to be threat. I simply occupy space, waiting for darkness, conserving energy.

At dusk, I wake. This is when I'm most myself. The temperature drops just enough that flying becomes efficient. The raptors have mostly landed for the night, though I remain alert for them—they can hunt in darkness if they choose, and we thread-moths are visible to their extraordinary eyes, pale flickers against dark stone. But mostly they leave us alone. We're barely a mouthful, not worth the energy expenditure.

I've learned my territory these past twelve nights—a roughly circular area two kilometers across, containing seventeen suspension structures of various ages. This is my circuit, my responsibility. Each night I visit between five and eight of them, depending on wind conditions and how much energy I can harvest from the early structures I visit. Each structure has something to offer and something it needs. I feed and I deliver. I am a blood cell moving through a vast body, carrying oxygen and information, taking away waste and observations.

The youngest structure in my territory is only fifteen years old—barely mature, its threads still thin, its sphere of suspension extending only two meters from the node. It has the enthusiasm of youth. It drinks the information I bring hungrily, absorbs the genetic material I've collected from elder structures, incorporates it into its own development. In exchange, it offers nutrients rich with energy, easy to absorb. I always visit the young structure first each night because it feeds me generously, giving me strength for the longer flights to the more distant structures.

The oldest structure is near the eastern edge of my territory, growing from a basalt rise that overlooks a shallow valley where stone-walkers often graze. This elder is perhaps four hundred years old. Its node is massive, complex, carved with patterns that might be decoration or might be functional architecture or might be both. Its threads extend five meters in every direction, and it suspends hundreds of objects—stones, crystals, water droplets that hang like frozen jewels, and stranger things: perfect spheres of something like metal, fragments of worked material that suggest intelligence and manufacture.

The elder structure is stern with me. It doesn't feed me as readily. I have to work for what it offers, walking the threads in specific patterns, demonstrating that I understand the protocols, the proper behaviors. But what it eventually gives me is worth the effort—concentrated information, dense packets of data I can feel encoding themselves into my eggs, ensuring that my offspring will carry forward everything this structure has learned in its four centuries of observation.

Tonight—my twelfth night—I'm visiting the elder structure third in my circuit. I've already fed at the young enthusiastic structure and at a middle-aged structure that specializes in atmospheric observations, tracking the sky-plankton, monitoring cloud formations, predicting rain cycles. Now I spiral upward on a thermal still rising from the basalt, following the elder's call.

The air at thirty meters above the plateau is noticeably thinner, and I can feel my flight becoming less efficient. My wings beat faster, working harder to maintain altitude. But the view—I don't have human eyes, don't see as they see, but I perceive the plateau spread below me in infrared gradients and electromagnetic textures and the geometric patterns of basalt reflecting starlight. The suspension structures glow softly, green-blue jewels scattered across darkness. I can sense all seventeen structures in my territory simultaneously, can feel their states, know which ones are actively processing information and which ones are in rest cycles.

The stone-walkers are clustered in the valley below, seventeen individuals breathing in synchronized rhythms, their body heat creating a warm mass that I perceive as amber-orange pooled in darkness. One of the raptors is perched on an outcrop two hundred meters north—I sense its alertness, the electromagnetic signature of its nervous system, the slight heat of its body against cool stone. It senses me too, I'm certain, but doesn't move. We have our understanding.

I descend toward the elder structure in a tight spiral, broadcasting my identity through pheromones, announcing my arrival. The structure's field reaches out, catches me gently, guides me to a landing thread. I settle, fold my wings, begin walking inward. The thread hums with complexity—this structure is always thinking, always processing, never resting. I can feel it analyzing me as I approach: measuring my body chemistry, reading the information I'm carrying, determining my reproductive status, calculating how long I have left to live.

Four days. The structure tells me this not in words but in a chemical marker it releases into the thread, which I absorb through my feet, which my nervous system interprets. Four more days before my body fails, before the programmed senescence written into my cells completes its work. This doesn't sadden me—I don't think I'm capable of sadness. It's simply information, useful for planning. Four more nights of circuits. Time enough to visit each structure in my territory at least once more, to complete the information exchanges, to distribute my eggs.

I haven't mated yet. This is unusual but not unprecedented. Male thread-moths are less common than females—perhaps one male for every four females—and they're even shorter-lived, surviving only seven to nine days. I've encountered three males in my twelve nights, but none were compatible. Compatibility is specific, determined by chemical markers we inherit from our parent structures. I can only mate with males who were raised near structures that are genetically complementary to mine, ensuring that offspring represent true diversity, genuine recombination rather than near-identical reproduction.

But tonight—I sense it as I walk the elder structure's threads—there's a male here. He landed minutes before me, is currently near the node, feeding. The structure has arranged this, I realize. It's been coordinating, communicating with other structures across the plateau, tracking male populations, calculating compatibility, guiding us together through the calls it sends out into the night.

I reach the node and find him there. He's smaller than me, as males usually are, with wings more translucent, body more delicate. He's from the northern plateau, sixty kilometers away—I know this immediately from his scent, from the chemical markers he carries. His parent structure is one I've never visited, never communicated with, which means our genetic backgrounds are sufficiently different. We're compatible.

The elder structure facilitates our mating, creates a small stable zone in its field where we can couple without falling, without being disturbed by wind or predation risk. The act takes only minutes—transfer of genetic material, his sperm packet absorbed into my reproductive tract where it will meet the partially-formed eggs, complete them, finalize their genetic specifications. When we separate, he grooms his antennae briefly, then flies away into darkness. I'll never see him again. He'll be dead in three days, his purpose complete.

My purpose is just beginning its culmination. The eggs in my abdomen are developing rapidly now, drawing on the nutrients I've stored, incorporating the information I've gathered from all the structures I've visited, encoding into their DNA not just genetic instructions but observational data, environmental conditions, learned behaviors. My offspring will hatch knowing things I never knew, carrying forward information from across the plateau, representing a synthesis of what dozens of structures have observed and concluded.

Over the next four nights, I'll lay these eggs in carefully chosen locations—cracks in the basalt near young suspension structures that will benefit from the information my larvae carry, that will incorporate my offspring into their network, that will feed them and be fed by them in the endless reciprocal exchange that keeps this system alive.

I feed from the elder structure now, taking what I've earned. The nutrients flood into me, rich and strange, carrying patterns I can feel but not comprehend—four centuries of observation compressed into chemical form. I drink it in, store it, add it to my eggs' inheritance. When I lay them, they'll carry this forward, these ancient observations, ensuring that what the elder has learned won't be lost.

The structure releases me, and I walk back along the threads toward the periphery. As I walk, I pass suspended objects—a perfectly spherical stone, a water droplet the size of a berry, a fragment of something that looks like worked crystal. Each object tells a story the structure has captured and preserved: the stone shows erosion patterns from winds that blew ten thousand years ago, the water droplet contains minerals from a rain that fell last month, the crystal fragment suggests manufacture by intelligence, by hands or tools, though whether human or other isn't clear.

I launch myself from the final thread, falling for a moment before my wings catch air, and I spiral upward again, leaving the elder structure to its contemplations. The night is half-gone. I have time to visit two more structures before dawn forces me back to my crevice.

As I fly, I'm aware of my place in the system. I'm tiny—nearly weightless, short-lived, individually meaningless. But I'm also essential. Without thread-moths, the suspension structures would be isolated, each one an island processing its observations alone, unable to share, unable to coordinate. We moths are the plateau's nervous system, the signals traveling between neurons, ensuring that the whole can function as more than the sum of its parts.

Below me, a stone-runner pads silently across the basalt, hunting or patrolling or simply moving through its territory. It doesn't notice me, can't see me, doesn't know I exist. To the stone-runner, the night is about prey and survival, about the immediate concerns of hunger and territory. But I exist in a different register entirely. I'm part of the plateau's deep structure, its information architecture, its long-term memory and coordination system.

The beetles, active now in darkness, are cousins of a sort—they also serve the suspension structures, also carry materials and information. But they work in physical space, harvesting threads, building cases, managing the material economy. I work in information space, carrying data, distributing observations, ensuring intellectual continuity. Between us—moths and beetles—we keep the structures alive, and the structures keep the plateau in balance, and the plateau supports all of us, stone and life together, inseparable.

I reach the sixth structure in my territory, a middle-aged one specializing in beetle observations. It's been tracking beetle populations, monitoring their weaving patterns, noting gradual changes in behavior over generations. I land, walk inward, exchange my information for its observations. I learn things about beetles I never knew, patterns I couldn't see because I'm awake when beetles are less active, because my attention is focused elsewhere. The structure feeds me data about beetle navigation, about how they're adapting to slight changes in the placement of suspension structures, about new weaving patterns emerging in the southern territories.

This information will go into my eggs, will be carried by my larvae, will eventually inform other structures across the plateau. The beetles will never know they're being watched, studied, analyzed. But the plateau will know. The distributed intelligence will accumulate this data, will use it to make tiny adjustments—perhaps a suspension structure will grow slightly differently to accommodate shifting beetle routes, perhaps resources will be distributed more efficiently based on population trends. The changes will be invisible, incremental, but they'll maintain the balance.

I lay my first egg here, pressed into a crack near the structure's base. The egg is smaller than a grain of sand, translucent, already containing everything my larva will need to know: which threads to spin its cocoon from, which materials to incorporate, which frequencies to listen for. The structure accepts the egg, extends a thin thread to touch it, begins feeding it nutrients immediately. My offspring is already part of the network.

Four more eggs to lay tonight. Four more careful placements near structures that need what my larvae will bring. By the time I die, I'll have laid perhaps thirty eggs across my territory, distributing them according to patterns I don't consciously understand but that my body knows absolutely. Not all will survive—beetles might find some, wind-spiders might take others, random chance will claim a few. But enough will make it to adulthood to replace me and my generation, to continue the work.

The sky is beginning to lighten in the east—dawn is perhaps ninety minutes away. I need to return to my crevice soon. But first, one more structure, the seventh for tonight. I fly toward it, feeling fatigue in my wing muscles, feeling the night's work accumulating. I'm burning through energy stores, consuming myself to fly, to carry information, to serve the system.

The seventh structure is young, maybe twenty years old, still learning its role. I land and find it eager, almost anxious—it has so many observations to share, so much data it's collected, and so few moths visit because it's peripheral, located near the edge of my territory where ranges barely overlap. I'm one of only two or three moths who regularly include it in their circuits.

I spend extra time here, walking its threads carefully, accepting everything it wants to give me. The young structure has been watching the stone-walkers intensively, has detailed observations about their social behaviors, their communication patterns, their water-finding strategies. It's proud of this data, I sense—proud is anthropomorphizing, but there's something like eagerness in how it offers the information, something like hope that this data will be valued, shared, incorporated into the plateau's larger understanding.

I validate that hope. I take everything the structure offers, encode it into my remaining eggs, promise through pheromone markers that this information will be distributed, will reach elder structures, will become part of the permanent record. The young structure practically glows with satisfaction—again, anthropomorphizing, but there's a measurable change in its electromagnetic signature, a brightening of its bioluminescence, a shift in its thread harmonics that suggests contentment.

I lay two eggs here, more than I'd planned, because this structure needs them. It needs the connection, the continuation, the assurance that it's part of the network. My larvae will serve it well, will incorporate its observations into their cocoons, will grow up understanding this structure's particular genius for stone-walker analysis.

Dawn is close now—the eastern sky is definitely lighter, stars fading in that direction. I launch from the structure and fly hard for my crevice, racing the sun. Daylight is dangerous for thread-moths. Our pale wings, so perfect for starlit navigation, become visible to every predator. Our bodies, so efficient at night temperatures, overheat quickly in direct sun. We're creatures of darkness absolutely, and we must be hidden before dawn completes.

I reach my crevice with perhaps fifteen minutes to spare. I fold myself into the familiar crack, settling between basalt layers that are already beginning to warm slightly with the approaching day. The wind-spiders are here, returned from their own night's hunting. The mites are active, processing the microscopic algae they've harvested from the stone's surface. We share this space in peace, each following our own rhythms, each part of our own networks.

I sleep—if sleep is the word for the reduced metabolic state I enter during daylight. My consciousness dims but doesn't disappear entirely. I remain dimly aware of the crevice around me, of the stone warming as the sun rises, of the wind-spiders moving occasionally, of my own body processing the night's accumulated nutrients, continuing to develop eggs, preparing for tonight's circuit.

Four more nights. Perhaps one hundred and twenty more eggs to lay. Perhaps twenty more structures to visit, revisiting favorites, saying goodbye—though moths don't conceive of goodbye—to the network that has defined my existence. My offspring will wake in four months, will emerge into a plateau I won't live to see, will fly circuits I'll never complete. They'll carry my memories encoded in their genes, will continue the work without break, without gap, the information flowing continuously through moth generations, through suspension structure networks, through the plateau's vast distributed consciousness.

This is what I am: a neurotransmitter in the plateau's mind, a messenger carrying data between processing nodes, a living packet of information that flies through darkness and gives itself completely to the work of keeping this system alive, integrated, aware of itself. I exist for three weeks, but the information I carry is immortal, passed forward through endless generations of moths and structures and beetles and stone, cycling and recycling, evolving slowly, maintaining the equilibrium that lets all of us—mites and moths, beetles and stone-walkers, shrubs and structures, predators and observers—live together in this high warm place where breathing is deliberate and silence speaks volumes and stone dreams through the suspended fields that have taken its name.

I am tiny. I am essential. I am nearly ended. I am forever continued. I am the plateau thinking about itself through wings made of starlight and dust. I am exactly what I need to be, and I am exactly where I belong.
