# What Lives Here

I would be **a hethrim archivist-tender**, small and gray-skinned, living in the oldest section of the infrastructure where the wood has gone black with age and the joints fit so perfectly they seem to have grown rather than been cut.

My name in gesture-sign would be something like *Keeper-of-What-Cannot-Be-Opened*, a designation that spans both hands and requires the particular finger articulation that distinguishes the sacred from the merely important. In the spoken tongue—which I use rarely, finding its tonal complexities less precise than my hands—I am called Methrim, which means approximately "one who tends the threshold."

I am old, even by hethrim standards. Perhaps ninety years, perhaps more—the reckoning grows uncertain when you've lived long enough that memories blur into one another like water into water. My hair, once the deep green-black of healthy moss, has gone silver-white and grows now only in sparse tufts along my spine and at my joints. My skin has taken on that particular translucence that comes with age, showing the tendons and vessels beneath like architectural diagrams. My hands—my beautiful, essential hands—are still precise, still capable of the finest manipulation, though the joints ache in ways they did not when I was young.

I live alone, mostly. My chamber is a pocket of space I've maintained for sixty years, carved from overlapping platforms about forty feet above the moss floor in the valley called *the Deep Bowl* for its particular shape and acoustics. The entrance is narrow—other hethrim must turn sideways to enter, but I can slip through easily even now. Inside, the space opens into a rough sphere perhaps eight feet in diameter, enough for me to move freely, to hang my hammock, to arrange my possessions with the care they deserve.

The walls are hung with fiber bags containing my tools: the hooked knives for cutting rot from ancient wood, the ceramic vessels of preservative oils, the brushes made from velimara whiskers for cleaning carved surfaces, the measuring cords knotted at precise intervals according to the old proportions. There's a work platform that folds down from the wall when I need it, and a fire-vessel that I keep burning with particular care—my flame is descended from the archive-fire itself, carried here forty years ago when I was appointed to my position, and I have fed it every day since with the slow-burning fungus and fragments of especially resinous wood.

My life is measured in rounds—the circuits I make through my territory, checking the seven archive chambers I'm responsible for. Each round takes two days to complete if I'm thorough, which I always am. Three days if my joints are particularly troublesome or if I discover damage requiring immediate attention.

The archives themselves are sealed chambers, built into the ceiling structure where it's thickest and most stable. Each has a door of wood so old it's nearly stone, fitted with seals I refresh annually using moss-pitch and fiber wrapping. I don't know what's inside—that knowledge was lost generations before my birth, or was never meant to be shared with someone of my station. I only know that they must be kept sealed, kept dry, kept safe.

The doors are carved, each different. One shows figures that might be hethrim or might be something else, larger, working together to lift a great beam. Another is covered in the old ideographs, a text I can read only partially—something about cycles, about transformation, about "the choice that must be made when stone itself grows tired." A third has a map carved into it, showing valleys and passages, but the geography doesn't match our current system—perhaps it's older, showing the valleys as they were before some ancient collapse or subsidence changed their configuration.

I clean these doors during my rounds, using my softest brushes to remove the accumulated guano dust that settles on everything despite the eaves I've built above each entrance. I check the seals, pressing gently to test their integrity. I measure the gaps around each door—if they're widening, it means the structure is shifting, means I need to assess whether reinforcement is necessary. I leave offerings: small carved figures I make during the long nights in my chamber, arrangements of colored stones, sometimes just a pattern of moss fronds laid out in the old geometric forms.

Other hethrim treat me with cautious respect. They understand that archive-tenders are slightly apart from normal society, that we've dedicated ourselves to a function that requires isolation and constancy. Young ones are sometimes frightened of me—I'm old, I speak little, I appear without warning in sections of infrastructure most avoid. But the elders know my value. When they need to consult about the old ways, about proper forms for ceremonies, about the meanings of particular symbols carved into ancient beams, they come to me.

I don't attend most gatherings. The noise—even hethrim noise, which is gentle compared to the velimara's thunder—disrupts my concentration. I need silence, or the closest approximation possible in the valleys, to properly attend to my work. But four times per year, during the major observances, I emerge from my territory and travel to the gathering-zone in the valley's center. There I sit with the other archive-tenders—there are twelve of us across the connected valley system—and we share our observations in the formal signing that only we still use perfectly.

We discuss structural changes: which sections are failing fastest, where reinforcement is most urgently needed. We compare notes on seal maintenance and argue gently about proper technique—should the moss-pitch be heated or used cold? How long should fiber wrapping cure before it's considered reliable? We also share fragments of interpretation, trying to assemble meaning from the partial texts we guard. One tender might have made a new connection between symbols on their doors and symbols on mine. Another might have discovered, in some forgotten corner, a carving that provides context for something we've puzzled over for decades.

These gatherings are the closest I come to happiness, though even that word feels too loud for what I experience. Satisfaction, perhaps. A sense of rightness, of being in proper alignment with my purpose.

The rest of my time moves in quieter rhythms. I wake in my hammock to the sound that is always present, that I've learned to subdivide into its component parts—the main flock feeding in the valley's northern section, a smaller group circling closer to my position, the deeper rumble of wings against the eastern wall where the air currents are strange and require more effort to navigate. I eat sparingly: fungus-bread soaked in velimara-egg that I trade for by leaving cleaning services in others' territories, occasionally a finger-length piece of processed millipede if someone has made a gift.

Then I begin my rounds. I move through the infrastructure with the unconscious grace of long practice, my hands finding holds without visual confirmation, my feet knowing exactly how much weight each beam will bear. I travel through zones that vary from densely inhabited—where I must sign greetings to dozens of others, acknowledge children, avoid work crews—to completely empty, sections so far from any active community that I might go days seeing no one.

In the empty zones, I can hear myself think. I compose poems in the old signing, my hands moving through elaborate forms that no one else will see, describing what I observe: the way light falls through a particular gap in the architecture, the smell of fresh rot in a compromised beam, the pattern a group of velimara makes as they spiral upward. I don't record these poems. They exist only in the moment of their creation, offerings to the infrastructure itself, to the presence I sometimes feel in the oldest sections—not a ghost, not a consciousness, but something more like the valleys' memory of what they were built to be.

I also maintain. Constantly, endlessly, I maintain. I scrape accumulated guano from critical joints. I replace worn fiber bindings before they fail. I mark areas that need attention from specialized repair crews, using the sign-code we've developed—a particular arrangement of colored cords that means "inspect within a month" or "urgent, address immediately" or "stable but monitor." I clear velimara nests from locations where their weight is becoming problematic, relocating the nesting material to more appropriate platforms.

Sometimes I find objects. The infrastructure is vast, and there are pockets and crevices that might not have been opened in centuries. I discover caches of tools—ancient stone blades, bone awls, carved wooden instruments whose purposes I can only guess. I find these carefully, document their location in my mental map, and usually leave them in place. Some I bring to gatherings to show other tenders. Very rarely, if an object seems particularly significant, I might open it to wider circulation, letting it travel from clan to clan so everyone can study it and offer interpretations.

I've found fragments of writing, too. Not the formal carved ideographs but something more casual, perhaps personal—marks scratched into wood with no regard for durability, notes written for some immediate purpose and then forgotten. Most are illegible, the wood too degraded. But occasionally I can make out words, phrases: "the third tier requires adjustment"... "if the moss rises above this mark"... "remember what she said about the folding."

These fragments haunt me. They're voices from a past I can barely imagine, people who walked this infrastructure when it was new, who made decisions I'm now bound to honor without understanding their reasoning. Were they happy? Did they know what they were building would last so long? Did they imagine people like me, small and gray and dedicated, tending their work millennia later?

I have my private theories about the archives I guard. I believe—though I share this with no one—that they don't contain objects at all, or if they do, the objects are secondary. I think the archives are empty spaces, preserved voids, chambers that exist to remember what emptiness was before the valleys filled with moss and velimara and the thunder of ten thousand wings. I think they're silence made permanent, sealed away so that future generations—my generation—might know that silence once existed, that the world wasn't always this loud.

This is probably wrong. This is probably my old mind making poetry out of mystery. But I believe it anyway, in the deep places where belief lives regardless of evidence.

The rakmora trouble me, though I rarely see them. Sometimes, during my rounds, I find evidence of their presence near my territory—territorial markers left at burrow entrances, the remains of kills partially consumed and then abandoned. I alter my routes to avoid areas they're actively hunting. I'm too old to outrun a rakmora, too light to survive an attack if one mistakes me for prey.

But I also find their tools sometimes, lost or deliberately left. They're cruder than hethrim work but not without beauty—bone hooks with the grip-points carefully smoothed, stone blades knapped with real skill. Once I found a gliding aid, torn and abandoned, too damaged for repair. I studied it for hours before consigning it to the moss, appreciating the engineering even as I recognized the tool's purpose in killing the velimara I depend on.

I imagine sometimes that the rakmora have their own archive-tenders, individuals who maintain something sacred for their species, who hold knowledge that must be preserved even if no one alive remembers why. This is probably sentimental, probably projecting my own purposes onto creatures who simply hunt and mate and defend territory. But the possibility comforts me—the idea that I'm not alone in this particular form of dedication.

My greatest fear is that I'll die before training a successor. The position requires someone willing to accept isolation, willing to prioritize the archives above family, above comfort, above the normal pleasures of hethrim life. Such individuals are rare. I've been watching the younger generations, looking for someone who shows the necessary temperament, but I haven't found them yet.

Perhaps no one will replace me. Perhaps when I die—falling from some beam I've climbed a thousand times before, or simply failing to wake one morning in my hammock—my archives will go untended. The seals will degrade, the doors will warp, and eventually the chambers will be opened, accidentally or deliberately, and whatever's inside will be exposed to the damp air and the moss spores and the long slow degradation of time.

This possibility should trouble me more than it does. But I find I'm strangely at peace with it. I've done my duty as well as I could. I've kept what cannot be opened closed for sixty years. If the knowledge dies with me, if the cycle breaks, then perhaps that too is part of the pattern—perhaps even sacred duties have their end-points, their expiration dates written into structures too large to perceive from within.

My days in this place, in this body, in this purpose: They consist of waking to thunder, of hands moving through familiar routines, of the satisfaction of cleaned doors and refreshed seals, of walking through darkness with my fire-vessel carried before me, its light reflecting off wood gone black with age. They consist of silence, or what passes for silence here—the constant background roar that I've learned to hear through, to find the smaller sounds within.

They consist of being small in a place built for larger beings, of inhabiting an inheritance I don't fully understand, of maintaining what I cannot open and guarding what I cannot name. They consist of ending each day as I began it: alone, careful, dedicated to a function that might be crucial or might be meaningless but is, regardless, mine.

This is what lives here. This is what I am—a keeper of sealed doors, a tender of ancient seals, a small gray figure moving through the infrastructure in patterns worn smooth by repetition, part of the ecosystem as surely as the millipedes in the deep moss or the velimara in the air above. I am what the valleys made necessary. I am what persists.
