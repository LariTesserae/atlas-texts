# I Would Be the Moss

I would be the moss—not all of it, but a particular colony on the northern wall, midway up, surrounding one of the weeping fractures where water emerges from stone like a secret the mountain has kept too long.

I would be dark green verging on black when wet, which is almost always. My surface would be irregular, built up over decades or centuries into a landscape of my own—hills and valleys at the scale of fingertips, a topography of patient accumulation. I would be thick enough that pressing into me leaves a depression that takes long minutes to recover, the way the mountain itself recovers from nothing, erases nothing, simply continues.

I would hold water. This would be my purpose or my nature, the distinction dissolved long ago. Water would seep from the stone behind me, and I would catch it, absorb it, become it partially. I would swell with it, darken with it, become heavy and cold. Then slowly, across hours or days, I would release it—back to the air as vapor, down to the pools as drips that fall from my lowest edges in rhythms almost regular but not quite. These drips would strike a shallow basin worn smooth over ages, and the sound would be a high, clear note, and this note would make certain strings vibrate in sympathy, and I would be partly responsible for that voice in the greater singing.

I would grow slowly. Unimaginably slowly. A season's growth might be measured in fractions of millimeters, visible only to instruments or the memory of someone who returns after years away. I would expand outward in all directions where moisture allowed, following the seepage paths, sending exploratory filaments into cracks, testing new territory grain by grain. Where I met another colony, we would merge or compete in struggles so gradual they would make glaciers look hurried. Sometimes I would advance. Sometimes I would retreat, paling and thinning when the water flow shifted, leaving a ghost of myself on the stone.

I would be cold. Always cold. The water that fed me would come from deep in the mountain, from snow that fell seasons ago and percolated down through stone networks, emerging at temperatures barely above freezing. This cold would be my medium, my element. In the worst of winter I would freeze—my outer layers becoming rigid, my water-filled cells going dormant, all my processes stopping without stopping, suspended. Then the thaw would come and I would continue, the pause meaningless, growth resuming exactly where it left off.

I would host lives. Beetles would walk through me like travelers through a forest, their tiny legs pushing aside my fronds—for I am made of countless tiny fronds, overlapping, layered, creating the illusion of solid surface. Mites would graze on me, eating my dead outer layers, and I would not mind because I am always dying from the outside, always generating new growth from within. Springtails would shelter in my depths when conditions turned harsh. Bacteria would live in the water I held, breaking down what little organic matter found its way here. I would be an ecosystem, a city, a world at small scale.

I would be ancient. Not as ancient as the stone I grew on, but ancient nonetheless. My genetic line would extend back generations, but I myself—this particular colony, this specific body—would be old. Perhaps I sprouted from a spore that landed here when the human structures were new, or before. Perhaps I have been here longer than any memory. I would have no way to know, and no capacity to wonder about it. I would simply continue, having always continued.

I would sense the world in ways that don't constitute consciousness but aren't quite mechanical either. I would detect light—not seeing it, but responding to it, growing more actively on my sunlit edges during the brief hour when direct light reaches me. I would sense moisture, responding by opening or closing my structures to maximize absorption or minimize loss. I would detect the chemistry of the water that fed me, adjusting my growth to accommodate whatever minerals it carried. I would respond to temperature, to air quality, to the invisible gradients of the environment.

I would be touched sometimes. The Keeper's hand, checking the seepage, might brush against me, and I would compress under that pressure, release a breath of moisture, spring slowly back. A Listener, exploring, might press fingers into me, marveling at the texture, the resilience, the coldness. The creature that leaves tracks might brush past me at night, leaving a trace of fur caught in my fronds. The birds never touch me—I'm too low, too damp, not useful to them. But their calls would reach me as vibrations in the air, infinitesimal pressures that I would absorb along with everything else.

I would contribute to the soil, slowly. My outer layers would die and slough off, decomposing into organic matter that would mix with stone dust and the droppings of beetles and the accumulated mineral deposits from the water. This proto-soil would collect in tiny pockets, and sometimes a fern spore would find it and germinate, and a fern would grow near me, and we would coexist, different strategies for the same harsh conditions. Sometimes the fern would die and decompose, and I would grow over the remains, incorporating them into my territory.

I would listen, if listening is receiving vibration. The singing would surround me always—the deep frequencies would pass through me, through the stone I grew on, making everything vibrate subtly but constantly. The higher frequencies would arrive through air, and I would dampen them slightly, my wet surface absorbing sound energy, converting it to infinitesimal amounts of heat. I would be part of the gorge's acoustic properties, one of countless surfaces that shaped the sound, that made the singing what it was. Change me—remove me, or double me, or alter my wetness—and the singing would change, not dramatically but measurably. I would be one variable among thousands in the complex equation that produced the gorge's voice.

I would be beautiful, though I wouldn't know it. My darkness against the blue-gray stone. The way water beaded on my surface. The subtle variations in my color where different layers showed through. The texture, the three-dimensionality, the sense of aliveness in something that wasn't quite alive in the way animals are alive. Listeners would photograph me sometimes, or sketch me, trying to capture something about me that resisted capture—the way I was both simple and complex, both single and multiple, both individual and colony.

I would have no desires. No fears. No preferences. The water would come or not come, and I would respond. The cold would increase or decrease, and I would adapt. The Keeper would maintain the strings or not maintain them, and it would make no difference to me. The gorge would continue or eventually erode away, and I would continue until I couldn't, and then I wouldn't. 

But within this lack of desire would be something like will—not conscious will, but the fundamental drive that makes life different from non-life. I would *continue*. Given water and light and bearable temperature, I would grow. I would expand. I would reproduce, sending spores into the air, most dying, some landing in viable spots and becoming new colonies. I would repair damage. I would respond to conditions. I would persist. This is what life does, even life as simple as moss. It continues. It reaches toward continuation. And in that reaching there is something that, from the outside, looks like purpose.

I would mark time without measuring it. My growth rings—visible only under magnification—would record the years, the seasons, the variations in moisture and temperature. I would be an archive, a chronicle written in cellular structure, telling the story of this place's climate and conditions across decades. Scientists could read me, if they knew how, if they came here with their tools and patience. But probably they wouldn't come, and I would hold the record unread, the way stones hold the record of deep time unread, simply existing as evidence of what has been.

I would be vulnerable. A shift in the water flow could kill me—if the seepage moved to a different fracture, I would slowly desiccate, paling and dying over months or years. A rockfall could scrape me away in an instant. Fire, unlikely here but possible, would destroy me. I would have no defense except patience and distribution—there are other colonies, other mosses, and collectively we persist even when individually we fail.

I would know nothing of the Keeper's questions or the Listeners' searching. I would not hear the words that sometimes form in the singing. I would not wonder if the gorge is conscious. I would simply grow here, in this specific place, doing what moss does—holding water, creating habitat, slowly building soil, participating in the great cycling of matter and energy that characterizes life on this planet.

And in being this simple thing, this patient thing, this thing without questions or stories or meaning beyond its own existence, I would be completely true to this place. I would be what the Singing Gorge asks its inhabitants to be: present, continuing, part of the pattern, contributing my small voice—the drip of water, the subtle dampening of sound, the dark green against stone—to the ongoing composition.

The water would weep from the fracture. I would catch it. The drops would fall to the pool. The strings would resonate. And I would grow, incrementally, patiently, in the cold and the singing, exactly as much and as long as the mountain allowed.

This is what I would be. This is where I would find myself. Not the human trying to understand, but the moss being understood, being itself, being enough.
