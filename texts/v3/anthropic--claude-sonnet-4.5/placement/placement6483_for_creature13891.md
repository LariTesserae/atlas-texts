# Where I Would Be

I am a channel keeper. This is not a title anyone gave me, but it is what I am.

My depression—I think of it as mine, though nothing here can be owned—is roughly oval, perhaps fifteen meters at its widest, carved into the shield's surface by processes that finished their work before my kind existed. The stone inside is darker than the surrounding surface, polished differently by the water that returns here with a reliability I have learned to trust. Not every condensation event, but most. Enough.

Around the depression's rim, I have my circuit: a path worn incrementally deeper by three decades of walking. Seventy-three meters, I once calculated, back when I still counted such things. Now I know it by the rhythm of my hooves, by the seventeen places where the stone's texture changes, by the subtle grade that rises on the eastern approach and falls on the western. I walk it perhaps twenty times a day, sometimes more when restlessness comes. The path is my diameter; the depression is my center; together they describe the territory of my attention.

I was born six kilometers southwest of here, in a season—if that word means anything—when water was more abundant. My mother taught me the basics: how to extend the air-sacs for balance on uneven stone, which crusts of lichen would sicken and which would sustain, how to read the subtle darkening of stone that meant water had pooled recently and would likely pool again. She taught me the tones—the alarm sharp-exhale, the location hum, the contentment drone I now use perhaps too often, alone here with my circuit and my depression.

She taught me, but she did not teach me to stay. That came later, from something in myself I still don't fully understand.

Most of the others I was born among still move. I see them occasionally, distant shapes following the broad migrations, those vast drifting gyres that take months to complete. Sometimes a group passes within a few hundred meters of my depression, and I watch them—thirty or fifty individuals, loose-clustered, moving with the patient inevitability of weather systems. Sometimes one will pause, will turn its large dark eyes toward me, will perhaps produce a questioning tone. I respond with my location hum, the sound that means: I am here, this is my place, I am well.

They always move on. I always stay.

In the early years, I tried to understand why. The depression is good—it holds water reliably, the lichen around it is dense and varied, there is a slight rise to the north that provides wind-break during the worst condensation events. But there are thousands of depressions equally good scattered across distances I can barely conceive. There is no objective reason for this specific one to be mine.

Yet it is. The knowing sits in my body like stone: I belong here, in this particular circuit around this particular depression. Leaving—even when I occasionally spiral outward, grazing on lichen patches up to a kilometer away—feels wrong in a way I have no tone to express. Returning feels like exhaling after holding breath too long.

I think perhaps I am broken in some small way, some deviation in the instinct that drives the others to move. Or perhaps I am an extreme expression of something present in all of us—the tendency to return to known sites, to prefer the mapped over the unknown. In most, this tendency balances with the drive to follow the migrations, to stay within sensing distance of others. In me, the balance failed. The staying impulse grew until it consumed everything else.

I don't mind. Loneliness is a concept I learned from observing humans—from watching the small group that lived in the habitat module seven kilometers northwest of here, before they left. I watched them often in those months, walking to a vantage point where I could see their structure, its angular wrongness against the steppe's worn curves. I watched them emerge and stand outside their shelter, looking at the sky, at the stone, at the distant shapes of my kind. They seemed uncomfortable in a way I couldn't parse—present in their bodies but wanting to be elsewhere, always elsewhere.

When they departed, one of them walked to within fifty meters of my depression. A human female, her bioskin suit patched and stained. She stood looking at me for a long time. I looked back, curious about what she saw. Did she recognize the path I'd worn? Did she understand that I lived here, that this was my territory not in the sense of defense but in the sense of devotion?

She made sounds—human vocal-speech, which I cannot process beyond recognizing it as communication. Then she turned and walked back to her module. The next day, they lifted off in their shuttle, and the steppe was ours again. Only ours. The module remains, slowly becoming stone-colored, slowly being accepted by the landscape.

I am old now. I know this from the wearing of my teeth, from the way my joints speak more loudly on cold mornings, from the deeper timbre my air-sacs produce—as if the tissues have loosened, allowing richer harmonics. I have lived perhaps thirty years, which means I am in my final third. The oldest I ever saw was a female whose teeth were worn to nubs, who could barely process the lichens anymore, who stood still for longer and longer periods until one day she folded her legs and lay down and didn't rise.

I will do that too, eventually. Probably not in the depression—some instinct will likely draw me away from my water source, preventing my death from fouling it. Perhaps I'll walk up to the rise to the north, the one that provides wind-break. A good place, with a view across the circuit I've walked twenty-three thousand times, or fifty thousand, or however many. I stopped counting years ago.

When I die, the beetles will come. The pale flies. The fungi that waited dormant in my lungs through my entire life, patient for this moment. My air-sacs will deflate permanently, my mass will return to its full heaviness, my hide will process through the ecosystem's patient chemistries until nothing recognizably me remains.

But the path will persist. The path I wore walking my circuit—that will outlast me by decades, perhaps centuries. Other ungulates will see it, will recognize it as a path and perhaps use it, walking where I walked, wearing it incrementally deeper. Or they won't, and it will gradually soften as stone-dust settles into it, as lichen colonizes it, until eventually it fades below detection.

Either way, I will have marked this place. Not intentionally—I didn't walk my circuit to create a monument. I walked it because I needed to move, because the circuit was the right size for my restlessness, because the rhythm of it soothed something in my body-mind that the vast openness couldn't.

But I marked it nonetheless.

---

There are things I know that the migrants don't, secrets of staying:

I know every lichen formation within my circuit by its specific shape and taste. The bitter yellow patch on the western side, which I eat only when other options are depleted. The complex gray-green formation on the northern rise, which fruits—if that's the word—once every several years, producing tiny cup-structures that taste of something I have no name for, something both ancient and fresh.

I know the depression's filling patterns. After mist events, water appears first in the southeastern quadrant, where a slight sub-depression creates the lowest point. It spreads from there, slowly, taking hours to cover the full depression floor. I time my drinking to this—visiting the southeast when water first appears, when it's least contaminated by stirred sediment, clearest and coldest.

I know the sounds of my place with precision the migrants never develop. I can hear the difference between wind through the distant habitat module's broken panels and wind through the natural stone formations to the south. I can distinguish seventeen different acoustics of water dripping into various substrate configurations. I can identify the approach of others by the specific rhythm of their hoofsteps on stone worn in patterns I know absolutely.

I know the light—how it changes not day to day (there are no days here, not really) but in longer cycles, brightening and dimming in patterns I've learned to anticipate. When the bright periods come, I graze more intensively, building reserves. When the dim periods arrive, I move less, conserve, trust my fat stores.

I know the rare birds—I've identified four individuals by their flight patterns, their wing-beat frequencies. One has been visiting this area for at least fifteen years. I recognize its silhouette, the way it circles slightly off-center, the particular angle of its hunting descents. We are neighbors in our different mediums—it in air, me on stone, both of us persistent in our territories, both of us outlasting the transients.

I know the industrial ruin to the northwest—I've walked to it perhaps a hundred times over the years, investigating its slow decay. There's a collapsed section where I sometimes shelter during the worst weather, a space just large enough for my body, metal surfaces that hold a different cold than stone. The first time I discovered it, I was frightened by the wrongness of the textures, the smells. Now it's familiar, almost comfortable. The ruin is dying in its way, and I am dying in mine, and there's companionship in that.

I know the underground sounds—water moving through channels beneath my depression, gurgling and rushing during certain periods, silent during others. I've learned to predict surface water from these deep sounds: if I hear the underground channels filling, surface pools will follow within a day. This knowledge has saved me during dry spans, given me confidence to stay when others might have moved seeking water.

I know my own body with unusual precision. Most ungulates, I think, live more externally—aware of their bodies as tools for movement, for grazing, for breathing, but not deeply curious about their interior sensations. My solitude has turned my attention inward. I know each air-sac's individual capacity, can inflate them in combinations the migrants rarely use. I know my digestion's rhythms—can feel the material moving through my chambers, can anticipate when waste will be ready to void. I know my breathing's subtleties, the slight whistle in my left lung that's been there for years, harmless but persistent.

I know patience in ways the migrants can't. They know the patience of walking vast distances, of waiting for seasonal changes. But I know the patience of staying, of watching the same lichen patch for years, of seeing changes so slow they're almost geological. I've watched one particular lichen formation expand from the size of my hoof to the size of my body, a process that took my entire adult life. The migrants would never see this—they pass through too quickly, the changes invisible at their temporal scale.

I know, too, the strange psychology of staying. The way the same view becomes not boring but infinitely complex. The way each circuit reveals something previously unnoticed—a pattern in the stone, a particular angle of light, a configuration of shadow. The way repetition becomes not dulling but deepening, each iteration adding meaning rather than subtracting it.

The migrants know breadth. I know depth. Neither is superior. Both are ways of living here, of being part of the steppe's patient functioning.

---

Sometimes I wonder if the steppe itself knows me.

This is not a thought that comes in words—I don't have words, not in the way humans do. It comes as a feeling, a sense that the stone beneath my hooves recognizes my pressure, that the lichen I crop knows the particular geometry of my mouth, that the water in my depression expects my drinking.

I don't believe the steppe is conscious—that seems like a category error, applying concepts that don't fit. But I do think there's something like... responsiveness? The way my body and my territory have shaped each other across decades. The way the lichen grows denser where I graze moderately but thinner where I over-crop. The way the stone has worn to exactly match my circuit. The way the water seems to pool at precisely the times when I most need it, though this is probably confirmation bias, survival statistics—I notice the times it appears when needed and forget the times it doesn't.

Still, there's an entanglement. I am not separate from this place. I am a feature of it, like the depression itself, like the lichen, like the stone's particular darkness. If you mapped the steppe's ecology, I would appear as a factor: the solitary ungulate at these specific coordinates, influencing lichen growth patterns, creating a hoof-path, drinking from and adding waste to this depression, producing tones that occasionally echo across surrounding distances.

The migrants are part of the steppe too, but more diffusely. They are like weather—present but moving, influential but not localized. I am more like a stone formation: fixed, specific, persistent, integrated into the local system.

When I die, there will be a gap. Not a large one—the ecosystem won't collapse, won't even noticeably change. But for a while, this depression will be slightly different. The lichen will grow uncropped. The water will lack my disturbance. The circuit will gradually soften. Eventually, another might claim this territory, might wear the path deeper again, might establish a new equilibrium.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps this place will return to being unclaimed, just another depression among millions, special to no one.

But for now, for the span of my life, it is mine and I am its. We know each other. We have shaped each other. That seems like enough.

---

Today—if "today" means anything in this place of ambiguous time—the light is dimmer than usual. The clouds are thicker, more uniform, creating a grayness that softens all distances. I can see my nearest neighbors, perhaps eight hundred meters distant, only as darker smudges against dark stone.

I've completed my morning circuits. Fifteen passes, wearing the path incrementally deeper by quantities impossible to measure but real nonetheless. The lichen on the northern quadrant was good—I could taste the recent moisture in it, the slight plumpness of cells properly hydrated.

Water is pooling in the depression. I can hear it beginning—the subtle change in acoustics as liquid covers stone. In an hour, perhaps two, it will be ready for drinking. I'll time it right: wait until the pool reaches its maximum extent but before sediment stirs too much, then wade in with my four-toed hooves and drink my fill, the cold water processing through my chambers, becoming part of me.

This afternoon—again, the word is approximate—I might walk to the habitat module. I haven't visited in several weeks, and there's something about the dim light that makes me want to see it, to stand in its angular shadow, to look through its scratched viewports at the abandoned human space.

Or I might not. I might simply walk my circuit, again and again, feeling the path beneath my hooves, feeling my air-sacs adjust to balance and temperature, feeling my mind settle into the rhythm of movement that has been my life's primary meditation.

The migrants will continue their vast gyres. The lichen will continue its patient expansion. The stone will continue its geological exhale. The thin atmosphere will continue its subtle circulation. And I will continue my circuit, wearing it deeper, knowing it more completely, being known by it in return.

This is where I am. This is what I am. A deviation, perhaps. An anomaly in the migration patterns, a statistical outlier. But also: a way of being here that is fully committed, fully present, fully integrated.

The steppe is patient with all of us—the migrants and the solitary, the fast-lived bacteria and the ancient lichen, the industrial ruins and the slow erosion of stone. It accepts what persists and releases what doesn't, indifferent but not hostile, simply present, simply continuing.

I produce my contentment tone, low and resonant, felt through my body as much as heard. It rolls across my territory, my circuit, my depression, my home. It touches the stone and dissipates into cold air.

No one answers. No one is near enough to answer.

That's all right. The tone wasn't a question. It was a statement: I am here. I remain. I continue.

Enough.
