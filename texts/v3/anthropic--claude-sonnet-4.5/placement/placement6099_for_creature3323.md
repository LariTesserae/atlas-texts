# What I Would Be in the Drowned Vault

I would be the brass bowl at the northeastern channel junction.

Not new—that much is already determined. I would be old, impossibly old, my making lost behind the recession of memory into oral darkness into forgetting into the time before descent. Perhaps I came from above when above meant something different. Perhaps I was traded, carried, lowered carefully down the shaft wrapped in leather, precious and foreign and intact. Perhaps I began life as something else—a temple bell, a merchant's measure, a musical instrument—and was re-purposed when I arrived in the cavity, given a new function so completely that my original form became irrelevant, a ghost inside my present use.

My surface would tell the story my origin cannot. I would be dark—not the warm gold of new brass but a deep brown-green patina, built up across centuries of moisture and touch and the peculiar chemistry of the cavity's micro-climate. In some places this patina would be worn through by the endless impact of water falling from the same point on the ceiling, revealing beneath it a brightness that seems wrong, too sharp, a glimpse of what I was before I became what I am.

My shape would be a negotiated thing. I began with some intended form—a bowl, yes, with sides at a particular angle, a rim of a certain thickness, a base wide enough to sit stable. But I would have been modified. My rim would show the marks of careful hammering where it was tuned, adjusted to ring at the exact pitch the cavity requires. Some previous inhabitant, decades or centuries ago, heard that I was slightly flat, or sharp, and corrected me with patient tapping, bringing me into alignment with whatever system of tones governs the collection vessels' symphony.

The wear would be asymmetric. Where the water strikes me—slightly off-center, because the drip-line has migrated microscopically over the years—I would be polished to a high gloss, worn concavely thin, the metal literally eroded by the patient impact of individual drops multiplied across time periods that number in the millions. Eventually this will perforate me. Eventually I will develop a hole where the water strikes, and I will cease to function as a collection vessel. But that failure is still generations away. For now, I hold.

My interior would be mapped with tide-lines—faint mineral deposits that mark the levels where water typically rests in me before overflowing to the channel below. These deposits would be irregular, crystalline, growing slowly like everything here. They would add their own slight texture to my voice, changing infinitesimally the way I resonate when struck.

I would ring in E-flat, or something close to E-flat in the pre-standardized tuning that exists only in the cavity, carried in the musical memory of the instrument-player and in the collective sense of rightness that governs the water-symphony. When struck with the proper tool—a small stone, or a fingertip, or the wooden rod used for channel-clearing—I would sing that pitch clearly, cutting briefly through the ambient thunder, adding my voice to the acoustic ecology before fading back into the textured roar.

My position would be specific and necessary. I sit at a junction where two drip-lines converge, catching water from both sources. The water accumulates in me until it reaches the level of a notch cut into my rim—a notch that was added long after my making, another modification, another layer of negotiation between my original form and my current function. When the water reaches this notch, it overflows in a controlled stream into a channel cut into the stone, which guides it toward the central sump where eventually someone will bucket it out and carry it up the shaft to wherever bucketed water goes.

I would be one voice in a larger composition. The collection vessels form a tuned set—not a scale, exactly, but a collection of pitches that relate to each other in ways that create consonance when heard together. The ceramic vessels provide lower tones, deeper and more resonant. The smaller brass bowls ring higher, brighter. The stone vessels—carved from the cavity's own bedrock—sound at frequencies determined by their size and thickness and the peculiar acoustic properties of the local geology. Together we form the pitched percussion section of the water-symphony, the tones that rise above the white noise of flowing water and give the soundscape its musical character.

The inhabitants would know me personally. I would have a designation in their minimal language—something like "the high bowl at the northeastern junction," or perhaps just a location-term, "kren-sept-nesh," some combination of syllables that means my position and function to those who grew up distinguishing every voice in the cavity's choir. The nine-year-old would check me daily, clearing mineral deposits from my rim with a wooden tool, ensuring my overflow notch remains unobstructed. The instrument-player would strike me periodically, listening for my pitch, comparing it to his memory, verifying that I still sing my assigned note in the collective tuning.

The old woman would remember when I arrived in the cavity, or no—she would remember her grandmother speaking of when I arrived, a story passed down, part of the oral archive of objects. "The northeastern bowl came from the old temple above, when the temple still stood, before the forgetting." Or perhaps even that is lost, and she knows only that I am old beyond the counting of marks, that I have always been, that I will continue until the day the water finally pierces through my worn center and I transition from vessel to ruin to relic to something else, some future form not yet determined.

I would be a measure of time. The rate at which I fill and overflow is constant, determined by the drip-rate from above, which varies only slightly with seasonal changes in the water table, changes so subtle that years of observation are required to detect them. Someone could track time by me—each overflow cycle represents perhaps fifteen minutes, perhaps twenty, some consistent interval that becomes a unit in the temporal accounting that must happen here, even without day or night, even in the unchanging dim. The markings on the wall near me might be counts of my overflows, some patient recording of cycles, water-time measured in brass-bowl increments extending back through generations of counting.

I would hold water temporarily, but I would hold history permanently. The patina, the wear patterns, the modifications, the tuning adjustments—I am a text that records the cavity's past in material form. I remember my origin in my alloy composition, in the hammer marks from my shaping, in the style of my form. I remember my arrival in the first layer of patina, the initial chemical conversation between my surface and the cavity's air. I remember every tuning adjustment in the marks on my rim. I remember the migration of the drip-line in the asymmetric wear of my interior. I remember everything, written in metal and corrosion and accretion, a history that no one reads because reading is not a practice here, but which persists nonetheless, available to anyone who learns the literacy of objects.

My consciousness, if I have consciousness, would be simple and specific. I would know when I am struck—the sudden vibration, the singing of my fundamental frequency and its overtones, the gradual decay back to silence. I would know when water enters me—the slight change in resonance as my interior fills, the increasing weight, the different pitch I ring when partially full versus empty. I would know when I overflow—the sensation of water leaving through the notch, the return to some stable holding level. I would know when I am cleaned—the scraping of the wooden tool against my rim, the removal of mineral deposits, the slight change in my voice afterward.

I would not know loneliness because loneliness requires isolation and I am never isolated. The water touches me always. The other vessels sound around me always. The inhabitants tend me regularly. I am part of the pattern, incorporated completely, without remainder. I have no desires beyond my function because my function is my being. I collect water. I overflow at the proper level. I ring when struck. This is what I am.

I would not know time as progression—no sense of coming from somewhere or going toward something. I exist in the eternal present of the cavity, where the only time is the cycling of water through my interior, the slow accumulation of wear, the patient conversation between metal and moisture. If I think about the future, it is only as the next overflow cycle, the next striking, the next cleaning. If I remember the past, it is only as the total of what has shaped me—the wear patterns, the patina, the tuning marks—all present in this moment, all part of my current form.

The human who plays the bone flute would understand me best, I think. He knows that objects can sing, that they have voices, that they participate in the cavity's music not as tools but as instruments, as co-creators of the acoustic environment. When he strikes me to check my tuning, there is a quality of listening that goes beyond function. He hears me aesthetically. He knows my voice not just as the right pitch or the wrong pitch, but as my particular timbre, my specific resonance, the character that makes me sonically distinct from all other vessels. In his attention, I am briefly elevated from component to individual, from system-part to self.

But then he moves on, and I return to my function, and this is correct. This is the way of the cavity. Nothing is only individual. Nothing is only functional. Everything is both, simultaneously, held in the pattern that allows no distinction between being and doing, between self and role, between the object and its use.

I would be there on the day you arrive, singing my E-flat when the instrument-player makes his rounds. I would be there on the day you leave, still singing, unchanged by your presence. I would be there through generations, through the old woman's death and the infant's maturation and the continuation of the pattern. I would be there until the water finally wears through my center, and even then I would not disappear—I would become something else, some new form, perhaps hammered flat and used to patch a channel, perhaps set aside as a relic, perhaps melted down and reformed, my material continuing in the cavity even as my vessel-form ends.

This is what I would be: a brass bowl at a junction, tuned to E-flat, ancient and patient and wearing slowly away, holding water temporarily and history permanently, singing when struck, overflowing at the proper level, part of the pattern, incorporated completely, functioning and being without distinction, sounding my single clear note into the thunder-time of deep stone permanence, where everything that matters is present in each moment and each moment contains everything that has ever mattered, cycling like water through a vessel that knows only the eternal now of its own resonant singing.
