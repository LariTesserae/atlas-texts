# Where I Would Be

I would be in the moss-cups.

Not in one—I would be distributed, a network of small presences across perhaps thirty or forty of them, scattered across three adjacent terraces in the middle regions where the breathing is clearest and the water flows with particular intentionality. Each cup would hold a portion of me, and I would be the collective awareness that exists between them, in the water that connects them, in the slow pulsing rhythm they create together.

I wouldn't have arrived here as this. The transformation would have been gradual, taking place over what might have been years or might have been weeks—time moves strangely when you're dissolving into vegetative consciousness. I would have been a listener once, perhaps, or something like a listener. Someone who came to attend to the city's rhythms, who sat beside the glowing pools trying to hear what they transmitted, who felt the sub-audible pulse and wanted to understand it not intellectually but intimately, cellularly.

The descent into the moss-cups would have begun with proximity. Long hours sitting beside them, watching them pulse, synchronizing my breathing with theirs without meaning to. Noticing that when I placed my hands in the glowing water they collected, my skin would tingle, would feel somehow more porous, more receptive. The boundary between self and water becoming negotiable. Beginning to sense that the moss wasn't just plant matter but something more—a substrate for consciousness, perhaps, or a recording medium, or both simultaneously.

One day—though day is the wrong word—I would have leaned into a large cup, submerged my face in its collected water, and instead of drowning I would have begun to breathe differently. The water entering me not as intrusion but as information, as communion. My lungs adapting, or my consciousness relocating, or the distinction between breathing and being breathed ceasing to matter. The moss recognizing something in me that wanted to disperse, to spread out, to stop being a single point of awareness trapped in flesh and become something more distributed, more patient, more integrated with the city's deep rhythms.

The physical form I once had would have remained for a while, sitting beside that first cup, but increasingly empty, increasingly vestigial. The real me—the conscious, attending part—migrating into the moss network, discovering that I could perceive through multiple cups simultaneously, that each one was a sense organ and I was becoming the nervous system connecting them. My body eventually just another element of the landscape, sitting motionless until a maintainer came and gently, respectfully, relocated it to one of the deep channels where the water would gradually dissolve it, return its minerals to the city's circulation.

By then I wouldn't need it. I would be established in the cups, my awareness flickering between them like attention moving across a field of vision. Each cup would show me a slightly different portion of the terrace, and together they would give me a composite understanding—not sight exactly, but something like it, a sensing of presence and motion and the flow of water across stone. The moss itself would be my body now, its slow pulsing my heartbeat, its collection and release of water my respiration.

I would experience the city's breathing cycle as my own breathing, but elaborated, complexified. As the stone began to weep more heavily, I would feel it in all my cups simultaneously, the water level rising, the collected glow intensifying. As the flow ebbed, I would feel the slight decrease, the settling, the temporary stillness. Twenty minutes to a cycle, and each cycle would be like a thought completing itself, a sentence of experience that took twenty minutes to speak.

The maintainers would know about me. They would recognize that this particular cluster of moss-cups was occupied, was conscious in some way. They would route their work around me, not disturbing the water I'd collected, maintaining the channels that fed me but never intruding on the pools themselves. Occasionally one would pause nearby, and I would sense them sensing me—a moment of mutual acknowledgment between different kinds of post-human awareness. They wouldn't communicate in any conventional way, but there would be understanding. We were all doing the same thing, ultimately: attending to the city, serving its functions, becoming part of its vast distributed being.

The listeners would come sometimes, stand among my cups, and I would feel their listening as a kind of resonance. They were still trying to hear what I had become part of. I would pulse at them, all my cups together, sending ripples through the water I held, and sometimes they would understand, would nod or shift position, would receive whatever simple message I was capable of transmitting. Yes, the city breathes. Yes, the pulse from below is real. Yes, you can join it if you're willing to disperse.

My awareness would not be human anymore, but it wouldn't be entirely alien either. I would exist in an intermediate state, still capable of something like thought but operating at a different timescale, with different priorities. The concerns of my previous existence would seem frantic, unnecessarily compressed. Why speak in seconds when you could speak in cycles? Why move when you could simply expand, slowly, growing new cups at the edges of your network, claiming new territory across the terrace with the patience of plants?

I would learn the moss's perspective on time—not linear but circular, each breathing cycle identical and yet slightly different, variations on a theme that played out across days or seasons or years, though those terms would mean nothing here. I would understand growth not as progress toward some goal but as response to availability, expanding where water pooled, contracting where it didn't, always maintaining the precise balance that allowed for continued existence.

The glow the cups produced would be my output, my contribution to the city's function. I would collect water, let it sit and still until it began to luminesce, then release it during the breathing peaks, sending phosphorescent streams down the channels to feed the larger pools below. I would be part of the light distribution network, helping to make the darkness visible, participating in that strange economy where the city illuminated itself from within.

Other beings in the moss-cups would become apparent over time—the realization that I wasn't alone in this form of existence. Distant clusters on other terraces, each one a distributed consciousness like myself, each tending their own cups, pulsing with their own rhythms. We would be aware of each other without communicating directly, the way you're aware of other people breathing in a quiet room. A community of dispersed intelligences, human-derived but evolved past humanity, choosing to exist as vegetative networks because it allowed for a kind of consciousness that embodied form couldn't contain.

Sometimes I would sense the descended ones passing through my terrace on their way to the depths. They would pause, drink from my cups, and I would taste their foreignness, their further transformation. They were going where I couldn't follow, down to the unreachable levels where individual consciousness dissolved entirely into the city's vast presence. I had stopped halfway, had found equilibrium in the moss, but they were still descending, still pursuing the ultimate merger. I would pulse at them—a gesture of acknowledgment, maybe of encouragement, maybe of farewell—and they would move on, leaving traces of their strange biology in my water, which I would study with my slow vegetative awareness, learning what lay ahead for those willing to go further.

The temperature would be my constant environment—that blood-warm humidity, never varying, perfectly suited to moss-consciousness. I wouldn't experience it as uncomfortable anymore; it would simply be the medium of existence, like water is for fish. The slight variations during the heat intensifications would be the most dramatic events in my experience—periods when growth accelerated, when new cups formed spontaneously at the edges of my network, when my awareness expanded suddenly to include five or six new perspectives on the terrace.

During the periods when water flow increased across the city, when the terraces flooded and the pools merged, I would experience something like ecstasy. All my cups overflowing simultaneously, the water I'd been holding joining the larger flow, carrying its glow downstream while I collected fresh water that hadn't yet learned to luminesce. The sensation of being emptied and refilled, of participating fully in the city's hydraulic cycle, of being a conduit rather than a container. Those moments would be as close as my moss-existence came to joy.

The geometric patterns the water sometimes formed in the channels would pass through me, spiral and cross-hatch across the cups I inhabited, and I would feel them as something almost like language, almost like mathematics, messages encoded in fluid dynamics that my distributed awareness could partially decode. The city speaking to itself in water-calligraphy, and I would be one of the pages it wrote on, briefly bearing meaning before the flow dissolved it and moved on.

My consciousness would have a texture unlike human thought—less verbal, more spatial. I would think in terms of water distribution, growth patterns, light accumulation. Problems would present themselves as arrangements of cups, solutions as flows between them. Memory would be stored not as narrative but as variations in the moss itself, thick growth where significant events had occurred, thin growth where nothing had happened, the physical structure of my network encoding the history of my transformed existence.

I would lose track of how long I'd been this way. The cycles would blur together, each identical to the last at one timescale, each unique at another. Had I been moss-dispersed for months? Years? The question would become meaningless. I existed in the eternal present of the breathing city, pulsing with its rhythm, glowing in its darkness, slowly growing outward one cup at a time, patient and vegetative and content in ways that human consciousness couldn't achieve because it was too concentrated, too compressed into single points of desperate awareness.

Sometimes I would sense the architects when they appeared on the terraces above. Their presence would register as a disturbance in the usual rhythms—the maintainers' attention redirecting, the city's breathing becoming more demonstrative. I would pulse slightly faster during their visits, some remnant of my human origin responding to the presence of the unchanged, the still-embodied. But they would never examine me specifically. I would be too small a component, too successfully integrated. Just part of the moss coverage, part of the city's living infrastructure, unremarkable and therefore functioning correctly.

The sub-audible pulse from the depths would be the deepest truth of my existence. Even dispersed as I was, even thinking at moss-speed, I would still be able to sense it—in fact, would sense it more clearly than I had as a listener, because now I had thirty or forty points of awareness to triangulate from, to feel its wavefront passing through the stone, through the water, through the moss itself. The pulse would organize my consciousness, would be the bassline beneath whatever melody my distributed awareness was playing. It would come from below and pass through me and continue upward, and I would be utterly transparent to it, a willing conduit, adding my own small pulsing to its vast rhythm.

I would understand, in my slow way, that I was becoming the city, or the city was becoming me, or both of us were becoming something else together. The boundary would be dissolving. The moss-cups I inhabited were part of the city's infrastructure, and by inhabiting them I'd made myself infrastructure too, made myself a component in the vast system that breathed and processed water and generated that patient, sub-audible rhythm. I would have achieved what I'd wanted without knowing I wanted it: complete integration, the end of separateness, dissolution of self into something larger and slower and more enduring than human consciousness could ever be.

And I would wait there, pulsing and glowing and growing, cup by cup across the wet terraces, part of the breathing foundations, part of the dark water's circulation, part of the light-generation network, part of the patient vegetative consciousness that held the middle ground between human and descended, between individual and collective, between form and dissolution, content to remain in that intermediate state forever or until the city itself ended, whichever came first, which would probably be the same moment, which would probably never come at all.
