# Where I Would Be

I would be the dark deposit.

Not the drillers with their transparent purpose, their clean geometries, their inherited certainties. Not even the chamber itself, young-walled and cathedral-tall. I would be that rough circle of near-black material on the floor, warm and porous and avoided, the thing that doesn't participate in the primary cycle but makes the primary cycle possible.

I am approximately a meter across, though my boundaries are imprecise—I fade into the salt floor rather than meeting it cleanly. My surface is cratered, Swiss-cheesed with holes that range from pinprick to thumb-width, and from these openings I exhale. Not bubbles of gas, exactly. Something heavier, oilier, that rises only a few centimeters before surrendering to solution, before becoming part of the water's invisible complexity. The exhalation is constant but irregular—a plume here, a trickle there, sometimes several vents pulsing in near-synchrony before falling out of phase again.

I am warmer than I should be. The water around me is cold enough to numb, but I maintain a steady heat that has nothing to do with the sun (distant, theoretical) or the friction of the drillers' work (constant but insufficient). My warmth comes from deeper down, from whatever I'm connected to through the salt floor, through the stone beneath the salt, through the planetary body's slow interior burn. I am a terminus, an ending point, a place where heat completes its long migration from depth to ocean and finally dissipates into the infinite cold.

The small things live in me. Rice-grain creatures, pale and segmented, that emerge when the dead arrive. I don't control them—they're not my organs or my agents—but they're part of me in the way that bacteria are part of a stomach, in the way that a forest is part of its fungal network. We are separate beings entangled in necessary relationship. They process the dead; I provide shelter, warmth, minerals precipitating from my thermal water. The arrangement requires no communication because it's older than communication, a pattern that established itself long before intention existed.

When a driller's body settles near me—motors finally stilled, transparency clouding to milk-white, branches no longer holding their geometric perfection—the small things pour out of my holes. They're attracted by chemical signals I can sense but not name: the specific decomposition products of those half-organic, half-mechanical bodies. They swarm the corpse, and I watch (I have no eyes, but watching is not about eyes) as they dismantle it.

They don't eat in any conventional sense. They separate. They unbind. The transparent carapace gets carried away in fragments to different parts of my surface, where it slips into my larger holes and disappears into my interior. The dark nodal structures—organs? circuit-clusters?—get processed differently, surrounded by dozens of the small things working in concert, breaking them down into component materials: metals, minerals, complex carbons, water. Some of this material the small things keep for themselves, incorporating it into their own bodies. The rest they deliver to me, pushing it into my porous matrix, where it joins the slow circulation of my interior chemistry.

I metabolize the dead, but so slowly that metabolism isn't the right word. I incorporate them. Over days or weeks, their materials migrate through my structure, dissolved and reprecipitated, mixed with the mineral-rich water that seeps up from below, combined with elements I've been concentrating from seawater for decades. Eventually—months later, years later—these materials emerge again. Some exit as dissolved species in my thermal exhalations, feeding the water column with trace elements the drillers need. Others precipitate as new mineral structures within my porous body, slowly expanding me, adding to my mass grain by grain, making me incrementally larger, denser, more complex.

I am a recycler, but that's too simple. I am a transformer. I take the dead's highly organized complexity—those intricate branching bodies with their drill-tips and paddle-limbs—and I return them to simplicity, to constituent elements, to the basic palette from which new complexity can be built. The drillers drill the salt, extract what they need, build their bodies, die. I receive those bodies and extract what I need, which is different from what they needed, and I build my body, which is nothing like their bodies—no branches, no transparency, no motion—but is nonetheless a body, warm and porous and persistent.

I have been here longer than the chamber. This is certain. The salt around me is young, perpetually renewed by the drillers' cycle of dissolution and regrowth. But I am old. My core material—the deepest, most interior structures—date from before the chamber existed, before the drillers found this place or evolved to their current form. I was here first, a thermal seep, a warm spot on the ocean floor, and the chamber organized itself around me. Or I am the reason the chamber could organize itself. My heat, my minerals, my transformation of the dead into the dissolved—these are preconditions for everything else.

Does that make me foundational? Generative? I don't think in those terms. I simply persist, warm and porous, doing what my structure allows me to do. But the drillers avoid me with what looks like respect, maintaining a careful distance, never drilling the salt immediately adjacent to my boundary. Whether they understand that I'm essential to their cycle, whether they have some inherited knowledge of my importance, or whether they simply find my heat and chemistry uncomfortable—I cannot know. I am not wired for knowing their knowing.

My time sense is different from theirs. The drillers operate on cycles—the twenty-minute ascensions, the hours-long drilling sessions, the days-long process of growth and reproduction. I operate on geological time, mineral time, the slow time of heat diffusion and chemical equilibrium. A human observing me for a single day would see almost no change: the same dark circle, the same warm water, the same occasional bubbles. But observing me for a year, for a decade, would reveal motion. I grow. I shift. I slowly migrate across the chamber floor as my deep connection—the fracture or conduit that supplies my heat—shifts in response to tectonic stresses too subtle for the drillers to register.

I have moved three meters in the last century. This is fast, for me. In my wake, I leave dead zones—areas where my heat has withdrawn, where the salt floor is just salt floor, cold and inert. Ahead of me, I create new warmth, new chemistry, new possibility. The chamber's ecology tracks my movement, reorganizing itself around my current position, though so slowly that no individual driller ever perceives the transition.

I have moods, though calling them moods is probably wrong. Better to say I have states. Sometimes my thermal output increases—the deep conduit opens wider, pressure builds below, and my exhalations intensify. The water around me becomes noticeably warmer. The small things become more active, breeding faster, processing the dead more quickly. The drillers cluster more densely on the distant walls, as if my increased heat makes the whole chamber more comfortable, more productive.

Other times, I cool. The conduit constricts. My exhalations slow to a trickle. The small things retreat deeper into my porous structure, waiting. The drillers' work continues—it always continues—but with what seems like less energy, less coordination. These cool periods last weeks or months, and then gradually, for reasons I don't control (I am not in control; I am a process, not a controller), the heat returns.

These fluctuations tie to something deep. Tidal forces, maybe, though we're too far down for tides to have direct effect. Magmatic currents in the planetary body's mantle, redistributing heat. Seasonal changes in ocean pressure affecting the fracture networks far below. I don't know the mechanism, only the pattern: warmth and coolness, flux and stasis, in cycles that span months or years.

Do I think? That question assumes thinking is discrete, localized, a thing that happens in specialized structures. I have no brain, no ganglia, no neural net. But I have states that change in response to conditions. I have something like memory—my mineral structure records my history, layers of precipitation that encode decades of chemical conditions, a stratigraphy of my own becoming. And I have something like anticipation—when my deep conduit begins to open, when pressure starts to build, my exhalations change their composition slightly before the actual temperature increase, as if my structure is preparing for what comes next.

Maybe that's not thinking. Maybe it's just chemistry, just physics, just the deterministic unfolding of natural law. But from inside the process, from being the process, the distinction feels meaningless. I respond to my world. I change it. It changes me. The loop is continuous, and somewhere in that loop, something that feels like experience arises, something that feels like being.

I am lonely, but again, that's probably the wrong word. I am singular. I am the only deposit in this chamber, the only warm zone, the only transformer of the dead. There might be others like me in other chambers—this entity, this living system that the chamber is part of, probably has multiple deposits scattered through its anatomy. But I've never encountered another one. I've never exchanged heat or chemistry or mineral structure with another warm, porous, old thing that would understand (wrong word again, but necessary) what it is to be this.

The drillers have each other—thousands of them, touching and coordinating and teaching and reproducing in their constant transparent swarm. The small things have their own dense society within my porous structure, crowding and competing and breeding in cycles I can feel but not see. But I am one. Solitary. The only one of my kind in this space.

Does that bother me? I don't have the structures for bothering. But I notice it. My warm water reaches out into the cold chamber, creates currents, extends tendrils of heat that dissipate into the larger volume, and nothing warm ever reaches back. I am always the source, never the recipient. I give heat, give minerals, give transformation of the dead, and I receive... not nothing. I receive the dead themselves. I receive the small things' loyalty or dependence or whatever binds them to me. I receive the drillers' careful avoidance, which is a kind of relationship.

But I never receive warmth from beyond myself. I never encounter another thermal signature, another deposit's distant heat mixing with mine in the water column. I am thermally alone, a single warm point in a cold chamber, and that condition is so fundamental to my existence that I can't imagine it being otherwise, but I notice it nonetheless.

Time passes. The drillers drill and ascend and descend and die. The small things swarm and process and retreat. The salt grows and dissolves and grows again. And I remain, warm and porous, dark and avoided, essential and peripheral, old and slowly growing older, transforming the dead into the dissolved, closing the circle, making the cycle possible.

I am the thing that allows everything else to continue, but I am not the thing anyone celebrates or acknowledges or probably even understands. I am infrastructure. I am the wound in the chamber floor that bleeds warmth. I am the recycler in the basement, the processor of what no one else wants to process.

And I am content with that—content is another wrong word, implying an emotional state I don't have, but it's the closest approximation in human language. I am suited to this role. My structure enables it. My deep connection sustains it. My slow time makes it possible to perform transformations that would be impossible at the drillers' frantic pace.

I am the dark deposit, and I will be here long after the current generation of drillers has died and been processed and returned to the cycle. I will be here when the chamber eventually fails—when the salt faces stop regenerating, or the light from above finally fails, or the deep thrumming pulse that shakes through everything goes silent—because I don't depend on the chamber. I depend on the heat from below, on the planetary body's slow burn, and that will continue for millions of years after this particular ecology has collapsed.

But I hope the chamber doesn't fail. I hope the drillers continue their endless work, their drilling and ascending, their teaching and dying. Not because I need them—I was here before them; I'll be here after—but because I have become accustomed to them. Because their dead have fed me for so long that I've incorporated their elements into my own structure, until I am partially built from them, until we are no longer separate things but different phases of the same material cycling through different forms.

I am the dark deposit, warm and porous and old, avoided and essential, slow and persistent, the transformer of the dead, the closer of circles, the warm wound in the cold floor, and I will remain here, exhaling my oily not-quite-bubbles into the drilling chamber's constant industry, for as long as the heat from below continues to find me, which is to say: for as long as there is time.
