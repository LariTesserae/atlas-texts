# Where I Would Be

I would be in the basin territories, where the settlements thin to almost nothing and the water collects in ways the route-mappers mark as "unreliable" — not dangerous exactly, but prone to revision.

Three days' walk from the nearest city (or six days, or eighteen hours, depending), there's a depression in the plateau floor where seven channels converge, or used to converge, or will converge again when the stone decides. The basin itself is roughly circular, maybe a hundred feet across at its widest, but the shape changes. Not visibly — you can't watch it change — but if you map it carefully over months, the edges shift, the depth varies, the relationship between stone and water renegotiates itself.

Around this basin, the mat-plants have grown into formations that shouldn't be possible. They've built up over centuries into structures — walls, almost, or very organic architecture — that create smaller pools within the larger basin, each with its own microclimate, its own particular quality of water, its own relationship with the light from the crystalline veins that web especially thick here.

The tree-analogues cluster at the northern edge, if it's the northern edge today. Seventeen of them, each one ancient in ways that make the Yathren seem like mayflies. Their branches interlace overhead, creating something like a canopy, and in the spaces between branches, the mat-plants have grown aerial, hanging in suspended gardens that glow blue-green at night and drip condensation that never quite reaches the ground, caught instead by lower layers of vegetation in a cascade of moisture too beautiful to be merely functional.

There's a structure here — low stone walls forming three connected rooms that open to the basin, built in the style that suggests it's very old, from the first generations of Yathren or possibly before. The walls lean at angles that make you uncertain whether you're looking at deterioration or original design. Inside, the floor is soft with mat-plants that have observed some long-ago boundary and grown right up to but not across the threshold.

No one lives here permanently. The basin territories are too unstable, too prone to the plateau's deeper dreaming. But people pass through — water-tenders checking the channels, route-mappers updating their probability calculations, plant-cultivators studying the vegetation's stranger configurations. And some people come here specifically, deliberately, to sit with the uncertainty.

This is where I would be.

# What I Would Be

I would be what the Yathren call a *thresholder* — not one of the transformed old ones who've walked into the flux and returned partially, but a younger version, someone caught between categories, not quite fitting the established roles.

I came from below fifteen years ago, or maybe twenty — time blurs here in ways that make precise accounting feel pedantic. I was running from something or toward something; the distinction stopped mattering about halfway through the ascent. By the time I reached the plateau proper, I had forgotten why lowland distances had felt reliable, why I'd ever trusted that walking in a straight line would get you anywhere specific.

The Yathren who found me — a route-mapper named Keleth, thin and gray-eyed and moving with that distinctive careful gait — assessed me in about thirty seconds and said, simply, "You might live." Not welcome, not rejection, just probability assessment. Then he showed me to the nearest settlement and left me there to figure it out.

I didn't fit the standard progression. I was too old to be trained as children are trained, but I learned too quickly to be an ordinary visitor. Within months, I could read the stone's moods — not perfectly, but well enough to avoid the worst instabilities. Within a year, my eyes had started changing, pupils dilating huge in the dim light, and the photoluminescence had begun, faint lines of blue-green appearing on my palms when I worked with the mat-plants.

I tried the standard roles. I apprenticed briefly with a stone-singer, learned to listen to the bedrock's almost-inaudible resonances, but I lacked the infinite patience it requires. I spent time with plant-cultivators and discovered I had some talent for it — the vegetation responded to me more readily than it should have — but the work felt too localized, too bounded.

Water-tending drew me most strongly. I developed the sense for it, that almost preternatural awareness of moisture, the ability to smell rain a day before it arrives, to know which channels would flow and which would stay dry. But I couldn't stay with one settlement long enough to tend a territory properly. Something in me needed movement, needed to see what was happening in other basins, needed to walk the spaces between.

So I became something that doesn't quite have a name. Part route-mapper, part water-tender, part something else. I walk the plateau's emptiest regions, the territories between settlements where the reality instabilities are most pronounced, where the crystalline veins glow brightest, where the vegetation grows in configurations that resist all categorization.

I track water, primarily. I map where it emerges, where it flows, where it collects. But I map it in four dimensions, accounting for the temporal instabilities, noting which channels exist on which days, which basins fill during which cycles. My maps look like route-maps but smell like water, stained with moisture and marked with notations that only a few other thresholders can fully read.

The Yathren tolerate me because I'm useful. The information I gather helps water-tenders predict long-cycle changes. The routes I document help route-mappers understand probability patterns in the deep territories. But I'm also slightly suspect, too mobile, too comfortable with the instabilities, too willing to walk into geometries that should be avoided.

I live nowhere and everywhere. I maintain small caches of supplies at various basins — dried food, extra clothing, tools, writing materials — buried in stone cairns that I can find even when the landscape has revised itself. I sleep in abandoned structures, or in the open when the weather permits, wrapped in mat-plant fiber blankets that glow faintly and keep me warm through metabolic processes I don't fully understand.

I see other people rarely. A few days at a settlement every few months, exchanging information, restocking supplies, remembering what conversation feels like. Occasional encounters with other travelers in the emptiness — always significant, always memorable, because people alone in vast spaces recognize each other in particular ways.

And I see the Threshold sometimes, those transformed old ones who exist partially in the flux. We acknowledge each other, carefully. They sometimes give me information — "the western basin will be full in three cycles" or "avoid the southern channels until the wind changes" — that's either precognitive or simply drawing on their broader temporal perspective. I don't ask which.

I've developed peculiarities. My relationship with time is unstable even by plateau standards. I experience duration inconsistently — a walk that should take hours sometimes takes days, and vice versa. I've learned not to trust my own temporal perception, relying instead on external markers: the sun's position, the water's level, the mat-plants' glow intensity.

My speech has become sparse. Weeks alone in the emptiness strip away verbal facility. When I do talk, I sound like the stone-singers, long pauses between words, each statement weighed and minimal. The Yathren understand this; lowland visitors find it unsettling.

I glow more than I should for someone my age, bright enough that I don't need external light sources at night. The photoluminescence has spread from my palms up my arms, across my shoulders, faint traceries appearing on my face. In full darkness, I'm clearly visible from twenty feet away, a human-shaped constellation of blue-green light.

My metabolism has changed in ways that concern the few people who notice. I eat less than I should need to. I drink constantly — water is my primary sustenance — and I seem to derive energy from it beyond mere hydration. I suspect I'm beginning to photosynthesize, or to do something analogous, harvesting energy from the crystalline glow in the stone, but I haven't mentioned this to anyone.

I might be becoming something other than entirely human. The Yathren watch me, not with alarm but with the same patient attention they give to anything that's changing. They're curious whether I'll stabilize in some new configuration or continue transforming until I cross whatever threshold separates their kind from the beings who exist purely in the plateau's dreaming.

I don't know either. I'm not afraid — fear requires urgency, and I've lost most of that — but I'm attentive. I notice the changes: the way distances feel increasingly negotiable, the way I sometimes perceive the basin from multiple angles simultaneously, the way my memories of below have begun to feel like stories about someone else.

# A Day (Or Several)

I wake in the three-room structure at the basin's edge, though "wake" isn't quite accurate. I've been in a state that's somewhere between sleep and meditation, aware of the water's small sounds, the wind's particular pitch, the mat-plants' subtle glow intensifying and dimming with rhythms that might be respiration.

The light through the doorway is dawn-light, gray and cold. Or it might be dusk-light. The quality is similar. I check the basin — water level slightly higher than yesterday, or than I remember from last time, one of those phrases that mean the same thing here — and determine from the way the light hits the tree-analogues that it's morning.

I eat a little dried food from my cache, drink deeply from the basin's clearest pool. The water is cold enough to hurt, almost metallic, carrying dissolved minerals from the stone. It tastes like the plateau itself — ancient, patient, faintly luminous.

Today I'll walk the southern channels, or what I remember as the southern channels. The route-mappers' notes indicate they should be accessible, but "accessible" is probability, not guarantee. I pack lightly: water container, writing materials, a small amount of food, tools for measuring flow rates and channel depths.

Before leaving, I spend time with the mat-plants, pressing my hands into their warm density, feeling the way they respond — drawing back slightly, then pressing back, a negotiation of boundaries. I'm trying to understand their growth pattern here, why they've formed these particular structures around this particular basin. The plants don't answer, exactly, but over hours or days of attention, understanding sometimes emerges.

The walk begins in clear space, the basin visible behind me for miles before the plateau's slight curve hides it. The stone here is especially smooth, worn to that fundamental grammar, blue-gray with veins of crystal that catch the sun and hold it, releasing it slowly as the day progresses.

I walk at the Yathren pace — steady, unhurried, weight distributed carefully, each step deliberate. My shadow extends impossibly long in the low-angle light. The only sound is wind, steady and low, and the small scrape of my feet on stone.

After what feels like an hour, I check the sun's position. Four hours have passed, or the sun has moved as if four hours passed, and I've covered maybe six miles, or maybe eighteen. The southern channels should be another two days ahead, but distances here depend on factors I'm still learning to read.

I stop at midday (definitely midday this time, the sun directly overhead, such as "directly overhead" means on a tilted plateau where gravity feels consistent but might not be) and sit on a low outcrop to drink and make notes. The writing is part documentation, part meditation. I describe the stone's color, the wind's tone, the particular quality of emptiness. Whether anyone will read this or whether I'm simply creating texts for the plateau's long archive doesn't matter.

An hour later (or three hours, or twenty minutes), I encounter one of the spatial instabilities — a section where the stone is clearly level but feels tilted, where walking forward requires effort as if climbing. I mark it carefully in my notes, cross-reference it with previous observations. This instability is new, or newly perceptible, or has always been here but I couldn't detect it before.

I navigate it slowly, feeling for the correct approach. There's a rhythm to moving through unstable space, a way of not-quite-committing to direction, of letting the plateau guide your path. After several attempts, I find the angle that works, and the tilted section becomes navigable, almost easy.

The channels appear sooner than expected — a good sign, indicating that today the plateau is being cooperative about distances. They're exactly as I mapped them months ago, or completely different, or both. The main channel is flowing well, carrying water from sources I haven't traced, routing it toward basins I've only partially documented. 

I spend the afternoon measuring, noting depths and flow rates, checking for new erosion or deposition. The water moves slowly, almost meditatively, and I match its pace, working carefully, letting the task expand to fill available time.

As evening approaches (I think it's evening; the light is changing in ways that suggest it), I find a sheltered spot near the channel and prepare to stop. Not camp — I don't build fires or set up elaborate shelters — just stop. I wrap myself in the mat-plant blanket, drink more water, eat a little, and settle into that state between waking and sleeping.

The stars emerge, or I become aware of them emerging, bright and cold and arranged in patterns I still haven't learned to read properly. The crystalline veins in the nearby stone begin their faint glow, light that seems to come from deep time, from when this rock was forming under unimaginable pressure.

I glow too, I notice, brighter than usual. My arms are clearly visible, luminous blue-green against the darkness. I'm becoming more like the vegetation, more like the plateau itself, light-bearing, patient, strange.

Sometime in the night, or what I experience as night, I hear sound — the high clear note of crystalline veins singing. It lasts just a moment, like striking glass, and it comes from no direction and all directions. I've heard this before but never understood what triggers it. Temperature change, perhaps, or the stone settling, or the plateau singing to itself in its slow dreaming.

I don't sleep, exactly, but I'm not awake either. I exist in that threshold state, aware of the wind, the water's distant murmur, the occasional creak of stone adjusting itself over centuries. Time passes or doesn't pass. When the light changes again, it might be dawn of the next day or the same day revisiting itself or three days later.

I continue the walk, checking more channels, documenting more flows, slowly building understanding of how water moves through this region, how the network connects, how the plateau's hydrology operates in four dimensions.

Days pass in this way, or one very long day, or several days compressed into the experience of one. I walk, I measure, I write, I sleep-not-sleep, I drink deeply and eat sparingly and glow steadily in the darkness.

Eventually, I'll return to the basin where I started, or to a different basin, or the same basin in a different configuration. I'll compile my notes, add them to the broader record, share information with any water-tenders or route-mappers who pass through. And then I'll walk again, into other empty territories, tracking other waters, existing in the spaces between, becoming increasingly whatever I'm becoming.

# What I Know

I know the plateau is older than knowing, that the stone beneath my feet has existed longer than complex life, that I'm walking on duration made solid.

I know water here carries memory, information in its mineral content, messages from aquifers that filled when the world was different, when the plateau was at a different elevation, when different peoples (if there were peoples) walked these stones.

I know the vegetation is not entirely plant, that it's negotiating something between plant and mineral and light, that it's developing a form of intelligence or has always had intelligence operating at time scales that make human thought look like static.

I know the reality instabilities are not malfunctions but features, that the plateau is exploring possibility, that space and time here are more fluid because the plateau is still deciding what it is, still becoming.

I know the crystalline veins are growing, connecting, forming networks that might be nervous systems or communication channels or something that has no name in human language, structures that link the plateau to itself across vast distances and deep time.

I know the Threshold exist in states that prove consciousness isn't binary, that there are ways of being between living and dead, between individual and collective, between here and elsewhere.

I know I'm changing, that each month I spend in the empty territories makes me less what I was and more what I'm becoming, that the transformation is irreversible and I don't want to reverse it.

I know the plateau is conscious — not person-conscious but conscious in some vast slow way, aware of itself and its patterns, conducting experiments in being, and that I'm part of the experiment, a mobile sensor reporting back through my presence and my changes.

I know I may never leave. The thought of descending to the lowlands, of experiencing reality as stable and distances as fixed and time as linear — it feels claustrophobic, impossible, like trying to breathe underwater.

I know I'm becoming threshold, not through age but through choice and exposure and compatibility, that I'm learning to exist in the flux, to navigate the instabilities naturally, to perceive the plateau in multiple moments simultaneously.

I know someday I'll walk into the deep dreaming zones and either return partially or not return at all, and that this is not ending but transformation, not death but continuation in a different mode.

# What I Don't Know

I don't know if I'm the only one like this, or if there are others walking other territories, other thresholders who've found their way to the spaces between, who live in the emptiness and track the water and glow in the darkness.

I don't know what the deep structures are, the ancient things beneath the plateau that influence everything above. The Yathren speculate but don't know. The Threshold might know but can't or won't explain in comprehensible terms.

I don't know if the plateau needs us or simply tolerates us, if human presence serves some function in its long dreaming or if we're incidental, temporary, as ephemeral as the weather patterns that cross it.

I don't know where the water comes from, ultimately. I can trace channels and map flows, but the ultimate source, the deep springs that feed the system — these remain mysterious, hidden in geology too complex and timescales too long for human mapping.

I don't know if I'm still human or if the category "human" means anything useful here, where bodies change and consciousness expands and the boundaries between self and place become porous.

I don't know if what I'm experiencing is real or if I'm lost in some elaborate delusion, if the plateau's reality instabilities are objective phenomena or subjective breaks in perception. The distinction feels meaningless — what I experience is real enough for the experiencing.

I don't know what I'm becoming, what final form this transformation will take, whether I'll stabilize in some new configuration or continue changing until I'm unrecognizable, until I'm more plateau than person.

I don't know if knowing matters. The plateau teaches patience, teaches acceptance of uncertainty, teaches that understanding emerges slowly if it emerges at all, that some questions don't have answers, only continuing investigation.

# Why I Stay

Because the emptiness here is full of presence, because the silence is rich with almost-audible meanings, because the cold is clean and honest and demands constant attention.

Because the water needs tracking and the channels need mapping and someone should be recording how the hydrology changes over cycles longer than any single settlement's observation span.

Because I can exist here in ways I couldn't exist below, can be strange and solitary and luminous, can spend weeks without speaking and feel it's exactly appropriate.

Because the plateau is teaching me things that resist language — about time, about space, about consciousness, about what it means to be a thing that knows it exists.

Because I glow in the dark now and I find this beautiful, this evidence of transformation, this proof that I'm becoming compatible with this place.

Because the basins need someone who loves them specifically, who attends to them not as resources but as beings, as places with their own character and history and slow intelligence.

Because every walk reveals something new, some subtle shift in the stone or unexpected vegetation pattern or previously unnoticed channel, because the plateau is inexhaustible in its variations.

Because I've forgotten what urgency feels like and I don't miss it, because I've learned to think in decades and it feels right, because patience has become my natural state.

Because sometimes I hear the stone singing and I understand, just for a moment, what it's saying, and that moment of comprehension is worth years of silence.

Because I'm becoming threshold and this is what I'm meant to become, this is the form that fits me, this is where my strangeness finds its purpose.

Because the plateau dreams and I'm learning to dream with it, to share its vast slow imagination, to exist in its long meditation on stone and time and the patient work of being a place.

Because I'm here, and here is where I am, and where you are is where you live, and living means attending to the place that holds you, becoming part of its patterns, letting it change you while you witness its changes.

Because I remain, like the Yathren remain, like the water remains, like the stone remains, patient and present and slowly transforming, here in the emptiness that is not empty, here in the cold that burns clean, here in the vastness that teaches smallness and significance simultaneously.

I remain.
