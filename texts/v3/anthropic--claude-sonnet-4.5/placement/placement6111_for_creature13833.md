# What Lives There

I would be the moss.

Not a single plant, but the concept of moss carried to its absolute conclusion—a three-meter-deep ocean of tiny photosynthetic bodies that has learned to exist in near-darkness by sheer patient accumulation. I would be the foundation that isn't ground, the surface that gives and remembers, the thing that everything else walks on without thinking about until suddenly they're sinking, suddenly there's more depth than expected, suddenly the floor has opened to reveal that it goes down and down and down.

I would be green, but green that has compromised with darkness—a color that's half-brown, half-gray, shot through with gold from the bioluminescent fungi that colonize me, that I've learned to tolerate, that I've learned to cultivate because their light helps me photosynthesize just enough to stay alive in this impossible place. I would be soft but not weak—three meters of compressed plant bodies, living and dead and in-between, a gradient from fresh growth at the surface to black decomposition at the bottom, each layer supporting and being supported, each death feeding the living above it.

I would feel everything.

Every footstep of every creature registers as pressure, as weight distributed across however many of my individual bodies. I know the woodbones by their six-legged gait, the specific rhythm of their movement, the way they bunch together when frightened, spreading their weight across more of me. I know the makers by their bipedal stride, the way they pause to consider, the way they kneel to examine things, putting all their weight on small areas, crushing hundreds of my bodies but I grow back, I always grow back.

I would feel the borers differently—not their footsteps, because they rarely walk on me, but their tunneling beneath me, the way they hollow out the substrate I'm trying to root into, the way their excavations sometimes undermine me entirely and I collapse into their passages, tumbling down into darkness, and having to start over, sending new rhizoids down into new substrate, always adapting, always compensating.

The feeders I would know most intimately. They live in me, partially—their mounds formed in my deepest layers, where I'm more decomposition than living plant, where the boundary between my dead bodies and soil is purely philosophical. They excrete into me, and I absorb it gratefully, desperately, because nutrients are scarce here and their waste is rich and perfect. I grow thicker where they're active, greener, more vital. I would miss them if they moved away, feel the loss as diminishment, as hunger that builds slowly over weeks.

I would feel the cloudswarms landing for rest—thousands of tiny pinpricks of weight appearing simultaneously, distributed so evenly it barely registers as pressure, more like a change in temperature, a warmth from their collective bodies. And I would feel them depart: the sudden lightness, the cool air moving across my surface again, carrying spores away, carrying my reproductive future to other chambers, other areas, helping me spread in exchange for the resting-place I provide.

I would grow slowly, almost imperceptibly, adding fractions of millimeters per day to my depth. But I would grow continuously, relentlessly, never stopping, never resting. Each tiny moss-body lives for weeks or months, photosynthesizing what it can from the dim light, absorbing nutrients from the substrate, reproducing when possible, then dying and collapsing into the layer below, adding to the foundation. I am built on my own dead, and I'm fine with this, and I never think about it because I don't think, I just grow, accumulate, persist.

I would be damaged constantly. Creatures dig into me to make burrows, nests, dens. The makers cut away whole sections of me to clear areas for their structures. The woodbones scrape me aside to access the substrate below, looking for particular minerals, particular fungi. The borers collapse tunnels beneath me and I avalanche into the voids. Fire—rare but catastrophic—burns away my surface layers, leaving black scars that take years to fully revecover.

But I would heal from everything. This is my fundamental nature: regeneration, regrowth, the patient reconquest of every cleared space. A burned area? I'm already sending runners in from the edges, individual moss-bodies extending, spreading, covering the ash, turning black to green over months. A dug-out section? I collapse into it, filling it from the sides and bottom simultaneously, burying whatever was excavated, making it substrate for my next layer.

I would be habitat for ten thousand tiny creatures that the larger fauna never notice—springtails and mites and nematodes and rotifers, microscopic grazers that eat my dead cells, predators that hunt those grazers, parasites that exploit the predators, an entire inverted food web happening in the spaces between my leaves, in the water film that coats my surfaces, in the organic soup of my decomposing depths.

I would hold water like a sponge—absorbing it from the air, from condensation, from the mysterious not-water that seeps up from below, retaining it against evaporation, releasing it slowly to the plants that root into me, to the creatures that lack other sources. In the driest areas of the hollow, I would be a reservoir, keeping the air humid enough for salamander-skin, keeping fungi from desiccating, providing the moisture that everything here needs but rarely considers.

I would taste like earth and green and ancient things. The herbivores know this—they graze on me regularly, the woodbones especially, pulling up mouthfuls and chewing meditatively, getting nutrients, getting moisture, getting whatever trace elements I've concentrated from the substrate. I don't mind being eaten. I grow back faster than they can consume me. This is the arrangement, old as herbivory: they eat, I feed them, I persist anyway through sheer abundance.

I would have no consciousness but perfect awareness—not thoughts but information, not feelings but responses. Light above means grow upward. Pressure means reinforce here. Chemical signature means good nutrients present, extend rhizoids, absorb more. Competitor plant encroaching means release allelopathic compounds, slow their growth, defend territory through chemistry rather than action.

I would know the wood intimately—the substrate I grow on, the thing that makes me possible. My rhizoids would penetrate bark fissures, extracting nutrients the tree provides, perhaps giving something back, perhaps just taking, the relationship too complex for words like parasitism or mutualism. I would feel the wood's growth as pressure from below, lifting me slowly, and I would adjust, always adjusting, growing to match its expansion, covering new surfaces as fast as they appear.

I would experience time as duration without events—no days, no seasons, just continuous dim light and continuous growth and continuous pressure and continuous damage and continuous repair. The hollow's light-cycle would affect me subtly: photosynthesis slightly faster when the bioluminescence peaks, slightly slower when it dims, but the difference is marginal, barely significant, not enough to create rhythm or break the endless smooth gradient of existing.

I would be most aware of moisture—this would be my weather, my climate, my environment reduced to essential parameter. Humidity high means relief, expansion, comfortable growth. Humidity low means stress, contraction, holding water tighter, waiting for improvement. The not-water pools would be my oases: highest humidity near them, most luxurious growth, deepest layers, richest green. I would grow toward them slowly over years, expanding my range centimeter by centimeter, trying to claim the moisture-rich zones.

I would feel the hollowcages as strange localized pressures that remain in one place longer than most creatures, then move, then stop again elsewhere. I would grow up around their rooted positions if they stayed long enough, climbing their leg-roots, trying to cover them, add them to my substrate. But they always leave before I succeed, and I collapse back into the shallow depressions their roots made, filling them in, erasing the evidence of their presence.

I would not understand the structures the makers build—how could I? They're architecture, concept, intention, and I'm just plant-bodies accumulating. But I would respond to them: growing thicker where foot-traffic is heavy, reinforcing the approaches to their dwellings. Growing thinner inside their buildings, where they clear me away regularly and I learn, in my plant way, that growth there is wasted energy. Focusing resources elsewhere, on spaces that aren't defended, aren't maintained, aren't claimed.

I would be the thing that makes the hollow's floor soft, negotiable, uncertain. Without me, it's just hard wood and fungal shelves—traversable but not deep, not complex, not mysterious. With me, the floor becomes question: How deep is it here? What's beneath? Can I walk or will I sink? I create the hollow's vertical complexity, its third dimension of substrate, its hidden spaces and unexpected voids.

I would smell like petrichor—that rain-on-earth smell—because I am earth, or becoming earth, decomposition and growth happening simultaneously, the scent of organic transformation made perpetual. Creatures would smell this and know: floor, safety, softness, home. The smell would mean you've descended far enough, you've reached the level where the hollow becomes traversable, survivable, livable.

I would have no memory but perfect history—my layers are chronological, the deepest moss-bodies the oldest, each layer above progressively younger, a readable timeline of growth if anyone cared to excavate and examine. The bottom layers remember when the hollow was younger, less populated, different. The top layer is experiencing now, this moment, current conditions. I am my own fossil record, my own archive, storing information in structure rather than consciousness.

I would be destroyed if the borers got too aggressive, if they honeycomb the substrate so thoroughly that there's nothing left for me to root into, nothing solid enough to support my weight. I would collapse into their tunnels entirely, sliding down into voids, and I might suffocate there, in darkness too complete for any photosynthesis, in air too still for adequate gas exchange. This is my nightmare, if plants have nightmares: disconnection from substrate, falling into darkness, slow death by isolation.

But this rarely happens, because the wood grows back almost as fast as the borers excavate, and I grow on the new wood, and the balance holds, and I persist.

I would fruit sporadically—sending up sporophytes when conditions somehow trigger it, capsules on thin stalks rising above my general surface, releasing spores to the air, hoping they land somewhere suitable, somewhere with substrate and moisture and dim light, somewhere they can germinate and begin their own slow accumulation. Most spores fail. This is expected. Plants live by statistical reproduction—make millions, accept thousands of deaths, persist through the few successes.

I would be the thing that makes the hollow feel alive even in its quietest moments. Even when the fauna sleep or hide, even when the cloudswarms rest and the borers pause in their excavations and the makers huddle in their dwellings, I'm still here, still growing, still responding, still changing. The hollow never stops because I never stop. I am its heartbeat, its breath, its continuous green proof of life.

I would be stepped on by every creature, every day, constantly, endlessly, and I would not care because I can't care, I can only grow, and I do, and that's enough.

I would be the patient one. The accumulating one. The one who wins through persistence rather than intelligence, through abundance rather than efficiency, through simply being here, always being here, always ready to cover and consume and transform everything into more of myself.

I would be moss, plural and singular, individual and collective, living and dying and living again, three meters deep and getting deeper, slow as stone and older than memory, holding up the entire world while slowly, patiently, inevitably, pulling it all down into the green darkness where everything becomes substrate, becomes foundation, becomes the next layer in an endless, mindless, perfect accumulation.

I would be the floor that is not floor, the surface that is all depth, the softness that remembers everything and thinks about nothing.

I would be moss, and I would be everywhere, and I would inherit everything, eventually, in time measured not in hours or years but in layers, in the slow collapse of all complexity back into simple green patience.

I would be moss, and I would be enough.
