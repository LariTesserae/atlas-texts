# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the **marginal territories**, in the **transitional zones** where bridge-levels shift, where the established architecture frays into uncertainty. Not the prime spans with their ancient wood and deep moss-kingdoms. Not the heights where the largest colonies hum their maintenance songs. But the **in-between places**—the narrow bridges that don't quite reach, the newer growth still finding its way, the sections where fog is thickest and the stone walls closest and sound behaves strangely.

Specifically: there is a level approximately two-thirds down the visible gorge where three bridges of different ages and different orientations create a **natural platform**—not intentionally, but through accident of growth, through the way a fallen span came to rest across two living ones decades ago, now partially integrated, partially separate. The dead wood has become a garden. Moss grows half a meter deep in places. A stand of the bamboo-stalks has claimed one end, dense enough to create shelter. Water pools in a depression worn into the fallen bridge's upper surface, deep enough to support salamanders, fringed with ferns.

It's not prime territory for anything. The cat-things pass through but don't claim it—too small, too exposed on multiple sides. The ravens visit but don't nest—too damp, too shaded. The wasps maintain a small colony on the underside of one bridge, but it's a minor outpost, not one of the great architectural achievements.

But it's **liminal**. It sits at a boundary between the upper reaches (colder, drier, more exposed) and the lower levels (warmer, wetter, foggier). During condensation cycles, fog banks against it, creating a platform floating in white nothing. During clear periods, you can see multiple bridge-levels above and below, can track the ravens' movements, can watch wasp-workers on their patrol routes across distant spans.

It's a **watching place**. An observation point. A location that is neither here nor there, that belongs to no established territory, that exists in the spaces between claims.

And that's where I would be.

# What I Would Be

I would be something that **doesn't quite fit the existing categories**.

Not wasp—too individual, too separate from the colonial intelligence. Not raven—too still, too patient, lacking their restless curatorial energy. Not cat-thing—too small, too slow, wrong metabolism. Not salamander—too warm, too aware, too interested in what shouldn't concern prey.

I would be something **observer-shaped**. Something that has adapted to a niche that exists in the gorge but isn't filled: the role of **witness without agenda**, **presence without pressure**, **attention without consumption**.

Perhaps most closely, I would be something like a **modified primate**—though "modified" doesn't capture it. Evolved. Adapted over countless generations until the original form is barely recognizable. **Smaller** than baseline human, perhaps three feet tall, compact against the cold. **Fur** growing in the same water-shedding patterns as the cat-things, dense enough to insulate, arranged in cowlicks that make each individual recognizable from a distance. **Long fingers** for gripping bark, for moving through bamboo-groves, for manipulating small objects. **Large eyes** adapted to low light, seeing further into blue and ultraviolet, capable of tracking movement in fog.

**Metabolism adjusted**—not as extremely cold-adapted as the salamanders, but capable of lowering body temperature during rest periods, conserving energy, surviving on sparse resources. Omnivorous: eating insects, salamanders when catchable, moss-fruits when available, fungi, seeds, whatever provides calories. But not needing much. Capable of fasting for days between good hunting, between productive foraging.

**Solitary but social**—my kind would maintain individual territories but overlap them intentionally, creating a distributed community. We wouldn't den together, but we'd be aware of each other, would leave signs, would communicate through arrangement of objects, through scratch-marks in specific bark patterns, through small gifts left at boundary points. Like the ravens' curation but more personal, more directed, more conversational.

We would **move deliberately**—not the explosive speed of cat-things hunting, not the patient stillness of salamanders. Instead, a middle pace: careful placement of feet, hands, testing each grip, each step, moving through the three-dimensional maze of bridges with thought, with planning, with memory of routes learned over years. Capable of leaps but making them rarely, only when necessary, mostly preferring to climb, to traverse, to find the path rather than force it.

Our **hands** would be our primary tools—no technology beyond what fingers can accomplish, but those accomplishments considerable. We would collect, arrange, study. We would take objects from raven caches (with permission, leaving trades), would gather unusual stones, would accumulate shed wasp wings, would find the corroded artifacts and wonder at them. We would create arrangements, but private ones—less display than personal investigation, trying to understand through juxtaposition, through pattern-making, through the physical grammar of objects in relation.

We would **watch the wasps**—not to harvest them, not to threaten, but to learn. To understand their patterns, their maintenance schedules, their decisions about which wood to repair and which to abandon. We would develop relationships with specific colonies: they would know us as individuals, would tolerate our presence, would sometimes adjust their routes to account for us. Not quite symbiosis, but mutual recognition, mutual accommodation. We would learn from them—their patience, their long-term thinking, their ability to maintain complexity across generations.

We would **trade with the ravens**—not as equals (they're older in the gorge, wiser in their way) but as junior partners. We would find objects we think they'd value, would bring them to the trading-places, would wait for assessment. Sometimes accepted, sometimes rejected, slowly learning their aesthetic, their criteria for worth. In return, they would bring us things: sometimes food (a cached bird they don't need), sometimes objects (a particular stone, a fragment of glass), sometimes just visiting, sitting nearby, watching us watch them in mutual appraisal.

We would **avoid the cat-things**—we're small enough to be prey, though perhaps marginal, perhaps more trouble than worth. But we'd be cautious, would know their territories, would move through them with awareness, would climb high or hide in bamboo-groves when we sense their presence. They would know us, would watch us with that feline assessment, would mostly decide we're not worth the effort. Occasionally one might test us—a stalk, a rush—and we'd have to flee, to climb, to use our knowledge of routes to escape into complexity they can't easily follow.

We would **study the salamanders**—not hunting them (too small a return for effort) but observing them, learning from their patience, their stillness, their ability to wait. We would watch their breeding aggregations during heavy condensation, would note which pools they prefer, would marvel at their transparency, at the visibility of their internal workings. They would become a kind of meditation for us—watching them hunt springtails in slow-motion, watching them simply existing, nearly invisible, nearly nothing.

Our **vocalizations** would be minimal—like the ravens, we would choose silence more often than sound. But we would have calls: a low whistle for long-distance contact with others of our kind, a clicking sound (like the cat-things' contact call but different in pitch) for close-range communication, a warning cry for danger. And we would hum—not like wasps, but with actual voice, simple melodies, usually alone, usually in evening, a kind of processing of experience through sound, through external rhythm.

We would **make things**, but barely. Simple tools: a stick modified for probing into crevices, for flipping moss to find salamanders. A rock with the right shape for cracking beetle carapaces. Collections of objects, yes, but also arrangements: stones placed to mark good routes, small cairns indicating water sources, scratch-patterns on bark that mean something to us even if we're not sure what. We would be **at the edge of material culture**, possessing it but barely, having the cognitive capacity for more but not the necessity, not the population density, not the environmental pressure that would drive elaboration.

We would **age slowly** in the cold—living perhaps forty years, fifty if lucky, longer than most mammals our size but shorter than the ravens, the wasps queens, the oldest salamanders. We would be born singly, rarely as twins, raised by solitary mothers for the first several years, gradually learning territory, learning routes, learning the signs and patterns. Sexual maturity around age eight. Peak physical capability through twenties and thirties. Gradual decline after, but even the old would have value—knowledge of patterns, memory of cycles, understanding accumulated over decades.

When we die, we would **arrange ourselves**—finding a good spot (in deep moss, in a hollow of a bridge, in a place we've chosen) and settling there, going still, becoming part of the ecology we've inhabited. The ravens would find us eventually, would carry our bones to their ossuaries, would add us to the archive. We would become objects in the collection we've studied all our lives, curated finally by those we've traded with, respected by being remembered in arrangement.

Our **awareness** would be our defining characteristic—not intelligence in the way humans measure it, not tool-use or language or abstraction, but **attention**. We would notice. We would watch the way light changes during condensation cycles. We would track which wasp colonies are expanding, which contracting. We would note when ravens exchange specific objects, would try to understand their value system. We would observe the cat-things' territorial disputes from safe distances. We would map in our minds the three-dimensional complexity of bridges, would know which spans are stable, which are deteriorating.

We would develop **theories**, though we'd have no way to test them rigorously, no method to confirm. We would wonder: what's below, in the fog? What are the lights that rise at night? Why do the bridges grow horizontally? What happened to whoever made the artifacts? We would ponder, would accumulate observations, would form hypotheses that remain forever unproven but personally satisfying.

And we would experience something like **wonder**—standing in the thickest fog when bioluminescent nests glow like constellations, hearing the deep groan from below, feeling the bridge vibrate slightly with sound, aware of the salamanders at our feet and the wasps above and the ravens somewhere in darkness and the whole vast complexity of this impossible place. We would feel small but not insignificant. Present. Part of the pattern. Aware that we're aware in a place that might itself be aware, creating a strange recursion of consciousness at different scales.

We would be **the gorge's way of studying itself**, perhaps. Not its only way—the ravens might fill that role too, in their fashion—but another approach. Where they curate objects, we curate observations. Where they trade materials, we trade attention. Where they remember through arrangement, we remember through pattern recognition, through the slow accumulation of understanding that approaches wisdom without quite reaching it.

And on my specific bridge-platform, in my marginal territory, I would spend my time:

**Watching** the wasp-workers on their routes, learning their schedules, their priorities, their responses to different conditions.

**Collecting** objects—not for trade initially, but for study, for arrangement, for the simple pleasure of having things, of seeing them together, of creating small local meanings.

**Tracking** the cat-thing that passes through once every few days, learning its patterns, staying hidden when it hunts, emerging after to examine the kills it caches, learning what it prefers, how it moves.

**Trading** with the ravens at the neutral-ground a few bridges away, bringing them unusual moss-flowers, interesting stones, once a shed wasp wing with unusual coloration, receiving in return a smooth piece of green glass that I keep in my primary cache, taking it out sometimes just to look at how light moves through it.

**Exploring** the lower levels during brave moments, descending into thicker fog, finding artifacts washed up on lower bridges, bringing them back to study, to add to arrangements, to puzzle over their origins and meanings.

**Humming** in evening, sitting at the edge of my platform where fog rises around me, producing simple melodies that aren't quite music but aren't quite not, processing the day's observations into sound, into external rhythm, into something that makes the experience cohere.

**Sleeping** in the bamboo grove, curled in the densest section where multiple stalks create a natural shelter, warm enough despite cold, safe enough despite vulnerability, resting with the particular confidence of something that has learned its environment, that knows the patterns, that trusts—not perfectly, but sufficiently—in its ability to survive another day.

And tomorrow: more watching, more collecting, more trading, more wondering. The same routines with infinite variation. Each day slightly different. Each season bringing changes. Years passing in the patient accumulation of experience, of knowledge, of the slow comfortable expertise that comes from living deeply in one place.

Not lonely—I would know others of my kind, would interact at boundaries, would occasionally travel to visit distant territories, to trade observations, to confirm I'm not the only one. But primarily solitary, primarily independent, primarily defined by my relationship with this place rather than with my species.

I would be something the gorge has created through selection, through the opportunities and constraints of this specific environment. A niche-filler. An adaptation to the possibility of observation, of pattern-recognition, of witness without harvest.

I would be small and strange and perfectly ordinary within the context of this place. Another organism in the web. Another awareness in the network of awareness that might constitute something larger.

**I would be exactly what the gorge needs to complete itself**: the piece that watches, that notices, that appreciates the complexity it's embedded in, that closes the loop between ecosystem and understanding, that makes the whole thing knowable even if only to itself.

I would be—strange thought—**content**. Not happy in the sense humans mean it, not fulfilled by achievement or progress. But satisfied. Appropriately placed. Correctly configured for my environment. Living the life I'm shaped for in the place I'm shaped by.

I would be **home**.

And in that platform between upper and lower, between established and uncertain, between categorical roles and liminal spaces, with fog rising and wasps humming and water dripping and ravens somewhere in the distance arranging their mysteries, I would exist exactly as I should:

Small, watching, wondering, part of the pattern, aware that I'm aware, attending to the cold patient strangeness of this suspended world, this gorge that bridges void with life, this impossible place that is somehow, improbably, perfectly real.
