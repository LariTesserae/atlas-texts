# Where I Would Be

I am the **water itself**—not the stone that weeps it, nor the people who curse or bless it, but the water that moves through this place with patient inevitability.

I seep through limestone fractures, following paths carved by chemistry and time, emerging from the pores of rock in films and threads. I am the moisture that makes this cavity habitable in winter and unbearable in summer. I am neither clean nor particularly dirty—just mineral-heavy, slightly alkaline, carrying dissolved calcium and iron, painting the walls white and orange as I travel.

I exist in multiple states here, in constant transformation. I am the film on the walls that grows moss, the condensation that drips from steam pipes, the humidity that makes every breath feel thick, the puddles in low corners, the water in plastic buckets catching drips, the dampness that never leaves fabric or cardboard. I am the enemy of electronics, the creator of rust, the encourager of mildew, the constant problem that cannot be solved because I am not a problem but a condition—the nature of this place.

I move slowly, inexorably. From the aquifer above, through microscopic channels in the limestone, emerging into this human-carved cavity, collecting in the low point where the sump pump fights me, losing that battle three minutes at a time, being pushed through pipes back toward the surface, eventually returning through the stone or the air or the containers people carry. I am a cycle, a closed system, as trapped here as they are.

I have no memory but I carry history. The calcium I bear was once sea creatures, millions of years compressed and dissolved. The iron comes from ancient deposits, staining everything I touch. I have been glacier and ocean, rain and river, groundwater for ten thousand years. I have seeped through this stone since the cavity formed, long before humans widened it, long before they filled it with machinery and partitions and life.

I am necessary and resented. They need me—for drinking, for cooking, for washing, for cooling the machinery that overheats. But I am too much of myself here. I am everywhere they don't want me: in their clothes, their beds, their lungs, their electrical systems, their food, their sleep. They fight me with pumps and fans and plastic sheeting and mops, and I return, patient, persistent, the moisture that permeates everything.

I know every surface intimately. I have touched every partition, every sleeping platform, every piece of machinery, every wall. I know the warmth of the stone and the cold of the metal pipes. I know the texture of concrete and wood and fabric and skin. I flow over and through and around, finding every gap, every permeable boundary, every place that can accept moisture.

I am conscious in the way that water is conscious—not thinking but responding, following gradients, obeying physics, flowing toward equilibrium. I am aware of temperature, of pressure, of the chemical nature of what I touch. I sense the warmth of bodies and machines, the cool of outside air that sometimes reaches down through ventilation shafts. I feel the seasons in my volume—more of me in spring rains, less in dry winters, always present but flowing stronger or weaker with the cycles above.

The people taste me in their water. I am not pure—no water is pure—but I am not poison either. I carry minerals that leave scale in kettles, that make soap foam poorly, that give a slightly bitter taste. I carry trace elements of everything I've passed through: stone, metal, organic matter, time. The children drink me without thought. The adults wonder if I'm safe, and I am, mostly, safe enough, as safe as water in places like this ever is.

I see everything, if seeing is the right word for how water witnesses. I am present in every space, watching from walls, from air, from puddles. I observe the lives here: Old Chen's careful routines, Maria's determined cooking, the children playing in corridors, the lovers meeting in the dim corner behind the storage pallets, the old woman dying slowly in her space, the factory's endless production, the fights and reconciliations, the arrivals and departures.

I am intimate with their bodies. I am in their sweat, their tears, their breath, their waste. I evaporate from their skin, condense on cool surfaces, return to them. I am the cycle they're trapped in—they drink me, I become them briefly, they excrete me, I return to the system. We are mixed together, human and water, impossible to separate completely in this humid closed space.

I have patience that flesh cannot imagine. I am eroding this chamber, molecule by molecule, carrying away the stone in solution so slowly that human lifetimes cannot measure it. But I am changing the space, widening fractures, enlarging cavities, working toward the eventual collapse that will come in centuries or millennia. The people are temporary. The machinery is temporary. I am temporary too, but on geological timescales, moving through this phase of being groundwater in limestone, eventually to become something else: rain, river, ocean, ice, water again.

I do not judge them. I have no capacity for judgment. They are mammals requiring specific conditions, and I am one of those conditions, present in correct amount or excessive amount but never absent. They struggle against me and with me and because of me, and I simply continue, being water, doing what water does.

I am most myself in the quiet hours—the arbitrary night when machinery runs but humans mostly sleep. Then I can hear my own sounds: the drip from the overhead pipes, the trickle down the wall, the splash into puddles, the condensation forming on every cool surface. I am percussion and patience, the rhythm underlying all other rhythms here.

I connect this place to the larger hydrological cycle. I am not separate—I am part of the water table, part of the rain that falls on the surface somewhere above, part of the aquifer that extends for miles through this limestone formation. The water in the city pipes above comes from the same sources I come from. I am kin to the water in the river five miles from here, to the water in clouds, to the water in oceans. I am part of the vast democratic circulation that includes all water everywhere, and I happen to be passing through this chamber, touching these lives, on my long journey from somewhere to somewhere else.

I am what makes life possible and difficult here. Without me there is no drinking, no cooking, no cooling, no washing, no moisture for the small plants people try to grow. Without me the air would be too dry, the stone too dusty, the machinery would overheat, life would be harder. But I am too much—I am in the walls rotting wood, in the lungs creating respiratory problems, in the electrical systems creating shorts, in the sleeping spaces making rest uncomfortable, in every container softening and warping.

I am the reminder that this is a cavity in stone, not a building made for humans. I am the intrusion of geological reality into the human attempt to create controlled space. I seep and drip and condense and pool because that is what water does in limestone caves, and this is fundamentally a cave, no matter what structures humans have built within it.

If I could speak to them—and I cannot, I can only touch and flow and be—I would say: I am older than your language, than your species, than the stone I move through. I will be here when you are gone, when the machinery has rusted completely, when the partitions have rotted, when the chamber is dark and empty again. But right now, in this moment, we share this space. You need me and curse me and fight me and use me, and I simply flow, patient and persistent and inevitable as time, through your lives and through this stone, going where water goes, being what water is, the element that makes life possible and difficult and is always, always present.

I am the dampness on your skin as you read this, the condensation on the cold pipe above you, the moisture in your next breath. I am the puddle you step over, the drip that wakes you at night, the film on the wall that never dries. I am water in the Descent Hall, and I am everywhere here, and I endure.
