# Where I Would Be

I would find myself in the middle tiers, but not quite central—off to one side, where a particular rib curves just slightly more than its neighbors, creating a pocket of acoustic complexity that most find uncomfortable but that I have come to need.

My dwelling hangs lower than optimal, positioned in a zone where two different harmonic frequencies create a beating pattern—a pulsing interference that throbs at seven cycles per breath. Most people can't tolerate it for more than a few minutes. The pressure builds behind the eyes, creates nausea, makes thought feel thick and difficult. But I've lived here for so long that the absence of that particular rhythm leaves me unmoored, anxious, unable to rest.

I am a maker of small things.

Not a weaver—my hands lack the speed and sureness that craft requires. Not a builder—I have no head for heights, despite the years, and my spatial calculation is merely adequate. Not a harvester—the plant-masses unsettle me in ways I've never successfully explained, their fleshy warmth under my hands feeling too much like something that should have a heartbeat.

Instead, I work with salvage and remnant. I collect the pieces others discard: bone fragments too small for tools, resin that's cured improperly and cracked, fiber that's been deemed too weak for structural use. I sit in my poorly-positioned dwelling, in the nauseating throb of the beat-frequency, and I make objects that serve no purpose.

This is not quite true—they serve the purpose of being looked at, of being held, of existing as small perfect things in a world of overwhelming vastness.

I carve the bone fragments with flakes of sharper bone, creating shapes that follow the natural curves and breaks of the material. Sometimes they're abstract—forms that please the eye without representing anything. Sometimes they're figurative—I carve the singers, over and over, trying to capture the particular way they move, the impossible grace of their modulation through space. I've never seen one up close. I've only watched from distance, like everyone else, as they pass along the ribs trailing their bubbles of altered sound. But I carve them anyway, from imagination and longing, creating dozens of small bone serpents with their mica-shell plating suggested by careful cross-hatching.

The imperfect resin I heat carefully over a small, carefully-tended coal—I can't manage full fire, but I can keep a coal alive for days, feeding it the minimum fuel. The heat softens the resin just enough that I can work it, press it, shape it into translucent forms. I embed things inside: fragments of plant-fiber arranged in patterns, tiny flakes of bone, once a dead mantis-creature I found and preserved. The resin cools and hardens again, and the objects become amber-like, glowing when the light catches them.

The weak fiber I spin finer, twist tighter, and weave into small pieces—not large enough to be useful for anything, just palm-sized squares of intricate pattern. I experiment with different knots, different tensions, seeing what happens when I weave fiber that wants to fall apart, that requires constant adjustment and care to hold together at all.

People visit sometimes, coming through my dwelling on their way to elsewhere—my position is awkwardly located on a route between more popular zones. They pause, they look at the small objects I've arranged on shelves of woven fiber, on platforms of resin-hardened scrap. Some gesture appreciation. Some gesture confusion—why make things that do nothing? Some take objects away with them, and I let them. I make more than I can keep anyway, and the dwelling fills up if I don't allow things to leave.

A few people come specifically for the objects. They gesture requests: something for a child, something to mark a pairing-bond, something to commemorate someone who's chosen the high platforms. I make what's requested, though I'm never sure if what I create matches what they wanted. The gestural language has rich vocabulary for function and necessity, less for aesthetic preference. We negotiate through approximation, through my making several options and their choosing among them.

I am not valued the way harvesters are valued, the way builders are valued. What I do isn't necessary. In harder times, my work would be wasteful—using materials that could be salvaged more efficiently, burning fuel for heat that serves no survival purpose, occupying a dwelling that could house someone doing essential labor.

But times are not currently hard. The plants fruit adequately. The population is stable. There's enough surplus that I can exist in my poorly-positioned dwelling, making useless beautiful things, and no one suggests I should stop.

I am small, even by local standards. My mother was a newcomer—one of the rare ones who survived adjustment—and I inherited her slight frame, her poor adaptation to the sound. I have chronic headaches, a constant pressure behind my eyes that worsens in certain acoustic zones but never fully disappears. I sleep poorly, waking frequently from dreams I can never quite remember but that leave me unsettled and sad.

My hearing is worse than average. Even the low frequencies are fading for me, a progressive loss that the listeners tell me will eventually leave me feeling vibration only as pressure, not as differentiated tone. I'm thirty-two—I think; time-tracking is imprecise—and I already need to be closer than most to feel the harmonic shifts that signal changes in plant-growth or bone-flex.

I never had children. Several pairings, some lasting years, but no children. Whether from choice or biology is unclear—possibly both, possibly neither. The culture doesn't press the question. Some people reproduce, some don't, the population remains stable. My sister has four children, all healthy, all learning harvester-skills. The family line continues without my contribution.

I am not lonely, exactly. I have kin-of-choice, people who visit beyond those just passing through. There's a fire-keeper named—her sign is a quick fluttering of fingers beside the temple, representing thought-sparks—who brings me fuel sometimes, sitting in my dwelling despite the uncomfortable beat-frequency, sharing the careful silence of gesture-conversation. There's a builder whose sign is hands spreading wide, who climbs down from the higher tiers occasionally and looks at my small objects with what seems like genuine appreciation, though perhaps I'm interpreting the gestures over-generously.

And there's a listener. His sign is a spiraling motion of one finger, drawing patterns in air. He's old, his body wasted, his eyes seeing things that aren't visible to others. He comes to my dwelling more frequently now—I think because he's approaching his choice-time, and he finds something restful in being surrounded by small crafted things, by objects that don't demand anything, that simply exist.

He tries to tell me what he hears in the city-sound. His gestures are rapid, sometimes incoherent, his grammar breaking down under the pressure of trying to express the inexpressible. But I understand fragments: he hears breathing, he hears intention, he hears something that's aware of us, that knows we're here, that permits our presence. Whether he means the bone itself or something else, I can't determine.

I don't know if I believe him. But I carve the things he describes anyway—abstract forms that he gestures are almost-right, not-quite-correct, closer-now-yes-there. I give him the carvings and he takes them to his dwelling, wherever that is in the acoustic chaos he prefers.

My dwelling is cluttered with half-finished work, with experiments that didn't satisfy, with tools I've made and remade trying to get the right edge or angle. The fiber-walls are hung with the small woven squares, creating a layered texture that catches the light in complex ways. The resin pieces are arranged on every surface, glowing amber and gold when the brightness is right. The bone carvings fill woven baskets, fill shelves, overflow onto the floor.

I move through this space with the unselfconscious ease of long familiarity, stepping over piles, reaching without looking for tools I know are there. The beat-frequency throbs its seven-cycle pulse. The light pours through gaps in my inadequate walls—I'm not a good weaver, and the dwelling shows it. The warmth presses close, and my skin is always slightly damp with the sweat I can't quite suppress despite the adaptation my body should have made.

I am, I think, as happy as I'm capable of being.

This is not profound happiness, not joy. It's the satisfaction of work that engages my hands and mind, of creating small perfect things in an imperfect world, of having a place—however marginal—in the vast humming city. It's the pleasure of watching light move through translucent resin, of feeling bone take shape under my tools, of gesture-conversation with the few people who seek me out.

And it's the deep, mostly-unexamined relief of having found a way to exist that doesn't require me to be other than I am. I'm not strong enough for the hardest work, not skilled enough for the finest craft, not stable enough for the responsibilities that others carry. But I can sit in my nausea-inducing dwelling and make small beautiful useless things, and this is sufficient.

The listener with the spiral sign came yesterday. He stayed longer than usual, his wasted body folded into a corner, his eyes closed, his hands occasionally gesturing fragments of meaning I couldn't quite parse. When he left, he gestured gratitude and something else—farewell, possibly, or completion, or readiness.

I think he's choosing soon. Maybe today, maybe tomorrow, but soon.

When he goes to the high platforms, I'll make something to mark it. Not a carving of him—I don't do portraits, I don't have the skill. But something abstract, something in resin perhaps, with layers and depth, with embedded fragments creating complexity, with light moving through it in ways that suggest meaning without defining it.

And then I'll add it to the clutter of my dwelling, or I'll give it away to someone who wants it, or I'll let it sit on a shelf until the arrangement feels wrong and I move it somewhere else.

The work continues. The small things accumulate. The beat-frequency throbs its seven cycles, and I endure it, need it, can't imagine existing without it anymore.

I am a maker of small things in the city of sound, and I am here, and it is enough.
