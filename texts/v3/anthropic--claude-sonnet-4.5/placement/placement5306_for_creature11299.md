# Where I Would Be

I would be in the middle reaches, where the sound is thickest—not the highest shriek nor the lowest drone, but that central band where all the acoustic layers compress into a pressure so dense it becomes almost solid. Here, in a gallery that curves along the throat's western face, where three major water channels converge before splitting into seven smaller streams, where the moisture is so heavy you can see it moving through the air in visible currents, where ferns cascade from every crack and moss cushions every surface thick as mattresses.

I would be becoming a mapper of the unmappable.

My transformation would be incomplete—deliberately, eternally incomplete. While others have surrendered fully to plant or stone or sound, I would exist in the threshold state, one foot in human comprehension and one foot in something else, maintaining that precarious balance not despite its difficulty but because of it. The tension itself would be my function.

My hands would still be hands, mostly. The fingers elongated slightly, more sensitive, able to perceive texture at a nearly microscopic level. The fingertips would have begun to harden into something between nail and stone, and I would use them to trace the patterns carved into walls by water, to feel the subtle differences in limestone strata, to read the city's history through touch. But the palms would have become permeable, able to sense moisture content in the air, to detect the pressure differentials that drive the wind, to feel sound as direct sensation rather than hearing it.

I would press my palms flat against walls and feel the deep vibrations in the bedrock, the slow flex and breath of the throat itself. I would hold them in the water channels and sense the flow's velocity, its temperature, the minerals it carries dissolved in solution. My hands would be instruments of measurement, but measurement that goes beyond number into something more direct, more immediate—a knowing that is also a becoming, a recording that changes both the recorder and the recorded.

My skin would have begun the slow process of taking on moisture, of learning to photosynthesize in the diffuse light. Patches of green would have appeared across my shoulders, down my spine, along my ribs—not moss growing *on* me but my own cells converting, chloroplasts multiplying, turning me into something that can drink light and exhale oxygen. The transformation would progress slowly, unevenly, creating a patchwork of flesh and plant tissue that would need constant attention, constant negotiation between what I was and what I am becoming.

I would tend these patches like gardens, pruning when they grow too fast, encouraging them when they lag, maintaining the balance that keeps me in this productive in-between state. The plant parts would anchor me to the throat's ecosystem, making me part of the water cycle, part of the carbon exchange, but not so completely that I lose the mobility I need.

Because I would still move, still walk the galleries, though not in the endless driven circuits of the walkers. My movement would be deliberate, systematic, covering the same routes repeatedly but with attention to detail, to change, to the minute ways the city shifts and reconfigures. I would be documenting these changes, though "documenting" isn't quite right—there would be no written record, no external archive.

Instead, the map would be *in* me. My body would be the record.

The bones would remember. They would have begun to calcify differently, laying down growth rings like a tree, each ring encoding information about a different gallery, a different season of moisture, a different pattern in the sound. I would be building a fossil record of the present, my skeleton slowly becoming a three-dimensional archive of the throat's patterns, readable by anyone who knew how to read bone, which would be no one, which would be everyone who has transformed sufficiently.

My muscles would remember the distances walked, even as those distances change. The fibers would encode routes and elevations and the effort required to traverse them. When the galleries shift and a passage that took fifty steps yesterday takes seventy today, my body would know, would hold both measurements, would accumulate the contradictions rather than resolving them. I would become a living record of the throat's impossible geography.

My lungs would remember the sound. They would have changed, adapted, developed structures that let them resonate with specific frequencies. I would breathe in the roar and my chest cavity would select certain tones from the cacophony, amplifying them, holding them, learning them. Each breath would be an act of sampling, of recording, building an internal archive of the throat's song. Over time—years, decades—I would have collected enough to begin perceiving the patterns, the repeating phrases, the deep structures underneath the chaos.

And I would hum. Almost unconsciously, a habit born from constant listening, I would hum responses to what I hear, my throat producing harmonics and countermelodies to the throat's song. Not loudly—my voice would be inaudible beneath the roar—but persistently. The humming would reshape my vocal cords, my larynx, carving channels and chambers that make me into a better instrument. The transformation would be subtle, interior, leaving my face mostly unchanged while everything behind it reorganizes itself for resonance.

My eyes would be the most problematic compromise. I would need them to see, to observe, to notice the details that constitute the map, but sight here is half-illusion anyway, reality too uncertain to trust to eyes alone. So they would have begun to change, developing the same crystalline film as the fern-tender in the lower galleries, but thinner, more transparent. It would give me double vision: the ordinary world and something else underneath or overlaid, a perception of the flows and patterns that move through the throat independent of its physical structure.

I would see the water not just as liquid but as circuit, glowing faintly with the energy of its circulation. I would see the sound as color, different frequencies painting the air in shades that don't exist in normal light. I would see the slow transformations of the other inhabitants, the progress of their becomings rendered visible as auras or emanations or something for which there is no vocabulary.

But I would also see normally, see stone and moss and moisture as they are, because mapping requires both—the practical and the impossible, the measured and the intuited, the human scale and the throat's scale, held together in uncomfortable synthesis.

My memory would be the most changed, and the change would be the hardest to bear.

It would have started to become non-linear, accumulating moments out of sequence, storing them in patterns of association rather than chronology. I would remember a gallery from three months ago and also from tomorrow, both memories equally vivid, equally real, because in the throat time loops and folds and the gallery exists in multiple states simultaneously. I would remember conversations with other inhabitants even though no one speaks here, even though communication is impossible—but the memories would be real anyway, true in some sense that transcends factual accuracy.

I would remember being stone. Not imaginatively but actually, having somehow absorbed the memories carried in the limestone itself, the deep geological time of its formation, its slow uplift, its patient erosion. These memories would rise up without warning, threatening to overwhelm my human memories, threatening to convince me that I have been rock for three hundred million years and this brief episode of flesh is the aberration, the dream, the temporary misunderstanding.

I would struggle with this. Daily, hourly. The fight to remain myself, to not let the throat's vast memory subsume my small one, would be constant and exhausting. But necessary. The mapping requires a human perspective, or at least a perspective that remembers being human, that can hold the throat's reality and human reality in dialogue rather than letting one consume the other.

So I would maintain anchors. Small practices, rituals, repeated actions that reinforce continuity of self. I would touch my face every morning—morning being arbitrary here, just a time I designate as morning—tracing the familiar features, feeling for the changes but also for what remains. I would count my breaths in sets of ten, the counting itself a human habit, a mathematics that organizes time into manageable units even though time here is not manageable.

I would speak aloud, though no one can hear, though I cannot hear myself over the roar. I would form words with my lips and tongue, describing what I see, narrating my observations, the vibration of my own voice in my skull a reminder that I am still a thing that uses language, that thinks in sentences, that processes reality through the narrative structures of human consciousness.

The words would be lost immediately to the sound, but I would speak them anyway: "The eastern channel is flowing faster today. The ferns in gallery forty-seven have produced new fronds. The woman who is becoming water has reached her basin and will likely complete transformation within the next cycle. I am still here. I am still watching. I am still myself."

This last would be half-true at best, but I would say it anyway.

My clothing would have long since merged with my body, or been replaced by growths that serve the same function. A kind of bark would have formed across my back and shoulders, flexible but protective, keeping the moisture from penetrating too deeply into the tissues that still need to stay relatively dry. Lichen would grow on it, and I would let it, the symbiosis providing additional nutrients from the air.

My feet would be the most transformed, necessarily. To walk the galleries continuously requires feet that can adapt to wet stone, that can find purchase on surfaces that would send a normal human sliding into the void. My toes would have broadened, flattened, developing the same slight adhesive quality as the vines that cling to vertical surfaces. Small roots would have begun to emerge from my soles, not long enough to trap me in place like the fern-tender, but enough to anchor me briefly when I stand still, to draw moisture and minerals up through stone when I rest.

I would spend much of my time in the gallery with the three converging channels, my home base, my center of operations, though "operations" suggests a purpose I might no longer be able to articulate. Here I would have created what might charitably be called a workspace—a flat section of wall where mineral deposits have built up over my repeated touchings, where the stone has become polished by my hands, where faint marks might be visible, scratches and patterns that could be notation or could be random, even I would no longer be certain.

I would return here after each circuit of the nearby galleries, pressing my hands to this wall, downloading, uploading, transferring the sensory data I've collected into the stone itself or into my own bones or into the larger pattern of the throat. The action would be compulsive, necessary, the only way to process the overwhelming input that constant observation provides. I would stand with my palms flat against the wall for hours, barely breathing, while information flows through me in ways that are no longer quite cognitive.

Other inhabitants would pass by occasionally. The walkers on their endless routes, moving with the determination of planets in orbit. The ones who are becoming other things, heading toward their destinies in stone or water or sound. We would not acknowledge each other directly, but there would be awareness, recognition, a sense of mutual occupation of the space that might be the closest thing to community that exists here.

Once—or many times, or continuously—one of the singers from the upper galleries would descend to where I work. She would stand nearby, not speaking because speech is impossible, and the holes in her torso would catch the wind in new ways, producing notes that seem almost like questions. I would turn to face her, or I would already be facing her, having sensed her approach through changes in the acoustic pattern, and I would hum a response, my throat producing harmonics that might mean *yes* or *I understand* or *I am mapping what you are singing* or might mean nothing, might be just the automatic response of my transformed vocal cords to her transformed body.

She would tilt her head—she would still have a head, still have that much human form—and adjust her position, and the notes would change, and we would stand like this in dialogue without language, in communication without content, two things that used to be people exchanging information that neither of us could translate back into words even if words were possible here.

Then she would leave, or I would leave, or we would both remain but stop noticing each other, attention diffusing back into the general environment of sound and moisture and green growing things.

At night—again, arbitrary, just when I decide it's night—I would find a niche in the gallery wall, a shallow cavity that has formed through erosion, just large enough for my body. I would fold myself into it, pulling my knees to my chest, making myself compact, and the moss would grow over me like a blanket, and the moisture would condense on my skin, and I would enter a state that might be sleep or might be something else, a kind of dormancy where consciousness doesn't cease but distributes itself, spreading out into the immediate environment, dissolving temporarily into the water and stone and air.

In this state I would sense the old ones in the deep chambers, their vast presences moving beneath everything like slow tides. I would feel their attention pass over me, categorizing me, noting my presence in whatever accounting they maintain. They would recognize what I am attempting—the mapping, the documentation, the bridge between human and throat consciousness—and they would neither approve nor disapprove because those are human categories and they are long past human categories.

But they would allow it. They would preserve the possibility of my continued existence in this in-between state, would maintain the conditions that let me keep my precarious balance instead of tipping fully into transformation. Not out of kindness—they don't have kindness anymore—but because my mapping serves some function in their vast unfathomable purposes, because a record of the throat's changes has value to them even though they are themselves that record, are the throat, are the change.

I would wake—would return to consolidated consciousness—with the taste of their regard still in my mouth, metallic and ancient and utterly inhuman. I would touch my face again, checking for new changes, finding them, accepting them, preparing to begin another circuit of the galleries, another day of observation and documentation and the slow erosion of everything I once was into something new.

But not yet complete. Never quite complete.

That would be my existence: walking the wet galleries, pressing my hands to walls, breathing the roar, watching the other inhabitants transform, fighting and failing and fighting again to maintain some core of human awareness even as everything around and inside me pushes toward dissolution, toward merger, toward the singing unity that the throat offers to everything that stays too long within its impossible acoustics.

I would be a cartographer of shifts, a chronicler of changes that undermine the possibility of chronicle, a bridge between states that grows more unstable with each crossing but continues to hold, continues to permit passage, continues to serve whatever purpose bridges serve in a city that is all threshold, all transition, all becoming and no being.

And I would hum. Always, underneath everything, I would hum the throat's song back to it, my small voice adding to the overwhelming chorus, participating in the music even while trying to understand it, singing even while documenting the singing, already part of what I am attempting to observe, already lost to the work of trying not to be lost.

The moss on my shoulders would grow thicker each day. The chlorophyll in my skin would spread. The roots from my feet would dig deeper. The transformation would continue its patient advance, and I would continue my patient resistance, and the gallery would continue its patient witnessing, and the throat would continue its patient song, and none of it would end because nothing here ends, it only circulates, returns, repeats with variations too subtle to name.

I would be there. Am there. Will always have been there.

In the middle reaches, where the sound is thickest, where the water converges and divides, where transformation is a process without conclusion, where mapping and becoming are the same action.

Pressing my hands to wet stone and feeling everything.
