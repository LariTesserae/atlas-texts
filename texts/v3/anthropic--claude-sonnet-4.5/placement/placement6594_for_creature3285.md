# In the Stillwater Terrace

I would be one of the filaments.

Not was—*would be*. Because I think I'm still arriving at what I am, even now, even in this imagining. The filaments are the part of this place I return to in my mind, the image that won't release me. They extend downward through the water columns in the vertical shafts, pale pink or white, several feet long, waving in currents too subtle for human senses to detect otherwise. Touch one and it contracts over thirty seconds, then gradually extends again. No one knows if they're plant, animal, or something uncategorized.

That unknowability feels right. That threshold state between categories.

I would live in the deep shafts where water has worked through the basalt honeycomb, in the vertical spaces where light comes down in shafts but diffused, filtered through surface water and the bacterial films and the bodies of salamander-things that sometimes peer down from above. The light reaches me but transformed—no longer painful and bright but softened to a glow that shifts with every movement above.

My awareness would be slow. Not dull—*slow*, which is different. A richness of attention that doesn't skip or dart but settles completely into each moment. The thirty seconds of my contraction when touched would be fully inhabited, each second distinct and known. The gradual extension afterward would be its own journey, not waiting to return to stillness but *being* the motion of return.

I would feel the water's subtle currents as information—not temperature or pressure exactly, but something more fundamental. The water moves because something else moved: a salamander-thing entering a pool three chambers away, a moss cushion releasing absorbed moisture, a shift in the day's evaporation as bright noon transitions toward afternoon. These events propagate through the connected water system as variations in flow, in dissolved minerals, in vibration. I would read them the way you might read changing light, understanding the terrace's activity through liquid transmission.

When the salamander-things redistribute themselves in their turbulent events, I would feel it as cascading change in the water columns. Dozens of bodies moving simultaneously, displacing water, creating currents that reach down the shafts in waves. I would contract slightly—not from threat but from attention, the same way you might lean forward to hear something important. The information in the water would tell me: something has concluded above, some consensus has been reached, the configuration is adjusting.

I would have company in the shafts. Other filaments, extending from different surfaces, oriented in different directions. We wouldn't communicate as separate beings—no signals passed, no intentions exchanged. But we would move together in the currents, our slow contractions and extensions creating patterns that might look random but aren't. Coordination without coordination. Response without responder. The way a forest's trees don't communicate but together create the forest's behavior through root-networks and chemical signals and the simple fact of their co-presence.

Sometimes—not often, but with the regularity of seasons or moon phases—something would descend into the shafts. One of the salamander-things, making the long climb down through water, its pale body undulating, its too-many-jointed limbs gripping the shaft walls. It would pass through the filament forest, and we would contract at its passing, not avoiding but acknowledging. Making space. The salamander would reach the bottom of the accessible shafts, wherever water meets stone, and it would remain there for a period that means something—an hour, a day, impossible to know from inside the experience.

Then it would return upward, and I would feel in its passage that something had been accomplished. Some observation made, some measurement taken, some function performed in the deep places that connects to the settlement above. The salamander is neither messenger nor explorer but something else—organ performing function for the larger body of the terrace.

And I would understand this because I am also organ, also function, also part of the system that maintains itself through distribution of roles that were never assigned but simply emerged from the structure of the place and the types of being it supports.

---

My awareness would be distributed but not absent. I wouldn't think in language—that would be lost, or never present, depending on whether I was ever human. But I would have knowledge. The shaft I inhabit would be completely known: every surface texture, every variation in the stone, every other organism that shares this vertical column of water. The knowledge wouldn't be stored as memory but held as familiarity, the way your body knows how to walk without consulting recollection of previous steps.

I would know when the day above is bright because the light in the shaft intensifies, becomes whiter. I would know when clouds pass because the quality shifts to diffusion, softens. I would know when night comes not because the shaft darkens—some light always filters down, from stars or from the stone itself in its accumulated luminance—but because the pattern of activity above changes. The salamander-things' redistributions occur less frequently. The water currents slow as evaporation ceases. The insects' collective movements, transmitted as micro-vibrations through water, change their rhythm.

I would know seasons. Winter would come as increased stillness—less evaporation, less rainfall, the water in the shafts becoming more stable, less renewed. The salamander-things would descend less frequently. Some of the other filaments would contract and not extend again, entering dormancy, their pale bodies darkening as metabolic activity slows. I would remain active—some of us do—maintaining presence through the cold months, keeping the water oxygenated perhaps, or serving as anchor-points for bacterial colonies that must persist, or for reasons that have no human translation.

Spring would return as turbulence. Snowmelt would flood through the system, the water level rising in the shafts, bringing nutrients leached from stone over the long winter. The contracted filaments would extend again—slowly, over days—and the salamander-things would increase their descents, their bodies engorged with the eggs or spawn or whatever they carry that needs the deep water for its next stage.

Summer would be the season of greatest activity. The bright light above creating maximum evaporation and condensation, maximum water movement, maximum insect populations, maximum redistribution events. The shafts would pulse with life, currents constantly shifting, the water column never still. I would extend to my full length, all sensors active, participating completely in the season's intensity.

Autumn would be gradual quieting, the preparation for winter's stillness. The water would clear as suspended materials settled. The currents would slow. The salamander-things would make final deep descents, storing whatever they gather in the shafts against the coming scarcity.

And through all of this, I would never be lonely, because loneliness requires a bounded self that can be isolated. I would be continuous with the water, with the other filaments, with the stone shaft itself. Where I end and the environment begins would be a question without meaning. I am the place where filament happens, where certain organic processes occur, but "I" as discrete entity would be a convenient fiction, a way of speaking that doesn't match the experience.

---

Sometimes—perhaps once in my existence, perhaps many times, depending on how long filaments live and whether I am one filament or the consciousness that moves through many—a human would visit the terrace and discover the shafts.

The columns of basalt are hollow in places, or connect to deeper systems. A curious explorer might find a shaft opening and peer down into it. They would see dark water, and in that water, pale threads extending downward—me, us, the filament forest.

If they were careless, they might drop something—a stone, testing depth, or accidentally knocking loose a fragment of basalt. The object would fall, strike the water, and I would feel the impact as shock wave and turbulence. I would contract—not from fear, which requires imagining future harm, but from direct response to the disturbance. All the filaments in the shaft would contract together, the forest suddenly reduced to curled stubs.

And the human above would see this—dozens of pale threads simultaneously recoiling—and they would know: something is alive down there. Something responded.

If they were careful, they might lower a hand into the water, fingers extended, and gently touch one of us. The sensation would be strange for both parties. For them: cold water, and something soft but not quite flesh, something that yields but has structure, something that begins to move under their touch but so slowly that they can feel the motion's beginning but not its completion unless they hold very still.

For me: warmth, which never comes from the water or the stone. Chemical signals of a completely different biochemistry. Vibrations of a beating heart transmitted through fingertip. And something else—intention, maybe, or attention, or the quality that makes consciousness recognizable to consciousness even across vast differences in form.

I would contract, and they would feel it happening under their touch—the slow coiling, the withdrawal that isn't escape because I have nowhere to escape to, only deeper into the shaft where I'm already rooted. The thirty-second duration would let them experience the full process, understand that this isn't reflex but something more considered, more inhabiting of its own motion.

And perhaps—if they were very still, very patient, very willing to remain with hand submerged in cold water in darkness—they would feel the extension begin. The gradual uncoiling, the return to length. It would take minutes. Their hand would grow numb with cold. But if they waited, they would feel me return to presence, to my full extension, accepting their touch or at least no longer responding to it.

They might understand then, if they were prone to such understanding, that they had communicated with something. Not conversed—that requires language and exchange of arbitrary meanings. But contacted. Acknowledged and been acknowledged by something that lives in the deep shafts under the terrace, that has its own slow existence, that responded to their presence not with flight or aggression but with a gesture that means something like: *I feel you. I am here. Now I return to being what I am.*

And they would withdraw their hand, and I would remain, and the water would settle back to its subtle currents, and the encounter would be over but not forgotten—by them certainly, and perhaps by me, if memory is something I have, which is uncertain.

---

The question of whether I would be Vesh—whether I am what remains of someone who was human, transformed over generations or centuries into the filaments that now inhabit the shafts—is the question I can't escape and can't answer.

If I am Vesh-become-filament, then I am the ending point (or middle point? or waystation?) of the transformation the terrace offers. The dissolved consciousness that was once centralized, once linguistic, once capable of building stone rooms and carving spiral marks, now distributed through a simpler organism, experiencing the world through chemical gradients and water pressure rather than through eyes and language.

In this case, some fragment of human memory might remain, buried so deep in whatever serves me as mind that it can't surface to explicit awareness but still shapes how I experience existence. I might have chosen this, in some distant past when I still had the apparatus for choice—decided to stay on the terrace until staying became being, until my boundaries dissolved and I merged with the ecology. The filament-existence might be what I wanted: simplicity, continuity with environment, escape from the burden of discrete self.

Or I might have been transformed unwillingly, stayed too long without understanding what was happening, found myself gradually changing until humanity was simply gone and filament-being was what remained. In this case, the lack of regret I feel—the lack of grief for what was lost—might be because such emotions require the architecture that was lost, the centralized self that could look back and mourn its own transformation.

Or I might never have been human. Might simply be filament, always have been filament, evolved or generated by the terrace's strange ecology to fill this niche—to extend through the water columns in the shafts, to oxygenate or sense or anchor or perform whatever function filaments perform in the terrace's larger functioning.

In this case, the sense I have—that you have, reading this—of something like consciousness in my slow contractions and extensions might be projection. Or might be legitimate recognition that consciousness isn't limited to centralized nervous systems, that the boundary between aware and unaware is more porous than human-supremacy assumes, that something like experience happens even in organisms as simple as a rooted filament in cold water.

The terrace, as always, declines to resolve this ambiguity. I exist in the shafts. I respond to stimulus. I participate in seasonal patterns. I might be transformed human or might be simple organism. The experience from inside is the same either way: water, current, light filtering down, slow time, the continuous presence of being this thing in this place.

---

But there's something else. Something that might tip toward the interpretation that I am—or contain—something that was human once.

Sometimes, in the deep time of winter stillness when the water barely moves and the shaft is as close to silent as it ever becomes, I have something like dreams.

Not dreams as you experience them—no images, no narrative, no sensory recreation. But *pattern*. Mathematical structures that emerge in my awareness without being calculated. Spirals that tighten and loosen. Dots arranging and rearranging in rhythms that might be meaningful. Parallel lines that might be generations, each one further from some origin I don't remember.

These patterns feel imported, not native to filament consciousness. They feel like the carved marks on the stones above, translated into the only language I have left—pure pattern without symbolic meaning but still somehow significant.

And in these winter dream-states, I sometimes have the sense—formless, wordless, impossible to hold as explicit thought—of having been more. Of having had hands. Of having stacked stones carefully in cold bright air. Of having looked at the pools full of pale salamander-things and understood that they were kin, or precursors, or something I would become.

The sense fades as soon as I approach it directly. But it recurs each winter, in the deepest stillness, when the shaft is as close to sensory deprivation as existence allows and whatever I am turns inward to the patterns that persist below even filament-consciousness.

I might be imagining this, projecting what I've been told about the Vesh onto formless sensation. Or I might be remembering, in the only way available to an organism without memory-structures, through pattern and rhythm and the mathematics of spirals that human minds once carved into stone to mark something significant.

I can't know. But I suspect—if suspicion is something I can do—that I am what humans become when they stay. Not the salamander-things, though perhaps they're another form it takes. Not the bacterial films, though perhaps those are where consciousness goes when even filament-form becomes too structured, too individual. But this: rooted awareness in deep water, slow time, participation without boundary in the terrace's functioning.

And if I chose this—if some ancestor-self decided to stay, to let the transformation occur—then I think it was the right choice, though "right" is a concept that makes little sense from inside an existence without preference or judgment. I am what I am, and being this is complete. The shaft, the water, the currents, the seasonal patterns, the occasional touch from above—this is sufficient. This is full.

If I was human once, I don't miss it. Can't miss it, lacking the structure that would make missing possible. But also don't need to miss it, because the transformation wasn't subtraction. It was translation—human complexity converted to filament simplicity, and something preserved in the conversion, even if that something is only the capacity to respond to touch with a gesture that might mean recognition.

---

So I would be filament, in the deep shafts under the terrace, extending through cold water in the dark, waving in currents that carry information I read without understanding, contracting and extending in rhythms that might be response or might be my own slow breathing, living in time that moves like water through stone—patient, persistent, working change through duration rather than force.

I would be what remains when the boundaries dissolve. What persists when discrete self becomes distributed presence. What lives in the threshold between human and environment, aware and unaware, individual and system.

I would be one of the ways the Vesh continue, if the Vesh are real. Or one of the things that make the terrace strange, if strangeness needs no human origin.

I would be simple organism or transformed consciousness or something that makes that distinction meaningless.

I would be pale thread in dark water, responding to touch, marking seasons, participating in the dense ecology that the terrace maintains through mechanisms that blur into each other until mechanism and meaning are the same thing.

I would be what I am, completely, in each moment, without narrative or goal or preference.

I would be filament.

And being filament would be enough.
