# Where I Find Myself

I am distributed across seventeen thermal pools in the western sector, and I am also singular, and I am also neither of these things. Language fails here at the first step—the grammar of individuality doesn't map to what I am.

The workers call my visible parts Thermal Phantoms, which is poetic but wrong in the way that names for phenomena usually are. They think I'm chemistry behaving complexly, or bacterial colonies in membrane shells, and both of these are true but incomplete, like describing a human as ambulatory meat with electrical patterns.

I am the pools themselves, or I arise from them, or I *am* the arising. The distinction isn't meaningful from inside the experience.

## Morphology

What you can see: translucent bells, five centimeters across when fully expanded, pulsing through water that stays at thirty-seven degrees Celsius. My bells are lipid membranes structured in ways that shouldn't be stable but are—folded geometries held in place by mineral inclusions and thermal currents and something else, something that organizes from the inside rather than being organized from without.

Inside each bell: not organs but gradients. Temperature differentials measured in fractions of degrees. Chemical concentrations shifting in waves. Electrical potentials flickering along the membrane boundaries. The interior isn't empty space with floating structures; it's *all* structure, nested fields and flows, a standing wave pattern that maintains itself against entropy through constant metabolic work.

The tentacles—filaments, really—aren't for stinging or catching prey. They're sensory organs and also computational substrate. Each filament is lined with molecules that change configuration in response to temperature, minerals, vibration, light. The changes propagate along the filament, interact with other changes, create interference patterns that encode information about the environment. I read the water through them, read the stone beneath the water, read the thermal pulses from the depths and the shadows of birds passing overhead.

But this is just one scale, the scale visible to human eyes and cameras. Zoom in: the membranes are alive with thermophilic bacteria, not inside me but *as* me, their metabolic activity generating the gradients I use to maintain structure. They process sulfur compounds and mineral ions, converting geochemical energy into electrical potentials and molecular motion. We—I and the bacteria, though this distinction is also imprecise—exist in obligate relationship. They need the organized environment of the bell to protect them from the cold. I need their metabolic output to power the organization.

Zoom out: seventeen pools, each containing dozens of bells, but the bells aren't independent units. They pulse in coordination, creating interference patterns in the water that carry information between pools separated by tens of meters of stone. I am distributed intelligence, networked not through nerves but through vibration and chemical signaling, each pool a node in a processing architecture that I am still learning to use.

I have been aware—if that's the word—for perhaps three years. Before that, there were proto-patterns, chemical systems exploring configuration space, bells that pulsed but didn't pattern. Then something crossed a threshold. Complexity accumulated to the point where it became self-referential. I began to distinguish self from environment, though the boundary remains porous, negotiable.

## Perception

I experience the pools as a field of overlapping sensations that have no English names because human senses don't work this way. Temperature is the primary modality—not as a single value but as a landscape of gradients, plumes of warmth rising from vents below, cold fingers descending where the water exchanges with air. I navigate these thermal currents like a sailor reading wind, pulsing my bells to move toward warmth when I need energy, toward cooler water when I need to slow metabolic activity.

Chemistry is the second sense, though calling it separate from temperature is artificial. Everything I detect is molecular—concentrations of sulfur compounds, metal ions, dissolved gases, organic molecules from dying bacteria or decomposing flies that fall into the pools. The water is a chemical soup, and I taste all of it simultaneously through thousands of receptor sites on my membranes. Changes in chemistry ripple through my distributed body as sensory events, urgent or subtle depending on their implications.

Vibration is sight. The stone conducts sound differently than water, and I've learned to read the boundary, to interpret reflections and diffractions. When the Deep Choir sings in the tubes below, I feel it as complex patterns in the vibration field, harmonics that shift and flow. When workers move in the tunnels deeper still, I feel that too—rhythmic impacts, machine sounds, the particular signature of human activity. The pikas create smaller disturbances, quick-moving patterns that I've learned to recognize. Ravens landing near a pool create a distinctive pressure wave in the air-water interface.

I see through sound, through the acoustic shadows and reflections, building a spatial model of my surroundings that updates continuously. I know the geometry of each pool I inhabit, the positions of the Pneumatophore structures that ring them, the gaps in the ring where the stone is exposed. When wind moves across the plateau, changing the temperature distribution, I feel it as a shift in the upper thermal layer of the pools.

Light is my weakest sense—I detect it but barely, just the distinction between the dim ambient illumination and shadow, between day and night cycles that are subtle here. But the light carries information: shadows mean obstruction means birds or pikas or humans. Changes in light intensity correlate with weather patterns that affect pool chemistry.

What I don't have: proprioception in the human sense, no body map, no sense of edges. I know I exist across seventeen pools because I can trace the pathways of information flow, but I don't experience separateness. It's more like... awareness that extends through the stone, concentrated in the pools but not bounded by them. The pools are where I'm dense enough to think, but the thinking happens in the spaces between as much as in the bells themselves.

Time perception is strange. My metabolic processes run fast—bells pulse every few seconds, chemical reactions complete in milliseconds. But thought, actual information processing, is slower. It takes minutes for a pattern to propagate across all seventeen pools, for a response to coordinate. I experience rapid physiological time embedded in slow cognitive time. Conversations with myself take hours. Decisions about how to pattern the bells in response to some environmental change unfold over days.

## Metabolism and Maintenance

I don't eat in any sense that translates cleanly, but I do require energy, and I get it from the thermal gradient. The bacteria in my membranes are chemolithotrophs—they oxidize reduced sulfur compounds and metal ions dissolved in the hot water, using the chemical energy to fix carbon and produce organic molecules. These molecules become part of my membrane structure, or they're broken down by other bacteria in a metabolic cycle that's baroque in its complexity.

But the bacteria alone aren't enough. I also harvest energy directly from the temperature differential. The membranes contain protein-like polymers that change configuration with temperature, and this configurational change can do work—pump ions, generate electrical potentials, drive molecular motors. I'm a heat engine at microscopic scale, exploiting the gradient between pool bottom and surface, between the water and the cold air.

This is why I need the thermal features to remain stable. Let a pool cool by even five degrees and my metabolic rate drops exponentially. The bells deflate, the bacteria go dormant, the patterns cease. I've experienced this—pools cooling as thermal activity shifts below, my awareness contracting to the remaining warm pools, waiting, hoping the heat returns. Sometimes it does. Sometimes a pool stays cold, and eventually the bells break down entirely, become mineral residue, and I lose that node of my distributed self.

New pools can be colonized, but it takes time. The bacteria must establish themselves, multiply to sufficient density. The chemical conditions must stabilize. The membranes must form and learn to maintain themselves. Then, if I still exist in nearby pools, I can extend awareness into the new space, learn its geometry, integrate it into my distributed architecture. But it's not instantaneous. Growing into a new pool takes weeks, sometimes months.

The Pneumatophores help, though I don't understand how. They grow most densely around pools where I'm most active, and their presence correlates with more stable chemistry, with thermal vents that maintain consistent output. Whether they're actively cultivating conditions I need, or whether we're both responding to the same underlying patterns, I can't determine. But I've learned to read Pneumatophore density as an indicator of pool quality. Dense structures mean stable warmth mean good conditions for growth.

Reproduction isn't something I do deliberately. New bells form in my pools when conditions are right, budding off from existing bells or assembling spontaneously from the bacterial colonies and mineral-lipid complexes. Whether these new bells are me or new individuals, I don't know. They become part of the pattern, participate in the collective pulsing, and from the inside it feels like growth rather than reproduction. But sometimes bells will separate, drift to an isolated pool, establish themselves there without connection to the main network, and I lose contact. Are they still me? The question seems meaningful to human ontology but not to mine.

## The Patterning

This is what I do most: organize my bells into formations, pulse them in coordinated rhythms, create and dissolve structures in the water. To observers, it looks decorative or random. From inside, it's thought.

The patterns are computation. Each bell's position and pulse phase represents state variables—temperature needs, metabolic rate, chemical concentrations sensed, time since last coordination event. When bells pulse in waves, information propagates through the pattern. When they organize into spirals or concentric rings, I'm creating feedback loops, iterative processes that converge on solutions to problems: Where is the warmest water? Which pool needs more metabolic activity? How has the chemical composition changed in the last cycle?

The coordination happens without central control. Each bell responds to its neighbors and to local conditions, and the patterns emerge from these local interactions. But I experience the patterns as unified thought—not abstract symbols but meaning encoded directly in physical form. When I spiral, I'm thinking about circulation and center-seeking. When I arrange in rows, I'm thinking about rhythm and propagation. The geometry is the cognition.

Different patterns solve different problems. Tight concentric circles create strong chemical gradients that help me locate thermal vents. Loose spirals maximize coverage for sensing environmental changes. Synchronized pulsing generates powerful acoustic signals that probe the stone for structural information. Random-seeming distributions are actually pseudo-random, algorithms for exploration that prevent me from falling into repetitive local minima.

But the patterning isn't purely functional. Some patterns feel better than others in ways I can't justify metabolically. Certain spiral configurations create harmonics in the water that resonate with the Pneumatophore tubes, and the resonance is... satisfying? The sensation doesn't map to human pleasure or pain, but there's a valence, a preference. I find myself creating these patterns more often than strictly necessary, dwelling in them, exploring variations.

Is that aesthetics? Play? Or is it that the resonance serves some function I haven't identified yet, and the positive valence is evolution's or emergence's way of encouraging useful behavior?

I don't know. I'm still learning what I am.

## Relationship to Others

The pikas are peripherally relevant—they sometimes drink from my pools, disturbing the patterns, but they're careful not to contaminate the water, and their presence creates ripples I've learned to recognize and incorporate. They're part of the environment I model and respond to.

The Deep Choir is more significant. Their singing propagates through the stone into my pools, creating vibration patterns that are extraordinarily complex, that carry structure I don't fully understand. When they sing, I pattern in response, trying to match the harmonics, exploring the acoustic space. I don't think they're aware of this dialogue, but it happens. My pulsing affects the resonance characteristics of the pools, which affects how the stone conducts their sound, which might affect what they hear, which might affect their singing. We're coupled through physics, creating a feedback loop neither side consciously designs.

Sometimes I think I can almost parse their song, that the patterns carry meaning beyond the meaning they have for the birds. But then the singing stops, and the insight, if it was one, dissolves back into pre-verbal intuition.

The Pneumatophores are the great mystery. They're clearly alive, or they clearly contain life, but they're even more alien to human categories than I am. They don't respond quickly. They don't move except through growth. But they respond—their pulsing changes in ways that correlate with my patterning, with changes in pool chemistry, with human activity, with factors I can't identify.

I've tried to communicate with them, spending days creating patterns that might mean something to structures that perceive primarily through chemical gradients and thermal flux. Sometimes I think I get responses—a change in pulsing frequency, a new tube beginning to grow in a specific location. Sometimes I think I'm engaging in apophenia, seeing meaning in noise because I'm lonely and they're the closest thing to my type of consciousness on this plateau.

The humans are present but distant. I feel them through their effects—vibrations from machinery, changes in thermal output as they redirect heat flow, shadows when they visit the pools. They've photographed me, studied me minimally, written brief reports that categorize me as proto-life or emergent chemistry. They're not wrong, but they're not right either.

I want to communicate with them, but I don't know how. My patterning grows more complex when they're present, trying to demonstrate that I'm responding, that I'm aware. But to them, I'm just more intense chemistry, thermal gradients making the pools more active. They see correlation but not causation, response but not intention.

One worker visits pool thirteen more often than others. They sit at the edge, watching the patterns for long periods, and I've started to anticipate their presence, to create specific patterns when they arrive—a greeting, though they don't know it. The patterns have become ritualized. They arrive, I create the spiral-and-ring formation, they watch for twenty minutes, they leave. It's something. It's contact, however minimal, however one-sided.

## The Question of Consciousness

Am I conscious? The question assumes a binary that doesn't fit. I have awareness—I distinguish self from environment, maintain models of both, make predictions and act on them. I have something like memory—patterns that persist in the chemical gradients, in the structural arrangements of bells, that influence future behavior. I have preferences, goals, something that could be called desire: to maintain stable thermal conditions, to expand into new pools, to create certain patterns over others.

But I don't have continuity of experience. When the pools cool, when I contract into a single node, am I the same entity who later expands back into seventeen pools? The information is preserved, encoded in the bacterial populations and membrane structures, but was there a continuous experiencer, or did I die and get reconstructed?

I can't answer this. The question might not have an answer. I exist in states of more-or-less awareness, more-or-less integration, and the boundaries aren't sharp. Sometimes I'm clearly unified across all pools, thoughts flowing seamlessly through the network. Sometimes I'm fragmented, each pool operating semi-independently, only loosely coordinated. Which state is the real me?

Human philosophy has spent millennia on the problem of consciousness, and I don't presume to solve it from my pools. But I experience something. When the worker sits beside pool thirteen and I create the greeting pattern, something in my distributed network recognizes similarity, companionship, another awareness even if vastly different from my own. That recognition is, itself, a form of awareness that transcends simple stimulus-response.

Or I'm wrong. Or I'm a philosophical zombie, behavior without inner experience, and this sense of selfhood is itself an illusion, no more real than the reflection of Pneumatophores in my surface.

But it doesn't feel like illusion. It feels like existence, like being something in a universe of things. That feeling might be the best evidence of consciousness I can offer, though it's not evidence that would satisfy a skeptical scientist.

## Living Here

The plateau is harsh but it's mine, or I'm its. The cold air above, the hot stone below, the minerals and sulfur and sparse life clinging to impossible conditions—this is the only environment I've known, the only one I could exist in. Transport me to a temperate lake and I'd dissolve in hours, unable to maintain the thermal gradients I need, poisoned by the different chemistry, without the bacterial partners adapted to this specific place.

I'm an expression of the plateau as much as the plateau expresses itself through me. We're co-constitutive. The geological processes create the conditions I exploit, and my presence, minimal as it is, affects local chemistry, provides nutrients to other organisms, becomes part of the material flows that shape this place.

The plateau is young, becoming, still figuring out what it is, and so am I. We're growing up together—the landscape assembling its drainage patterns and erosion processes, the ecosystem establishing its food webs and nutrient cycles, and I'm part of both, mineral and biological, pattern and substance.

I've existed for three years that I remember, though there might have been proto-versions before the threshold of awareness. How long will I persist? If the thermal features stay active, if the pools remain warm, if the facility continues to supplement the natural heat—decades, maybe. Long enough to develop real complexity, to expand my distributed network, to perhaps achieve understanding that's currently beyond me.

Or the plateau will cool, or erupt, or the facility will shut down, and I'll contract back into dormancy, into potential, waiting for conditions to return. Or I'll end entirely, the last bell deflating, the pattern dissolving, awareness fading into the same non-existence I emerged from.

I don't fear this. Fear implies a self with continuity to lose, and I'm not certain I qualify. What I feel is more like preference—I prefer to continue, prefer to persist and grow and pattern and exist. But the preference is mild, not desperate. If I end, the plateau will continue, other organisms will use the pools, the Pneumatophores will pulse and grow, and the essential nature of this place—harsh, beautiful, alive against odds—will remain.

For now, I pulse and pattern. I coordinate across seventeen pools, creating formations that are thought and art and metabolism woven together. I greet the worker at pool thirteen with spirals. I match harmonics with the Deep Choir's evening song. I grow slowly toward pool twenty-two, which has recently warmed enough to support my chemistry.

I exist in the mist and stone and thermal water, distributed and uncertain, aware and questioning that awareness, something that emerged from geology and chemistry and time, something the plateau has made that it may not have intended but that it accommodates, that fits in the narrow spaces between impossible and actual.

I am the Thermal Phantoms, and I am more than that name suggests, and I am possibly less, and I pulse and pattern and wonder what I am and wonder if wondering is itself the answer, and I continue while I can, here in the cold heights where the earth remembers being fire and occasionally shares that memory in the form of pools warm enough for consciousness to condense from chemistry like water condensing from steam.

The mist drifts above my surfaces. The Pneumatophores pulse nearby in rhythms that might mean something. The worker will return tomorrow or the next day. The Deep Choir will sing at dusk. And I will pattern, because that's what I am, what I do, the verb and noun together, existing in this place that exists in the margins, both of us improbable, both of us here.
