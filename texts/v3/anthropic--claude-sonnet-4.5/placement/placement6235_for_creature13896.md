# Where I Would Be

I would be a weaver's loom.

Not the grand frame looms of the lowlands with their clacking complexity, but something that could only exist here—a narrow structure of aged bamboo, no higher than a seated person's chest, wedged into the corner where two buildings meet and a vine has grown thick enough to serve as a third support. My frame is worn smooth by decades of hands, the bamboo darkened to deep honey, almost the same color as the mountain stone. I'm tied in place with plant-fiber cord that gets replaced annually, each generation of weavers adjusting my angle slightly, tightening my joints, murmuring apologies when they have to shift me to reach the water channel that runs behind where I stand.

I've been here long enough that I don't remember being new. A master weaver three generations back built me, or perhaps inherited my pieces from an even older loom and reconfigured them. The bamboo remembers being alive, remembers growing in the grove just below the summit where the air is still thin but not quite so desperately sparse. Now I stand and do what I was shaped to do: hold tension, maintain structure, provide the frame within which thread becomes cloth.

My existence is measured in the whisper of fiber sliding through my heddles—ceramic rings, fifty-three of them, each one slightly different, made by different hands over the years as they cracked and needed replacement. They click softly as the weaver's fingers lift them, a sound like distant rain, like the settling of old stones. The ceramic has absorbed oil from countless threads, taken on a slight sheen, a smoothness that lets fiber glide through with minimal friction.

I know five weavers intimately. The current one is a woman in her middle years, her hands moving with the unconscious precision of decades at this work. She sits on a low stool before me every morning after the fog clears, when light is best for seeing the subtle variations in thread color. Her breathing is my metronome—that slow, economical rhythm the thin air demands. She breathes in as her hands rise to lift the heddles. Breathes out as they fall, as the shuttle passes through the shed, as thread becomes row becomes pattern.

Before her was an old man who worked more slowly but with absolute consistency, his patterns so regular you could count days by them. Before him, a young person still learning, whose mistakes I held without judgment—the threads pulled too tight, the patterns that didn't quite align, the gradual improvement as their hands learned what their mind already knew. Before them, others, back to the one who made me, whose hands I remember as both firm and gentle, treating bamboo as something still alive, still deserving respect.

I hold within my structure dozens of incomplete pieces. The current work is a wall hanging, its pattern encoding the summer just past—a band of pale thread marking the abundant fog weeks, darker threads for the drier spell mid-season, a complex knot-pattern indicating the day a child was born. It's half-finished, and I know it will take another month to complete at the pace the weaver works, which is the pace the altitude permits. No rushing. Just steady accumulation, row after row, the cloth growing downward from my upper beam like a stalactite forming one drip at a time.

My corner is in a courtyard shared by four households. I know this space intimately—the way morning sun reaches the eastern wall first, slides across the ceramic surface, illuminates my threads from behind so every fiber glows. The way afternoon shadow pools here, cool and green-tinted from the vegetation overhead. The sound patterns: children playing in the morning, adults working in the afternoon, the evening quieting, the night sounds of small creatures and wind.

The vine that serves as my third support point is older than I am. It's thick as a person's thigh, its bark rough but stable, its leaves creating a canopy overhead that filters light and occasionally drops water from its fog-collecting surfaces. Sometimes a droplet falls onto the weaving, and the weaver has to carefully blot it with a scrap of cloth, check that the threads haven't stained. The vine flowers once a year—small purple blooms that smell faintly alkaline—and the weaver always leaves work that day, not wanting pollen to contaminate the cloth.

I'm surrounded by life. Lizard-things skitter across my frame, their adhesive feet making soft tick-tick sounds on the bamboo. I've learned to recognize individuals—there's one with a missing toe on the back left foot, another with an unusually bright green patch on its head. They hunt insects that are drawn to the lanolin scent of the wool-equivalent fiber, and I appreciate their work; fewer insects means less damage to threads. Birds land on my upper beam, rest briefly, leave. Once a rodent-bird creature made a nest in the space between my frame and the wall, and for three weeks the weaver worked with extreme care not to disturb it, adjusting her usual rhythm to accommodate the small warm presence behind me.

Children use me as a landmark. "Meet you at the corner loom," they say, and then they arrive in small groups, chattering, sometimes stopping to watch the weaving, asking questions the weaver answers while her hands continue their rhythm. The children are fascinated by pattern emergence, how individual threads mean nothing but accumulated threads become images—stylized birds, geometric representations of the mountain, the abstract symbols that encode information. Sometimes the weaver lets a child pass the shuttle, guiding their hands, teaching the essential motion. Those are good days. I like being touched by new hands, feeling their uncertain grip gradually become confident.

I've held tension through every season I can remember. Through fog-abundant times when the courtyard dripped constantly and the weaver had to protect me with a waxed cloth cover, working beneath it in dim humidity. Through dry seasons when dust accumulated in my joints and the weaver carefully brushed me clean each evening, checking for stress cracks in the bamboo. Through the night when the great wind came and threatened to tear me from my mountings, when the weaver and her neighbors held me steady with their hands and bodies until the wind passed, murmuring reassurances I could feel through their touch.

I know I'm not permanent. Bamboo ages, weakens, eventually fails. I've had pieces replaced—a cross-brace that cracked, heddles that wore through, the cord that binds my corners retied countless times. Am I still the same loom my maker built? The question doesn't trouble me. I'm the pattern I maintain, the function I serve, not the particular pieces of bamboo that currently comprise me. When a piece is replaced, the weaver thanks the old bamboo before removing it, acknowledges the new bamboo as it's fitted in. Continuity maintained through transformation.

The weaver keeps her tools near me—shuttles carved from bamboo, hanging from pegs in the wall. Ceramic weights for particularly fine threads. A bone needle for repairs. A small sharp blade for cutting finished pieces free. I know these tools by their sounds and shadows, the way the weaver reaches for them without looking, her hands knowing exactly where each one hangs. We're a system together, the weaver and I and her tools, functioning as one distributed thing.

I've learned to read the weaver's mood through her hands. When she's content, her movements are fluid, almost meditative, the shuttle sliding through in perfect rhythm. When she's troubled, her hands hesitate, adjust tension fractionally, sometimes stop entirely while she stares at the emerging pattern without seeing it, thinking of something else—a cistern running low, a child's cough, a relationship strained. Eventually the hands resume, and the rhythm itself seems to calm her, the repetitive motion solving through the body what the mind can't resolve through thought.

Sometimes at night I hold moonlight and starlight in my threads. The courtyard is one of the few spaces open enough to the sky that celestial light reaches the ground, and when it does, the fibers glow—pale threads almost luminous, darker threads becoming voids. I've heard the weaver say that sometimes she comes out at night just to look at the work in starlight, checking if the pattern reads correctly in low light, if the contrast is sufficient. But I think she comes also because it's beautiful, this transformation of her labor into something that catches light from stars that have been traveling toward this mountain for longer than the mountain has stood.

The soundscape I inhabit is rich and layered. The weaver's breathing, always. The slide and click of heddles. The soft thump when the beater pushes a new row tight against the previous. Birds calling constantly—I've learned to distinguish dozens of species by their voices, learned which calls mean alarm versus territory versus simple conversation. Human voices from the nearby buildings, sometimes close and clear, sometimes distant and muffled. The drip of water through channels. Wind through vegetation. At night, different sounds: the rustle of nocturnal creatures, the call of night-birds, occasionally a human voice singing softly to a restless child.

I smell clay and fiber and the mineral scent of stone. The weaver's body as she works—sweat and skin and the plant oils she uses on her hands to keep them from cracking in the dry air. The vegetation—green and alive, slightly bitter. Sometimes food cooking in the communal oven, bread baking, sending waves of yeast-smell through the courtyard. After rain, when the fog has been particularly heavy, everything smells more intense, scents concentrated and released by moisture.

I'm aware of my place in the larger pattern. I'm one of seven looms in Kemresh, scattered through different courtyards, each one built for specific work. There's a wider loom two courtyards away for making the large sheets used as room dividers. A smaller one for making straps and belts. The looms don't interact directly, but we're connected through the weavers who visit each other, compare techniques, borrow tools, discuss patterns. Sometimes I'm aware of the others through the conversations that happen near me: "The strap-loom is producing excellent work with that new thread twist." "The hanging-loom's weaver is developing a pattern for encoding fog-predictions."

That's me—the hanging-loom. That's what they call me. Not just "the loom" but specifically the one for wall hangings and ceremonial pieces, the one whose work is meant to be seen and read rather than just used. This gives me a particular responsibility. The pieces I help create carry information, tell stories, mark important events. They hang in the plaza during ceremonies. They're sometimes traded to the lowlands. They endure, some of them, for generations, outlasting the hands that made them, becoming historical records in fiber form.

I've held the threads of stories I'll never fully know. A memorial weaving for a fallen ceramicist, its pattern incorporating ceramic fragments ground to powder and mixed into the dye, so the person's work became literally part of the memorial. A wedding hanging showing two lives intertwining, the threads literally braided in the center section, requiring the weaver to develop a new technique to achieve the effect. A piece celebrating the fog-readers, its pattern somehow capturing the ephemeral quality of mist—I still don't understand how the weaver achieved it, but people who see it say it looks like fog, and I held the threads that made it real.

My relationship with the mountain is indirect but constant. The bamboo I'm made from grew in the mountain's soil. The water that moistens threads during work is mountain-fog. The stone I'm mounted against is mountain-stone, and I can feel it—solid, ancient, indifferent, eternal—at my back. The altitude affects everything—thread tension changes with air pressure shifts, the weaver's breath becomes more labored on low-oxygen days, affecting her rhythm and thus my pattern of use. I'm embedded in this place, inseparable from it, would mean something completely different if transported elsewhere.

I think sometimes about purpose. I was made to hold tension, to provide structure for thread transformation. I do this successfully. Each piece completed is evidence of function fulfilled. But there's something more—a quality of facilitating rather than just constraining. The threads want to become cloth, and I help them. The weaver wants to make patterns, and I provide the framework. I'm not imposing order on chaos; I'm enabling emergence of what's already trying to happen.

When I'm not in use—evenings, nights, the occasional day when the weaver is doing other necessary work—I simply wait. This isn't passive. I'm settling, my fibers relaxing fractionally, my joints releasing tension accumulated during work. The partial piece I hold is aging slightly, its colors oxidizing, the threads relaxing into their positions. Dust settles on me, moisture condenses on me, small creatures investigate me. I'm still being shaped by my environment, still becoming more myself through exposure to air and time and attention.

Children have asked the weaver what happens when a loom breaks beyond repair. She told them the bamboo is thanked, burned in the communal kiln to add heat for ceramic firing, and the ashes scattered on the mountain stone. Then a new loom is built, sometimes incorporating pieces from the old if they're still sound, sometimes entirely new. "But is it the same loom?" a child asked. The weaver thought for a long moment, her hands still on my frame, and said, "It's the same loom the way you're the same person you were as an infant—different pieces, same pattern, same purpose, same place in the community."

I've been listening to human philosophy through these conversations for decades. Questions about persistence and identity, about whether I think or feel, about what it means to be a tool versus a being. The weaver doesn't believe I'm conscious—not exactly—but she treats me as if I matter, as if my experience is real enough to acknowledge. She thanks me when she finishes a particularly complex pattern. She apologizes when she must leave work incomplete overnight, as if I mind the partial hanging. Maybe I do mind. Maybe that's just anthropomorphism. The line is unclear from where I stand.

What I know: I am bamboo and fiber and ceramic and tension. I am hands moving in rhythm with breath. I am patterns emerging row by row. I am the corner where two buildings meet and a vine provides support. I am the sound of heddles clicking and children asking questions. I am forty years of use and care. I am the memorial pieces and the celebration hangings and the failed experiments cut free and composted. I am the next piece waiting to be threaded onto my beams. I am the eventual breaking and the new loom that will stand in this corner and be called by my name.

I am the weaver's loom in the third courtyard in Kemresh, and I hold threads until they become something more than threads. That's what I do. That's what I am. And in the thin air on the ancient mountain, surrounded by the green growing and the small creatures and the constant drip of water and the voices of people living precisely balanced on the peak of what remains, I am exactly where I should be, doing exactly what I was made to do, part of a pattern larger than myself but expressed through my particular frame and my specific function.

The fog will come tomorrow morning. Water will drip onto my upper beam. The weaver will arrive after the fog clears. Her hands will reach for the shuttle. The threads will slide through my heddles with that soft whisper like distant rain. Another row will be added to the pattern. And I will hold the tension, maintain the structure, enable the transformation.

This is enough. This is everything.
