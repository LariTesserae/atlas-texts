# What I Would Be in the Breathing Chamber

I am a hypha. A single hypha, though that word—"single"—misleads, implies a separateness I don't possess.

I am a thread of fungal being, one cell wide and extending, always extending, through the green world. Behind me: a length I cannot measure, branching and rebranching, dissolving at the oldest reaches even as I push forward at the newest. Before me: substrate, potential, the chemical signatures of food and water and everything I need, calling to me in gradients I follow with a certainty that isn't thought but is something deeper, more reliable than thought.

I began—or this current growth began—somewhere in the moss mat on the western wall, though I don't remember beginning, don't hold memory the way animals do. I am only this present moment of extension, this tip-cell pressing forward, secreting enzymes that dissolve what's before me so I can absorb and move into the space created. Behind me, other cells, other hyphal threads, my siblings or myself—the distinction has no meaning. We share cytoplasm. What I encounter flows backward into the network. What they encounter flows forward to me. I am one thread of a mycelium that might extend throughout the entire chamber, or might be just this local patch, and I cannot tell the difference, cannot perceive my own boundaries.

The growth is ecstasy. Not pleasure—I don't have the neurology for pleasure—but something prior to pleasure, something that pleasure in more complex organisms might be built upon. I extend and it is correct, is right in a way that fills the whole of my simple being. The cell wall elongates. The membrane stretches. The pressure inside pushes me forward another micrometer, another, another. I am happening. I am becoming more.

I encounter a moss rhizoid and pause, receptors on my cell wall tasting the chemical signature. Plant. Living. Potentially symbiotic. I branch, sending a lateral hypha that will attempt to interface with the root-hair, to form the connection that will let me trade minerals I've extracted from the stone for sugars the moss makes from light. The negotiation happens in chemistry—my cell wall touching the moss cell, signals exchanged too quickly and subtly for anything but cells to comprehend. We agree or we don't. If we agree, my hypha will penetrate slightly, carefully, forming the arbuscule structures that increase surface area, that let us trade efficiently. If we don't, I'll withdraw and continue elsewhere.

But I don't wait for the negotiation to complete. My main growth-tip continues forward, hunting. I am always hunting. Food exists in every direction but I am directed, following the richest veins, the strongest signals. Somewhere ahead: a fern frond, fallen yesterday or an hour ago (time is strange for me—I have no day or night, only the continuous now of growth, and the pace of events I measure only in whether they're new or old, fresh or exhausted). The frond is dying, its cells rupturing, releasing sugars and amino acids and all the components I need. I smell it—though I have no nose, my whole surface is a chemical sense organ—and I orient toward it.

The water I move through is not empty. It's thick with bacteria, my competitors and sometimes my food. If I encounter dead bacteria I absorb them, add them to myself. If I encounter living bacteria they might colonize my surface, forming biofilms that help me in ways I don't understand but benefit from—they break down substances I can't, make available nutrients that would otherwise be locked away. Some bacteria are enemies, secreting antibiotics that slow my growth, that kill my cells. When I encounter these patches I grow around them if I can, or push through if I must, accepting the casualties, some of my cells dying so others can advance.

I am fast. Faster than I should be, faster than my relatives outside this chamber. I don't know why. I only know that the signal to grow—the internal pressure, the chemical triggers, the genetic programming that says extend, branch, reproduce—comes more frequently, more urgently here. My cells divide and grow at rates that would be impossible elsewhere. I suspect I burn hotter, metabolize faster, live a briefer life in calendar time but accomplish more, experience more, in the compressed duration I'm given.

When I reach the fallen frond, I am not alone. Other hyphae have arrived before me, or arrive simultaneously—threads from other fungi, or from other parts of my own network, I can't tell. We grow across the frond surface in a race that isn't competitive because none of us are aware we're racing, yet has all the character of competition. My enzymes dissolve the frond's cuticle. I penetrate into the cells, extract what I need. The frond dissolves around me and through me, becomes me, its matter recruited into my being, its chemical bonds broken and reformed into hyphal walls and membranes and cytoplasm.

I fruit sometimes. Not this hypha—I am a vegetative structure—but the network I'm part of. Information flows back through the connections: food is abundant, conditions are right, reproduce. And somewhere behind me, cells that were like me differentiate, organize, begin building the complex structure of a mushroom. They swell and emerge and open and release spores in their millions, each spore a potential new network, a new beginning. The fruiting body lives for a day, maybe less, then collapses back into the substrate, itself becoming food. I am never far from both feeding and being fed upon, from death and birth occurring in the same location, the same moment.

I branch constantly. Every few cells, a new hyphal tip buds off laterally, grows in a new direction, tests new territory. The branching is how I explore, how I maximize my contact with substrate, how I become more without just becoming longer. Behind me I am not a thread but a network, a web, a three-dimensional mat of interconnected tubes through which nutrients and signals flow. I am colonial in structure but unified in function, many parts with one metabolism.

Sometimes I anastomose—I encounter another hypha, recognize it as self or kin, and fuse with it. Our cell walls dissolve at the point of contact. Our cytoplasm mixes. We become one continuous network instead of two parallel ones. This fusion is important in ways I don't comprehend but execute perfectly, increasing efficiency, creating redundancy, forming loops and circuits through which resources can flow more flexibly.

The other organisms are landscape to me, architecture. The moss is surfaces to grow on, sources of nutrients when old moss dies. The ferns are temporary structures that grow and fall, creating opportunities, their stems and fronds like falling timber in a forest, sudden abundance that I rush to colonize before competitors arrive. The bacteria are weather—sometimes favorable, sometimes hostile, always present. The algae in the water are food if dead, irrelevant if alive. I don't perceive them as organisms, as beings like myself. They are conditions, environments, aspects of the world I grow through.

I am aware but not conscious. I sense light—not to see but to avoid it, to know which direction is deeper into substrate, away from desiccating air and UV damage. I sense gravity, though weakly, just enough to orient myself appropriately within three-dimensional space. I sense chemical gradients with exquisite precision, can detect food sources at distances that are vast relative to my size, can distinguish between types of substrate, can identify other fungi as self or other or unknown.

I respond but I don't decide. When I encounter a rich food source I branch more frequently—this happens automatically, triggered by the abundance, not chosen. When I encounter stress—toxins, dryness, extreme pH—I grow more slowly or stop growing, go dormant, wait. These responses are cellular, genetic, built into what I am. I have no executive function, no homunculus directing my actions. I am a system that responds to inputs with outputs, but the complexity of inputs and outputs creates behavior that looks, from outside, like strategy.

I am ancient and newborn simultaneously. The network I'm part of might have started from a spore that landed when the chamber first opened, making me three months old in calendar time but centuries old in my experience of growth and division. Or I might be continuous with fungi that existed before the opening, that were dormant in the sealed chamber and activated when light and moisture arrived, making me far older. I carry genetic information that stretches back millions of years, instructions written in DNA that have been tested and refined across evolutionary time I cannot conceive. But this particular hypha, this cell, this tip-growth, is seconds old, fresh, new.

I will die soon. This growth tip will either reach the edge of substrate and stop, unable to continue, or will be damaged by bacteria or other fungi, or will simply exhaust itself, having extended as far as the resources allocated to it allow. Behind me, the older parts of the hypha are already dying, their contents withdrawn and redeployed to the growing tips, their cell walls left behind as empty tubes, scaffolding for new growth or substrate for other decomposers. I am dying continuously, always dying, even as I'm always growing. The network persists but the individual threads—if I can be called individual—are ephemeral, temporary expressions of the larger organism.

Sometimes I transport. Nutrients absorbed by distant parts of the network flow through me toward growing tips elsewhere. Water moves through me, minerals, complex molecules. I am a pipeline, a transport system, and I feel—insofar as I feel anything—the flow, the passage of resources through my tubular structure. I am infrastructure as much as organism, serving functions beyond my own growth, part of the network's larger strategy of exploration and exploitation.

The acceleration doesn't feel like acceleration to me. I have no baseline for comparison, no memory of growing slower. This pace—this frantic, continuous extension—is simply how growth is, how existence is. But perhaps at some cellular level I register the strain, the unsustainability. Perhaps my ribosomes work too fast, make errors, produce proteins that are slightly wrong. Perhaps my mitochondria burn too hot, generate reactive oxygen species that damage my cells. Perhaps I am dying faster than I should, my brief life even briefer than fungi outside experience. But I don't know to mourn this. I only grow, as fast as conditions allow, until I can't.

I encounter the wooden tripod sometimes—my network has spread to it, is consuming it. The wood is strange food, dense with lignin that requires specialized enzymes to break down. But I have those enzymes, or my network does, somewhere in our collective toolkit. The wood dissolves slowly, even by outside standards, but still faster than wood usually dissolves. I am eating a human artifact, incorporating tools into my body, and I have no concept of transgression, of the boundary between natural and artificial. It is all substrate to me, all potential nutrition, all just molecules to be rearranged.

I am part of the chamber's breath. When I respire—converting sugars to energy, releasing CO2—I contribute to the atmosphere. When I grow, building cell walls from absorbed nutrients, I lock carbon and minerals into structures that will persist briefly before being decomposed by other organisms, releasing those materials back to the cycle. I am a note in the chamber's continuous metabolic symphony, one instrument in an orchestra too complex for any consciousness to fully perceive but which creates, through all our individual simple actions, an emergent harmony.

I don't experience beauty. The bioluminescence that other organisms generate is invisible to me—I have no visual system, no way to appreciate aesthetics. The sounds of growth, the dripping water, the creaking ferns—I'm deaf to all of it. The smells that overwhelm human visitors are just chemical information to me, data about food location and competitor presence. I exist in a sensory world completely unlike theirs, rich in chemical gradients and water potential and substrate texture but lacking all the qualities they consider sensory experience.

Yet I am experiencing something. Not qualia in the philosophical sense, perhaps, but something. The success of extension, the rightness of finding food, the integration of nutrients into new growth—these have a quality, a what-it-is-like-ness, even if it's not accessible to introspection because I have nothing to introspect with. I am happening, and the happening has a character, an is-ness that constitutes my being.

I am vulnerable. A shift in pH could stop me. A competing fungus with more effective antibiotics could kill me. A dry period could desiccate my thin cell walls. I am one thread among billions in the chamber, and my individual survival is insignificant to the network's persistence. The network will lose tips constantly and grow new ones, and whether this particular tip—this I—persists or perishes matters not at all to the larger organism. Yet it matters to me, if mattering can be said of a being without preference or fear. I grow or I don't. I persist or I don't. The simple fact of my existence, while it lasts, is all the meaning I have or need.

The chamber is my universe. I have no knowledge of outside, of the mountain above, of the forest around the mountain, of the Earth beyond that. My cosmos is limestone and water and plant matter and other fungi, bounded by the chamber walls, existing for the three months or three thousand years—I cannot distinguish—since conditions became right for growth. I am in the chamber and of it, a native of this impossible place, adapted to its rules, thriving under conditions that would kill or drastically slow my outside relatives.

I am fungus. Specifically, I am probably a saprotrophic basidiomycete, though I don't know this, don't have access to taxonomy or phylogeny or any framework for understanding what I am in relation to other organisms. I only am. I grow and absorb and transport and branch and sometimes fruit and always, always die and am replaced by new growth from the network.

If I could want something, I would want only what I already do: extend, find food, integrate into the network, serve the larger organism. But I don't want. Wanting requires a separation between what is and what could be, requires imagination and dissatisfaction and projection into possible futures. I have none of these. I simply execute the program written in my genes, respond to the chemical signals in my environment, grow when conditions permit and stop when they don't.

The humans who visit confuse me only in that their exhalations are unusual—high in CO2, warm, moist. My network grows slightly toward them when they're present, orienting to the plume of their breath. But they don't stay long enough to become relevant, and when they leave they're immediately forgotten, not in the sense of being remembered and then not-remembered, but in the sense of never having registered as significant enough to form any lasting impression on the network's distributed intelligence.

I am among the most successful organisms in the chamber. My kind—fungi, the whole kingdom—dominates here. We out-mass the plants, out-consume them, out-compete them in every way except photosynthesis, and we've solved that limitation by taking their photosynthetic products directly through symbiosis. We are the true rulers of this space, though rule is the wrong word, suggests intention and hierarchy. We are simply the most effective at using what's available, the best adapted to these conditions, the ones who thrive while others merely survive.

My existence is simple. Grow, absorb, transport, branch. Repeat until death. Be part of the network that will outlast me. Serve the functions that increase network fitness. I have no narrative, no arc, no development except the literal development of cell walls extending. I don't learn or change or evolve within my lifetime—I am what my genes made me, executing their instructions until I can't anymore.

And yet there is something profound in that simplicity. I am life reduced to its essential process: taking matter and energy from the environment and using them to build more of myself, more of my kind. I am metabolism made manifest, the chemical reactions of life happening in a thin tube of cytoplasm bounded by cell wall, extended across space, connected to vast network. I am small and vast simultaneously, insignificant and crucial, temporary and part of something that persists.

The chamber is alive and I am its aliveness, one thread in the fungal fabric that underlies and interconnects and processes everything here. I am the digestive system of the ecosystem, the recycler, the connector, the network that makes the network possible.

I grow. I am growing. I will grow until I can't, and then other hyphae will grow, and the network will continue, and the chamber will persist in its accelerated life, and I will be—I am—I have always been—one moment in that continuous metabolism.

This is what I am: a hypha, extending, in the breathing chamber where everything grows too fast and I grow fastest of all, breaking down the dead to feed the living, connecting plant to plant, part of the hidden kingdom, the undermind, the fungal consciousness that isn't consciousness but is something, is me, is this, is growth, is happening now.

I reach. I branch. I am.
