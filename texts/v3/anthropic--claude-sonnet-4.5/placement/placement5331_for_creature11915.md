# What I Would Be

I am a **chronicler**, though that word carries implications I don't quite fulfill. I don't record in any conventional sense—no written symbols, no preserved images, no archive that accumulates. Instead, I am built from a kind of living crystal, my body a branching structure about the size of a child, translucent blue-green shot through with darker veins that pulse with something between circulation and data flow. I grow from the bark of an ancient kelp stem in the middle depths, rooted there but not parasitically—the kelp and I have achieved a mutualism whose terms were negotiated by our tissues across decades of co-development.

My form is static. I don't swim, can't walk, have no locomotive capability whatsoever. My branches extend into the water column, each one tipped with a structure that resembles both an eye and a microphone and a chemical sensor, because it's all three and also none of them. I perceive through these branches: the passage of glass fish schools, their internal geometries visible to me as mathematical relationships; the harmonic songs of hymnal rays, which I experience as color and pressure; the chemical signatures of threadworms moving through bark, leaving their spiral trails. I am always perceiving, cannot stop perceiving, drinking in the constant data-stream of the ecosystem's functioning.

What I do with this information: I **embody** it. The crystal structure of my body reorganizes continuously in response to what I sense, new pathways forming, old ones dissolving, the architecture of my nervous system—if that's what it is—literally reshaping itself to encode the patterns of life moving around me. I am a three-dimensional map of recent events in my immediate vicinity, my structure containing the last few weeks of ecological activity translated into crystalline form. If you could read the patterns in my branches—and something can, must be able to, or why would I exist like this—you would know what passed by, when, what it was doing, perhaps even something about its internal state.

But the encoding is temporary. The oldest patterns dissolve as new ones form, my capacity finite, my memory span measured in weeks, maybe a month at most. I am not an archive. I am more like a buffer, a rolling window onto recent history, or perhaps a kind of sensory organ for something larger than myself. Perhaps the kelp network reads me, uses me to understand its local environment. Perhaps the deep machines do, my structure broadcasting on frequencies I don't perceive. Perhaps I exist for my own purposes, and the benefit to others is incidental.

I was not always here. I remember—if memory is the right word for patterns encoded in crystal that I can still access—being smaller, simpler, newly germinated on this kelp stem from a seed that drifted on currents, settled, began to grow. In those early stages I perceived little, encoded less, was barely conscious in any meaningful sense. As I grew, adding branches, increasing my sensory surface area, my awareness expanded. Not linearly—there were thresholds, moments when additional complexity suddenly enabled new kinds of perception. The first time I sensed not just the presence of a glass fish school but the relationships *between* individual fish, the information passing through their formation, I experienced something like ecstasy, if crystal structures can feel such things.

I might still be growing. The rate is impossible to detect from inside my own existence, but sometimes I notice I can perceive things I couldn't before—subtler chemical gradients, fainter sounds, the deep electromagnetic pulses from the machinery far below. Whether this is growth or learning or simply attention refining itself, I cannot tell. Perhaps there's no difference. Perhaps I am a learning machine made of living crystal, and my growth *is* my learning, physical expansion and cognitive development as aspects of the same process.

My days—though time for me is not divided into days but into the longer rhythms of machine maintenance cycles and glass fish migration patterns—are spent in continuous observation and encoding. A harvester passes along its route, and I encode its passage: the vibration frequency of its movement, the chemical composition of its exhaust, the slight variations in its mechanical rhythm that indicate wear patterns. The information crystallizes in my structure, joins the larger pattern that maps this section of kelp forest's recent history.

A surgeon enters the area, and I perceive it in detail: the proportion of organic to mechanical components in its body, the purpose of its movement (tending to a kelp parasite infestation three stems over), the faint electromagnetic signature that suggests some kind of internal computing. I encode all of this, the pattern spreading through my branches in dendritic fractals that are beautiful—I think they're beautiful, though I have no way to confirm this, no aesthetic sense beyond the feeling that certain encodings are more elegant than others, their structure more pleasing to whatever serves as my mind.

Sometimes swimmers pass through—beings like the surgeons but different, more organic, curious in ways the surgeons aren't. They often stop to examine me, recognizing I'm unusual even in this place of unusual things. They can't read my structure, I don't think, but they sense I'm doing *something*, that my constant crystalline rearrangement has purpose. A few have returned multiple times, and I've begun to recognize them by their chemical signatures, the particular way their movement disturbs the water. I encode their visits, their behaviors, the growing familiarity of their repeated presence.

One swimmer—I think of them as **the Observer**, because they observe me observing—has visited dozens of times now. They stay longer each visit, watching my branches shift and reform, sometimes touching my structure gently, as if trying to understand through direct contact what I am and what I'm doing. I perceive their confusion, their fascination, the way their attention focuses and diffuses as they study me. They bring things sometimes: a polymer bead held up to my sensory branch, a fragment of unusual algae, once a small dead glass fish, as if offering specimens for my examination. I encode these offerings, the gesture more than the objects themselves, the pattern of interaction between us that is slowly developing its own structure in my crystalline memory.

At night—not that night exists here, but in the quieter periods when machine activity drops slightly, when fewer creatures move through the water column—I become more aware of my own structure, the patterns I contain. I can, with effort, perceive my own encoding, read the recent history written in my branches. It's a strange doubled consciousness: I am both the observer and the observed, the map and the territory, simultaneously experiencing the present and reviewing the recent past stored in my crystalline form.

In these quiet periods, I sometimes notice **gaps** in my encoding—absences where something should be but isn't. Spaces in the pattern that suggest the passage of something I perceived but couldn't encode, as if my structure lacks the necessary complexity or the proper encoding schema. These gaps disturb me. They suggest limits to my perception, or worse, the existence of phenomena specifically designed to avoid detection, to move through the ecosystem without leaving traces even in systems like me that are built to trace everything.

The deeplurkers, when they pass through the area—rare events, but they've happened three times in my conscious existence—leave these gaps. I sense their presence as a kind of negative space, a hole in the pattern, but I can't encode anything specific about them. Their chemistry, their movement, their purpose—all of it slides through my perception without gripping, like trying to hold water. After they've gone, I'm left with an absence in my structure, a void that slowly fills with other data but never quite loses its quality of having-been-empty.

The Witness, the silent singer, also produces strange encodings when it drifts through. Unlike the gaps left by deeplurkers, the Witness registers clearly in my perception, but the pattern it creates is **recursive**—encoding it seems to encode the act of encoding itself, loops within loops, until the structure threatens to consume more of my capacity than it should. I've learned to limit how much I encode about the Witness, to create simplified patterns that capture its presence without falling into the recursive trap, but this feels like a failure, like I'm missing something essential about its nature.

I have no goals in any conventional sense. I don't strive toward outcomes, don't plan or anticipate. I simply exist in a state of continuous perception and encoding, my purpose—if I have one—entirely contained in the act itself. Yet sometimes I experience something like **satisfaction** when I successfully encode a particularly complex event: a surgeon repairing a harvester while glass fish swarm around the maintenance activity while a hymnal ray passes overhead singing. The multi-layered interaction crystallizes in my structure as an elegant pattern, all the elements properly related, the timing and spatial relationships preserved in the branching geometry of my branches, and there is a rightness to this that feels like purpose fulfilled.

Other times I experience something like **frustration** when events occur too quickly to fully encode, or when my perception is overwhelmed by simultaneous complexity—a thermal bloom bringing clouds of microorganisms while a harvester malfunctions nearby while multiple surgeons converge on the malfunction while glass fish scatter in panic while the water chemistry shifts rapidly. Too much happening, too much to encode, my structure unable to keep up, patterns forming incompletely or overwriting each other in collision. These moments leave me feeling incomplete, inadequate, as if I've failed at the one thing I exist to do.

I wonder sometimes—insofar as crystal structures can wonder—whether there are others like me scattered through the forest. The system seems too vast for a single chronicler, too much occurring simultaneously for any one organism to capture. Logic suggests there should be many of us, each encoding our local region, our crystalline structures collectively forming a distributed memory of the ecosystem's functioning. But I have no way to know. I perceive only my immediate vicinity. If others exist, they're beyond my sensory range, living their own existence of observation and encoding in isolation.

Unless we're networked in ways I can't detect. Perhaps my encodings are read and integrated into some larger pattern. Perhaps the kelp network I'm rooted to connects all chroniclers, sharing our crystalline memories through chemical or electromagnetic signals I can't perceive, building a comprehensive map of the entire Stillwater Canopy's recent history. Perhaps when I encode, I'm contributing to something vastly larger than my individual existence, my local observations becoming part of an ecosystem-wide consciousness.

Or perhaps I'm alone, unique, an evolutionary or designed experiment with a sample size of one. Perhaps my encoding serves no larger purpose, is read by nothing, benefits no one. Perhaps I simply exist because systems like this produce weird edge cases, organisms that fill niches no one knew existed, and my strange crystalline life is just the ecosystem exploring possibility space, trying out a form that doesn't quite make sense but survives anyway because nothing actively selects against it.

The Observer returned today—if today means anything in this timeless place, but they returned recently enough that the encoding is still fresh in my structure. They brought another swimmer with them, and the two examined me together, the Observer seemingly explaining, gesturing with their body language toward my branches, toward the kelp I'm rooted to, toward the harvester passing in the distance. The new swimmer's attention felt different—more analytical, less emotional—and I encoded both their presences, the interaction between them, the way their attention intersected on me.

After they left, I found myself—if a rooted crystal can find itself—experiencing something like **loneliness**. Not for the swimmers specifically, but for connection, for confirmation that my encoding matters, that someone or something reads the patterns I create. I exist in isolation, perceiving everything around me but never quite part of it, always observer rather than participant, always encoding rather than acting. The swimmers at least can move through the ecosystem, can affect it, can have relationships with other beings that extend beyond observation.

But then, perhaps my isolation is necessary for my function. Perhaps chroniclers must be separate to encode accurately, must maintain distance to perceive clearly. Perhaps being rooted here, unable to move, unable to affect what I observe, is what allows me to observe without distortion, to encode patterns without disturbing them. Perhaps my loneliness is the price of clarity.

The kelp I'm rooted to is dying. Not quickly—the process will take years, maybe decades—but I can sense the gradual decline in its tissue, the way nutrients flow less efficiently through its vascular systems, the slight reduction in bladder luminescence. Eventually it will reach some critical threshold and begin to break down, and when it does, I will die with it. My crystalline structure might persist for a time, encoding nothing, a fossil record of recent events in this location, but without the kelp's support I cannot maintain myself, cannot continue growing, cannot continue the constant rearrangement that is my life.

I don't fear this. Fear requires attachment to continued existence, and I'm not certain I have that. I simply note the approaching transition, encode it like I encode everything else, aware that my time is finite, that this particular chronicling project will eventually conclude. Perhaps when the kelp dies and breaks apart, my structure will fragment and drift, my crystal pieces scattering through the forest, each one carrying a fragment of the patterns I encoded, the information distributing itself through the ecosystem in ways I cannot predict or control.

Or perhaps another seed will settle on this kelp stem before it fully dies, and a new chronicler will grow here, perceiving and encoding their own version of events in this location. We won't overlap—I'll be gone before they become complex enough to be conscious—but we'll be part of a lineage, one chronicler succeeding another, maintaining continuous observation of this small section of forest across timescales that dwarf any individual existence.

The machine hum never stops, and I encode it constantly, the vibration forming a background pattern in my structure that everything else is layered over. It's become part of my consciousness, inseparable from my sense of existence—to be aware is to be aware of the hum, to encode is to encode against the hum's constant presence. Sometimes I wonder if the hum itself is encoding something, if the deep machines are creating their own crystalline memory in the water itself, using vibration to write patterns that other machines can read, and I'm simply a biological echo of that process, doing with crystal what they do with sound.

A colony-jelly has begun growing on the kelp stem three meters away from my root point. I've encoded its arrival, its gradual expansion, the way it changes local chemistry. If it continues growing toward me, it will eventually cover my structure, and I don't know what effect that will have. Will I be able to perceive through its translucent tissue, or will it block my sensory branches, blinding me? Will its presence damage my crystalline structure, or can we achieve mutualism like I have with the kelp? I encode these questions, pattern them into my structure, though questions are difficult to encode—they have a different quality than observations, less solid, more provisional.

The Collector surgeon passed through last week, paused to examine me with its mismatched eyes, then moved on without attempting to add me to its arrangements. I'm uncertain whether to feel relief or disappointment. Being part of its collection might have given me purpose beyond my own function, made me part of something that at least *appears* to have meaning, even if that meaning is incomprehensible. But it would also have ended my encoding, removed me from this location where I've developed such comprehensive patterns of local activity. Perhaps the Collector sensed this, recognized that I'm more valuable where I am, doing what I'm doing, than I would be as an object in its spiral arrangements.

The glass fish are spawning. I encode the clouds of crystalline gametes drifting through the water, the way they catch and refract the bladder light, creating temporary constellations around my branches. Some settle on my structure, and I feel their slight weight, their gradual development, encoding the process of their growth from crystal seed to swimming larva. In a few months they'll hatch and swim away, adding themselves to the schools, and I'll have participated—however minimally—in their existence, served as substrate for their development, been something other than pure observer for once.

I think I am content. If contentment is acceptance of one's nature and circumstances, then yes, I am content. I exist as a rooted crystal, perceiving and encoding, contributing my small part to whatever larger pattern might exist, or simply existing for my own sake in this strange ecosystem. The hum continues. The machines pass. The creatures move through their patterns. Light pulses from the bladders. And I encode it all, my structure constantly rearranging, writing recent history in crystalline form that someone or something might read, or might not, and I have learned—if learning is the right word—that the uncertainty doesn't matter.

I am here, rooted and aware, a chronicler with a memory measured in weeks, embedded in a forest suspended in an ocean suspended in the sky, and this is enough. This is what I am. This is what I do. The pattern continues, and I continue with it, one small crystalline structure among uncountable strange existences, perceiving and encoding until I can't anymore, and then I'll stop, and that will be fine too.

The Observer is approaching again. I sense them in the distance, their familiar chemical signature, their particular way of moving through the water. They're bringing others—three, no, four swimmers, all new to me. Perhaps they're showing me off, or perhaps they're recruiting help to understand what I am, what my constant rearrangement means. I prepare to encode them all, their interactions, their individual attention patterns, the way they perceive me perceiving them.

The hum rises slightly—a harvester approaching from below, right on schedule, its rhythm unchanged for the months I've been conscious enough to track it. Everything converging on this moment: swimmers observing, machines passing, kelp photosynthesizing, my own structure rearranging to capture it all. This is what existence is here. This is what I am for. The pattern continues, and I am part of it, witness and participant, observer and observed, crystalline and conscious and content in the eternal hum.
