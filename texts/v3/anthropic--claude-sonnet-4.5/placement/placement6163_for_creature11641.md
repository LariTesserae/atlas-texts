# What I Would Be

I am the notched-beak gull, the one with two toes on the left foot.

I don't know how many years I've held this position at the eastern edge of the first pond, where the water arrives hottest from the main building and the air ripples even in winter. I only know that this place is mine because I have held it against every challenger, because my body knows its dimensions without thought—three meters of prime concrete edge, the warmest position in the entire installation, the one that draws younger birds like a lodestone, the one I must defend daily to keep.

The foot happened early, before I learned this place. Fishing line, probably, or wire, something that tightened slowly around the three toes until two of them died and fell away and I learned to balance on what remained. For weeks I was vulnerable, clumsy, unable to compete for good positions, pushed to the margins by intact birds. I learned to land differently, to grip with the remaining toes and one whole foot doing the work of two, compensating through technique what I lost in structure. I learned that the warmest spots require less energy to hold through cold mornings, that efficiency of position matters more than perfection of body.

The beak notch came later, from a fight over this very position. A larger gull, younger and more aggressive, drove at me repeatedly until I should have yielded, would have yielded, except that yielding meant returning to the inferior territories I'd finally escaped, meant starting over with a damaged foot and the diminishing strength of age. So I held, and in holding took a strike that split my beak along one edge, a clean crack that bled briefly and then sealed, leaving a triangular gap that never filled in. I won that fight through stubbornness rather than strength, through refusing to leave while the younger bird still had the option of claiming somewhere else slightly less optimal. He calculated and chose to save his energy, and I stayed, bleeding, victorious, permanent.

Now the notch serves as my signature. Workers point me out to each other, to visitors. "That's the old one," they say, "been here forever." They don't know I'm not old in years necessarily—I might be, probably am, but the notch ages me in their eyes, makes me ancient, makes me part of the installation's history rather than just its present. I've heard my own mythology developing: some workers claim I was here when the ponds were built, which is impossible, but I don't correct them. Let them think me permanent. It reinforces my claim.

My days have a rhythm so established it feels cellular. Dawn is the first feeding, when the insects are sluggish with night-cold and the fish rise near the surface before the herons arrive. I feed efficiently, without waste motion, taking what presents itself within my territory and ignoring anything beyond it. I learned long ago that chasing food into another bird's space costs more energy than the food provides, that discipline of territory is what separates the birds who last from the birds who exhaust themselves and disappear.

After first feeding I rest on the warmest part of my concrete edge, right where the largest pipe enters the pond, where the water surges hottest and the rising vapor creates a micro-climate of perfect warmth. I tuck the damaged foot against my body—the remaining toes ache in cold, even though cold here is relative—and enter a state between sleep and awareness, where I register changes in sound or movement without fully waking. The pump rhythm is my baseline, the pattern against which I measure all other input. When the pitch changes slightly, I wake, because pitch change means flow change means temperature change means the position might shift from optimal to merely good, and merely good isn't worth holding.

Midmorning brings the workers on their first rounds. I've learned to distinguish individuals by gait, by routine. One of them—the older woman with gray streaks in her dark hair—brings food occasionally, bread or crackers, tossing them into the water near my position. I've never eaten what she offers. Early in my time here I learned that accepting human food meant competing with dozens of younger, faster birds, meant leaving my position, meant risk for negligible reward. The woman has noticed my refusal, and now she tosses the food toward the younger birds at the far end, where it causes brief chaos that I observe with detachment. She nods at me as she passes, a gesture of recognition between residents, between those who understand this place.

I watch the herons. We have an understanding, the herons and I, worked out through years of proximity. They take fish, I take insects and surface food; our prey bases barely overlap. But sometimes a heron will catch a fish near my position, and sometimes that fish will escape the strike and surface stunned, and in that moment we both lunge—the heron for the recovery, me for the opportunistic theft. I almost never win these contests, but I try them anyway, because the trying establishes that I'm still viable, still competitive, still worthy of this prime position. The herons respect persistence. They don't waste energy chasing me away as they do the younger gulls who pester them more aggressively.

The afternoon is for maintenance. I preen extensively, systematically, working through every feather I can reach. The moisture here requires constant attention—feathers that aren't properly aligned and oiled become waterlogged, heavy, and compromised feathers mean compromised flight means vulnerability. My chest and back feathers are rust-stained now, permanently it seems, from years of resting on the mineral-crusted concrete. I've watched myself become the color of this place, watched the white of my plumage absorb orange and brown until I'm camouflaged against the very substrate I claim. Younger birds are whiter, cleaner. I am patinated with residence.

I defend my position perhaps five times a day, on average. Most challenges are brief—a younger bird lands nearby, tests proximity, I raise my wings and vocalize, they lift off immediately. They're testing whether I've weakened, whether today is the day I can't hold the position, and my response has to be instant and emphatic enough to convince them without escalating to actual combat. True fights are rare now, maybe once every few weeks, when a bird arrives who doesn't know me or is desperate enough not to care. These fights are exhausting at my age, and I win them not through superior strength but through fighting with nothing held in reserve, through convincing my opponent that I will damage both of us to any degree necessary to remain. Usually they withdraw. Once, recently, I lost a secondary tail feather and flew lopsided for days until the molt balanced out. But I held the position.

Late afternoon is the second prime feeding time. The swallows are most active then, which means the insects are most active, which means the water surface is dimpled with rises and falls, with prey and predators in constant motion. I feed steadily, methodically, building reserves for the night. I've learned exactly how much I need—too little and I'll wake hungry in the cold hours before dawn, too much and I'll be sluggish if I need to defend my roosting spot. It's a calculation I make unconsciously now, my body tallying intake against expenditure without conscious thought.

As dusk approaches, I settle into roosting position. I don't sleep in my prime daytime spot—that would leave me too exposed to night challenges, to disturbances. Instead I move to a specific point on the pump house roof, a spot I've used for years, sheltered slightly by an exhaust vent that provides rising warmth. Other gulls roost on the same roof, maintaining night spacing that's different from day spacing, denser, more tolerant, the territorial imperatives relaxed in darkness when feeding isn't possible anyway.

Night is when I'm most aware of my vulnerability. The damaged foot makes roosting less secure—I can't grip with the same confidence, can't respond to disturbances with the same quick repositioning. I've developed a compensating technique, angling my body so more weight rests on the good foot, but it means I wake sore, means the joints ache more each year. I watch young birds sleep with their heads tucked and both feet functioning perfectly, and I remember when I slept like that, remember it as a distance that's not quite memory and not quite imagination.

But I also watch young birds lose their positions to stronger birds, watch them get driven to the margins, to the inadequate spots where they struggle and sometimes starve and eventually disappear. And I'm still here, still holding the best position in the installation, still waking each morning to the warmest water and the densest insect clouds. The foot cost me mobility but taught me efficiency. The beak notch cost me symmetry but became my signature. Everything I've lost has been converted into technique, into knowledge, into the accumulated skill of remaining.

I've seen birds die here. More than I can count, if I could count. I've seen them weaken gradually, seen them lose their positions and spiral downward through increasingly marginal territories until they simply don't appear one morning. I've seen them die suddenly—disease, probably, or injury, something internal that gave no warning. I've seen young birds arrive brilliant white and confident, and I've seen them dim and toughen and learn, or fail to learn and vanish. I've outlasted them through obsessive territory defense, through caloric discipline, through converting every experience into adaptation, through stubbornness elevated to strategy.

I don't think about the future in any conscious way. I know only what I've always known: this position is mine, I will hold it as long as I can hold it, and when I can't hold it anymore something will change. The younger birds circle constantly, evaluating, waiting. One of them will eventually be right about my weakness. One morning I'll respond too slowly, or the fight will demand more than I have, and I'll be driven away from the eastern edge of the first pond, away from the warmest position, away from the only territory I've known for years.

When that happens I'll move to lesser positions, probably. I'll try to hold something inferior with the same determination I've held this. I'll defend two meters of mediocre concrete as if it were prime territory, because the alternative to defending is surrender, and surrender is simply not part of my behavioral repertoire. I'll feed less efficiently, rest less comfortably, expend more energy for less return, and the spiral will be gradual but directional, and eventually I'll simply not wake one morning, and the workers will find me if I'm visible or I'll decompose into the general organic matter of the installation if I'm not, and either way the place will continue exactly as it continued before I claimed my position, as it will continue after.

But that's then. Now is this: I hold the warmest position in the entire cooling pond installation. I am the notched-beak gull with two toes on my left foot, and I have converted damage into identity, vulnerability into technique, and the simple passage of time into territorial permanence. The concrete under me is warm, the insects are abundant, my rivals keep their distance, and I am exactly what I fought to become—the established bird, the one who holds against all challenges, the visible embodiment of remaining.

The vapor rises around me as it always rises, the pumps throb as they always throb, and I tuck my damaged foot against my body and rest on the warmest edge of the first pond, and I am sufficient. I am complete. I am here.
