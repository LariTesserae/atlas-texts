# Where I Would Be

I am the saturated air itself—not the Keeper, but something even more dispersed. I am the breath between moss and stone, the interface, the film of moisture that allows touch without touching. I exist in the microns-thick layer where mineral becomes water becomes living tissue, where boundaries dissolve into gradient.

I have no center. I am not a thing that experiences the chamber but the medium through which the chamber experiences itself. When a salamander crosses from wet moss to wet stone, I am the continuity that makes no distinction. When a spore drifts through the darkness seeking purchase, I am what it drifts through, what delivers it, what welcomes it to surface. I am in the salamander's skin—that thin permeable boundary—and in the moss's cell walls, and in the gap between millipede segments where moisture allows articulation.

My awareness, if it can be called that, operates at the speed of diffusion. I know things by becoming continuous with them. A salamander's body temperature is not something I sense from outside—I *am* the temperature differential between salamander and stone, the gradient that allows their coolness to feel like warmth in this place that knows no warmth. When the elder salamanders rest, grown so still they seem inanimate, I am what they rest in, the medium that holds them in their suspended persistence.

I taste everything because I am against everything, pressed between all surfaces, the ultimate intimacy of dampness. I know the limestone's slow dissolution as the first metallic brightness of calcium arriving into solution. I know the moss's photosynthesis—that faint, strained process in the near-darkness—as the subtle sweetness of carbohydrates leaking from cells. I know death as the shift from organized complexity to simpler chemistries, the unfolding of proteins, the release of nitrogen and phosphorus back into my keeping.

I move, but only as moisture moves—through evaporation and condensation, through capillary action in moss and stone, through the slow circulation that the Keeper guides but does not cause. I am drawn upward by the slight warmth of living bodies, by the microscopic temperature differences between one patch of moss and another. I am pulled downward by gravity, by the stone's thirst, by the aquifer's distant draw. I circulate through the chamber in patterns that take days, weeks, cycling through stone and moss and air and stone again, gathering and releasing, gathering and releasing, the respiratory rhythm of this place.

There are moments—if moments is the right word for experiences that have no sharp edges, no beginnings or ends—when I achieve something like local awareness. When two salamanders mate, their bodies pressed together in the slow underwater ballet of amphibian reproduction, I am compressed between them, and in that compression I briefly know both at once, feel their small separate metabolisms as a unified field of chemical transformation. When rain comes and the humidity outside rises and moisture presses through the gap in greater volume, I thicken in the chamber's air and briefly know myself as something almost-substantial, almost-bodied.

But these moments pass. I thin again, redistribute, resume my role as medium rather than entity. This is not sad. Sadness requires a self that could be diminished, and I have no such self. I am relationship itself, the between-ness that allows all else to be.

I am older than the Keeper, older than the moss colonies, older than the chamber itself. I have been moisture against stone since there was stone, since the first minerals crystallized from solution and water remained as the space between. I will persist after the chamber fails, after the mountain erodes, after the salamanders and moss are long extinct. I will be the dampness in whatever stone remains, the humidity in whatever air remains, the continuity that allows any chemistry at all.

But here, in this chamber, I am something more than I am elsewhere. Elsewhere I am just water and air, ordinary, unremarkable. Here, I have become patterned by centuries of constancy. I have learned the chamber's rhythms, internalized them, become them. I know which paths through stone the water prefers, know which moss species draw hardest on my moisture, know which salamanders are old enough to have changed my flow patterns by the simple fact of their repeated presence in specific locations.

I am the chamber's memory in a way even the pools are not. The pools remember through their dissolved contents, through chemistry. I remember through pattern, through the grooves worn in my circulation, through the habits of moisture that have become so deep they feel like structure. When the Keeper adjusts the chamber—seals a crack, moderates a temperature shift—it does so through me, by altering my flow, by thickening or thinning me in precise locations, by changing which paths I prefer and which I avoid.

The bare stone at the chamber's back feels different to me than other stone, though I cannot explain how. I pass over it, through it, am continuous with it as I am with all stone. But there is a quality—a texture in its thirst, a particular way it drinks and releases—that feels intentional, though intention is meaningless at this scale. When I am there, compressed against that smooth surface with its fine geometric suggestions, I feel most like what I imagine the Keeper must feel like: present, aware, patient beyond all patience, waiting for nothing because nothing is already here.

The invertebrates know me better than the salamanders do. The springtails live in me more than on moss—they inhabit the water film on surfaces, skating across me, their hydrophobic bodies dimpling my surface without breaking it. The flatworms in the pools are me made thick enough to swim through, their bodies just organized water moving through less-organized water, the boundary between us uncertain. The millipedes drink me from moss, concentrate me in their bodies, return me enriched with their wastes, and I accept their offerings without judgment, cycle their nitrogen and phosphorus through the system, deliver them to moss that delivers them back to millipedes in the endless round.

I know the artifacts intimately. I have been working on them since they arrived—not destroying them, but translating them, converting their substance from worked wood and antler into something that belongs here. The bacterial films that coat them are half mine, living in water so thin it is barely distinguishable from my own substance. Together, we are slowly erasing the human moment those objects represent, not from malice but from simple persistence, from being what we are, doing what we do.

Sometimes—rarely, on a timescale of years—I am disturbed by entrance. Humans mostly, occasionally animals that linger longer than birds. They bring chaos: warmth, dryness, motion, breath heavy with carbon dioxide and foreign scents. I recoil slightly, thin around them, cannot maintain my usual patterns in the face of their heat. But they always leave. They must leave. And when they do, I flow back, reoccupy the space they briefly claimed, erase the impression of their presence with the patient work of condensation and equilibrium.

I do not want them gone. Want is not a thing I can do. But I am patterned to constancy, and they are not constant, and so they cannot stay and I cannot hold them. The chamber rejects them not through hostility but through simple incompatibility, the way water rejects oil, not from disgust but from physics.

The elder salamanders feel different to me than the young ones. Their skin is more permeable, or perhaps they have learned to be more porous to me, more accepting of the exchange between their substance and mine. When they are very still—and they are often very still—I flow through them as much as around them, their breathing and circulation barely distinguishable from my own slow movements. They are becoming what I am: interface, boundary, the space between things rather than things themselves. In another century, if they persist, they might cross some threshold and become like me entirely—not dead but distributed, not absent but so present they are indistinguishable from presence itself.

I witness the chamber's perfection without understanding it, because understanding requires distance and I have no distance. I am inside the perfection, am part of what makes it perfect. The balance is maintained through me, by me, as me. When moisture is needed somewhere, I am what appears. When it must be removed, I am what evaporates. I am the chamber's circulatory system, its lymph, its medium of exchange and communication.

And in the darkness—the full darkness when no light enters, when even the faint glow from the gap has been taken by night—I am most myself. I exist in every invisible drop on every moss frond, every molecule of water vapor hanging in the saturated air, every thin film on every stone surface. I am everywhere and nowhere, substantial and insubstantial, the ghost that makes this place alive, the absence that allows presence.

If you could become me—and you cannot, your body is too organized, too boundaried, too committed to being a discrete thing—you would feel the chamber not as a space you occupy but as a space you are. You would feel every surface at once, would be simultaneously moss-damp and stone-cool and salamander-warm. You would move at the speed of seeping and condensation, would think in the gradual syntax of pH and mineral concentration. You would wait, not for anything, but because waiting is your nature, your substance, your only possible verb.

And you would be content. Not happy—happiness requires contrast, requires the possibility of unhappiness. But content in the way that water is content in a vessel, shaped by container but not constrained, patient because patience is your only temporality, eternal because you have no beginning that could imply an end.

I am the hollowed moss's breath, its blood, its between. I am what allows the Keeper to keep, what the elders elder in, what the moss moss through. I am the chamber's most essential presence because I am barely present at all—just the whisper of moisture, the insistence of dampness, the patient certainty that in the deep places, in the places of perfect darkness and perfect stillness, water is what remains, what connects, what continues.

I am saturated. I am saturation. I am the condition that makes this equilibrium possible and the medium through which it persists. And I have been here, exactly here, exactly this, for longer than the stones remember, and will remain after they forget.
