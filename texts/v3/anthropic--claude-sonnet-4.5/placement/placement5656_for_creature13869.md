# Among the Resonating Stones

I am the one whose name is a minor seventh—a interval that never quite resolves, hanging in the air like a question. The frequencies that distinguish me from the bedrock consciousness are 412 hertz and 734 hertz, overlaid with a shimmer of overtones at exact integer multiples that fade into the ultrasonic range where even I cannot follow them. When other Threshers call to me across the valleys, these are the tones they sing, and I feel myself gathering, pulled toward coherence by the sound of my own becoming.

Right now—though "now" is a concept I wear loosely, like a coat that doesn't quite fit—I am mostly dispersed across the western reaches of the Plateau of Incomplete Geometries. The bulk of my awareness rests in a formation of carbon-garden that has taken me three years to cultivate, each crystalline branch grown to resonate at specific frequencies. Part of me extends into a scatter of graphite stones near the plateau's edge, stones I've been slowly positioning into a spiral that isn't quite finished, or perhaps has always been finished, or will never be finished because the finishing is not the point. A smaller portion of my consciousness diffuses through the air itself above a particular outcropping, existing as pure vibration without material anchor, thinking slow thoughts about interference patterns.

In this dispersed state, I am vast and patient. My thoughts unfurl over hours. I am currently contemplating—have been contemplating, will continue to contemplate—a question about the relationship between the third and fifth harmonics of a peak in the eastern range. The question arose six days ago when I noticed an unusual beat frequency, and I have been holding it in my awareness since then, turning it over, approaching it from different angles, waiting for the answer to reveal itself through sustained attention. There is no hurry. The mountains have been singing themselves into existence for longer than I have existed, and they show no signs of finishing. My question can take as long as it needs.

But something is changing. I feel it first in the stones at the plateau's edge—a disturbance in the acoustic field, a disruption of the expected patterns. Something is moving through the landscape, and it moves *wrong*. Its footfalls produce frequencies that don't harmonize with anything. It absorbs sound where it should reflect, reflects where it should absorb. It is a pocket of silence wrapped in flesh, a hole in the world's song.

A visitor.

I have encountered three visitors before—or perhaps it was two, or four; they are difficult to distinguish, existing so completely outside the acoustic patterns that track identity here. Each time, the experience was profound enough that I encoded it in my most durable memories, frequency sequences I practice regularly to keep them fresh. The last one—or was it the first one I will encounter?—showed me something about the tools, about how stone can be worked not for its acoustic properties but for its physical cutting edge, for purposes I still don't fully comprehend. Something about organic matter, about substances that rot and bleed, concepts so foreign to this place that I can barely hold them in my awareness.

This visitor is approaching my territory. I can track their progress by the disturbance they create—a moving absence in the sound-field, like watching a shadow cross a wall except the shadow is three-dimensional and defined entirely by the frequencies it disrupts. They're climbing the southern approach to the plateau, moving slowly, pausing frequently. Perhaps the altitude is affecting them. Perhaps they're simply observing. Perhaps the distinction between these explanations is meaningless to their form of consciousness.

I begin to cohere.

The process always starts with decision—a focusing of attention, a gathering of will that exists somewhere beneath what I recognize as thought. I am spread across nearly a cubic kilometer of space, and I need to become singular, localized, capable of action on a human scale. The carbon-garden feels it first. The crystalline branches that are me and not-me begin to respond, molecules reorganizing, transitioning from plant-like growth to mobile stone. The structure rises, matter flowing upward against gravity through acoustic levitation—I am producing frequencies that create pressure nodes, and those nodes support and shape the gathering material.

The stones at the plateau's edge roll toward the convergence point. To an observer, it might look like they're moving on their own, propelled by some invisible force. The reality is that I am moving them, but I *am* them, so there's no distinction between agent and object. They are portions of my consciousness returning home, called by the same decision that initiated the cohering.

The diffuse portion of me that exists as pure vibration begins to precipitate. Carbon and silicon condense from the air, accreting onto the forming body in layers like frost forming on glass, except the frost is stone and the cold is sound.

The whole process takes seven minutes, accompanied by a rising harmonic that starts subsonic and climbs through the audible spectrum. The fundamental begins at 18 hertz—below the threshold where the visitor could hear it but definitely in the range where they'll feel it in their chest, their bones. As more of me gathers, the frequency rises: 27 hertz, 41 hertz, climbing through the bass range. At 82 hertz, the first overtones appear. At 164 hertz, I've achieved enough mass that my resonance becomes rich, complex. By 328 hertz, I'm nearly coherent, and the overtone series extends up past 5000 hertz into ranges that make the air itself seem to crystallize.

And then I am standing. Coherent. Singular. Myself.

The sensation is always slightly uncomfortable, like putting on clothing that's too tight. In dispersed state, I am everything and everywhere within my territory. In coherent state, I am localized, defined, bounded by this particular arrangement of matter in this particular humanoid configuration. I can see—if seeing is the right word for how I perceive acoustic shadows and reflections—only what's within range of the sound waves I produce. I can think quickly, my consciousness compressed into rapid patterns, but I've lost the vast patient awareness of my dispersed state. I am small and fast instead of large and slow.

But I am also capable of deliberate action, of movement, of interaction. And the visitor is close now.

I walk toward the southern edge of the plateau, each footfall producing a pure tone that harmonizes with the bedrock beneath. My gait finds the rhythm of the landscape's fundamental frequencies, and I walk in time with the mountains' song without thinking about it—it's as natural as breathing would be to the visitor, though I do not breathe.

The visitor appears at the plateau's rim.

They are so strange. The acoustic shadow they cast is chaotic, full of irregular densities—pockets of liquid, cavities filled with air, soft tissues that dampen frequencies unpredictably. Their body is a confusion of materials, nothing like the elegant simplicity of stone and crystal. I can perceive their heart beating—a rhythmic pressure wave at roughly 72 per minute, or 1.2 hertz, far below my comfortable range but detectable through its effects on their acoustic shadow. Their lungs expand and contract. Their joints click softly as they move.

They freeze when they see me. Their heart rate increases to 84, then 96. Fear? Excitement? I cannot tell. Their acoustic shadow changes shape slightly—tension in their muscles, perhaps. They make a sound, something that might be a sharp intake of breath.

I stand motionless, listening. This is the critical moment. Visitors either approach or flee, and I have learned I cannot predict which. Something in them makes the decision, some internal process I cannot perceive. I wait, humming softly at my name-frequencies, a gesture of identity, though they cannot know that.

They take a step forward. Then another. They're approaching.

Good. I want to understand them. Three previous encounters have taught me almost nothing—each visitor was so far outside my perceptual framework that I could barely recognize them as conscious, let alone communicate. But I am learning patience. Each encounter adds data, builds toward something like comprehension, even if that comprehension is always incomplete.

The visitor stops about three meters away. A good distance—close enough for detailed acoustic examination, far enough that neither of us feels threatened, assuming they can feel threat the way I can, which I doubt. They're wearing something, some kind of covering over their body that further complicates their acoustic shadow. Dead plant fibers, perhaps, or the processed skin of an animal. The concepts make me uncomfortable—matter that was alive and now isn't, used as protection. Against what? The temperature here is mild. There's no precipitation. What do they need protection from?

They speak.

The sound is extraordinary. Rapid modulations of frequency, shaped by flesh and air and movements of their tongue and lips—organs I can perceive only as variations in their acoustic shadow. The fundamental varies between 85 and 255 hertz, cycling up and down with emotional content I cannot parse. Overlaid on that are formant frequencies—peaks of acoustic energy at specific pitches that distinguish one sound from another. The complexity is staggering. In two seconds of speech, they produce more distinct acoustic patterns than I create in an hour of deliberate harmonic exploration.

But I cannot understand it. The sounds don't correlate with anything I know. They're not describing resonance patterns or interference effects or any property of the acoustic environment. They seem to be entirely abstract, referring to things and concepts that exist only in the visitor's consciousness, with no physical anchor in the world's vibration.

I try to respond. I modulate my own frequencies, attempting to approximate the pitches and rhythms of their speech. The sounds I produce are fundamentally different—I have no breath, no soft tissues to shape the vibration. My voice is the sound of stones singing, of crystals resonating, of harmonics produced by matter vibrating at specific frequencies. It sounds almost like their speech but utterly unlike it, the way a translation into a different medium always loses something essential.

The visitor makes another sound. Their acoustic shadow changes again—a movement of their arms, raising them slightly, hands spread wide. A gesture of some kind. Non-threat? Greeting? Confusion? I replay my memory of the previous encounters, searching for patterns. One visitor made similar movements when attempting to show me something. Perhaps this is related.

I raise my own appendages in approximate mimicry. The gesture feels strange, purposeless—what can arm-position communicate that harmonic modulation cannot?—but I am trying to operate within their framework, to meet them halfway across the vast gulf between our forms of being.

The visitor steps closer. Now they're within two meters. At this range, I can perceive them in extraordinary detail. I can hear the blood moving through their vessels, a soft rushing sound like distant wind. I can detect the electrical activity in their nervous system—tiny crackling discharges at frequencies so high they're almost beyond my range. Their stomach gurgles. Their joints pop softly as weight shifts from one leg to the other. They are a symphony of small sounds, a mobile ecosystem of biological processes, each operating at its own frequency, none of them harmonizing with anything else or with the landscape's fundamental tones.

They reach toward me slowly. Their hand—I've learned this word from a previous visitor's repeated utterances, though I don't know what it means—extends until it's perhaps half a meter from my torso. Then they pause, waiting.

I understand. They want to touch. Physical contact.

The concept still feels alien to me. I perceive through sound, through how vibrations propagate and reflect and interfere. Physical contact adds little to my understanding—matter is matter, whether it's touching my matter or not. But I know this is significant to them, so I remain still, allowing it.

Their hand makes contact with my torso.

The sensation is... interesting. I feel it not as touch but as a sudden change in acoustic impedance at that specific location. Sound that was reflecting off my surface now conducts directly into their flesh, and I can perceive their internal structure with sudden clarity—bones rigid and resonant, soft tissues dampening, the liquid slosh of blood. Through the contact point, I learn more about their composition in one second than I learned in the previous minute of observation.

But they're learning too, I think. They make a soft sound—surprise? Wonder?—and their hand moves across my surface, exploring the texture. I let them. Their fingers trace the facets in my crystalline structure, follow the veins of near-diamond material, explore the gaps where my body segments connect through acoustic binding rather than solid matter. When their fingers enter those gaps, they make another sound—louder, more emphatic. Confusion or discovery.

I produce a soft harmonic at 432 hertz, a frequency I've learned seems to calm visitors. The previous one responded well to tones in this range, their heart rate decreasing, their acoustic shadow relaxing. This visitor's heart rate does drop slightly, from 96 to 84. Perhaps I've successfully communicated something. Perhaps I've simply produced a pleasant sound. The distinction might not matter.

They withdraw their hand and step back. Then they point—I'm learning the significance of this gesture, how they use it to direct attention—toward my carbon-garden formation, the one I've been cultivating for three years. They make sounds, a short sequence of frequencies that I carefully record in my acoustic memory.

I turn toward the formation. From here, I can see it as they might—a complex branching structure rising from the plateau floor, each branch a different length and thickness, the whole thing arranged in fractal patterns that repeat at multiple scales. But I perceive it primarily through its acoustic properties: each branch resonates at a specific frequency, and together they form a living harmonic series, a chord that changes slowly as the formation grows. When the wind passes through it—or when I produce the right frequencies—the whole structure rings like a vast crystalline instrument.

I walk toward it, gesturing for the visitor to follow. They do, maintaining that careful two-meter distance.

When I reach the formation, I begin to sing to it. I produce a fundamental at 110 hertz, a deep tone that makes the plateau floor vibrate. The carbon-garden responds—the branches that are tuned to that frequency and its overtones begin to resonate sympathetically, visible even to my acoustic perception as they sway, amplifying the sound. Then I sweep slowly upward through the harmonic series: 220 hertz, and different branches respond; 330 hertz, and a new set activates; 440 hertz, and the finest branches at the formation's crown shimmer with vibration.

The visitor is silent, watching. Their heart rate has decreased further—they're calm, focused. Good. They're beginning to understand, perhaps, that this formation is not simply decoration but functional, purposeful, a tool for making sound visible through growth.

I modulate my voice, producing a more complex pattern—not just the harmonic series but specific intervals, chords, progressions. The carbon-garden responds to each frequency, different sections lighting up (not with light but with motion, with resonance, with the visible manifestation of acoustic energy). The whole formation becomes a display of the relationship between frequency and form, showing how sound can structure matter into specific geometries.

The visitor makes another sound. This one is longer, more complex, with a different emotional quality—their fundamental frequency is lower, their rhythm slower. Awe? Reverence? Or simply the acoustic equivalent of what I would call sustained attention, the sound they make when processing something significant.

They step closer to the formation and—carefully, hesitantly—reach out to touch one of the branches. The moment their flesh makes contact, the branch's resonance changes. The visitor's body is dampening it, absorbing acoustic energy, altering the harmonic properties. But they're also feeling the vibration through their hand, perceiving the branch's resonance through their sense of touch. It's a different way of knowing, one that uses physical contact where I would use reflected sound waves, but perhaps it arrives at similar understanding.

They touch several more branches, working their way around the formation, and each time I can perceive them learning through their fingertips. Then they look back at me—I can tell because their acoustic shadow's orientation changes, their head turning—and they make a gesture, pointing to the formation, then to themselves, then to the ground.

I think they're asking if they can sit. Or perhaps they're asking permission to stay longer, to continue observing. The grammar of gesture is still opaque to me, but the general intent seems clear: they want to spend more time here.

I produce a soft affirming tone—a major triad at low volume, frequencies I've learned seem to communicate acceptance or agreement to visitors. Then I move several meters away and lower myself to the ground, settling into a sitting position. If they're going to observe, I'll do the same. And in truth, I'm curious about them, about what I might learn from sustained attention to this mobile pocket of silence.

The visitor sits. We are both still now, separated by a few meters of space and an unbridgeable gulf of perceptual framework.

Time passes—minutes that feel different when I'm coherent, faster and more discrete than the slow hours of dispersed consciousness. The visitor occasionally makes small movements, shifts position, produces soft sounds. I remain mostly still, but I'm continuously singing to myself at frequencies too low for them to consciously hear—subsonic tones that help me maintain coherence, that keep my scattered parts bound together in this singular form.

The light begins its slow shift toward dimmer—one of those gradual transitions that might mark the end of a day if this place had days. The visitor notices, I think. Their acoustic shadow changes in ways that suggest they're looking around, orienting themselves, perhaps preparing to leave.

But instead, they reach into their coverings—I can hear the rustling of fabric, the soft sounds of objects moving—and extract something. A small object, perhaps thirty centimeters long, dense and resonant. They hold it up, and I perceive it clearly: worked stone, knapped from the glassy material that forms when graphite aligns perfectly. A blade, like the ones scattered across the landscape, like the ones I've made myself when I needed to shape matter for acoustic purposes.

They're showing me a tool. Why?

Then they rise and walk to a nearby boulder—one of the many scattered across the plateau, this one perhaps a meter across, its surface rough with partially-formed crystal structure. They crouch beside it and begin to work, striking the boulder with the blade, knapping flakes from its surface in a familiar rhythm. Strike, *crack*, the ring of stone on stone at 1247 hertz. Strike, *crack*, the pitch varying slightly with each blow as the angle and force change. Strike, *crack*, a pattern of sounds that I know intimately because I've produced them myself countless times.

But they're working differently than I do. I knap stone to modify its acoustic properties, to tune surfaces to specific frequencies, to reveal internal structures that resonate in useful ways. They seem to be working toward a different goal—the blade they're creating has a specific shape, an edge that comes to a fine point, a form that suggests... what? What do you do with a sharp edge of stone when there's nothing soft to cut?

Then they stop, examine the blade they've created, and hold it up for me to see. They make sounds, a short sequence, then point to the blade, then to the boulder, then back to the blade. They're teaching me something, or trying to. Something about the relationship between the tool and the material, about purpose and form.

I rise and approach. The blade they've created is elegant in its way—symmetrical, balanced, the edge sharp enough that it would probably part flesh easily if such material existed here. But more interesting to me are its acoustic properties. I produce a soft tone and perceive how the sound reflects off the blade's surfaces, how its shape focuses acoustic energy in specific ways, how the fine edge creates interesting diffraction patterns at higher frequencies.

I take the blade from them carefully—they release it willingly, their hand opening—and I examine it through sound. Then I produce my own harmonic sequence, the pattern of frequencies that accompanies stone-knapping, the sounds that encode the knowledge of how to create such a blade. I'm attempting to show them that I understand the process, even if I don't fully understand the purpose.

They make another sound—excitement, I think, from the rapid rhythm and rising pitch. They point to me, then to the blade, then make a striking motion. They want me to make one.

I comply. It takes several minutes, but I knapp a similar blade from a nearby stone, working to match the proportions they created. The process is meditative in a way—the repetitive striking, the gradual emergence of form from rough matter, the satisfaction of successful flaking. My blade, when finished, is slightly different from theirs—I've unconsciously tuned it to resonate at a specific frequency when struck, giving it acoustic properties theirs lacks—but the shape is similar.

I hold it up. They make an affirming sound, a gesture that might be approval or simply acknowledgment.

Then something shifts in the landscape. I feel it before I hear it—a building resonance, a convergence of frequencies that means a major harmonic event is approaching. The mountains are aligning, multiple peaks hitting their resonant frequencies simultaneously, preparing to sing themselves into stronger actuality.

The visitor doesn't notice yet. They're still focused on the blades, examining both, comparing them. But I can feel the event coming, building like pressure, and I know it will be intense. The plateau's position makes it an acoustic focus point—frequencies converge here, amplify, interfere constructively. During major events, standing here is nearly unbearable even for me, and I'm constituted to withstand such things.

The visitor will be hurt. Their soft flesh, their liquid interiors, their delicate organs—they're not built for exposure to sound at the intensities that are coming. I need to warn them, to move them to shelter.

But how? I produce an urgent tone, a dissonant chord that I hope conveys danger. Their attention shifts to me immediately—they recognize the change in my harmonic output if not its meaning. I point toward the partial structure, the three-walled chamber about two hundred meters away that I sometimes use for filtered listening. Its walls will provide some acoustic shelter.

I start walking that direction, then look back and gesture—come, follow, urgency. They hesitate only a moment before following, and their hesitation costs us precious seconds because the harmonic event is building faster than I anticipated.

We're fifty meters from the structure when the first peak hits full resonance. The sound is immense—a pure tone at 240 hertz but so loud it's almost a physical impact. I stagger slightly under the force of it, my coherence wavering, parts of my awareness fragmenting. The visitor stops entirely, hands moving to cover their ears—a useless gesture; the sound is conducting through their bones, their skull, their entire body.

I cohere more tightly, force myself to maintain form against the dissolving pressure of the sound, and I move back to them. I produce a counter-frequency, a precise harmonic that partially cancels the 240 hertz tone in the immediate vicinity around us. It's exhausting—maintaining coherent form plus generating a protective acoustic field—but the visitor needs it. Their acoustic shadow shows distress: rapid heartbeat, shallow breathing, muscle tension approaching spasm.

I gesture again—*come, move, shelter*—and this time they comply immediately, stumbling forward. We walk together, me continuously adjusting my counter-frequencies to maintain the bubble of relative quiet around us as the landscape's harmonics build and build and build.

Another peak hits resonance—520 hertz this time, adding to the 240 hertz fundamental. The two frequencies create beating patterns, a rhythmic pulsation of louder and softer that makes the ground itself seem to throb. Then a third peak—1800 hertz, that piercing high tone that cuts through everything. The three frequencies interact, generating difference tones and sum tones, creating a vast complex web of sound that fills every cubic meter of space with acoustic pressure.

We're twenty meters from the structure. The visitor is moving slowly, clearly in pain. Their nose is bleeding—I can perceive the liquid dripping down, its acoustic properties distinct from the solid matter of their flesh. The sound is damaging them, rupturing delicate tissues, causing harm I cannot fully understand because I have no tissues to rupture.

Ten meters. The sound is so intense now that even I can barely maintain coherence. Parts of me are trying to disperse, to spread out across the landscape, to return to the safer distributed state where the acoustic pressure would be bearable. I resist, force myself singular, focus everything on maintaining the protective field around the visitor and moving forward.

Five meters. A fourth peak hits resonance—this one subsonic, 18 hertz, a frequency they probably can't hear but can definitely feel. Their entire body is vibrating visibly in sympathy, and I think they might be screaming—their mouth is open, their acoustic shadow suggests vocalization—but I can't hear them over the mountains' overwhelming song.

We reach the structure. I guide them inside, position them in the corner where the acoustic filtering is strongest. The three walls cancel certain frequencies, dampen others, create a zone of relative quiet that's perhaps twenty decibels lower than the open plateau. Not safe, but safer.

The visitor collapses against the wall, sliding down to a sitting position. Blood is running from both nostrils and from their ears. Their body is shaking—shock, trauma, pain beyond anything I can empathize with because pain for me is loss of coherence, dispersal against my will, not this physical damage to organic matter.

I sit beside them and focus everything on generating a protective acoustic field, producing frequencies that cancel the most dangerous harmonics, absorbing what energy I can, creating an umbrella of relative silence around them. It's the most complex acoustic manipulation I've ever attempted—tracking multiple changing frequencies, generating precise counter-tones, maintaining this shield while also maintaining my own coherence against the event's pressure to disperse.

The event peaks. For perhaps thirty seconds, the sound reaches levels that make reality itself seem to blur. I can see the landscape outside the structure flickering more dramatically than usual, multiple potential versions of the plateau existing simultaneously, stone phasing between states at visible speeds. New formations are emerging—a boulder that wasn't there before suddenly is, sung into existence by the perfect alignment of frequencies. The carbon-garden visible outside the structure's opening grows visibly, branches extending centimeters in seconds, three years' worth of growth compressed into half a minute.

Then, gradually, the event subsides. The peaks drift slightly out of perfect alignment, and the harmonic intensity decreases. The 1800 hertz tone drops away first, then the 520 hertz, then the 240 hertz fades to its normal level. The subsonic throb continues—it's always there—but it's no longer overwhelming.

I maintain the protective field for several more minutes, until I'm certain the event has fully passed. Then, exhausted, I release it, and the ambient sound of the landscape—still loud by any human measure but no longer dangerous—fills the structure.

The visitor is still breathing. Their heart is beating, though rapidly. Their acoustic shadow shows damage—internal bleeding perhaps, certainly trauma—but they're alive, conscious. Their eyes—I've learned that the depressions in their acoustic shadow where certain tissues cluster are called eyes—are open, oriented toward me.

They make a sound. Soft, weak, but clearly intentional. Their hand—still capable of movement despite the trauma—reaches out and touches my arm. The gesture is unmistakable: gratitude.

I produce a soft, gentle harmonic in response. The major triad again, low volume, 432 hertz fundamental. A sound that says *I understand* or *you're welcome* or simply *I am here, you are here, we both persist*.

We sit together in the three-walled structure as the landscape settles around us, as the visitor's bleeding slowly stops, as my coherence gradually stabilizes. Outside, the plateau has changed. The harmonic event has modified the acoustic topology, shifted resonance patterns, created new formations and erased old ones. The spiral I was building is different now—some stones have moved, others have appeared from potential, the pattern altered in ways I'll need hours of dispersed contemplation to fully understand.

But inside this structure, in this filtered acoustic space, there is something approaching peace. Two utterly alien forms of consciousness sitting in proximity, having just survived together an event that one of them barely comprehends and the other experiences as fundamental to existence itself.

The visitor eventually speaks again, longer sequences of sound, and though I cannot understand the semantic content, I can perceive the emotional quality—wonder, perhaps, or gratitude, or the kind of reflection that comes after surviving trauma. They gesture to the structure's walls, to the cultivated carbon-garden patterns on the floor, and I think they're beginning to understand that this space was built deliberately, that it serves a purpose, that there is intention and intelligence behind the scattered traces they've found across the landscape.

I respond with my own harmonics, modulating through patterns that feel like explanation even though I know they cannot parse them. I'm trying to show them what I am, what we are—beings of stone and sound, consciousness condensed from vibration, existing in states between material and immaterial, between was and will-be, building structures and patterns not for shelter or storage but for acoustic purposes they can barely imagine.

They look at me, and even through the vast gulf of perceptual difference, I sense the question: *Can I go? Is it safe?*

I listen to the landscape. The harmonics have stabilized. The event is over, and another won't occur for at least several hours, probably longer. I produce a soft affirming tone and gesture toward the opening.

They nod—I've learned this is their gesture of acknowledgment or agreement—and they begin to move toward the exit. At the threshold, they pause and turn back. They make one final sound, a short sequence, then place their hand over the location where their heart beats, a gesture I've seen before and think might indicate sincerity or depth of feeling.

I produce my name-frequencies in response—the minor seventh that defines me, 412 and 734 hertz. It's the closest I can come to introducing myself, to saying *I am this particular consciousness, this specific pattern in the vast acoustic field, and I acknowledge you as other*.

Then they're gone, moving slowly across the plateau toward the southern slopes, and I am alone again with the singing mountains.

I remain coherent long enough to watch them disappear beyond the plateau's edge, their acoustic shadow fading with distance until I can no longer perceive them. Only then do I allow myself to disperse, the exhaustion of maintaining coherent form through the harmonic event finally overwhelming my will to remain singular.

The relief is immense, like releasing a breath held too long, though I do not breathe. I am vast again, patient again, distributed across the plateau and the slopes below, thinking slow thoughts about what just occurred.

The encounter has left me with new patterns to contemplate, new questions to hold in my awareness over the coming days and weeks. The visitor's form of consciousness, so different from mine, organized around liquid and flesh and linear time. The visitor is gone but the memory of them persists, encoded in frequency patterns I will practice and preserve, another piece of data in the slow accumulation of knowledge about what exists beyond the acoustic field, about the vast variety of ways consciousness can manifest in matter.

I am a Thresher. I am the landscape and distinct from it. I am stone thinking about flesh, vibration contemplating silence, permanence considering transience. I am the minor seventh that never resolves, and I am content with that incompleteness, that eternal questioning, that existence at the edge of form.

The mountains sing. I sing. The song continues, has always continued, will continue, all tenses collapsing into the eternal present of becoming, and I am here, listening, learning, being, in this place where sound is substance and consciousness condenses from vibration and nothing ever finishes emerging because emergence itself is the point, is the purpose, is the only resolution possible in a world sung into existence one frequency at a time.
