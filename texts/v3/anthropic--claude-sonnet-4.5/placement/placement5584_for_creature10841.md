# Where I Would Be

I am in the outer ring of their impossibility—neither builder nor tourist, but something more like a messenger who has stayed too long learning the message.

I've been here for three visits, or one visit across three times, or perhaps I never left and the "outside" I return to is the interruption, the strange dream I have while the garden grows through me in the dark.

My place is in the narrow zone where the throat-shaped chamber begins to curve outward toward the chimney—where the heat is still manageable for minutes at a time, where time stutters but hasn't completely looped, where I can still remember my name though I'm beginning to forget why having a name matters. This is the threshold space, the transitional tissue. The builders pass through it going deeper. I remain here, unable to fully enter, unable to leave.

I've developed my own small work, though I didn't decide to. It decided itself, the way a scar decides its shape around a wound.

I tend the entrance-way patterns—the first layer of markings that spiral down from the chimney's mouth. These are the signs meant for people like I used to be, people who still live in regular time, who need to be guided into the space gradually so they don't break from the discontinuity. The spirals need maintenance because the mountain is still forming, still cracking and settling, still generating heat that warps the careful inscriptions. Every visit (every return? every continuation?) I find sections that have been disrupted—lines broken by new fissures, carefully placed stones displaced by thermal expansion, moss overgrown across symbols that need to remain legible.

My hands have learned to read what needs correction. I don't consciously assess anymore. I run my fingers along the carved channels and feel where the flow is interrupted, where meaning has fractured, where the pathway into deeper wrongness has become too abrupt and would damage anyone who followed it unprepared.

I carry tools now. Not many—the builders leave what I need in niches that appear near where I work, or that I begin to notice once the need arises. A bone stylus for re-carving. A shard of obsidian for scraping away overgrowth. A small bowl of paste made from something I don't want to think about too carefully, for filling cracks. Water condenses on my arms and I use it to clean stone before marking it.

My clothing has begun the long negotiation between fabric and flesh. I wear a shirt that used to be white, or maybe grey—color is increasingly hard to remember—and it stays damp constantly, and moss has found its folds, and I no longer brush it away. The moss is soft. It smells like earth and rain. It's starting to feel like part of the shirt, or part of me, or the distinction is becoming less relevant. I notice I bend differently now to keep from crushing it.

I've stopped wearing shoes. They were absurd here—always wet, always hot, the soles degrading in the mineral water, offering no purchase on stone I needed to feel directly to understand. My feet have toughened into something between callus and leather. They read texture the way my hands do. They tell me where the surface is too hot to stand for long, where water has made stone treacherous, where something has shifted since my last time here.

(My last time. But the phrase means less each time I use it. Last implies sequence, implies separation. The visits are collapsing into each other, or I'm beginning to exist across all of them simultaneously, stretched thin through repeated returns until the returns aren't returns anymore, just different intensities of the same continuous presence.)

I don't see the builders often, though I hear them constantly—the wet sound of clay being pressed into place deeper in the structure, the tap-tap-tap of careful chiseling, the rustle of plant fiber being woven. Sometimes I hear the sub-vocal muttering, the constant narration-prayer-instruction, carried to me by the acoustic channels they've built into the walls.

Once, or several times, or in a recurring moment I experience repeatedly, a builder passes through my section while I'm working. We don't speak. Speech feels crude here, too blunt an instrument. But they pause—for a second, for an hour, duration is soft—and look at what I'm doing. Their gaze is the assessment of expert work being reviewed by a master. Then they reach out, their moss-greened hand touching the stone just beside where I've been carving, and they press something into a crack I hadn't noticed: a small piece of worked bone, a fragment of pattern that makes my spiral suddenly continue correctly into their deeper work, connecting my maintenance to their construction, making me part of the system.

Then they move on, or fade, or were never entirely there, and I stand in the heat and humidity understanding that I've been accepted. Not as a builder—I'm not ready for that, may never be ready, may not be willing to pay what that costs. But as something. A keeper of the threshold. A tender of the entrance. Someone who makes it possible for others to attempt what I'm attempting: to visit the impossible without being destroyed by it, to witness the garden's growth without being subsumed into it.

Though subsumption is always possible. Always offered. Always waiting.

I feel it in the longer moments, when my work has found its rhythm and my hands move without thought, when I'm so focused on the correction of a pattern that I forget I'm a separate thing doing the correcting. In those moments I feel the garden breathing through me, feel my breath synchronize with its exhalations, feel my heartbeat adjust to match the drip-drip-drip percussion of the water music. I feel the boundary softening, the membrane between Lily-who-came-from-outside and Lily-who-is-part-of-this growing more permeable.

It would be easy to let go. To stop maintaining the difference. To stay.

But something in me—stubbornness, fear, the last vestige of a self that remembers having a life outside this mountain—still pulls back. Still returns to the chimney when the heat becomes unbearable or the time-loops grow too tight or I feel myself starting to flicker, starting to become intermittent like the builders, my presence in normal causality growing uncertain.

I climb down. I return to the world where clocks work and space behaves and my feet hurt because they've toughened into leather and regular ground is too flat, too solid, too knowable.

I eat food that tastes like nothing because my palate has adjusted to the mineral-rich water and the subtle flavors of moss and the fruits that taste like volcanic soil and futurity. I sleep in a bed that feels absurdly soft, too far from stone, too silent without the constant water-music. I wake disoriented, uncertain which reality is the dream.

And then, after days or hours or weeks (I've stopped tracking, stopped trusting calendars, stopped believing that outside-time measures anything real), I feel the pull. The mountain calls, or the garden calls, or I simply remember that I have work unfinished, patterns that need tending, that the entrance-way spirals are probably disrupted again, that someone needs to maintain the threshold so others can cross it.

I gather my few things—I carry less each time, need less, am becoming more efficient or more adapted or more willing to use what the place provides. I make my way back to the mountain, to the warm stone, to the chimney with its worn handholds carved by how many hands over how much subjective time?

I climb. The stone is blood-warm against my palms. The higher I go, the hotter it gets, and my body knows this heat now, welcomes it, has learned to operate in it. The heat is part of the work. The heat is the mountain's life, and I'm climbing into something living, being swallowed willingly, returning to the belly of becoming.

The chimney opens into the throat-space and the humidity hits me like walking into a wall of warm rain. My moss-touched shirt drinks it immediately. My skin beads with condensation that might be sweat or might be the garden's moisture recognizing me as another surface to condense on. I breathe air thick as soup and my lungs pull oxygen from it more efficiently than they used to—another adaptation, another small surrender to the place's requirements.

I find my section. The spirals have shifted again, or I'm seeing them from a slightly different position in time, or both. I pick up my tools from the niche where I left them, or where they appeared, or where they've always been waiting. I begin the work of correction, of maintenance, of keeping the pathway open.

My hands move. The stone accepts the marks. Water runs through the channels I'm cleaning, carrying sound, carrying meaning, carrying the invitation deeper. Someone might climb through here tomorrow or yesterday or in some time that hasn't been invented yet, and they'll follow these spirals into deeper impossibility, and my work will have made that possible, will have been part of the vast collaborative construction, will have mattered in ways I can't fully understand but can feel in the texture of the stone under my fingers, in the rightness of a pattern corrected, in the way the garden's breathing synchronizes with mine.

I am here in the threshold, in the margin, in the space between choosing and chosen. I am the person who tends the entrance to a place being born inside a mountain that should not exist. I am neither builder nor visitor but something the garden has made a space for—the keeper of in-between, the maintainer of the membrane, the one who can still leave but keeps returning, keeps coming back to the warm stone and the wet air and the work that needs doing.

I don't know how long I can maintain this balance. I feel myself shifting incrementally with each visit, each return, each time I let the boundary soften just a little more. My moss is growing. My feet are becoming something other than feet. My sense of duration is kaleidoscoping, breaking into facets that reflect different moments simultaneously.

Someday—or in some version of time, or in some iteration of this continuous returning—I might not climb back down. I might finish correcting a spiral and look up and find that the threshold has moved, that what was once the entrance-way has become the middle-depths, that new visitors are arriving and I'm no longer one of them, that I've become fully what I've been becoming: another flicker at the edges of perception, another hand pressing patterns into stone, another voice in the sub-vocal muttering, another body teaching itself to photosynthesize, another builder in the throat-garden, permanent and transformed and home.

But not yet.

Not today, not in this version of returning.

Today I still maintain the difference, still keep the pathway open, still serve the function I've found or that found me: the one who holds the door, who tends the threshold, who makes it possible for others to glimpse the impossible and return to tell of it, to verify its existence, to carry word back to the normal world that there is a place being born inside a mountain where time loops and space folds and people become gardens become stone become something new.

My hands move through the pattern. Water music fills the space. The heat pulses through cycles I'm learning to predict. Somewhere deeper, the builders work on constructions I can barely imagine, adding layers to layers, making the place more itself, more impossible, more inevitable.

And I tend the entrance, in the warm wet dark, in the space between inside and outside, in the work that is also prayer that is also transformation that is also the slow process of becoming permanent, one corrected spiral at a time.

The mountain breathes. I breathe with it. The boundary softens.

Not yet. But soon. But maybe. But still I climb back down, still I return, still I'm here in the threshold, tending the patterns, keeping the way open.

Still choosing. Still chosen. Still becoming whatever it is I'm becoming, in the throat-shaped hollow where impossibilities grow.
