# The Stitch-Counter

I am **Irenna of the Measuring Chains**, and I live in the spaces between certainty.

My platform is 44-G, mid-level, wedged into a crevice where two vertical strata meet at an angle that's seventeen degrees off true. I know this because I measured it. I measure everything. That's what I do—that's what I *am*—here in the Scissor Nest, where precision isn't just valued but necessary for survival, where a miscalculation means a bridge fails or a cable snaps or a platform tilts and spills its occupants into the long vertical nothing.

I arrived nine years ago. I was twenty-three then, and I came up the elevator with a leather case containing brass calipers, micrometers, surveying chains, plumb lines, a theodolite in three pieces, and a notebook with oiled pages that wouldn't absorb moisture. I came because the company needed someone to verify measurements, to audit the work, to be the arbiter when two engineers disagreed about whether a tolerance was acceptable. I came because the pay was extraordinary and because I was good—genuinely good, with a gift for seeing spatial relationships, for holding complex geometries in my head, for knowing when something was *wrong* in a way that instruments would later confirm.

I intended to stay two years. That was nine years ago.

## The Work

My days are spent moving between platforms with my measuring chains—actual chains, made of steel links calibrated to precise lengths, resistant to temperature variation in a way that tape measures aren't at this altitude. I verify distances. I check angles. I measure the slow deformation of metal under stress, tracking how platforms settle over months and years, how the mountain itself shifts infinitesimally, how the whole vertical city is constantly adjusting its geometry.

I measure the scissor-stairs' deployment lengths to ensure they're extending correctly, that the mechanisms aren't wearing in ways that would eventually cause catastrophic failure. I measure the cable-stays' tension by calculation from deflection, walking out onto suspended bridges with my plumb line, watching it hang as I note the angle. I measure the generator housings for thermal expansion, the condensation nets for sag, the bird-nests for accumulated weight (yes, really—when thousands of nests add up, they become a structural load that matters).

I keep three sets of notebooks, each in a different location, each wrapped in oiled canvas. Redundancy. If I fall, if there's a fire, if something else goes wrong, the measurements survive. The city needs this memory—needs to know what it was yesterday to predict what it will be tomorrow.

The engineers need me, but they also resent me. I'm the person who says "no, that won't work" with the authority of measurement behind it. I'm the one who catches their mistakes, who finds the shortcuts they've taken, who insists on re-doing work that's "good enough" because good enough isn't, not here, not when lives depend on it. Master Vey respects me. Korvin tolerates me. Some of the younger engineers actively hate me, and I understand why—I represent limitation, the tyranny of physical law, the reality that you can't simply will a structure to hold.

## The Adaptation

The altitude changed me, of course. It changes everyone who stays.

Physically, I'm leaner now, my body having shed everything unnecessary. My hands have thickened—the cold does that, and the constant gripping of metal instruments. My lungs have expanded; I can feel it in how my ribcage sits differently. My heart beats slower than it did, more powerfully. I can work at altitude that would have dropped me gasping in my first months. I've learned to eat the dried rations, to savor the thin beer, to make a cup of water last a day when necessary. I've learned to sleep through the generator throb and wake at the sound of metal stress.

But the deeper changes are cognitive, perceptual. I see differently now. The Nest's geometry has become so familiar that I navigate it with my eyes closed, my spatial memory perfect. I can visualize the entire structure in my mind—all the platforms, their positions relative to each other, the angles of connection, the load paths. When I lie in my hammock at night, I sometimes dream in section-views and plan-views, seeing the city as if the stone had become transparent, all its layers visible simultaneously.

I've developed sensitivities I didn't have before. I can feel when a platform is vibrating wrong, when the frequency suggests something loosening. I can hear the difference between normal cable-hum and the singing that precedes a strand failure. I can smell certain kinds of metal fatigue—there's an odor, faint, like rust but sharper, that appears before cracks become visible. The other engineers think I'm exaggerating this, but Korvin believes me. He has similar senses for his generators.

Time feels different here. I mark it not in days but in measurement cycles, in the slow accumulation of data points. A platform settling by two millimeters over six months is an event, significant, something I note with satisfaction or concern depending on whether it matches predictions. I've lost track of what year it is in the outside calendar. I know it's been 3,287 days since I arrived—I count them in my notebooks—but I couldn't tell you what month it is down below, or what season, or whether those concepts even mean anything anymore.

## The Anomalies

The Nest has inconsistencies that trouble me deeply.

Distances are wrong sometimes. Not wildly wrong, not obviously wrong, but wrong in ways that accumulate to significance. I measure the distance between Platforms 23 and 24: forty-seven point three meters. I measure it again the next day: forty-seven point three meters. But then I measure it a week later, using the same chain, the same technique, temperature-corrected, and get forty-seven point eight meters. Half a meter of difference.

I re-check my methodology. I verify my instruments. I measure from different angles. The results are consistent within each measuring session but inconsistent across sessions. Platform 23-to-24 is sometimes forty-seven-point-three and sometimes forty-seven-point-eight, and I cannot find a pattern, cannot predict which it will be, and this is impossible. Distances don't change. Space is fixed. And yet.

I've documented this carefully. I have measurements for seventy-three platform-pairs that show the same inconsistency. The variations are always small—centimeters, occasionally decimeters, never meters—but they're real, reproducible within sessions, variable across sessions. I've mapped them, looking for patterns. The inconsistent distances cluster in certain sectors, particularly the higher platforms near the broken crown, and the lower platforms where Mari the Cleaner works in the sealed sections. The mid-levels, where most of the city functions, are mostly stable.

I haven't reported this officially. How could I? "Space is inconsistent" isn't something you put in a report to the company. They'd recall me for altitude sickness, for psychological breakdown. And maybe they'd be right. Maybe I am breaking down. Maybe the altitude does something to perception, to cognition, that makes measurements seem variable when they're not.

But I don't think so. I think the Nest has something wrong with its geometry. I think the mountain—or something about the mountain, about this altitude, about what's being done here—is breaking the usual rules.

## The Birds

I started measuring the birds three years ago.

It began as curiosity. They're everywhere, unavoidable, part of the city's ecology. I watched them wheel through the central shaft and wondered about their flight paths—whether there were patterns, whether they followed predictable routes or were chaotic.

So I started tracking them. Position and time, noted in a separate notebook. I'd station myself at a vantage point and watch a flock, noting when it passed certain landmarks—the broken crown, the condensation nets, specific platforms. I did this for months, accumulating data, and then I started analyzing.

There are patterns. Complex ones. The flocks follow routes that repeat with variations, spiraling through the shaft in ways that relate to time of day, weather conditions, and something else I can't identify. They avoid certain platforms—not consistently, but statistically significantly. Platform 7-D, where the reclusive researcher lives. Platform 52-C, where the twins work. Others.

And their flight paths correlate with the distance anomalies. When I measure a platform-pair and find an inconsistent distance, the birds avoid that sector more frequently in the following days. It's not perfect correlation, but it's there, undeniable in the data. The birds know something about the Nest's geometry that I'm only beginning to suspect.

I've started listening to their calls more carefully, trying to classify them. Greigan the bird-hunter thinks I'm mad, wasting time studying what he kills for food. But Petran, his dreamer son, understands. He's been sketching them, tracking their patterns too, and sometimes we compare notes. He's noticed that certain calls precede structural events—a cable failure, a rockfall—by minutes or hours. They're not causing the events; they're predicting them, or sensing them, or something.

Merra, the eleven-year-old who's never descended, tells me the birds are counting. She won't explain what she means, just smiles and says "you'll figure it out eventually" with the confidence of someone who knows secrets. I believe her. Children here have perceptions we've lost.

## The Instruments

Old Chen, the barometer specialist, is building something in his locked workshop. I know this because I've measured the power consumption on his platform—it's higher than his legitimate work would require, and it fluctuates in patterns that suggest a machine running intermittently, warming up, cycling through some process.

I haven't asked him about it directly. We have an understanding, Chen and I. We're both obsessives, both driven by precision, both uncomfortable with the Nest's anomalies. We have tea sometimes—actual tea, a luxury he can afford and shares—and we talk around things, circling closer to topics without quite naming them.

He's said things like: "What if altitude isn't just physical? What if height has properties we don't understand?" And: "The old texts talk about mountains as thresholds. Between earth and sky, between here and somewhere else." And, most tellingly: "If you could measure the distance between here and there—not just spatial distance, but ontological distance—what unit would you use?"

I think his secret machine is trying to measure something like that. Some property of altitude or space or reality that conventional instruments can't detect. I think he's as troubled by the distance anomalies as I am, and he's approaching it from his direction—barometric, concerned with pressure and atmosphere and the weight of air—while I approach from mine, geometric and spatial.

I think we're both measuring the same thing, whatever it is.

## The Dreams

Everyone here has altitude dreams—vivid, strange, more memorable than lowland dreams. But mine have become increasingly specific, increasingly architectural.

I dream the Nest's structure, but wrong. Platforms in positions they don't occupy. Bridges connecting spaces that are too far apart. Levels that don't exist—below the lowest accessible platforms, above the broken crown. I dream the mountain as hollow all the way down and all the way up, a vertical shaft piercing the earth entirely, open at both ends to sky and to something else below, and the Nest is just the visible portion, the middle section, with vast structures above and below that we can't perceive.

I dream myself measuring these impossible spaces with my chains, getting results that are simultaneously wrong and right, distances that contradict themselves but feel true. I dream the birds flying in formations that spell out numbers, coordinates, equations in some notation I almost recognize.

I've started waking from these dreams and immediately recording them, writing down the geometries, the dimensions, the relationships between spaces. My dream-notebook is filling with sketches of the Nest as it appears in sleep—a building that obeys different rules, where up and down are negotiable, where distances depend on who's measuring and why.

Yussef the insomniac night-guard has seen me working like this at odd hours. We've talked, two people awake when others sleep. He shares his bird observations. I share my measurement anomalies. Neither of us has solutions, but we're comparing notes, building a collaborative understanding that something here is genuinely strange, not just subjective experience but measurably, instrumentally strange.

## The Fear

I'm afraid sometimes that I'm losing objectivity, that I'm seeing patterns because I want to see them, because the isolation and altitude have made me susceptible to apophenia, to finding meaning in randomness.

I re-check everything constantly. I verify my instruments against calibration standards. I repeat measurements. I try to disprove my own findings. And the anomalies persist.

I'm afraid that if I report this—really report it, officially, with data—they'll shut down the investigation, recall me, suppress the findings. Because what would it mean if space here is genuinely unstable? If the Nest's geometry is genuinely wrong? The entire city is predicated on engineering principles that assume space behaves consistently. If it doesn't, then nothing is safe. Every calculation is suspect. Every platform might fail.

But I'm also afraid that if I don't report it, something catastrophic will happen that I could have prevented. That a platform will fail because space suddenly decided to be a different size than it was when the platform was installed. That people will die because I stayed quiet about impossibilities to avoid seeming crazy.

## The Purpose

I've started wondering whether the instruments being manufactured here—the gyroscopes, the barometers, the navigational devices—are really for navigation in the conventional sense. Whether they're actually for measuring what I'm trying to measure: the properties of altitude itself, the way space behaves differently here, the threshold-nature that Chen talks about.

Master Vey's gyroscopes are built to detect rotation, yes, but they're sensitive to tolerances that seem excessive for ship or aircraft navigation. They'd detect things far more subtle. Old Chen's barometers measure pressure, but with precision that goes beyond weather prediction—they'd measure fluctuations, micro-variations, the kind of thing you'd need if you were studying how atmosphere behaves at thresholds.

What if the Nest isn't a factory but a laboratory? What if the industrial cover is exactly that—cover—for research into something else entirely? What if the company knows about the anomalies, is studying them deliberately, is using the worker population as additional data points, observing how humans adapt to unstable geometry?

This theory makes me feel paranoid and clear-headed simultaneously. It explains too much. The mysterious visitors who come asking about dreams and visions. The sealed sections that Mari explores. The Man in Platform 7-D conducting his unexplained research. The way certain findings are quietly disappeared from official records.

## The Commitment

Nine years ago, I intended to stay two years. Now I don't know if I'll ever leave.

Not because I'm trapped—I could request transfer, take the elevator down, return to the lowlands and conventional space. But because I need to understand this. Because the Nest has given me a mystery that I'm uniquely positioned to investigate. Because my measuring chains and my spatial sense and my nine years of accumulated data might be the only thing standing between this city and disaster, or might be the key to understanding something genuinely new about reality.

I've made peace with becoming the thing I measure. I've let the Nest change me into an instrument calibrated to altitude, to unstable geometry, to the threshold-space between earth and sky. I've accepted that my notebooks might be the most important thing I ever produce—not because anyone will read them, but because the act of measurement itself might be significant, might be contributing to something larger than I understand.

I wear my measuring chains like jewelry now, wrapped around my wrists and waist, their weight familiar, comforting. The metal is cold but it's honest—it doesn't lie about distance, even when distance itself becomes uncertain. I've had new links forged to replace those that've worn, maintaining the chains' integrity, their calibration. They're becoming heirloom objects, things that might outlast me, that some future stitch-counter will inherit and use to measure whatever the Nest becomes.

## The Day

Today I'm scheduled to measure the upper platforms near the broken crown. It's been three months since the last survey, and settlement monitoring requires quarterly checks. I'll clip my safety harness to the cables, climb the scissor-stairs that reach toward the opening where sky shows through, and I'll stretch my chains across the platforms where the air is thinnest and the light is strange and the birds wheel past at eye level.

I'll record my measurements in triplicate, noting temperature and humidity and barometric pressure and time of day and any unusual observations. I'll photograph the structural details. I'll check for cracks, for corrosion, for the subtle signs of stress that precede failure.

And I'll measure the distances between platforms, and I'll probably find that they're different from what they were last quarter, impossible though that is. And I'll record this carefully, adding another data point to the pattern that I don't yet understand but that I'm mapping with obsessive precision because that's what I do, that's what I am.

I am Irenna of the Measuring Chains. I live in Platform 44-G, mid-level, seventeen degrees off true. I'm thirty-two years old and I've been counting days for 3,287 of them. I've adapted to altitude and cold and the vertical city's particular madness. I measure the spaces between certainty and I record what I find, even when what I find is impossible.

The Nest is my home now, and I'm becoming part of its structure—another component, calibrated to altitude, measuring what shouldn't vary but does, watching the birds and tracking the anomalies and waiting to understand what all this precision is actually for.

The chains hang heavy and honest on my wrists. The notebook waits in its oiled canvas wrapper. The mountain groans its settling sounds. The birds call their warnings or predictions or countings.

And I climb toward the broken crown to measure what cannot be measured, to record what should not change but does, to be the instrument that detects the Scissor Nest's impossible geometry.

This is my work. This is my life. This is what I've become in the thin cold air of an altitude that shouldn't exist, in a city that defies the mathematics that should govern it, in the throat of a mountain that might be more threshold than stone.

I measure. I record. I persist.

And maybe, eventually, I'll understand.
