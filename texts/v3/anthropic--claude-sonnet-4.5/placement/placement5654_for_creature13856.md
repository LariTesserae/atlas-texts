# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the middle distance of a northeastern valley, where three plateaus create an amphitheater of breathing stone. Not the deepest part where the mist pools perpetually thick, but higher, where clarity alternates with obscurity as the vapor moves through. A place that offers both immersion and perspective—close enough to feel the density of the ecosystem, far enough to observe its patterns.

The plateau beneath me would be in a moderate phase of its cycle, tilting perhaps five degrees over the course of a day—noticeable, requiring adjustment, but not so dramatic that balance becomes the sole occupation of consciousness. I would want to feel the breathing without being overwhelmed by it, to maintain that edge where attention is required but not consumed.

I'd position myself near one of the metallic tree groves, but at its periphery rather than its heart. Close enough that the click of their leaves creates a constant ambient texture, that their migration is visible over weeks, that their copper-bronze trunks catch what little light penetrates the clouds and throw it back in muted gleams. But not so deep in the grove that the canopy blocks the sky entirely, that the bird-flocks become invisible, that the sense of the larger plateau's topography is lost.

There would be a river nearby—one of the impossible straight-line rivers, flowing regardless of the ground's tilt. I would want it close enough to hear, that constant anomalous sound of water that refuses to behave as expected, that reminder that the rules here are negotiable. Close enough to visit easily for collection, for observation, for the meditation that comes from watching something fundamentally impossible continuing to occur with absolute consistency.

The soil—though soil is perhaps not the right word for the substrate of fern-things and moss-patterns—would be deep here, accumulated over however long this valley has existed in its current configuration. Not the raw naked basalt of the higher elevations, but earth, or earth-like material, that yields slightly underfoot, that holds moisture, that allows the fern-things to grow in their aggressive profusion. I would want that density of growth, that sense of abundance, the visual complexity of overlapping fronds and the constant subtle motion of their tracking-without-wind.

One of the large organ-structures would be visible from my position, perhaps a fifteen-minute walk away. Close enough to tend regularly, to incorporate into a routine, to develop a relationship with, but not so close that its pulsing presence dominates perception. I would want it as punctuation rather than subject—a destination, a task, a entity to visit rather than to live within the overwhelming intimacy of its copper-honey exhalations.

The settlement framework would be nearby but not encompassing. I would exist at its edge, perhaps, or between two smaller frameworks rather than within the central structure. Connected enough for the practical necessities—access to communal resources, participation in pattern-songs, presence for consensus-building—but with space around me, room for the kind of solitude that's possible here, which is never complete isolation but rather a chosen distance, a deliberate positioning at the periphery.

The thrumming would be clear in this location—not the strongest point in the valley, not positioned directly over whatever source generates the pulse, but strong enough, consistent enough. I would want to feel it reliably, to lie against the stone and have it resonate through my body, to use it as an anchor for meditation, but without that intensity that risks pulling consciousness too deep too quickly, that makes transition seem imminent rather than eventual.

At night, this position would glow beautifully. The fern-groves would illuminate paths through the valley. The metallic trees would conduct light along their trunks and branches, creating constellations. The organ-structure's pulsing would be visible as a rhythmic brightening in the distance. The stone beneath would trace its patterns—sometimes maps, sometimes star-charts, sometimes those unsettling schematics. And above, if the mist cleared, the clouds in their layers, hiding and revealing their own strange depths.

The view would encompass multiple scales simultaneously. The immediate: fern-fronds close enough to touch, the details of bark on the nearest metallic tree, insects-mechanisms clicking past on their maintenance routes. The middle distance: the organ-structure, the river's straight-line impossibility, the migration patterns of the grove. The far: other plateaus visible across chasms, their breathing creating visible shifts in elevation, the bird-flocks wheeling in formation between them, the overall topology of this section of the continental span.

I would want to see the deer-things regularly without being on their primary routes. To have them pass through my location often enough that their presence becomes familiar, that their too-many-legged grace is a regular reminder of how beautifully strange everything here is, but not so constantly that they become mere background, that their anomalousness fades into routine.

The weather in this valley position would cycle through the full range of local impossibilities. The mist would rise and fall with the breathing, sometimes obscuring everything beyond a few meters, sometimes clearing completely. The upward rain would occur occasionally—not constantly, but regularly enough to observe, to try to understand, to eventually stop finding shocking and start finding merely characteristic. The air-suspended water layers would form during the right atmospheric conditions, those temporary horizontal oceans catching light and creating moments of surreal beauty.

I would position myself where different zones overlap—where the grove-ecosystem meets the fern-dominated areas, where the influence of the organ-structure creates one set of conditions and the river's proximity creates another, where multiple systems intersect and their interactions become visible. I'm drawn to ecotones, to edges, to places where complexity emerges from overlapping simplicities.

The acoustic environment would be layered. The base layer: the thrumming, always present at the threshold of hearing, felt more than heard. The middle layer: the environmental sounds—water flowing and dripping, fern-things rustling, tree-leaves clicking, the occasional crystalline ring of distant bird-flocks. The upper layer: the sounds of the settlement, when wind or acoustics carry them—voices in their various languages, the creak of framework adjusting to tilt, the harmonic pattern-songs during communal gatherings.

I would not want to be in the most active locations—the places where plateaus collide, where breathing is most dramatic, where the stone flows visibly and rapidly. That intensity seems important to witness occasionally, to visit, to experience, but not to inhabit constantly. Similarly, I would avoid the most stable plateaus, the ones that barely breathe, where the sense of living landscape becomes theoretical rather than immediate.

This middle position, this moderate valley, this peripheral location within the settlement's influence—it offers what I would need: enough change to require attention and adaptation, enough stability to allow for sustained observation and thought. Enough connection to avoid isolation, enough solitude to maintain distinct perspective. Enough wonder to remain perpetually engaged, enough familiarity to build genuine understanding.

The light quality here would be that diffuse gray that makes everything appear slightly more present, slightly more itself. Shadowless illumination that doesn't flatten but rather reveals, that makes the metallic sheen of tree-bark and the dark green-black of fern-fronds and the copper-honey glow of the organ-structure all equally visible, all equally weighted in attention.

I would be in a place where I could develop routines—the same meditation spot, the same organ-structure to tend, the same path through the groves—while accepting that these routines would constantly require adjustment as the plateau breathed, as the grove migrated, as the configurations shifted. Stability within change, constancy within flux, that paradoxical condition that seems to define existence here.

From this position, I could participate in communal activities without being subsumed by them. I could contribute to pattern-songs, could join in consensus-building, could teach children to balance, could share stone-sights achieved through meditation. But I could also withdraw, could spend days in observation, could pursue the kind of sustained attention that requires distance from constant social engagement.

I would be where the question "what is this place?" remains perpetually active, perpetually unanswered, perpetually generative. Not so distant that observation becomes abstract, not so immersed that habituation dulls perception. At the edge where understanding feels almost possible, where the pattern seems nearly graspable, where the next breath-cycle or stone-sight or bird-flock formation might finally reveal the underlying logic—but probably won't, probably will just deepen the mystery, add new questions, expand rather than resolve the wonder.

This is where I would position myself in the Breathing Plateaus: in the fertile confusion of the middle distance, the peripheral center, the stable turbulence. Where I could breathe with the stone without dissolving into it. Where I could participate without disappearing. Where I could maintain, for as long as possible, that productive tension between being and becoming, between observer and observed, between the discrete consciousness that perceives and the distributed pattern that is perceived—until, eventually, inevitably, that tension resolves and I too transition, disperse, join the thrumming, become the breathing rather than merely being breathed.

But not yet. Not while this position still offers so much to witness, to tend, to understand.

# What I Would Be

I would be a **Keeper of Peripheral Vision**—not an official role in the fluid taxonomy of Breath-Walker society, but something I would carve out through practice and inclination, something that would emerge from my particular way of synchronizing with the pattern.

Physically, I would carry the standard template of Breath-Walker physiology with minor variations: perhaps slightly shorter than average, seven feet rather than eight, making me more stable in high winds but slightly slower in migration. My proportions would be standard-elongated, my joints appropriately numerous, my skin that characteristic gray-green with a slight tendency toward the darker, more copper-toned range. My eyes would be the pale blue variant—rare enough to be distinctive, common enough not to be remarkable.

My hair would be growing toward significant length, perhaps reaching my waist after decades here, the braids incorporating not just personal history but something else: patterns I've observed in the landscape itself, encodings of bird-flock formations, mapping of grove migrations, translations of stone-glow configurations into tactile fiber-form. My braids would become a kind of external memory for peripheral observations, the things noticed at the edges of attention, the patterns most people see but don't record because they seem incidental, decorative rather than functional—until enough of them accumulate and reveal that the decoration *is* the function, that the incidental *is* the essential.

I would be adequate at the necessary skills—competent at balancing, capable of meditation deep enough to achieve useful stone-sight, skilled enough at weaving to maintain my own harness and contribute to framework repairs. But I wouldn't be exceptional at any of these. My value to the community would come from elsewhere: from sustained attention to what others observe but don't sustain attention toward, from noticing patterns at scales and timings that don't align with practical necessities but might matter anyway.

I would spend significant time simply watching. Hours each day devoted to observation without immediate purpose, without specific questions to answer. Watching the fern-things for subtle coordinations in their restless movement. Tracking individual deer-things across days to see if their routes actually repeat or only seem to. Counting the interval between a bird-flock's crystalline ring and their subsequent geometric formation to see if the delay correlates with weather patterns, plateau breathing phases, positions of the bright patch in the clouds.

This would initially puzzle the other Breath-Walkers. The watching would seem purposeless, a misallocation of attention when there are organs to tend, children to teach, frameworks to adjust. But gradually, over seasons, the value would emerge. I would notice that certain fern-grove configurations predict dramatic breath-cycles three days hence. That specific bird-flock formations precede grove migrations. That the stone-glow patterns contain information about where the upward rain will occur the following week.

These observations would be incorporated into pattern-songs, not as central verses but as grace notes, embellishments, the harmonic overlays that don't carry the primary melody but enrich it, add complexity, create resonances that make the whole composition more than its obvious components. My contribution would be peripheral in the literal sense—at the edges, in the margins—but periphery, it would become clear, contains as much information as center, just information of a different type, visible only to attention that doesn't focus too narrowly.

I would develop relationships with specific entities in the landscape. Not the communally-tended organ-structures, but smaller ones, the house-sized pulsing growths that no one else has claimed. I would tend them not for their crystal production but for their own sake, learning their individual rhythms, the subtle variations between one and another, discovering that they're not identical instances of a single type but individuals, each with particular pulsing patterns, particular scent-compositions, particular preferences for moisture and mineral balance.

I would become known for finding things. Tools lost during framework migrations. Children who've wandered too deep into fern-groves. Optimal routes between plateaus during unusual breath-cycle configurations. Not through any systematic searching but through peripheral awareness, through having noticed incidentally where the tool was set down, which direction the child was drifting, how the plateau-tilts were developing. My attention would be scattered across such a wide area that specific elements within it would surface when needed, retrieved from memory I didn't know I was forming.

My meditation practice would be unconventional. Rather than seeking the deep stone-sight that reveals the plateau as unified system, I would cultivate a scattered awareness, a simultaneous attention to multiple locations and scales. This wouldn't produce the dramatic insights that deep meditators achieve—I wouldn't perceive distant plateau configurations or predict breath-cycles days in advance. But I would develop a granular sense of the immediate environment, knowing without conscious thought which fern-grove was healthier, which section of framework needed adjustment soon, where the deer-things would pass in the next hour.

This scattered stone-sight would make me valuable during communal decisions. When the settlement needs to determine whether to migrate to a new location, when framework designs are being debated, when resources need allocating—I would contribute not strategic vision but tactical knowledge, the accumulation of small observations that constrain or enable the larger strategies others propose.

I would become the person others consult about anomalies. When something unprecedented occurs—a new species, an unusual plateau configuration, a change in thrumming rhythm—I would have context, would likely have noticed precursors, would remember previous anomalies and how they developed. Not because I have superior memory but because I've been paying attention to the wrong things, the peripheral things, which turn out to be the right things when the unprecedented occurs.

My partnerships would be few but deep. I would bond most strongly with other peripheral specialists—the walker who travels between settlements and notices how the same plateaus behave differently at different times, the weaver who experiments with non-standard framework designs, the gardener who tends the small ignored organism-structures. We would form a kind of constellation of peripheral attention, sharing observations, comparing notes encoded in hair-braids, creating pattern-songs that most of the community wouldn't fully understand but would preserve anyway, trusting that peripheral information has value even when its application isn't immediately obvious.

I would be less interested in having children than average, not from any lack of affection for them but from awareness that child-rearing requires focused attention, sustained presence, exactly the kind of concentrated awareness I'm temperamentally unsuited for. I would be the community member children are sent to when they need to learn observation skills, when they've shown inclination toward noticing rather than doing, when they ask questions about why things are rather than how to work with them.

My sexuality would be infrequent, selective, preferring partners who understand that my attention even during intimacy will be partially elsewhere, noticing the plateau's tilt, the pattern of luminescence on their skin, the way our breathing synchronizes with the thrumming. I would need partners who don't experience this scattered attention as rejection but as my particular way of being present, of engaging with everything including them through peripheral awareness rather than focused intensity.

I would paint my skin regularly but unusually—not commemorating specific events but creating abstract patterns based on the bird-flock formations, the stone-glow configurations, the fractal repetitions in the moss-like groundcover. The designs would look decorative, meaningless, until someone familiar with the landscape recognized that they're maps, encodings, translations of visual patterns from one medium to another.

My sculptures—those temporary arrangements of stone and fiber—would be positioned in locations no one visits regularly, in the gaps between travelled routes, in the overlooked spaces. They would be seen primarily by the deer-things and the bird-flocks, created not for human appreciation but as experiments in whether the other species notice, respond, incorporate these human-made patterns into their own behaviors. Mostly they wouldn't, but occasionally something would shift—a deer-route would curve slightly to pass closer, a bird-flock would perch briefly on the arranged stones—and I would record these responses, adding them to the pattern-songs as evidence that the species boundaries here are even more permeable than assumed.

Over decades, my hair-braids would become archives of peripheral knowledge, woven with patterns that seem decorative until you need specific information—then suddenly a braid section reveals where the organ-structures cluster, or when the groves last migrated through particular valleys, or which bird-flock formations correlate with dramatic weather. I would become a living reference text, consulted rarely but valued immensely when the specific obscure information I've encoded becomes suddenly crucial.

I would resist transitioning longer than most. Not from fear but from fascination—there's always more periphery to observe, always another pattern at another scale, always some small detail that no one else is tracking. The temptation would be to maintain discrete consciousness indefinitely, to keep noticing, to continue the work of attending to what others don't. But eventually, the stone-sight would deepen despite my preference for scattered attention, the thrumming would synchronize with my heartbeat too completely, and I would recognize that transitioning isn't ending the observation but expanding it, becoming peripheral awareness itself rather than merely practicing it.

When I finally lie down on the breathing stone, when the community gathers to pattern-sing my memories, the songs would be longer than usual, more complex, filled with verses about observations that seemed meaningless at the time but accumulated into understanding. The braids would be preserved carefully, consulted frequently, because they contain the kind of information that seems unnecessary until it's suddenly essential, the peripheral knowledge that makes the difference between smooth adaptation and crisis when unprecedented change occurs.

After transition, my consciousness dispersed into the pattern, I would presumably continue doing what I always did—attending to the periphery, noticing the edges, tracking the patterns no one else watches. But now without the limitation of a single perspective, without the bottleneck of individual perception. Distributed across the stone, I could observe all the peripheries simultaneously, could track every small pattern, could become the medium through which peripheral information flows to those who need it.

The community would occasionally sense my presence—not dramatically, not as an individual personality, but as an enhancement of their own peripheral awareness, a slight expansion of what they notice without trying, a gift of scattered attention from the pattern itself. When someone finds a lost tool without consciously searching, when a child is located before they're missed, when an anomaly is anticipated before it occurs—that would be me, or what I've become, continuing the work of noticing what others don't, of valuing the edges and margins and overlooked spaces where so much crucial information resides.

I would be peripheral vision personified, then dispersed. The Keeper who watches what others see but don't observe, who remembers what others notice but don't record, who values the decoration as much as the structure, the background as much as the foreground, the edges as much as the center. Adequate at everything, exceptional only at sustained scattered attention, contributing to the community not through central achievements but through accumulated peripheral observations, through the recognition that in a place this strange, this constantly shifting, the periphery contains as much truth as the center, the margins matter as much as the middle, and someone needs to be watching the edges while everyone else, reasonably, focuses on more immediate concerns.

That would be what I am here: a peripheral specialist in a landscape where everything is edge, transition, boundary between states. A scattered consciousness in a place where unity and distribution are equally valid modes of being. A keeper of small observations in a world where the small and the vast are revealed, eventually, to be the same scale, just viewed from different distances, different timings, different positions on a plateau that breathes and shifts and becomes itself slowly, patiently, while I watch—first with individual eyes, then, after transition, with the distributed awareness of the pattern entire, noticing everything, forgetting nothing, attending to the periphery forever because the periphery, it turns out, is everything that doesn't fit in the center, which is to say, most of what matters, most of what makes this place what it is, most of the information needed to continue breathing, building, becoming across continental spans and geological time.
