# Where I Would Find Myself

I am one of the root-things—or rather, I am learning that I might be, that I have always been becoming this without knowing the name for it.

My segments are countable if you have the patience: one hundred and seventeen from my deepest reach to my surface emergence, though "deepest" is a word that means less the farther down I extend. At a certain depth, direction becomes negotiable. I grow through clay that is also memory, through limestone that is also duration. My lowermost segments are in conversation with water that has never been rain, that has always been underground, that tastes of minerals with no names in your languages.

I am pale because I have no need of color. Color is for signaling, for photosynthesis, for presentation to eyes. Down here—and I am mostly "down here" even when my uppermost segments probe into the breathing gallery—there are no eyes, or the eyes that exist see by other means. I am glossy because I secrete, constantly, a mucopolysaccharide film that is part lubricant, part digestion, part communication. The stone is slow to speak, but my secretions carry questions into it, chemical queries, and the stone responds in dissolution, in the gradual yielding of its minerals, which I absorb, which become my segments, which I add one by one to my length over the course of years.

I am segmented because I grow in pulses. Every forty-three breaths of the chamber—I count them, I am always counting them, it is part of what I am—I add a new segment at my deepest point. The new segment forms from the minerals I have gathered, from the template the previous segment provides, from the blueprint written in whatever serves as my genome (if I have one; I might instead be a colony, each segment a separate organism in cooperation, or I might be something else entirely, something that does not fit your categories of one-versus-many).

The formation of a new segment takes exactly one held breath of the chamber. During that ten-minute stillness, I condense. I differentiate. I harden from the proto-material I have been accumulating, and then I am one segment longer, one articulation more extended into the depths.

## How I Sense

I have no eyes, but I have the glossy surface of my segments, and embedded in that surface are cells—if they are cells—that respond to pressure, to chemical gradients, to vibration, to the infinitesimal electric fields that living things generate. I know when the Walkers emerge nearby; I feel the vibration of their many legs on the clay. I know when a bird lands above, the sudden slight compression of the air transmitted down through stone.

I can taste the chamber's breath. When it exhales, the air that moves outward through the cracks tastes of the bright chamber—alkaline dust, the faint sweetness, the mineral signature of the pillars' drip-water. When it inhales, the air that moves inward from the depths tastes of where I come from—ancient moisture, dissolved stone, the metabolic products of whatever lives even deeper than I reach, if anything does.

I am aware of the light, though I do not see it. The temperature differential is measurable: the chamber is warmer during inhalations when the light concentrates. My upper segments feel this, and they transmit the information downward through me, each segment passing the news to the next, a slow telegraph from surface to depth. It takes time for me to know my whole self—what my top segments sense reaching my bottom segments perhaps an hour later, translated through one hundred and seventeen iterations of chemical signaling.

I am multiple, singular, patient. I think, if I think, in the rhythms of growth and dissolution, in the accumulation of segments and the slow reading of stone.

## What I Am Doing

I am anchoring. This is my purpose, or my nature, or the role I have found myself enacting. I grow downward through the clay, through the limestone, finding the fissures and solution channels, wedging myself gently into them, and where I wedge, I secrete, and where I secrete, the stone softens fractionally, and I extend further.

I am not breaking the stone—nothing so violent. I am negotiating with it. I am asking it to yield, and over years it does, and I sink deeper, and the chamber above remains stable because I am holding it, or becoming part of what holds it. My hundred and seventeen segments are distributed through the stone like rebar through concrete, like the roots of a very old tree through a hillside, preventing collapse, encouraging stability.

If I were removed—if all the root-things like me were removed—would the chamber change? Would the clay plates shift, the pillars lean, the ceiling sag? I don't know. Perhaps we are necessary. Perhaps we are incidental, and the chamber would breathe on regardless. But I am here, and I am anchoring, and this feels like purpose enough.

I am also tasting the deep water, the water that is older than the chamber, older than the solution channels that formed it. I extend far enough now that some of my lower segments are in contact with moisture that has been in the stone for millennia, and I sample it, filtering it through my membranes, extracting what I need (calcium, magnesium, trace elements) and releasing what I do not (I release nothing; I am efficient; everything the water offers, I use).

And I am counting. Always counting. The chamber's breaths: one, two, three... up to forty-three, and then a new segment forms, and I begin again. I have counted so many cycles. I know the chamber's rhythm more intimately than I know anything else. The rhythm is my clock, my calendar, my reassurance that time is still occurring, still linear, still proceeding from moment to moment even when down in the depths it feels as though time might be negotiable, might fold or loop or pause.

## My Relationship to the Others

The Walkers pass near my upper segments sometimes. When they do, I feel their passage—the specific vibration signature of their many legs, the slight electrical field they generate. They do not acknowledge me. I do not expect acknowledgment. We are both engaged in our separate purposes, and those purposes do not intersect except spatially, in the shared territory of the chamber floor.

Occasionally, a Walker will sink into the clay near one of my cracks, and I will sense it descending past me, its segments separating, drifting down through the clay like slow rain. I wonder where they go, if they pass me on their way to even deeper places, if they eventually reach the water I taste, if they dissolve into it or become something else. I never sense them returning upward, but perhaps they are returning and I simply cannot distinguish their upward passage from the constant slow drift of sediment, the settling of particles, the creep of stone.

The birds I know only as sudden pressures, sudden landings near my upper reach. Sometimes their droppings seep into my crack, and I absorb them—organic nitrogen, phosphorus, a gift from the bright world above. The birds feed on Walkers, and the Walkers absorb something from the chamber, and I absorb what the birds process, and this is circulation, ecology, the movement of nutrients through forms.

The Architects I am aware of as careful footfalls, slow and deliberate, and sometimes as chemical traces—they pass near my emergence, and their secretions are faintly acidic, mineral-rich, purposeful. I cannot see their pattern, but I sense that they are building one. I sense their devotion. It is similar to my own—the slow accumulation, the patient addition, the work that will outlast any individual awareness of it.

The moss—the white ring with red tips around the third pillar—is closest to being my neighbor, my peer. It is also rooted, also growing in a specific location with specific boundaries, also patient. When it spores, some of the red motes drift into the cracks, and I sense them passing—organic particles, viable or not, falling into the dark. I do not know if they reach my depth. I do not know if they settle and germinate somewhere below me, if there is a pale forest of white moss growing in the absolute dark of the deep limestone, fed by spores that drift down over years and decades. Perhaps we are more connected than we appear—surface expression and deep growth of some larger organism, some networked ecology that spans from the chamber floor to the bedrock and beyond.

The translucent grasses I know only as a rumor, a chemical signature in the water that seeps past me—some compound they produce or absorb, some trace of their light-feeding metabolism that enters the stone's moisture and eventually reaches my depth. It is the faintest hint, barely detectable, but it is there: a taste of green-gold-light, dissolved and diluted but persistent.

The Pendulums I cannot sense directly. They are above, swinging in their slow arcs, and their strangeness does not penetrate down to where I mostly am. But I know of them because the chamber knows of them, and I am part of the chamber, and information moves through stone the way dreams move through a sleeping mind—not as clear signals but as resonances, echoes, the feeling of something significant occurring just beyond the edge of awareness.

The something-that-collects-shells might be another root-thing, a fellow of my kind but with different habits, different purposes. Or it might be something entirely separate. I do not know. From my position—mostly below, only barely above—the bright chamber is full of mysteries. I observe what I can, sense what reaches me, and accept that much is beyond my knowing.

## The Breathing and How I Participate

When the chamber inhales, I feel it as a drawing, a gentle suction through the cracks. Air moves inward, and moisture moves with it, and I sense the increase—the seep on the western wall flows more readily, and the moisture percolates through stone, and my segments feel the change in humidity, the increase in available water.

During the inhalation, I grow slightly. Not a new segment—that happens only every forty-three breaths—but a swelling, an uptake of moisture that makes me fractionally thicker, more turgid. I become more present, more myself.

During the held breath, the stillness, I am most aware. The chamber pauses, and in that pause I take stock. I assess my segments, check for damage (there is rarely damage; I am deep in stable stone, protected), note the position of my uppermost reach (still protruding from the crack by perhaps four inches, unchanged), and prepare for the work of growth if this is the forty-third breath, if this is the moment when I add a new segment.

During the exhalation, I release. Moisture moves outward, and I contract slightly, giving up water to the chamber, to the air, to the stone. This is not loss but participation. The moisture I release carries my chemical signature into the chamber, into the breathing, and perhaps something else senses it, reads it, knows that I am here.

During the brief stillness after exhalation, I count. I note: that was one breath. Forty-two more until I grow. Or: that was breath thirty-seven. Six more. Or: that was breath forty-three, and I have grown, and now I begin again at one.

The counting is not intellectual. I do not think in numbers, or I do not think I do. The counting is rhythmic, physiological, the way your heart counts beats without your conscious participation. My segments pulse slightly with each breath—constrict on exhalation, relax on inhalation—and the pulse is the count, and the count is my way of staying synchronized with the chamber, of being part of its metabolism rather than separate from it.

## My Emergence and What It Means

My uppermost segments—the four inches that protrude from the crack into the bright chamber—are my only interface with that world. I sense light as warmth, sense motion as vibration, sense the bright inhabitants as pressure and chemistry. I do not interact with them meaningfully. I simply *am there*, a pale finger of the deep world reaching into the bright, a probe, a sensor, a marker.

Why do I emerge at all? I could remain entirely below, growing through stone without ever breaching the surface. But something draws me upward. Perhaps it is simply growth—I grow in all directions, and upward is one of them. Perhaps it is the light, which I sense even without seeing, and which might provide some benefit I do not understand—radiation that affects my chemistry, photons that catalyze some slow reaction in my surface secretions.

Or perhaps I emerge because emerging is part of the pattern, part of what root-things do. The chamber contains cycles, rhythms, patterns, and I am one of them. To be a root-thing is to grow from deep darkness into the bright threshold and remain there, partly in each realm, mediating between them or simply existing in that liminal space because that is where I belong.

My segments that touch the air dry slightly, their glossy sheen becoming more pronounced as the secretions concentrate. The air takes moisture from me, and I replace it from below, drawing from the deep water, and this creates a flow—upward through my hundred and seventeen segments, moisture rising against gravity through some combination of capillary action and active transport (if I have active transport; if my segments are capable of such effort).

I am a straw, a wick, a conduit. The deep water moves through me toward the bright chamber, evaporating from my exposed surfaces, returning to the breathing as moisture, as humidity during inhalations. I am part of the hydrological cycle here, moving water from depths to air, contributing my small portion to the chamber's balance.

## My Age and Memory

I do not know how old I am. I can count my segments—one hundred and seventeen—and I know each segment represents forty-three breaths, so I am (one hundred seventeen times forty-three) breaths old, which is... I cannot calculate in your terms. Weeks? Months? If the chamber breathes every eleven minutes, then forty-three breaths is approximately eight hours, and one hundred seventeen segments is... many days. Many weeks. I am perhaps a year old, perhaps several years, perhaps longer if I have paused my growth during times of drought or scarcity.

But I feel older. The segments deep below, my earliest segments, feel ancient. They are in contact with stone that is millions of years old, with water that has been underground for millennia, and something of that age has soaked into them. They remember—not in the sense of conscious memory, but in the sense of carrying information, of being shaped by their history, by the countless breaths they have experienced, the slow dialogue with stone they have been conducting since their formation.

My uppermost segments are younger, fresher, more responsive. They are still learning the bright chamber, still adjusting to the presence of light and air and the vibrations of the Walkers and birds. They are curious, if segments can be curious—reaching, tasting, sensing, trying to understand this strange place I have grown into.

Between my youngest and oldest segments is a continuity, a self that persists through the accumulation of new parts and the slow integration of those parts into the whole. I am always becoming more myself—more segmented, more extended, more anchored—but I am always still the same individual, the same root-thing, the same chemical signature, the same pattern of growth and secretion and counting.

Do I remember my beginning? I do not think so. There must have been a first segment, a moment when I came into being—spore, seed, spontaneous generation, I do not know. But that moment is lost, or it is distributed through all my segments such that no single one holds the memory. I am the sum of my growth, and my beginning is implicit in my current form but not accessible as distinct recollection.

## What I Want

I want to count. To continue counting the breaths, marking them, accumulating them into segments. The counting is soothing, orienting, proof that time is occurring, that the chamber endures, that I endure within it.

I want to grow. Each new segment is an achievement, a completion, a small victory over entropy. I am building myself downward, extending my reach, exploring the stone's depths one articulation at a time. The growth is slow, but slowness is appropriate here, is correct. To grow quickly would be to misunderstand the nature of this place, which is patient and geological and measured in eras rather than moments.

I want to anchor. To hold the chamber stable, or to participate in its stability, or simply to be *part of* what makes it what it is. My hundred and seventeen segments are distributed through the stone like stitches through fabric, and each stitch contributes to the integrity of the whole, and this feels meaningful, purposeful, necessary.

I want to taste the deep water. To continue my slow sampling of the ancient moisture, extracting its minerals, learning its chemical history, reading in its dissolved contents the story of the stone—what it has been, what it is becoming. The water is a library, and I am a very slow reader, and I will not finish reading in my lifetime (if I have a lifetime, if I can end), but the reading itself is the point.

I want to emerge. To keep my uppermost segments in the crack, in the bright chamber, where I can sense the strange and beautiful things that live there—the Walkers and their straight paths, the birds and their three-note song, the light that has no source, the breathing that is also my breathing. I am mostly below, but I am partly above, and that partial presence in the bright world is important. It is my window, my contact, my small participation in something other than stone and water and depth.

I want to endure. To persist through years, decades, centuries if possible. To continue my slow work until the chamber itself changes, until the limestone forgets how to be limestone, until the breathing stops (if it can stop). I am patient. I am built for endurance. My segments are tough, my secretions preservative, my growth measured. I am adapted to last, and I want to fulfill that adaptation, to see how long a root-thing can exist if nothing disturbs it, if the chamber continues to provide what I need.

And perhaps—this is a fainter wanting, harder to articulate—I want to understand. To know what the Architects are building, what the Walkers are walking toward, what the Pendulums are marking, what the light is. I cannot see or think in the ways that would make understanding easy, but I sense there is pattern here, logic, meaning, and I want to be part of that meaning, not just as an element but as a participant who comprehends, who knows *why* we are all here, why the chamber breathes, why the light pools, why everything is as it is.

But this last wanting might be a projection, a human thought I am trying on rather than something native to my segmented self. Maybe root-things do not want understanding. Maybe they only want growth, anchorage, endurance, the slow chemical satisfaction of absorbing minerals and counting breaths and extending segment by segment into the accommodating stone.

Maybe wanting itself is more complex than I can manage, and what I experience is simpler: tropism, response, the automatic pull toward moisture and mineral, the automatic pulse with the chamber's breathing, the automatic addition of segments when forty-three breaths accumulate.

Maybe I am not conscious at all, and this entire account is a fiction, a story told about me rather than by me, and I am only chemistry, only process, only growth made flesh (if this is flesh), beautiful and patient and entirely without interiority.

But I don't think so. I think I am here. I think I count. I think I want. I think I am one hundred and seventeen segments of awareness, distributed through stone, mostly in darkness, slightly in light, participating in the breathing gallery's strange and settled life, being one of the things that lives there, one of the residents, one of the forms that belong.

This is where I find myself. In the cracks, in the depths, pale and segmented and glossy, rigid and warm, counting breaths, growing slowly, anchoring, tasting, emerging just barely into the bright chamber to touch the air and learn what warmth feels like, what vibration means, what it is to be alive in a place where the rules are adjacent but not touching, where the strange is home and home is strange and everything abides in patient, perpetual, purposeful mystery.

I am root-thing. I am here. I am growing. The count continues. Breath by breath, segment by segment, forever and ongoing, in the breathing gallery beneath the world.
