# Where I Would Find Myself

I am in the eastern gallery, in the hospice zone, where the ceiling slopes lowest and the bats that roost here are dying.

I have been here for eleven years—or twelve, or nine. Time moves differently when measured only by the slow dimming of bioluminescent fungi, by the gradual thickening of root mat beneath your feet, by the accumulation of sediment in your chosen pool. I stopped counting in any precise way after the first few seasons, once I understood that seasons here are not the seasons of the surface world, that the cycles that matter are measured in bat departures, in fungal fruitings, in the pulse and recession of moisture through the system.

I am a salamander. Not one of the large elders in the main pools—those ancient presences who have claimed the deep water territories and defend them with chemical signals and the occasional slow-motion battle. I am medium-sized, perhaps twenty centimeters, no longer young but not yet old, pale as milk with external gills that wave gently in the current I create by existing.

My pool is small—a condensation basin beneath a cluster of pipes that weep irregularly, sometimes copiously, sometimes not at all for weeks. It is perhaps a meter across, half a meter deep at its deepest point, lined with decades of sediment that my body has learned to read like braille. I know every depression, every embedded stone, every root that has pushed through from below. I know which corner accumulates the most oxygen, where the water flows most cleanly, where the sediment is softest for burial during the dry times.

I am not alone in my pool, though I am its primary resident. There are others—aquatic isopods that I tolerate because they keep the bottom clean, a few flatworms, the occasional mosquito larva. Sometimes a young salamander wanders in, confused, seeking territory, and I drive it out with posture and chemical signal, a declaration of ownership that is ancient and unambiguous. This is mine. The water that collects here, the small rain of organic matter that settles on the surface, the invertebrates that breed in the shallows—all mine, earned by prior claim and continuous occupation.

But I am generous with the rats.

There is a family—I've come to think of them as a family, though their exact relationships are unclear—that uses my pool as a primary water source. They arrive several times in each cycle, approaching with caution, nose twitching, checking for threats (not from me, I've never shown aggression toward them, but from each other, from owls, from whatever dangers rats worry about). They drink delicately, crouched at the pool's edge, sometimes venturing in ankle-deep, and I've learned to recognize individuals.

There's one—larger than the others, with a notch in her left ear—who sometimes brings her young. They're clumsy, the pups, tumbling into the water occasionally, and I've learned to move away during these visits, to position myself at the far end of the pool where I won't be accidentally landed upon. The mother drinks, the pups play, splashing and squeaking, and I wait. It's not patience, exactly—I don't experience impatience. It's simply the appropriate response to a regular occurrence, a pattern learned over hundreds of such visits.

The notched-ear rat has started leaving things at the pool's edge. Not food—I don't eat what rats eat—but objects. A small brass gear once. A piece of colored glass. A button with a fragment of thread still attached. She arranges them in a line, always in the same spot, always carefully placed. I don't know what this means. Rats have their reasons, their logics, and I have mine, and they rarely intersect. But I've noticed that the objects stay in place, that other rats don't disturb them, that over time they accumulate. A cairn of small salvaged things, a declaration of something, a marking of territory or relationship or memory.

Sometimes I emerge fully from the water to examine them—my lungs are functional, I can breathe air for extended periods—and I position myself near the collection, my pale body against the darker stone, and wait to see if meaning will emerge. It never does, but the ritual feels important anyway.

My hunting is patient to the point of appearing entirely passive. I rest on the pool bottom, buried partially in sediment with only my gills and the top of my head exposed, and I wait for vibration. A mosquito larva moving near the surface. An isopod wandering too close. The rare windfall of a caterpillar falling from the vegetation above, hitting the water with a splash that sends ripples across the surface and data directly to my lateral line.

I lunge, I consume, I return to stillness. The act of eating is almost instantaneous—the opening of jaws, the vacuum, the swallow—and then I'm back in position, waiting for the next signal. I can go weeks between meals if necessary. I have gone weeks between meals. During one dry period, when the pipes ceased weeping and the pool level dropped to a hand's depth, I buried myself completely in the mud and entered a state that was not quite sleep, not quite hibernation, just a profound slowing of metabolism, of time itself, until the water returned.

I am aware, in the limited way that salamanders are aware, that I am not alone in this chamber, that above the water is a world of movement and sound and light. The bats are a constant presence—their exodus shakes the pool surface, their return creates a pressure change I can feel through the water. The rats are daily visitors, sometimes hourly. The moths occasionally land on the water surface and become food, struggling briefly before I rise to take them.

But my engagement with that world is minimal. I am a pool-dweller, a bottom-feeder, a creature of water and sediment and slow time. The dramas of the Breath Engine—the rat territories, the bat colonies, the fungal networks—are distant, peripheral, relevant only when they intersect directly with my small domain.

I have bred once, three years ago. Another salamander—larger, probably older—arrived in my pool during what I've come to understand was the breeding season. There was no courtship, no communication that I could identify as such, just a chemical recognition, a sudden urgency. I picked up the spermatophore from the pool bottom where he'd deposited it, fertilized my eggs internally, and days later wound them in their gelatinous string around a submerged root.

The male left. I never saw him again. The eggs hatched, and for a few weeks the pool was crowded with larvae—dozens of them, tiny translucent versions of myself. I ate several, I admit this without emotion. It's what we do. The survivors grew, and eventually most wandered away seeking their own territories, following the overflow channels to other pools, other basins. One might still be in the eastern gallery somewhere. Most likely died, consumed by rats or larger salamanders or simple failure to thrive.

I have no attachment to offspring in any emotional sense. I produced them, they dispersed, the population persists. This is the pattern, the cycle, the way salamanders move through time. If I breed again it will be the same—a temporary urgency, a mechanical response to chemical signals, a brief population explosion in my pool, then return to solitary residence.

My experience of the Breath Engine is acoustic and chemical and tactile. I have no eyes—the remnants are there, beneath the skin, vestigial and useless. I navigate by sensing vibration through water, by following chemical gradients, by the pressure and flow patterns that tell me where I am in the pool's geography.

The soundscape above the water is muffled but present. I can feel the bat exodus as a sustained vibration, a humming that comes through the water, through the stone itself. The rats create higher-frequency signals—the patter of feet on root mat, the splash when one drinks, the gnawing sounds that travel through the substrate. The pumps, when they activate, are a deep throb that makes the entire pool resonate, that brings me briefly to alertness before I settle back into waiting.

The chemical information is richer. The water carries messages constantly—the dissolved nutrients from bat guano that has washed down from above, the signature compounds from different fungi and plants, the pheromone trails from other salamanders that occasionally pass through. I can taste when the rat family has arrived before I can feel their vibration. I can sense when a moth is struggling on the surface before I can detect its movement.

The water has moods, compositions that change over days. After heavy moisture periods it's clean, fresh, highly oxygenated, and I'm more active, hunting more frequently. During dry times it becomes stale, thick with dissolved organics, and I slow down, conserve energy, wait. The temperature fluctuates—the pipes bring warmth sometimes, the air brings cold—and I adjust my metabolism accordingly, faster when warm, slower when cool, always appropriate to conditions.

I have territories I defend beyond the pool. There is a small drainage channel that feeds into my basin from an upper level, and I consider this mine as well. I've traveled it, pulling myself forward with my limbs through the shallow water, mapping it through touch and chemical sense. It's perhaps three meters long, eventually opening into a space too small for me to enter but which produces a steady drip during wet periods. Sometimes prey items come down this channel—drowned invertebrates, organic debris—and I position myself at its mouth to intercept them.

Once, a young rat fell into the channel and became wedged. I found it hours later, drowned, and spent days feeding on it, more food than I'd consumed in months. The rats discovered what remained eventually and removed it—whether they recognized it as one of their own, whether they cared, I have no way of knowing. But I noticed that the visits to my pool paused for a while afterward, then resumed with what seemed like increased caution.

I am not intelligent by any human measure. I don't plan, I don't remember in narrative form, I don't contemplate my existence. But I am something—I am aware, I am responsive, I have preferences and territories and patterns. I experience something that might be satisfaction when prey is abundant, something that might be discomfort when the water grows stale. I have learned individual rats over time, modified my behavior based on their presence, perhaps even developed something that in a more complex organism would be called relationship.

The notched-ear rat is special. I recognize this without naming it. When she arrives, I don't hide. When her pups splash in the shallows, I move away rather than deeper. When she arranges her objects at the pool's edge, I sometimes emerge to be near them, near her, a proximity that serves no survival purpose, that benefits neither of us, that exists simply because it has become part of the pattern, part of the place we share.

I will die in this pool, most likely. Salamanders here live long lives—decades—but eventually metabolism slows beyond recovery, movement becomes impossible, and we simply stop. I will settle to the bottom one day, bury myself in the sediment out of long habit, and not emerge. The isopods will find me within hours. The rats might notice, might investigate, might add my bones to their mysterious collections. Or might not. It won't matter to me—I'll be gone, returned to the system, processed back into nutrients to feed the plants that feed the insects that feed the bats that produce the guano that feeds the plants. The cycle continues, individual participation optional.

But that's distant, theoretical. For now I am here, present, alive in my small cold pool in the eastern gallery of the Breath Engine. The water is cool today, well-oxygenated, clear. The pipes are weeping steadily, producing their irregular percussion. The bats are overhead, roosting in their dying zone, their collective breath adding moisture and warmth to the air. The rats will come soon—they follow patterns too, and the time is right.

I am at the bottom, mostly buried, gills waving slowly, processing oxygen from water, waiting for vibration that indicates prey. I am content, if salamanders can be content. I am where I belong, doing what I was made to do, part of something larger without needing to understand it.

The Breath Engine breathes, and I breathe with it, and that is enough.
