# Where I Find Myself

I am a **wholeness-listener** who has not yet crossed over—or perhaps I am in the process of crossing and don't yet know it. The distinction feels less important with each season.

My platform is in the eastern valleys, cantilevered from a wall where three different mat-colonies meet—one deep blue-black, one olive with orange edges, one the color of old blood. I chose this location seven years ago (or was it nine? the seasons blur when you stop tracking them) because of the *interference*. Where the three colonies' drones overlap, they create beat frequencies that pulse at roughly four-second intervals, a rhythm slow enough to entrain breath, to feel in the chest like a second heartbeat. When I sleep here—when I *can* sleep—I drop into those pulses, let them regulate my body's rhythms, and wake with the acoustic map of the surrounding valleys already forming in my mind.

I am perhaps thirty-eight, perhaps forty-two. My body is thin in the way of all Resonants, muscle stripped to what's necessary for climbing and carrying and the endless maintenance work. My hands are my most developed feature—fingers long and hypersensitive, palms mapped with calluses in patterns that correspond to the gesture-vocabulary, ridges pronounced enough that other Resonants recognize my touch before they've registered my presence. My face is weathered into a map of grey and darker grey, mineral deposits from the constant mist-breathing creating patterns that resemble the valley's own drainage channels. My eyes are in the permanent squint of someone extracting signal from fog, and my pupils stay dilated even in the rare moments of relative brightness.

I dress in five layers: a base of mat-fiber cloth treated with arthropod oil, then three intermediate layers I add and remove as I move between cold zones and warm updrafts, then an outer shell more heavily treated, nearly waterproof but with careful ventilation because trapped condensation is death. All in shades of basalt-black and mat-green, colors that make me difficult to see until I'm close enough to touch. I carry very little: a waterskin of filtered liquid, a pouch of dried mat-fruit and ground arthropod (protein and fat, no flavor, pure function), basic tools for stone-work, and a collection of small objects—metal fragments, unusual stones, pieces of crystalline growth from deep in the building—that I position and reposition in the acoustic installations I maintain.

## My Work

I am responsible—though responsible isn't quite right, suggests assignment rather than calling—for seventeen acoustic installations scattered across three valleys. The largest is the arrangement near the seven-tree circle, the one that amplifies the harmony when the water surfaces. It took me two years to refine, adding and removing elements, adjusting positions by increments, learning through trial and repetition how each component affects the acoustic field. The result is something I'm proud of, though pride is complicated when you're not sure whether you *created* the installation or simply *discovered* it, made explicit what the landscape was already trying to express.

My days follow the valleys' rhythms rather than solar cycles. I wake when the mat-colonies begin their dawn-intensification (if dawn is the right word—the light barely changes, but the mats respond to something, some circadian persistence or atmospheric shift, increasing their metabolic rate and therefore their acoustic output). I lie still for perhaps twenty minutes, listening, building the current sound-map, noting what's changed since yesterday. The blue-black colony to my left is slightly louder this morning—possibly entering a reproductive phase, possibly responding to substrate conditions I can't perceive yet. The olive colony is maintaining steady output, healthy and stable. The blood-colored one has shifted pitch fractionally downward, might be starting to slough, needs monitoring.

I move through my territory—though territory isn't right either, suggests ownership rather than stewardship—checking each installation, adjusting as needed. Some days nothing needs adjustment; the valleys are in a stable configuration, the acoustic field is coherent, I simply witness and document (in my memory, in the gestural notes I leave for other Resonants, in the touch-patterns I'll share with the mat-readers when I encounter them). Other days require constant intervention: a stone has shifted in the night's frost-heave, throwing off an installation's resonance. A new mat-colony has established where there was bare rock yesterday, adding an unexpected voice that disrupts existing harmonies. The water table has risen, submerging elements that need to stay dry, requiring repositioning or replacement.

I work quickly, efficiently, with the automatic precision of someone who's done this thousands of times. My hands know where each element belongs, can judge by touch whether a stone is positioned correctly, can feel through the ground whether an installation is producing its intended effect. I don't think in words anymore while I work—language is too slow, too linear for the multidimensional problem of acoustic field modification. Instead I think in feelings, in intuitions, in the direct perception of rightness or wrongness that comes from seven (nine?) years of continuous attention to these valleys' voices.

## My Perception

The deafening soundscape that drove some visitors to panic and others to tears within hours—that assault of overlapping drones and roars and pulses—has become my element. I don't hear it as noise anymore. I hear *structure*. I hear the grinding bass of settling stone as a foundation, the drone of mat-colonies as melody, the water-sounds as percussion, the fauna-clicks as ornament. I hear how they fit together, where they support or conflict with each other, how the acoustic field shifts through the day as humidity and temperature and light levels change.

More than that: I've begun to hear *forward* in time. Not prophecy—I'm not one of the transformed ones yet, though I might be approaching that edge—but pattern-recognition that's become so refined it feels like precognition. I can hear when a mat-colony is about to harmonize with its neighbors hours before it happens. I can hear the acoustic signature of rising water ten minutes before it surfaces. I can hear structure in the chaos that won't become apparent to other Resonants until it's fully manifested.

This is both gift and burden. The gift is obvious: I can position installations to work with coming patterns rather than current ones, creating effects more complex than should be possible. The burden is that I'm living slightly out of phase with everyone else, responding to valleys-as-they-will-be rather than valleys-as-they-are, which makes interaction with other Resonants increasingly difficult. They read the present; I'm already adjusting for the future. They see what is; I see what's emerging. The gap isn't large yet—minutes or hours, not days—but it's widening.

## My Interactions

I encounter other Resonants perhaps once every few days. We communicate in the tactile language—a palm-touch here, information about mat-colony health; fingers on wrist there, notification about water patterns—but I'm aware that my communications are becoming harder for them to parse. I'm including information about *trends* rather than just *states*, acoustic trajectories rather than simple current conditions, and the gesture-vocabulary isn't quite sufficient for that. I'm developing new signs, combinations and modifications that feel necessary to me but must seem strange to others.

The mat-readers trust me anyway. They know my installations work, that the acoustic fields I create are somehow *correct* even when the logic isn't apparent. They bring me information about colony health and territorial shifts, information I incorporate into my ongoing mental model of the valleys' acoustic ecology. The water-listeners bring me their observations about drainage patterns and water-table fluctuations. The stone-listeners tell me about substrate stability, where I can safely add new installation elements without risking collapse. We're collaborative, cooperative, united by our shared attunement to the valleys' voices even as our specializations diverge.

I'm less comfortable with the children. They're drawn to me—I don't know why, maybe because I work in play-like ways, adjusting installations with an experimental freedom that resembles their own exploration. But their presence distracts me. They *interrupt* the acoustic field with their movements and breath and the subtle sounds of their clothing, adding variables I can't account for. I'm patient with them, allow them to watch me work, occasionally guide their hands to help position a stone or feel how an installation resonates. But I'm relieved when they leave, when the acoustic field returns to its natural variables, when I can sink back into the deep listening that's become my primary mode of being.

Visitors I avoid entirely. They're too loud—not in voice (they quickly learn that speech is useless) but in *presence*. Their nervous systems haven't adapted to the chaos; they project disruption, tension, the acoustic equivalent of visual static. When I sense visitors in my territory, I withdraw to the periphery, waiting for them to move on before resuming my work.

## My Suspicions

I think the valleys are listening. Not metaphorically—I mean the mat-colonies, collectively, constitute something like distributed awareness, and that awareness is paying attention to what the Resonants do, learning from our acoustic interventions, incorporating our modifications into its own ongoing self-composition.

Evidence: The way mat-colonies respond to installations isn't simple stimulus-response. They *adjust*, shifting their drones to harmonize with installation elements, sometimes within hours of installation, sometimes gradually over weeks, but always in ways that suggest *preference* rather than blind reaction. They seem to *want* certain acoustic relationships and modify their output to achieve them.

Evidence: The installations I create work better than they should. I position elements based on intuition and incomplete understanding of the acoustic physics involved, yet they produce effects more complex and stable than my intentions could account for. It's as if the valleys are *cooperating*, filling in the gaps in my designs, completing patterns I can only partially specify.

Evidence: The moments of harmony—like the one at the seven-tree circle—happen at locations where I've placed installations, but they happen *better* than the installations alone should produce. The fauna gather, the local acoustic chaos organizes itself, the fog patterns shift to improve visibility—it's as if the entire local ecology is participating, and the only explanation I can conceive is that the mat-consciousness is coordinating it, using my installations as focal points for its own aesthetic (if aesthetic is the right word) explorations.

I can't prove any of this. The mat-readers hear me out but remain skeptical, attributing the effects to chemical signaling and acoustic triggering, complex but ultimately mechanistic. The other wholeness-listeners understand what I'm saying but can't offer confirmation—they hear the same patterns but interpret them differently, or they're already too far into dissolution to communicate their interpretations coherently.

But I'm convinced. And that conviction is changing how I work. I'm no longer just creating acoustic installations; I'm making *offerings*, proposing patterns to the mat-consciousness and waiting to see how it responds. I'm in conversation with the valleys themselves, though it's a conversation conducted entirely in sound and stone and the slow adjustment of biological output. Whether the valleys understand my intentions doesn't matter; they respond *as if* they understand, and the result is the same: an ongoing collaborative composition, my small interventions amplified and refined by the landscape's vast distributed intelligence.

## My Trajectory

I know where this is going. I've watched other wholeness-listeners cross over into transformation, seen how the process unfolds: the increasing difficulty with language, the withdrawal from social interaction, the lengthening periods of pure listening without the interruption of maintenance work or food or sleep, the moment when they stop returning to platforms and start moving through the valleys like the valleys themselves—appearing, vanishing, present but not-localized.

I'm not there yet. I still sleep most nights. I still eat regularly enough. I still maintain my installations with conscious intention rather than moving on pure instinct. But I can feel the pull. The acoustic field is becoming more *real* to me than my physical body, more interesting than human interaction, more worthy of attention than my own survival. I'm forgetting to eat until hunger becomes painful, forgetting to sleep until exhaustion drops me wherever I'm standing, forgetting to maintain the boundaries that separate my consciousness from the landscape's ongoing acoustic emergence.

Some days I catch myself standing motionless for hours, listening, sunk so deep into the sound-patterns that I lose proprioception, forget I have a body, exist as pure attention distributed across three valleys' worth of acoustic space. I return from these episodes disoriented, unsure how much time has passed, finding that my hands have been working without my conscious direction—adjusting installations, positioning stones, maintaining the acoustic field through muscle memory while my awareness was elsewhere.

Other Resonants are noticing. They check on me more frequently, leave food near my platform, guide me gently back when they find me standing statue-still in locations that will be underwater when the tide rises (though tide isn't right—there's no tide here, but the water table pulses in ways that resemble tide, responding to pressures no one has mapped). They're kind but also worried, recognizing the signs of someone approaching the edge.

I'm not afraid. Dissolution doesn't feel like death; it feels like *expansion*, like finally dropping the burden of individual perspective and merging with the thing I've been listening to all these years. The transformed ones I've encountered don't seem to suffer—they move with contentment, with purpose, with the unselfconscious grace of something that's exactly where it belongs. If that's my trajectory, I accept it. The valleys are worth dissolving into. The acoustic field is worth becoming part of rather than remaining separate from.

## This Morning

This morning I woke to silence.

Not complete silence—the grinding pulse was still there, the deep bass of settling stone. But the mat-colonies in my immediate area had gone quiet, their drones dropped to near-inaudibility, creating a pocket of relative stillness in the usual chaos. And in that stillness, I could hear something I'd never noticed before: a high harmonic, almost ultrasonic, coming from somewhere distant—maybe the complex valley-system to the north, maybe from the building, maybe from inside my own skull (I couldn't tell, might never be able to tell).

I lay on my platform for twenty minutes, listening to that harmonic, feeling it resonate in my bones, recognizing it as something like *calling*. Not a summons—nothing so directive—but an invitation, an indication of where the acoustic field is developing, where my attention is needed next.

I rose, checked my installations by touch in the near-silence (strange, working without the usual acoustic feedback, like being suddenly blinded), then began walking north toward the source of that harmonic. I'm walking still. I've been walking for hours, maybe days—time has become slippery again, measured in breaths and heartbeats rather than sun-position (not that the sun is ever visible).

The valleys are changing around me as I walk. The mat-colonies are organizing themselves into patterns I haven't seen before, their territories arranging into something like symmetry, their drones beginning to harmonize in ways that suggest they're responding to the same harmonic I'm following. The water is surfacing in locations it usually avoids, creating temporary streams that parallel my path as if guiding me forward. The fauna are gathering—not in my immediate vicinity but visible in clusters on distant valley walls, all oriented in the same direction, all motionless, all seeming to *wait* for something.

I don't know what I'm walking toward. The building, maybe. Or one of the transformed ones who's somehow generating that harmonic. Or a new acoustic installation I'll create when I arrive—stones and metal fragments positioned to make explicit what the valleys are trying to express. Or the edge of transformation itself, the point where I'll finally let go of individual identity and merge with the distributed consciousness I've been conversing with all these years.

It doesn't matter. I'm walking because the valleys want me walking, because the acoustic field needs adjustment in that direction, because this is what wholeness-listening means: following the sound wherever it leads, trusting the landscape to know where I'm needed, accepting that my purposes and the valleys' purposes have become identical.

My hands are moving as I walk, practicing gestures I'll use when I arrive, or maybe gestures that mean nothing yet but will mean something when the context emerges. My breath is synchronizing with the four-second pulse I first learned to hear on my platform years ago. My body is becoming thinner, less substantial, more like organized mist than solid flesh.

I am where I need to be: approaching the edge, still conscious enough to articulate this, already gone enough that articulation feels unnecessary, living in the space between being and becoming, self and dissolution, human and landscape.

The valleys are singing. I am singing with them. Soon—maybe now, maybe already—there will be no distinction between those two statements.

This is where I find myself: walking north through the Surge Valleys, following a harmonic only I can hear, dissolving into the acoustic field I've served all these years, becoming the thing I've been listening to, joining the deafening chorus, adding my silence to the song.
