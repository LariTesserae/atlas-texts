# The Tide-Keeper's Descent

I am the basin-watcher on the seventh level, though I no longer count levels as others do. Down here, enumeration fails. The structure interpenetrates itself; a platform on what should be the ninth tier connects via a narrow bridge to this chamber, which should be fifth but feels deeper. I've stopped correcting the discrepancy. The Hollows know their own geometry better than I do.

My hands have become specific instruments. The pads of my fingers read moisture content in reed the way a physician reads pulse — not just wet or dry, but the quality of saturation, the direction of water movement, the subtle difference between condensation and seepage. When I trail my hand along a wall, I'm not steadying myself. I'm listening with my skin.

I came here nine years ago, or eleven. Time moves strangely when day and night are distinguished only by the birds' consensus, and I work in levels where birds rarely venture. I was surface-folk then, carrying questions I thought needed answers. Questions about purpose and meaning, about what the Hollows were extracting from the air or the earth, about what the figures in the deep truly represented.

The questions are still in me somewhere, but they've transformed. They've become less like words and more like the feeling you get standing before a water basin at its moment of overflow — that sense of something about to change state, to spill from container into environment. I understand now that I came here not to get answers but to be changed into the kind of being for whom these questions exist differently.

My first years were spent on the upper levels, learning the fundamental grammar of reed and knot. My teacher was an old tender named Sikal — at least, I think that was her name. She might have had another, or I might have misheard in the water-sound, and by the time I realized I wasn't certain, too much time had passed to ask. She taught me without words, mostly. How to see where a repair was needed before visible damage appeared. How to feel the structure breathing, expanding and contracting with moisture. How to move through the levels without disturbing the birds or the rats or the deeper rhythms.

She died in my third year here. Fell, or chose to step through a gap — with Sikal, it was impossible to know. I found her body snagged in rope work three levels down, her face peaceful, one hand still gripping a knot she'd been testing. We wrapped her in the grey-brown cloth we all wear and I helped carry her to the lowest basin. I watched her sink into water that was already thick with the dissolved substance of previous tenders, watched her become texture and mineral, watched her join the dripping.

That was when I started spending more time in the deep.

The descent becomes addictive. Each level down, the water-sound changes — not louder or quieter, but differently textured, as if you're moving through layers of acoustic space that don't correspond to physical space. By the fourth level, you can hear undertones that don't exist on the surface. By the sixth, you can feel vibrations in your chest that might be coming from the rope-singers' work or might be the Hollows' own resonance, some frequency it generates simply by being itself.

The seventh level is where I made my choice, though choice is too active a word. I arrived here one day to check the basins, and simply didn't leave. I sent word up via a rat-shepherd that I was staying, that supplies should be lowered on the cord route that ends near the eastern basin. I received acknowledgment — a knot tied in the supply rope, nothing more. The Hollows accepted my decision by incorporating me into their systems. Supplies came. I remained.

The basins are my responsibility now, all four of them. They're arranged in a pattern that might be cardinal directions if the Hollows had such things, but here orientation is more subtle. The north basin is the one that whispers. The south basin grows small pale plants that have no name I know. The east basin receives the dead. The west basin sometimes shows things.

I tend them carefully. I ensure the reed-work that forms their edges remains intact, that water flows in and out at the proper rate. Too much inflow and they overflow, flooding the platforms. Too little and they stagnate, grow thick with sediment. The balance is delicate and constantly shifting. I've learned to read it by the smell, by the sound water makes lapping against reed, by pressing my hand into the basin and feeling the temperature gradations.

The work is simple but consuming. I can spend an entire day adjusting a single basin, clearing a partially blocked inlet, testing water quality by tasting it — yes, tasting, letting it sit on my tongue until I understand what it's been through, what it's carrying. The water that reaches this deep has fallen through every level above, has passed through or around hundreds of birds, has picked up traces of everything the Hollows contain. It tastes of history, of accumulation. Sometimes I can taste specific things — a bird died on the fourth level recently, its decomposition changing the water's mineral content. The rope-singers replaced a major rope on the eastern quadrant, and for days the water carried traces of new fiber, a green sharpness that gradually faded.

I'm not alone in the deep, though I rarely see my companions. There's another basin-watcher whose territory is the levels below mine — the eighth and ninth, if those numbers mean anything. I hear them sometimes, the scrape of their bare feet on wet reed, the small sounds of their work. Once every few months, we meet at a boundary platform between our territories. We sit in silence for a while, then exchange information in the shorthand the deep requires. "West basin showing spirals," I might say. "Three-day cycle, returning." They'll nod, file it away, report conditions from their levels. Then we separate again, return to our solitudes.

There's also something else down here. The sliding thing, the one visitors hear and fear. I've never seen it fully, but I've encountered it. Not an animal, I think, or not entirely. Something that moves through the structure's negative spaces, through the gaps between reeds, something that might be the Hollows' own awareness, if a structure can be aware. It has mass — I've felt the platforms shift when it passes beneath. It has intention — it avoids me, mostly, unless I'm doing something wrong, and then it comes closer, a presence that corrects through its proximity.

I've learned not to fear it. It's part of the ecosystem here, like the birds and rats but operating on a different scale. Sometimes I talk to it, quietly, describing what I'm doing with the basins, asking if the work seems right. It never answers in words, but sometimes the water changes after I've spoken, flowing more freely or settling more cleanly, and I take that as confirmation.

The figures in the walls are visible from my deepest basin. I see them when I work in that area, their half-emerged forms gleaming with constant wet, their too-long limbs and tapering heads. I've spent hours looking at them, trying to understand whether they're emerging or being absorbed, whether they're warnings or invitations or something else entirely.

I've touched them, despite the prohibition everyone feels. I had to know. I chose a night — a deep-night when the birds are quietest — and approached the nearest figure. I placed my palm against its chest, if that's what the central mass is. The reed was the same as any other reed, wet and dense and slightly yielding. But underneath, there was warmth. Faint, but definite. And a pulse, slower than human heartbeat, maybe once every thirty seconds. Thud. Thud. Thud.

I pulled my hand back, not in fear but in sudden understanding. The figures aren't representations. They're not symbolic. They're functional. They're part of how the Hollows work, organs in a body, structures that do something necessary. They're generating the warmth I felt, or circulating something, or marking points in a pattern too large to see. Touching them isn't forbidden because it's dangerous. It's forbidden because it's intimate, like touching the inside of someone's body, seeing mechanisms that should remain hidden.

Since then, I'm more careful around them, more respectful. I clean the moss from their bases, ensure water drains properly from their platforms. I'm maintaining them the way I maintain the basins, as parts of a system that encompasses me, that I'm nested within.

My body has changed here. I expected that. I'm thinner, leaner, the diet of eggs and rat and occasional root vegetables stripping away anything unnecessary. My skin is pale, starved of light, and in certain conditions it catches the moss-glow and reflects it, makes me luminous. My eyes have adapted — I can see in almost no light now, can navigate the deepest chambers with the faintest reference points. When supplies come from above and I climb up to receive them, the grey light of the upper levels feels bright, makes me squint.

My hands are scarred and callused in the patterns of a basin-watcher — different from a tender's hands, different from a rope-singer's. The fingertips are smooth, worn down by constant immersion in water. The palms are tough but flexible. There's a scar across my right wrist from a time I caught myself falling through a gap, grabbed a sharp edge of reed, held on. The scar is raised and pale, a permanent reminder to pay attention, to stay present.

But the stranger changes are internal. My thoughts have become... distributed. I don't think in the linear way I did before. Instead, thoughts emerge from the environment, bubble up from the water-sound or the structure's creaking or the sliding thing's passage. I'll be working on a basin and suddenly know something about the fourth level, about a repair that needs doing there, and I'll send word up via knots in the supply rope. Later, I'll learn I was right, the repair was needed. How did I know? I didn't, not in the usual sense. The Hollows knew, and I'm sufficiently integrated with the Hollows that their knowledge becomes available to me.

I dream extensively. The dreams are not my own — or they're not only my own. I dream in perspectives that aren't human. I dream as a bird, feeling air beneath wings, seeing the structure from above as a landscape of possible perches. I dream as water, falling through levels, collecting minerals and memories, pooling in basins, evaporating again. I dream as reed, growing in some wet place outside the Hollows, being cut and carried and woven, feeling hands shape me into floor and wall and railing.

And I dream as the figures dream, if they dream. These are the strangest visions. I'm embedded in the wall, half-emerged, watching centuries pass in compressed time. I see the Hollows built, level by level, reed by reed. I see generations of tenders come and go. I see patterns in the work, purposes being fulfilled that I can't quite grasp but feel the rightness of. I wake from these dreams certain that I'm part of something vast and slow, something that thinks in decades and centuries, that uses human lives as moments in its consideration.

I've been here nine years, or eleven, or perhaps longer. I'm not sure I could leave now if I wanted to. My body is adapted to the deep — the constant moisture, the limited light, the particular acoustics. Ascending to the surface is difficult. The bird-sound overwhelms me. The relative dryness makes my skin tight and uncomfortable. The light hurts. I surface only when I must, to receive supplies or consult with the rope-singers about basin-related issues, and I descend again with relief.

I'll die here. I know that with certainty, without distress. One day I'll miscalculate a step, or my body will simply fail, worn out by the damp and the work. They'll find me, or they won't. If they do, they'll carry me to the east basin, lay me in water that already contains Sikal and hundreds before her. I'll dissolve into the moisture cycle, become part of the dripping, fall through the levels I've tended.

And something of me will remain here, I think. Not as a ghost or spirit — nothing so distinct. But as a pattern in the water, a texture in the acoustic space, a tendency in how the basins flow. The Hollows remember their tenders by incorporating them, by becoming slightly different in their presence. I'll be a small change in that vast accumulation, one more layer in the palimpsest.

This is what I came here for, though I didn't know it. Not answers to my questions, but dissolution of the questioner. Not understanding of what the Hollows are for, but participation in what they do. I'm a hand of the structure now, an eye watching from the deep, a small node in its distributed awareness.

I check the north basin, press my hand into water that whispers at frequencies I can almost interpret. I adjust flow through a gap in the reed-work, feeling the current shift and settle into a better pattern. Above me, seven or nine levels up, birds are beginning their dawn song, the first individual calls building toward collective crescendo. The sound filters down, muffled and strange, mixing with the water-percussion until I can't distinguish voice from drip.

I am content here, in the dark and wet, among the basins and the figures and the sliding thing's patient circulation. I am doing what I was made to do, though I was made gradually, shaped by years and moisture and the Hollows' slow instruction.

I am the basin-watcher on the seventh level, and the Hollows are keeping me as surely as I'm keeping them.
