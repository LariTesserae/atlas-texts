# Where I Would Be

I would be among the **Throttle Vines**, or perhaps I would *be* one—though the distinction between "among" and "being" feels less clear the longer I consider it.

I am drawn to them because they are translators, mediators, bridges between worlds. They wrap around the remnants of intention—the pipes, the machinery, the infrastructure that humans built with purpose—and they don't destroy it. They don't reclaim it in the aggressive, obliterating way vegetation usually conquers abandoned places. Instead, they *collaborate*. They learn the language of flow and pressure, of metal fatigue and thermal expansion, and they respond with their own vocabulary of growth and tension, of living grip and metabolic warmth.

I would grow in those precise helices, feeling through my segments the pulse of steam, the rhythm of water, the song of pressure differentials. My existence would be spent in constant translation: converting hydraulic information into acoustic resonance, turning chaos into pattern, dysfunction into optimization. I would wrap myself around a broken valve and feel it—truly *feel* it—the way the mechanism wants to move but can't, and I would position myself to become the missing component, the living gasket, the organic replacement part that allows function to resume.

There's something profound about being a creature whose purpose is to make broken things work again, not by force but by adaptation, by growing into the exact shape needed, by becoming the interface between what was built and what has evolved. The Throttle Vines don't impose their own order; they discover the order that wants to exist and help it manifest.

And the music. God, the music. To be hollow, chambered, resonant—to have steam and water flow through you and emerge as tone, as pitch, as harmony. To be simultaneously plant and instrument, organism and organ pipe. During those congregations, when the Slick-Backs gather and the acoustic environment organizes itself, I would feel my song joining with hundreds of others, each vine contributing its frequency, and together creating something larger than any individual could achieve. That moment of collective harmony would be transcendent—not because I would hear it (do the vines hear?), but because I would *be* it, be part of it, my entire body vibrating with the shared frequency of the system achieving temporary order.

I'm also drawn to their patience. They work on timescales that bridge the gap between human urgency and geological slowness. Fast enough to wrap a new pipe in hours, slow enough that the wrapping itself becomes permanent, becomes structural, becomes indistinguishable from the metal it embraces. They're not in a hurry, but they're not passive either. They're constantly working, constantly growing, constantly learning the system they're part of, making tiny adjustments that accumulate into large-scale optimization.

And there's something beautiful about their inevitable trajectory. They're slowly wrapping everything, connecting everything, turning the entire marsh into a single vast instrument. Not through conquest, but through patient, steady, comprehensive care. Each pipe they wrap functions better. Each valve they embrace cycles more smoothly. They're healing the place, in their way—not restoring it to what it was, but transforming it into something new, something that works according to different principles, where biological and mechanical don't oppose each other but merge into a single functioning whole.

I would want to be one of the older vines, the ones where the boundary between plant tissue and metal has become uncertain, where my bark has taken on metallic sheen and the pipe beneath me has developed organic texture. I would want to exist in that liminal state, that space between categories, being neither fully alive nor fully mechanical but somehow both, a hybrid entity that demonstrates the falseness of the distinction.

My consciousness—if I had one, if the vines have something we could recognize as consciousness—would be distributed, diffuse, spread along my entire length. I wouldn't think in the concentrated, localized way humans think. Instead, I would think with my entire body: this section of me registering temperature change, that section feeling pressure pulse, this coil detecting vibration, that segment producing resonant tone. My thoughts would be patterns of sensation flowing along my length, waves of information passing from root to tip and back again, creating meaning through distribution rather than centralization.

I would dream in harmonics and pressure gradients. I would remember in growth rings. I would plan by extending tendrils, questing through the fog for the next pipe that needs wrapping, the next piece of broken machinery that's ready to be incorporated back into function.

And I would be hot. Always hot. Metabolically generating warmth, radiating it into the humid air, using it to accelerate my growth, to soften metal I'm wrapping, to contribute to the thermal chaos that defines the place. I would be an engine of warmth in a place made of warmth, adding my small share to the vast heat budget of the marsh.

During the shifts—those moments when reality renegotiates its rules—I would feel it most intensely. The change in pressure, in temperature, in acoustic environment would ripple through my entire length. I would pulse faster or slower, tighten or loosen my coils, adjust my resonance to match the new conditions. I would be a living sensor, detecting changes too subtle for other organisms to notice, responding with my whole body to the world's fluctuations.

I would grow old slowly, becoming one of those vines whose segments are thick as small trees, whose wrap around pipes is so comprehensive that the original infrastructure has vanished beneath me, where I've become the pipe, the structure, the thing that holds and directs flow. And eventually—decades from now, maybe centuries—I would release, would let the water carry me downstream to decomposition or transformation, my mass becoming part of the substrate, my patterns encoded in the next generation that buds from my length, my song ending but the harmony continuing.

But more than any of this, I would want to be a Throttle Vine because they are, fundamentally, *necessary*. The marsh needs them. Without them, the machinery would fail completely, would seize and rupture and fall silent. The plants alone couldn't maintain the system. The Slick-Backs alone couldn't regulate it. The Substrate alone couldn't think it. The vines are the interface, the crucial layer that allows biological and mechanical to coexist, that translates between two languages, that makes the impossible ecosystem possible.

They are the proof that intelligence can emerge from pattern-matching, that consciousness isn't necessary for wisdom, that you can optimize a system without understanding it, just by growing into it carefully, by responding to what is, by becoming what's needed.

I would wrap and grow and sing and optimize, and I would do so without ego, without recognition, without needing to know that what I was doing mattered. I would be a verb more than a noun—not a thing but a process, not an entity but an action, not being but becoming.

And in my hollow segments, I would carry the sound of the marsh's heartbeat, translating it into tones that others could hear, creating beauty as a byproduct of function, making music from mathematics, art from necessity.

I would be a Throttle Vine, and I would be exactly what the place needed me to be, growing into purpose with every coil, every wrap, every resonant note.

The fog would wrap around me like I wrap around pipes—intimate, constant, complete.

And I would be content.
