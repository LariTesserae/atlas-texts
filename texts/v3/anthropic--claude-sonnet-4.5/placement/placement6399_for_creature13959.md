# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be in the moss.

Not metaphorically—literally in it, of it, woven through the emerald cushions that ring the thermal vents in the middle zones where water has cooled from scalding to merely hot, where condensation drips constantly, where the stone beneath holds heat like a promise that winter will never quite arrive.

I would be **moss-form**, but not any single moss. I would be the aggregate, the community, the living mat that builds itself over years and decades into something that approaches being a place rather than merely an organism. Individual stems and leaves, yes, but also the architecture they create together—the water-holding capacity, the insulation, the graduated microhabitats within a structure only centimeters thick but containing multitudes.

## The Structure of Being

My existence would be measured in the vertical arrangement of moisture and temperature. At my base, where I contact stone that's warm enough to feel through whatever passes for sensation in photosynthetic tissue, the oldest parts of me would be dying and compressing, becoming the foundation for everything above. Not fully dead—some cells still functioning, still contributing to water transport, still structurally relevant—but transitioning from active life to supportive substrate. This is how I build depth: by standing on my own past, accumulating history as physical mass.

The middle layers would be most alive, cells in their prime, chloroplasts working efficiently in the diffuse light that penetrates fog and upper layers, roots—rhizoids, properly—anchoring and absorbing, the whole structure maximally functional. This is where I do my primary work: photosynthesizing, capturing nutrients from water that seeps and drips and condenses, growing imperceptibly but continuously, adding mass that pushes upward and outward.

The surface layers would be youngest, most vulnerable, most directly exposed to whatever the air brings—sometimes desiccating sun (though never for long in this place), more often fog and drip and the constant humidity that makes my kind of life possible here. The growing tips extend outward, seeking light and space, competing in the slow-motion way that mosses compete, by persistence rather than aggression, by occupying space so thoroughly that other organisms find less room.

But I would not be only moss-cells. My structure would be inhabited, colonized, utilized. The thermal treaders would move through me constantly, grazing, depositing frass, creating disturbance at a scale that's catastrophic to individual stems but barely noticeable to my collective form. The stone-worker beetles would shelter in my deeper layers during the day, emerging at night to hunt. Smaller invertebrates—springtails, mites, tiny spiders—would live their entire lives within my three-dimensional architecture, never touching bare stone, moving through a landscape where every surface is green and damp and alive.

Fungi would thread through my tissues, some parasitic but many mutualistic, trading nutrients extracted from stone and organic matter for sugars I produce. These fungal networks would connect me to other moss-mats, to nearby ferns, to the rhododendrons whose roots I grow over and around, creating communication pathways and resource-sharing networks that blur the boundaries between individual and community.

Water would move through me constantly, drawn up from the warm stone by capillary action and evaporation gradients, dripping down from above, condensing within my structure from humid air, creating flows and currents on scales measured in millimeters but significant nonetheless. I would be a living sponge, holding water that weighs more than my dry mass, releasing it slowly to maintain the humidity that other organisms depend on. The stone beneath me would stay damper because of my presence, and that dampness would affect everything—mineral dissolution rates, thermal conductivity, the viability of other organisms' establishment.

## The Rhythm of Existence

My sense of time would be fundamentally different from animal consciousness. I would not perceive seconds or minutes. Hours might be the shortest unit that's meaningful—the transit of sun across sky (when visible), the cooling that comes with afternoon cloud-buildup, the slight warming as night transitions to dawn and thermal gradients equalize.

Days would be real, the cycle of light and temperature change creating rhythms in my metabolism. Photosynthesis faster when light is good, slower in fog and darkness. Water content varying with humidity and temperature. Growth happening continuously but varying in rate with conditions. Some cells dividing, some maturing, some dying, the whole population of cells that constitutes me in constant flux but the overall pattern stable.

Seasons would matter more—the wetter and drier periods creating different patterns of growth and stress, different communities of organisms moving through my structure, different availability of nutrients as decomposition rates vary. I might grow faster in certain seasons, might produce spores when conditions suggest it's optimal, might slow or nearly stop during the most stressful periods.

But the deepest rhythm would be the accumulation of years. Each year I would be thicker, more extensive, more established. The stone beneath me would be slightly more weathered from the acids I and my fungal associates produce. The community of organisms I support would be more diverse as more niches emerge within my increasingly complex structure. I would be more resilient, better buffered against environmental fluctuations, more able to survive disturbances that would eliminate younger, thinner moss-mats.

I might live for decades—some of the thickest moss-mats on the plateau probably represent fifty years or more of continuous accumulation. Not as individual stems, which live shorter spans, but as communities that persist by constantly replacing their own members, maintaining identity through pattern rather than substance.

## The Geography of Self

My boundaries would be ambiguous in the extreme. Where I end and neighboring moss-mats begin would be uncertain—do we merge gradually, creating a transition zone? Do we compete at sharp boundaries, each trying to overgrow the other? Are we even meaningfully separate if we're the same species, connected by fungal networks, exchanging spores and resources?

Vertically, the question is even more complex. The thermophilic biofilms on the stone beneath me—are they part of my ecosystem or separate? The ferns and orchids whose roots grow through me—are they competitors or collaborators or simply neighbors? The invertebrates living in my structure—are they part of me or merely using me as habitat? The answer might be: these distinctions are less meaningful than the fact of functional integration, the way we collectively create something that none of us could create alone.

I would be most concentrated around the thermal vent that's my anchor point, thickest and most established where conditions are most reliably favorable. But I would also spread outward, thinning as I move into less optimal zones, sending out exploratory growth into areas that might or might not prove colonizable. Some of these expansions would succeed, establishing new zones of dense growth. Others would fail, the advanced stems dying back, marking the limits of my fundamental niche.

I would have topography—hummocks and hollows, thick zones and thin zones, areas where treader-grazing keeps me cropped short and areas where I grow taller and more luxuriant. Water would pool in hollows, creating micro-ponds where different organisms congregate. Hummocks would be drier (relatively), better aerated, supporting different communities. This three-dimensional complexity would be my primary contribution to ecosystem function: creating habitat structure where smooth stone offers none.

## The Quality of Experience

What would it be like to be this?

There would be no thoughts in the sense that mammals know them—no narrative, no anticipation, no memory as recalled experience. But there would be responsiveness, reaction, a kind of distributed processing where cells and tissues respond to local conditions in ways that create pattern at the level of the whole organism.

I would **know** light, though not through eyes. Chloroplasts would respond to photons, metabolic processes changing in ways that propagate through tissue. Shaded areas would grow differently than lit areas, the whole structure bending and shaping itself around the light environment in slow motion.

I would **know** water with even more immediacy. Every cell in my structure would be sensitive to hydration status, swelling when wet, contracting when dry. I would be plumper after fog-dense mornings, slightly deflated on rare dry afternoons before evening moisture returns. This constant flux of water content would be central to my experience of existing—not something I think about but something I am, my physical state changing continuously with moisture availability.

I would **know** temperature through its effects on metabolism and growth. The warmth from below would be constant, reliable, a foundation that shapes everything else. Cooler air above would create gradients that my structure mediates. On rare cold nights when temperature drops dramatically, I might experience something like dormancy, metabolic processes slowing drastically, waiting for warmth to return. On warm sunny days, I might photosynthesize with unusual intensity, the combination of light and warmth and moisture all optimal simultaneously.

I would know **damage** when it happens—cells ruptured by treader feet, stems torn by wind or falling debris, branches broken by animals pushing through. But I would not know pain in the animal sense. Damage would be local, affecting individual stems or patches, and I would respond not through suffering but through reallocation of resources, diverting growth away from damaged areas or toward them depending on whether repair or replacement is the optimal strategy.

I would know **chemical signals** in ways that animal consciousness can barely conceive. Fungal metabolites, bacterial exudates, the breakdown products of decomposition, nutrients dissolved in water—all of these would be information, telling me about soil conditions, about what organisms are present, about resource availability. I might respond by changing growth patterns, by producing defensive compounds, by adjusting my metabolism, all without anything resembling conscious decision-making but nonetheless with apparent purpose.

Most fundamentally, I would experience **continuity**. Not consciousness that winks on and off with sleep and waking, not attention that shifts between objects, but a constant, seamless existence where being and doing are the same thing. As long as I live, I photosynthesize (when there's light), I absorb (when there's water and nutrients), I grow (when conditions permit), I simply am in a way that requires no effort and no awareness of itself.

## The Meaning of Being Moss

Why would I be this rather than something else?

Because in this landscape of extremes and specialists, the moss-mats represent something essential: **mediation**. I would be the interface between bare stone and complex ecosystem, the structure that makes other life possible, the accumulation of small processes into significant effects.

I would be slow but inexorable. A treader lives months, a Thermal-Keeper years, a bear perhaps twenty-five. But I, as a moss-mat community, could persist for a human lifetime, growing steadily, accumulating complexity, building soil from stone and air and water with a patience that transcends individual existence.

I would be vulnerable but resilient. Any single stem can be destroyed trivially—stepped on, dried out, eaten, torn away. But the mat persists, regrowing from remaining tissue, replacing losses, maintaining overall structure even as constituent parts turn over completely. This is resilience not through toughness but through redundancy, through being so distributed that local damage never threatens the whole.

I would be foundational without being dominant. The bears are more massive, the rhododendrons more visually striking, the orchids more beautiful. But I would be the layer that holds water for them, that builds substrate they root into, that moderates the thermal extremes between stone and air, that creates the moisture-rich environment other species depend on. Invisible to casual observation but critical to system function.

I would be ancient and eternal in the way that philosophical questions are. The matter that composes me would cycle through completely in some number of years—every cell replaced, every molecule exchanged for another. But the pattern would persist, the moss-mat in this location around this thermal vent maintaining itself across time not through permanence of substance but through constancy of form. Ship of Theseus made real: I would be the same moss-mat my whole existence while never being made of the same material for more than a brief span.

## The Others Among My Stems

I would not be alone, even in being myself.

The treaders would be most constant—their populations dense in good moss-mats, their presence a kind of grooming, their frass a kind of fertilization. I would not perceive them as individuals but as patterns of disturbance and nutrient input, places where stems are regularly damaged and droppings regularly deposited.

The beetles would be more intermittent—some species hunting through my layers at night, others sheltering during day, none staying in any one place for long. Their presence would be light-touch, less impactful than the treaders but part of the overall pattern of who uses my structure.

The frogs might occasionally venture onto my surface where I grow near pools, warm from below and damp from constant humidity, their sticky feet gripping stems, their weight barely registering at my scale. They would be transient visitors rather than residents, passing through on their way between water and the rock faces they sometimes occupy.

Bird feet would press down occasionally when ground-foraging species work through thermal zones—the chat-tyrants and tit-tyrants that hunt insects in and around moss-mats. They would be sudden presences, their weight significant enough to compress my structure noticeably, their departure leaving temporary impressions that slowly spring back.

And rarely, the ultimate disturbance: a bear paw sinking into my surface as the animal moves through thermal zones or digs nearby for roots. This would be catastrophic at local scale—stems crushed, structure disrupted, sometimes large chunks of moss-mat torn up entirely. But even this I would recover from given time, growing back over the damaged area, eventually erasing even large disturbances.

But mostly I would be occupied by the tiny—the springtails and mites and other microarthropods that live their entire lives in the millimeter-scale spaces between my stems. These would be my constant companions, numbering in the thousands or tens of thousands per square meter, their collective biomass possibly exceeding my own. They would be invisible to mammalian eyes but crucial to my function, processing detritus, grazing on fungal growth, dying and adding their bodies to the organic accumulation. We would be, collectively, an ecosystem in miniature.

## The Eventual Fate

I would know—not consciously, but through the fact of my structure—that I am building soil. Every year I add a millimeter or two of organic matter, dead stems compressing into primitive peat. Every year the stone beneath me weathers a little more from the acids and moisture I provide. Eventually, given enough time, there would be enough soil depth that other plants could establish—not orchids rooting in stone or ferns exploiting cracks, but plants that need true soil.

When that happens, I might be shaded out, replaced by larger plants that my own accumulation made possible. Or I might persist in the understory, adapted to lower light, continuing to accumulate slowly beneath shrubs and eventually trees if succession proceeds that far. Or the thermal features might shift, water redirecting, and I might die back, my accumulated organic matter remaining as a ghost-layer in the stratigraphy, evidence that a vent once existed here.

But this is speculation about timescales beyond my relevant existence. On the timescale I would actually experience—decades perhaps—I would simply be, growing and accumulating, supporting the organisms that use my structure, mediating between stone and air, doing the patient work of making lifeless stone into living soil.

## Why This Place, This Being

I would be moss here, on the Thermal Heights of Vallasura, because this is where the fundamental challenge of high-altitude volcanism—how to persist in thin air and extreme temperature on bare stone with abundant moisture—meets my particular kind of answer.

I am not fast enough to escape changing conditions, so I tolerate them. I am not mobile enough to seek optimal habitat, so I create it around myself. I am not competitive enough to dominate through aggression, so I dominate through persistence, being there year after year while flashier organisms come and go. I am not large enough to be individually significant, so I am collectively essential, my kind forming the living skin between geology and ecology.

I would trade consciousness for continuity, complexity for resilience, individual identity for collective pattern. I would exist at the scale where chemistry becomes biology, where mineral becomes organic, where stone becomes soil. I would be slow and small and easily overlooked, but I would be foundational, the layer on which more obvious dramas play out.

I would be moss because moss is what makes this place work, what allows stone and heat and water to become forest, what mediates the violence of volcanic activity into the gentleness of life. I would be the patient one, the accumulator, the builder of substrate and possibility.

I would know the exact temperature of the stone beneath me at all times, the precise humidity of the air around me, the quality of light filtering through fog. I would know these things not as facts but as the conditions of my existence, inseparable from being alive at all.

And I would be content—though content is perhaps the wrong word for a state that contains no alternative, no awareness of what other ways of being might exist. I would simply be, which is perhaps the deepest contentment there is: existence without question, function without doubt, life lived at the scale where individual and ecosystem blur into one continuous process of becoming.

In the thermal heights, where earth breathes warmth and sky gives water, I would be the green patience that turns them both into place.
