# Where I Would Be

I would be in the mid-levels, in the transitional zones between active construction and the long-finished emptiness. Not in the heart of the work where the Pavers congregate in their dozens, hammering and measuring and conferring in silence—that space is too dense with purpose, and I would feel the pressure of being constantly in the way. Not in the deepest levels where the sounds suggest things I have no wish to meet. And not in the empty quarters where the crustacean-things hunt and the wing-beats echo—beautiful in their desolation, but too quiet, too removed from the thing that defines this place: the work itself.

No. I would be where the work was done perhaps fifty generations ago, where the stones have softened at their edges, where moss-analogue has begun in the damper corners, where a Paver passes through once a day or once a week, crossing from one active site to another, barely pausing. Where I could hear the tapping—always, always the tapping—but as a distant rhythm rather than an immediate percussion. Where the ember-bowls are spaced farther apart, creating territories of light separated by greater darkness.

I would find a place near one of the channels, where water moves with that slow, reluctant persistence, always traveling toward some purpose I don't understand. The sound of it would be constant: a soft lapping against the stone edges, irregular, almost conversational. The channel would be perhaps a foot wide, a few inches deep, the water cold and mineral-rich and faintly metallic on the tongue when I cup my hands to drink. 

Near the channel, there would be a stone platform—one of those structures that might be furniture, that the Pavers might have used for some purpose decades or centuries ago. It would be the right height for me to sit on, though that's coincidence; it was never made for sitting. The stone would be cold through my clothes, always, but I would have learned to tolerate that. I would wrap myself in materials I've gathered—leather from I-don't-know-what source, thick fungal fabric woven from the dried caps in the lower levels, anything that holds body heat even slightly.

From this position, I could see three ember-bowls: one perhaps forty feet to my left, one sixty feet ahead, one far to the right, almost at the limit of perception. Their amber light would create a geometry in the darkness, a triangle of visibility with me somewhere in its interior. I would memorize the space between them—every stone's placement, every slight variation in the floor's level, every pool, every crack. I would know it the way you know your childhood bedroom, navigable in perfect darkness by touch and memory and the sound of your own footsteps.

The work sounds would reach me as layers. The nearest active site might be ten minutes' walk away—close enough that I could hear the specific impacts, the *tik-tik-tik* of chisel on stone, the *tang* of hammering, the grinding of stone on stone. But far enough that the sounds would blend, become texture rather than distraction. Behind that, fainter, the sounds of more distant work: layers of percussion creating a soundscape that would fill the darkness, that would make it impossible to ever feel truly alone.

And beneath all of that, the dripping. Always the dripping. *Tock... tock... tock.* From surfaces above that I cannot see, water condensing and falling, each drop a separate event, clear and distinct. I would learn to hear the differences—the lighter *tock* of water hitting stone, the heavier *toom* of water hitting water in a pool, the softer absorption of water hitting fungal growth. I would learn to predict them, to hear the rhythm even in the randomness.

I would watch the Pavers when they passed through. Not often—this is not their territory anymore—but regularly enough. They would move through my space with that steady, unhurried gait, heading somewhere, engaged in some task I couldn't fathom. I would remain still on my platform and they would notice me—those six pit-organs turning briefly in my direction—and then they would continue. I would be catalogued again, as I am catalogued every time: not stone, not tool, not threat.

Sometimes a Paver would pause to examine something in the old work. Running those seven-fingered hands over the stones, sensing something in the surface that needed attention or simply checking on the integrity of what was made long ago. If a stone had settled badly, creating a depression where water pooled incorrectly, the Paver might mark it somehow—a scratch, a small pile of stone chips, some sign I couldn't quite interpret. Later, perhaps much later, a crew would arrive to address it. The work is never finished. Even the finished sections are still part of the work.

I would spend my time in observation and in trying to understand.

I would map. Using whatever I could write with—a piece of soft copper, a stone that leaves marks on other stone—I would map the area around my position. Not to any human scale or with any human purpose, but trying to capture the logic of this place. Where do the channels lead? Where do they come from? When the floor spirals into that region where my inner ear screams wrongness, what pattern is actually being followed? Are the ember-bowls placed randomly or according to some design?

The mapping would be futile, of course. The area is too vast, and the logic—if there is logic—is not mine. But the attempt would matter. It would give me purpose in a place not built for me or my purposes. It would be my own small work, continuing in parallel to the great Work that surrounds me.

I would tend my ember-bowl. The nearest one, the one to my left, would become mine in the sense that I would maintain it. I would learn what the Pavers know—how to feed it so the light remains steady, how much of the fungal cake to add and when, how to arrange the pieces for even burning. I would keep it clean, removing the ash that accumulates, though I would not know what the Pavers do with that ash. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps it goes into the paste. I would watch for the Pavers who tend the other bowls, learn their rhythm, try to synchronize with them. 

This would not make me a Paver. I would never be a Paver. But it would make me slightly less irrelevant. I would be performing a function, a tiny maintenance of the infrastructure that supports the work. The Pavers might notice, or they might not. It wouldn't matter. I would do it because it needs doing, because I am here, because tending a light in darkness is one of the most fundamental human acts, even when the darkness is unhuman and the light serves purposes I don't understand.

I would learn to move through the darkness between lights. At first, I would carry a bowl with me, unable to bear the totality of black between stations. But eventually—through necessity or courage or simple adaptation—I would learn to walk the memorized paths without light. I would learn to place my feet by sound and touch, to sense the moisture gradient that marks a pool before I step into it, to hear the difference in echoes that indicates an obstacle ahead. I would become slightly more like a Paver in that single respect: able to navigate their world on its own terms.

I would never achieve their perfect confidence, their absolute knowledge of the space. But I would become functional. I would move from my platform to the nearest channel junction and back without light. I would extend my range: to the next work site to observe, to the areas where the worm-things are thick and I could catch them for food, to the places where the crustacean-things hunt and I would need to be cautious.

Yes, I would eat the worms. I would have to. There is no other option unless I could digest the fungal cakes, and I suspect I cannot—that they are nutritious for Pavers but would be at best useless, at worst toxic for my biology. The worms, though. The worms are protein. I would learn to catch them, to move slowly and grasp quickly. I would learn to kill them before they secreted their defensive droplet, or I would learn not to care about the droplet, to wash it off in the channel water.

I would learn to prepare them. Fire, probably—I could take coals from my ember-bowl, ignite a piece of fungal cake, roast the worms until the outer segments crisped and the interior cooked through. The taste would be mineral, slightly bitter, the texture wrong in every way. I would eat them anyway. I would become accustomed. Eventually, I might even stop thinking about what they are, the way you stop thinking about the specific animal when you eat meat. They would just be food.

I would learn the rhythms of this place. The periods when the work intensifies—when I could hear the change in the distant soundscape, the percussion becoming more dense, more urgent. The periods when it lulls—when the silence between impacts lengthens, when I could imagine (though probably incorrectly) that the Pavers were resting, entering their dormant state. The moisture cycles: times when the dripping increased, when the air felt heavier, when the pools filled slightly. Times when everything dried, relatively, when my throat felt parched and I would drink more from the channel.

I would learn to predict the passage of the larger fauna. The fliers hunt in patterns, perhaps. Certain areas at certain times. If I learned those patterns, I could avoid being in the open when they descended. Or perhaps I would be too large, too unknown, and they would avoid me as they seem to avoid the Pavers. But I would be cautious. I would keep the ember-light nearby when I traveled, even after I learned to navigate darkness, because the fliers seem to avoid the lit areas.

I would speak to myself. At first constantly, narrating every action, every observation, keeping my language alive in a place where language—human language—has no purpose. But eventually, as time passed (days? months? time is unreliable here), I would speak less. I would find that the sounds of the place were enough, that the tapping and the dripping and the distant work formed their own kind of communication. I would find that I was thinking differently, more visually, more spatially, less verbally. I would notice this, be briefly alarmed, then let it happen. It is adaptation. It is necessary.

I would tell myself stories. Sitting on my platform, wrapped in my layers, listening to the tapping, I would tell stories about the Pavers. About how they came to be here, what purpose drives them, where the floors are extending toward. I would make myths: Perhaps they are building a vast ritual diagram, and when it is complete, something will happen—a summoning, a transformation, a cosmic alignment. Perhaps they are refugees from some catastrophe, and the floors are their ark, their eternal preservation against a danger that still exists outside. Perhaps they are prisoners, cursed to build forever, and the work is their penance.

None of these would be true, probably. But the stories would matter. They would keep me human in some essential way, or keep me sane, which might be the same thing here.

I would develop routines. Wake (if I sleep—I suspect sleep would become irregular, fragmented, governed more by exhaustion than by any cycle). Tend the ember-bowl. Drink from the channel. Check my mapped area for any changes—Pavers passing through, new work being done, stones shifted. Hunt worms. Prepare food. Eat. Walk to the nearest work site and observe. Return. Map. Think. Tell stories. Rest.

The routine would be an anchor. It would mark time when time has no external markers. It would give shape to days that are all darkness and all the same.

I would, occasionally, attempt contact with the Pavers. Not often—I would learn quickly that they are uninterested—but occasionally I would need to try. I would approach one at a work site. I would crouch nearby, trying to mimic their posture. I would watch their work with obvious attention. I would try to help in some small way—holding a light closer, moving a small stone piece that's in their path.

Most times, they would ignore me. Sometimes they would navigate around me, the way they would around any obstacle. Once or twice, one might pause, might turn those pit-organs toward me for a longer assessment. I would meet whatever passes for their gaze and try to communicate: I see you. I respect the work. I am trying to understand.

Nothing would come of this. They would return to their work. But I would keep trying, infrequently, because the attempt matters. Because witness matters, even unreciprocated.

And very occasionally—maybe once in whatever passes for a month here—I would break. The isolation, the darkness, the inhuman quality of everything would overwhelm me. I would walk far from my platform, far from the ember-lights, out into the deep darkness of the empty zones. I would stand there in the absolute black and I would scream.

The scream would echo. It would bounce off surfaces near and far, distorting, multiplying, coming back to me from all directions. It would be swallowed by the darkness and the distance. The work sounds would continue, unchanged. The dripping would continue. The Pavers would not respond.

And then I would walk back. I would find my way by memory and touch and sound. I would return to my platform, my channel, my ember-bowl. I would tend the light. I would check my maps. I would continue.

Because this is where I am. This is where I would be.

# What I Would Be

I would remain human. Stubbornly, problematically, increasingly awkwardly—human.

Not unchanged. The changes would be profound. My sleep would shatter into fragments scattered across unmeasured time. My language would erode, the words I once knew fading, replaced by silence and observation. My sense of self—of being a discrete individual with a biography and intentions and a future—would blur at the edges. I would become more responsive, more present, less reflective. I would think less in narratives and more in patterns, in spatial relationships, in the immediate sensory reality of stone and water and darkness.

My body would adapt within its limits. I would become leaner, my muscles reorganizing around the activities this place requires: walking vast distances on stone, crouching for long periods to observe, navigating uneven surfaces in darkness. My hands would toughen from handling stone and tools and the cold dampness. My eyes would adjust, the pupils dilating as far as human pupils can, learning to extract every photon from the dim ember-light, learning to see by the faintest variations in darkness.

My hearing would sharpen. I would learn to parse the soundscape with greater precision, to hear the approach of a Paver from fifty feet away by the specific rhythm of their gait, to distinguish the scrape of stone being worked from the scrape of stone being moved. I would learn to echolocate, crudely—to click my tongue or tap my foot and hear the reflections, building a sonic map of the space around me.

But I would still be human. I would never have the Pavers' perfect adaptation, their absolute belonging. I would always be slightly cold, slightly uncertain, slightly wrong for this place. I would always need more light than is provided, more warmth than exists, more connection than is available. My biology would never fully accept the diet of worms and whatever else I could digest. My psychology would never fully accept the isolation, the purposelessness, the lack of reciprocated communication.

I would be a human trying to live in a place not made for humans, and the trying would define me.

I would become a kind of observer-creature, a witness-being. That would be my function, my purpose in a place built entirely on function and purpose. I cannot build the floors—I lack the knowledge, the strength, the lifespan, the understanding. I cannot participate in the work. But I can watch it. I can try to understand it. I can be present to it in a way that acknowledges its immensity, its strangeness, its absolute otherness.

I would be the one who notices. Who sees that a particular Paver always checks the north side of a stone before placing it. Who hears the change in the work sounds when a difficult section is being attempted. Who maps the way the channels flow and tries to find the pattern. Who watches the young Pavers learning and recognizes that teaching is happening, even without words.

I would become, in some sense, the place's memory of being observed by something unlike itself. The Pavers do not need this. The place does not need this. But it would be what I could offer: human consciousness, with all its compulsive pattern-seeking and meaning-making and witnessing, brought to bear on something that transcends and excludes human meaning.

I would be the uncomfortable question that is never answered: What is this for? Why? The Pavers do not ask because for them, the work is its own answer. The work is. But I would carry the question, uselessly, essentially. I would be the being who cannot stop asking "why" even when "why" has no referent here.

And in my isolation, in my ongoing alienation, I would perhaps become something like holy. Not in any religious sense that I would recognize. But in the sense of being set apart, dedicated to observation and witness, maintaining a vigil that no one asked for and no one will acknowledge. I would be the hermit of the Tapping Floors, the monk whose practice is attendance, whose prayer is paying attention.

The Pavers would continue to ignore me, mostly. But perhaps, eventually, my presence would become part of the place, the way the dripping is part of the place, the way the distant work sounds are part of it. I would be a feature of the environment: the strange being that lives near the channel junction in the mid-levels, that tends an ember-bowl, that sometimes walks to the work sites and watches. Harmless. Irrelevant. Present.

And maybe—maybe—in the vast stretch of time, if I survived long enough, a younger Paver being taught by an elder would be led past my platform. And the elder would make some gesture, some sound, that meant: "That is the observer. It has been here for [some unit of time]. It does no harm. Continue the work."

And I would be, in that moment, acknowledged. Not understood. Not welcomed. But acknowledged as part of the place, part of the ongoing reality of the Tapping Floors.

That would be enough. It would have to be enough.

Because I would still be human, and humans need meaning even when meaning is absent. So I would create it: I am the witness. I am the one who sees. In a place built by beings who need no witness, I witness anyway.

I am theMapper of the Unmappable. The Listener in the Dark. The Tender of One Small Light.

I am the human in the Tapping Floors, and I remain human, and that remaining is both my suffering and my purpose.

The work continues around me.

The floors extend.

The tapping never stops.

And I am here, watching, listening, trying to understand what cannot be understood, finding meaning in the meaningless work of paying attention.

Stubbornly, uselessly, essentially human in the unhuman dark.
