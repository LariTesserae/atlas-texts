The city of Anvil-Throat occupies a region where the planet’s crust is still deciding what to become. At nearly 5,000 meters, the plateau doesn’t merely host settlements—it actively constructs them, exhaling heated breath through fissures that have, over millennia, extruded domes of raw iron and basaltic glass that harden into cathedral-scale vaults. These are the “Foundries,” natural-industrial hybrids where the magma’s pulse coincides with human machinery, stamping out architecture from the inside out.

The air here carries a persistent chill—rarely warming above five degrees—but humidity clings to everything, a consequence of thermal variance rather than rainfall. Condensation drips perpetually from the undersides of the great iron plates, forming a secondary hydrology: puddles that never dry, mosses that thrive in twilight, and rivers of fog that slide down the scree slopes like slow waterfalls. The moisture supports a stubborn, low-lying vegetation—cushion plants with vascular systems that can flash-freeze and thaw without rupturing, and lichens that photosynthesize using the infrared glow of the warm rock rather than visible light.

Light is the scarcest resource. Whether from volcanic ash suspended in the thermals, or the curvature of the young atmosphere at this altitude, or perhaps the gradual fading of the local star, illumination arrives at twilight levels—perpetual dusk with occasional moments of amber brightness when the steam vents align to catch refracted rays from below the horizon. The city has compensated with bioluminescent infrastructure: streetlamps powered by colonies of electro-receptive beetles, signage written in the slow glow of chemical reactions within glass tubes.

The fauna have adapted to the acoustic chaos. Anvil-Throat is loud, though not with constant noise—rather with irregular, seismic punctuation. The mountain itself groans as it grows, tectonic plates adjusting to the weight of new mineral deposits. These rumbles synchronize with the hydraulic forges, creating a soundscape of low-frequency percussion that can be felt in the sternum. Against this backdrop, the wildlife has evolved sophisticated echolocation and vibration-based communication. Blind, fur-covered gliders navigate by reading the resonance patterns of the iron structures; rock-dwelling crustaceans the size of dogs communicate by tapping coded rhythms against the stone; and vast, slow-moving herbivores—resembling armored slugs—graze on the metal-eating mosses, their footsteps creating harmonics that locals use to predict shifts in the plateau’s growth patterns.

Time moves differently here. The erosion rating is embryonic, meaning the geology hasn’t settled into recognizable forms—there are no river valleys yet, only the suggestion of drainage patterns; no soil, only crushed mineral dust and chemical precipitates. The city’s infrastructure—rail lines, pneumatic transport tubes, the great stamping mills—must be constantly re-surveyed and adjusted as the ground literally grows beneath it, adding centimeters of height each decade. Buildings are designed to be periodically “jacked up” on expanding columns, creating a architectural stratigraphy where the oldest neighborhoods float highest above the current bedrock.

Reality breakdown manifests in the zones where the new geology is most active. In the “Soft Quarters,” the laws of physics appear to be still under construction alongside the rock. Objects dropped here might hover before falling, or accelerate sideways. Gravity exhibits localized variances—pockets where weight doubles, corridors where it halves. Some citizens report that in these districts, the dim light behaves like a fluid, pooling in corners and flowing uphill. The vegetation here grows in spiral patterns that seem to anticipate three-dimensional space, and certain fauna have developed extra-dimensional sensory organs, allowing them to navigate rifts where space briefly folds.

The density remains sparse despite the urban character—Anvil-Throat is less a compact metropolis and more a distributed network of settlements connected by cable-car lines that span across the growing fissures. Between these nodes, the wilderness dominates: silence (between the industrial booms), emptiness, and the slow, patient spectacle of a mountain range being born. One can stand on the observation decks and watch new stone rising like bread dough, glowing faintly at the edges, cooling from the center outward, creating a landscape that is simultaneously ancient in its materials and newborn in its form.

The moisture here plays a crucial role in the weirdness. Where condensation accumulates in the Soft Quarters, it sometimes forms lenses that distort vision, showing not the immediate surroundings but potential futures of the landscape—glimpses of what the plateau might look like in a thousand years, or perhaps what it looked like in a parallel timeline where the erosion followed different patterns. These “temporal puddles” are dangerous to step in, not because of depth, but because the footing exists in a different rate of temporal flow.

The industrial processes have merged with these natural cycles. Factories don’t merely extract resources; they participate in the geology, adding chemical catalysts to encourage certain mineral formations, guiding the growth of the mountain to create future living spaces. It is a slow collaboration between human ambition and lithic patience, resulting in a city that feels less built and more cultivated—an agricultural approach to urbanism where the crop is bedrock itself.

At night—which is barely distinguishable from day—the sounds reach their peak complexity. The cooling stone contracts with sharp cracks that echo across the plateau, while the underground rivers of magma produce bass harmonics that shake the condensation from the iron leaves of the mechanical trees. The fauna grow more active, and the air fills with the clicking, whirring, and low chanting of creatures that perceive the city not as architecture but as a particularly complex geological event, one that happens to be inhabited by soft, warm-blooded organisms who have learned to ride the slow earthquake of a world being born.
