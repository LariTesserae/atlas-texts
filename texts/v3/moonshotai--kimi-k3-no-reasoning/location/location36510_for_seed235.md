# The Hanging City of Terosthenes

---

The city occupies a rift. Not a canyon — a canyon implies a river, a carving force, a narrative of formation. This is a *separation*, a place where two great plates of dark basalt simply pulled apart at some deep moment of geological history and then, for reasons no erosion can explain, *stopped*. The walls drop eleven hundred metres from the rim to the valley floor, nearly vertical, wet black stone sweating at every surface. The rift is perhaps eight hundred metres across at its widest, narrow enough that the far wall presses on your vision from anywhere within it. Looking down, you cannot see the bottom from the rim. Looking up, you cannot see the rim from the bottom. Both are lost in the same white haze — the suspended, near-saturated air that lives permanently inside the crack, condensing against the stone, condensing against the leaves, condensing against every built surface until the whole rift runs with the slow, patient drip of a breathing thing.

The vegetation fills it. This is the first impossibility, though not the strangest. The rift should be a barren slot — too steep for soil, too dark at depth, too narrow for a real canopy to form. Instead, it is *packed*. Trees with pale, swollen trunks erupt from the sheer walls in horizontal clutches, their roots cracking into fissures that should not hold them, their crowns woven together so densely that from above the rift appears to be filled with a continuous mass of dark green flesh, undulating slightly, like the surface of a slow organ. Below this roofline, the air inside the rift is dim and wet and green-tinted, and the growth only thickens: vines the diameter of ship's cable descend from the canopy in curtains so dense that entire sections of the rift walls are invisible behind them, and epiphytes cluster on every horizontal surface in rosettes of red and bone-white and near-black purple, their leaves broad as shields, waxy, beaded permanently with condensation. Fungi colonise the shaded underlayers — not mushrooms but vast shelf-growths, metres wide, pale as old paper, stacked in tiers along the walls like the ruffles of some enormous formal garment. Nothing in the rift is bare. Nothing is uncovered. The density of life is total, suffocating, a biological pressure that fills every cubic metre of the space.

The heat is total too, and motionless. The rift traps it. Some thermal engine deep below — geothermal, perhaps, or something else — pushes warmth upward through the crack, and the dense canopy caps it, and the result is a permanent, unvarying heat of perhaps thirty-seven degrees, the temperature of a body, with humidity so complete that sweat does not evaporate and breath feels thick, tasted. There is no wind. There has never been wind inside the rift. The air is stable, stratified in subtle layers of scent — the sweet-rot of decomposing canopy litter at the bottom, a mineral coolness nearer the walls, something faintly electrical near the top where the open sky bleaches the green — but these layers do not mix. They sit upon each other like sheets of glass, permanent.

And through all of this, threaded into the vegetation and against the walls and across the void, is the city.

It is difficult to say how large the city is, because it is difficult to say where it ends. Structures emerge from the green at every level: platforms of fitted stone, cisterns, towers that bridge from one wall to the other, staircases cut directly into the basalt, buildings that seem to grow from the living rock as if secreted by it. The architecture is dense, layered, additive — wall upon wall, room upon room, a construction logic of accretion rather than design. There are no streets. There are paths, but they are organic: worn routes through the growth, along the backs of fallen columns, across bridges woven from living root and dried vine that sway with a sickening patience over the void. Buildings cluster at nodes — where a buttress of rock juts from a wall, where the rift narrows enough to span, where a natural platform offered purchase — and between these nodes the city thins into isolated towers, hanging gardens, single rooms perched on ledges like swallows' nests. The whole settlement is vertical. It has no ground plan. It is a three-dimensional labyrinth, a city the way a coral reef is a city.

The construction is old. Not ruinous — *established*. Every structure has achieved a settled relationship with the vegetation that consumes it. Roots have entered walls and become structural. Trees have grown through roofless rooms and now hold those rooms together. Stone that cracked long ago has been re-cemented by mineral deposits from the ceaseless drip of condensation, healed by the very water that eroded it. The city and the rift and the growth have reached a stasis, a dynamic equilibrium so complete that it is impossible to tell where architecture ends and geology begins. A staircase might be carved stone or a natural formation colonised by footsteps. A wall might be masonry or a flow of basalt that cooled into something useful. The whole place is *weathered into itself*, a settled form, everything in its long-established state.

There are no people.

This bears emphasis because the city is *maintained*. The paths are clear. The root-bridges are sound. Cisterns are full and clean, their stone lips uncracked. Certain rooms contain objects — not tools, not devices, nothing wrought or manufactured, but *arrangements*: stones placed in deliberate geometries on flat surfaces, bones (animal bones, though there are no animals) stacked in careful cairns, dried seedpods threaded on vine tendons and hung in doorways where they clatter, gently, in air that should not be moving. Someone — something — tends this place. But there are no people, and there have never been people, and the maintenance is not performed by hands.

The arrangements rearrange themselves. This is observable. A cairn of bones, left unvisited, will be in a different configuration the next day — not scattered, not destroyed, but *revised*, as if some editorial intelligence has reconsidered the composition. The hanging seedpod strands will be longer, or shorter, or restrung in a different order. The geometric stone arrays shift toward forms that are almost recognisable — a spiral that is nearly logarithmic, a branching pattern that is almost the vein structure of a leaf, an arrangement that is almost, but not quite, a face.

This is where the rift stops being explicable.

The sounds. The rift is *loud*. This is the second thing you notice, after the green, and it does not stop. The loudest sound is water: not a single source but thousands, millions, the total sum of all the condensation dripping from every surface, every leaf-tip, every fungal shelf, every stone overhang in the entire eleven-hundred-metre crack, all falling at slightly different rates, striking different materials — leaf, stone, water, wood, more water — producing a continuous, layered, roar-hiss-patter that is not quite white noise because it has too much *texture*, too many individual components that resolve and dissolve as you listen. Underneath this is a deeper sound, a groaning, the sound of the great horizontal trees flexing in their wall-sockets, wood under enormous slow stress, a bass creaking that rises and falls over periods of minutes, like the breathing of something enormous and patient. Underneath *that*, barely audible, is a hum — not mechanical, not insectile (there are no insects), but something in the stone itself, a resonance in the basalt that you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears, a frequency that seems to carry information the way a carrier wave carries a signal, though the signal, if it exists, has never been decoded.

The volume is physically punishing. At certain nodes in the rift — places where the wall geometry focuses and amplifies — the combined sound reaches levels that make vision blur and balance fail. These nodes are marked by the city. The architecture around them is different: thicker walls, curved surfaces, chambers that seem designed to absorb or redirect sound. Whether the builders understood what they were doing, or whether the city's own unconscious intelligence shaped these structures the way a body forms calluses, is unknowable.

There are no animals. This is not absence — it is *exclusion*. The rift should teem: with birds, with insects, with amphibians drawn to the wet, with everything a hot dense vertical jungle should harbour. There is nothing. No bird has ever been seen inside the rift. No insect hums in the canopy. No frog calls from the cisterns. The fish that should live in the deep pools at the rift's base do not exist. It is as if the entire ecosystem — the towering trees, the strangling vines, the fungi, the mosses, the epiphytes, the city itself — constitutes a single organism, and that organism does not tolerate other life within its boundaries. Animals approach the rim and stop. Migrating birds divert around the rift entirely, flying kilometres out of their way. Domesticated animals brought to the edge will scream and refuse and, if forced inside, die within hours — not of poison or predation but of something like despair, a cessation of the will to continue, their hearts simply stopping.

People do not die inside the rift. But they do not remain people for very long.

The reality breakdown is subtle at first. It begins with the arrangements — the shifting cairns, the restringing seedpods, things you can attribute to wind or settling or your own faulty memory. Then it progresses. The paths change. Not dramatically — a stairway that led to a platform now leads to a different platform, a bridge that crossed to the east wall now crosses to the west — but the change is not physical. The stones have not moved. The bridge is where it always was. What has changed is *where it goes*. The topology of the city is not fixed. Routes between points are unstable. Two people can stand on the same platform, set off in the same direction, and arrive at different destinations. A person who descends for an hour may find themselves higher than they started. The city is not a maze. A maze has a solution. The city is a *negotiation*, and the terms are always being revised.

Distances are wrong. The rift is eight hundred metres across, but certain bridges feel much longer to cross — not difficult, not slow, but *long*, as if the space beneath them contains more distance than the gap they span. Looking down from a high platform, the valley floor seems kilometres away, a green haze at the bottom of an abyss. Looking down from the next platform over — a metre to the left, same height — the floor is visible, close, detailed; you can see individual leaves on the bottom-growth. These two views are simultaneous. They do not reconcile.

Time behaves strangely near the deep cisterns. The water in them is perfectly still — there is no flow, no current, no input or output that anyone has identified — and it is perfectly clear, and it reflects nothing. You can look into a cistern and see the stone at the bottom, several metres down, with optical clarity. But you cannot see your own face. You cannot see the canopy above. The water accepts light and returns only the interior of the cistern, as if the surface is not a surface but a window, and what it looks onto is only itself.

At the deepest levels of the rift — below the city, below the last root-bridge, below the point where the canopy light fades to a green so dark it is functionally night — there is a floor. It is flat. It is stone. It is wet. And it is covered in arrangements.

Thousands of them. Cairns, spirals, branching patterns, seedpod strands, bones (so many bones — where do the bones come from, if there are no animals?), all placed with the same deliberate, revising intelligence that maintains the city above, but here in such density that they cover every square metre of the rift floor, overlapping, interpenetrating, a single vast composition that has been in progress for as long as the rift has existed. It is not a message. It is not a map. It is not art, or worship, or communication. It is a process. It is the city thinking, using the materials at hand, working through something that has no end and no beginning, maintaining itself in perfect, permanent, dynamic equilibrium.

The sound down here is beyond description. The combined drip-roar of the entire rift, funnelled and focused by eleven hundred metres of converging walls, arrives at the floor as a physical force. It is not sound anymore. It is pressure. It is the rift speaking to itself in a language made of water and stone and time, and it does not stop, and it has never stopped, and the arrangements on the floor shift in response, slowly, a cairn re-formed here, a spiral tightened there, the whole vast floor breathing with a patience that makes geology look hasty.

The city does not change. This is the deepest strangeness, the one that encompasses all the others. Despite the shifting paths, the unstable topology, the rearranged arrangements, the city as a whole is *fixed*. It has always been exactly this size, exactly this density, exactly this complex. The buildings do not crumble. The vegetation does not advance or retreat. The water level in the cisterns is constant. The temperature never varies. The sound never fades. The rift exists in a state of absolute, frozen, screaming equilibrium — a system that has consumed all its own gradients, flattened every differential, achieved a stasis so complete that change itself has been reduced to a kind of decoration: the rearranging bones, the shifting paths, the wrong distances, all of it superficial, all of it the city's equivalent of dreaming, its mind idling at the bottom of a crack in the world, forever.

Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. Nothing is born and nothing dies. The green hangs in the crack, wet and dense and hot, and the sound of a million drips falls and falls and falls, and the city maintains itself, and the bones move, and the water shows you nothing, and the bridges lead somewhere different every time you cross them, and the whole thing persists, settled, established, in balance, a closed system at the extreme edge of altitude and heat and strangeness, suspended in a state that is not life and not death but something the rift invented for itself, something for which there is no word because there is no one left inside who needs one.
