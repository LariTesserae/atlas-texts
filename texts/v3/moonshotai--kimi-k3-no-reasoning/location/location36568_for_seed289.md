You slip through the fissure behind the hillside where the ordinary rain runs cold, and you are in the **Gutt**.

It is a single room, perhaps thirty strides across, but it holds more life than a mountain. The ceiling is a dome of raw limestone, weeping, barely taller than your raised hands, and every inch of it is crowded. The floor is not stone but a compression of roots—white, translucent, interlaced like fingers—tight enough that you cannot see the earth beneath them. They do not grow. They have never grown. They are simply *becoming*, suspended in the instant of emergence, pale tendrils thick with sap that never moves.

The water arrives without weather. It condenses on the ceiling in silver beads that fall upward. You watch them rise, trembling, into the darkness above, drawn back into the stone. The dampness is intermittent—a wave of wet that passes through the air like a breath—yet the room never dries. Moisture hangs in the packed atmosphere, clinging to your lungs, tasting of iron and cold sugar.

Light comes from the stone itself. The walls are crystalline, fractured, bright with an internal luminescence that throws sharp shadows across the crowded floor. It is a white, shadowless brilliance, bright as afternoon snow, yet the room feels intimate, enclosed, a pocket of light embedded within the dark world.

The sound is deafening. It is not a cacophony but a density of noise—the buzzing of the **whistle-moths**, thousands of them, their wings vibrating at a frequency that makes your teeth ache. They are everywhere, packed wing-to-wing on the roots, on the walls, clinging to your clothes. They are the color of the light, translucent, and they feed on the condensation, their proboscises sipping the upward-falling drops before they escape the room. Alongside them, the **glass-snails** click their shells together in a rhythmic percussion, a sound like typing on crystal, and the **root-shrews**—blind, pale, no larger than your thumb—scurry through the interstices of the floor, their claws scratching against the dense tangle.

There is no room to move without displacing something. The vegetation—sparse in kind, a single species of pale fern that grows in clumps—fills every available centimeter. The ferns do not sway. The air does not move. Everything is packed tight, saturated, a full occupancy of life and stone and water.

Here and there, you see the marks. A scattering of artifacts—simple stone bowls, half-embedded in the roots, their surfaces covered in the same crystalline growth as the walls. They are old, but not worn; they are merely *becoming* more encrusted, more integrated into the room’s slow accretion. Someone was here, once, and left these vessels to catch the upward water. Now they are part of the geology, raw and undeveloped, as if the room is still deciding what they are for.

The strangeness settles on you slowly. The room is below the surface, yes, but it does not feel buried. It feels *prior*. It feels like a place before the world decided on gravity, or time, or the direction of water. The roots are not dead; they are not alive. They are in the state of **emerging**—the stone around them is soft, not from decay but from incompleteness, as if the mountain is still making up its mind about this space.

You stand there, cool air on your skin, the bright light pressing against your eyes, the loud buzzing filling your ears until it becomes a kind of silence. You realize that nothing here will ever change. The ferns will not grow taller. The moths will not die. The water will always rise. The room is finished in its unfinished state—perfectly packed, perfectly still, a single bright note held forever beneath the damp, dark earth.

You could stay. You could become another presence in the density, another artifact half-buried in the roots. The Gutt would make room for you, somehow, in its equilibrium, packing you in among the moths and the bowls and the pale ferns, until you too were bright, and cool, and never-changing.
