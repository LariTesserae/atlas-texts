The city lies in a shallow basin, no more than a mile across, carved long ago into the floor of a larger emptiness. It is a pocket of existence—dim, hot, and roaring with sound—sealed away from whatever lies beyond the rim. The air is thick with a faint, acrid tang, dry as old bone, and heavy with the weight of something ancient and self-contained. There is no water here, no river, no dew, no condensation on the walls at night. The city drinks from the deep stone itself, tapping into veins of mineral oil that seep up through the bedrock—thick, black, slow-moving, but enough to sustain the strange forms of life that have taken root.

The vegetation is not green. It cannot afford to be. Instead, the plants are vast, fleshy, and translucent—pale amber, rose-gold, and bruised violet—growing in great, lobed clusters from the cracks in the pavement and the hollows between buildings. They are not rooted in soil but in the city’s own structure, their tendrils worming into the mortar of old bricks, their leaves unfurling like fans of waxed paper. They pulse faintly, a slow, rhythmic contraction that makes the whole district seem to breathe. The sound is not a whisper but a low, resonant thrum—a deep, wet vibration that rises and falls like the heartbeat of something enormous just beneath the surface. The plants are not silent; they groan, they creak, they release sharp, percussive clicks as their membranes stretch and snap. It is loud—not with the clamor of voices or machines, but with the slow, deliberate labor of a living city digesting itself.

The streets are narrow, winding, and built of dark, fired clay bricks—some smooth with age, others cracked and swollen from the heat. The buildings are low, squat, and densely packed, their walls thick enough to keep the worst of the heat at bay. There are no windows in the upper floors, only narrow slits near the ground, where the plants have begun to creep inside, their roots finding the cool, damp spaces beneath the floorboards. The roofs are flat, covered in a layer of fine, white dust that never settles—only shifts, rearranging itself in slow, deliberate patterns as if guided by unseen hands.

The city is old, but not in the way of ruins. It is settled, in its own way. The bricks have been fired and refired, the mortar ground and re-mixed with the powdered husks of long-dead plants. The streets have been repaved so many times that the original stones are buried beneath layers of compressed clay, their edges worn smooth by the passage of bare feet and the slow, grinding weight of time. There are no carts, no wheels—only the occasional sled of woven reeds, dragged by hand over the dust. The people here move slowly, deliberately, their bodies lean and darkened by the dim light, their voices low but insistent, a constant murmur beneath the plants’ deeper song.

There are no animals. Not even insects. The only movement comes from the plants and the people, and the occasional slow, deliberate shift of the city itself. The walls breathe. The streets expand and contract with the heat. At the height of the day, when the sun is a pale, smothered disc behind the dust-laden sky, the air shimmers, and the plants open their leaves wider, releasing a fine, silver mist that clings to the skin like oil. It is not water. It is something else—something that stings faintly, that smells of iron and old parchment.

The city is not empty. It is dense—not with people, but with presence. The buildings press close, their walls thick with the weight of accumulated time. The spaces between them are narrow, almost intimate, as if the city has folded in on itself, creating pockets of shadow and heat where the plants grow thickest. There are no plazas, no open squares—only alleys that twist and double back, leading to small, enclosed courtyards where the air is still and the sound of the plants is muffled, like a held breath.

At night, the city does not sleep. The heat lingers, rising from the stones in slow, visible waves. The plants do not close; they only shift, their membranes tightening, their colors deepening to near-black. The sound changes—no longer the slow, deliberate thrum of daylight, but a sharper, more urgent rhythm, like the tapping of fingers on a drum. The people move through the streets in small groups, their footsteps muffled by the dust, their voices low and quick. They do not sing. They do not laugh. The city does not encourage such things.

There are no lights, save for the occasional glow of a bioluminescent fungus that grows in the cracks of the walls—pale green, faintly pulsing, like a dying ember. It is enough to see by, barely. The people do not need more. They know the streets by heart, by the shape of the stones beneath their feet, by the way the air moves in the narrow spaces between buildings.

The city is not a ruin. It is not a ghost. It is a living thing, slow and deliberate, breathing in its own time. It does not need water. It does not need light. It does not need animals. It needs only the heat, the dust, the slow, patient labor of the plants, and the people who tend them—who cut back the overgrowth, who gather the oil that seeps from the stone, who grind the dead leaves into a paste that they spread on their skin to keep the heat at bay.

It is a place of quiet intensity, of contained, relentless motion. It is not kind. It is not cruel. It simply is—hidden, hollow, and humming with the sound of its own existence.
