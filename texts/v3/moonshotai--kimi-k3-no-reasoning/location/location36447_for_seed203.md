# The Hollow Gardens of the Veiled Chasm

---

They say the world was never meant to be seen this way — split open, its under-skin laid bare to a light that should never have reached it. But the Veiled Chasm cares nothing for what was meant.

---

## The Descent

You come to it from above, though "above" is a generous word for the cracked, frozen plateau that stretches in every direction — a slab of grey ice-clay, windswept and featureless, where nothing grows and nothing lives. The plateau is the world's lid. You walk it for days, your breath streaming white, your boots cracking against frozen mud, until you find the seam.

The seam is not dramatic. It is a hairline. A dark thread in the earth that you might step across without noticing, if not for the light. The light comes up from below like a slow, patient exhale — pale gold, faintly green at its edges, as if the sun has been filtered through leaves for a thousand miles before reaching your eyes. It is faint here at the crack, but it is unmistakable: the earth is glowing from underneath.

You follow the seam, and it widens. Over the course of a day, the hairline becomes a fissure wide enough to swallow a house, then a canyon, then — you stand at the edge and the word "canyon" fails entirely, because the Veiled Chasm does not behave like any canyon should.

It falls away beneath you not in rock walls but in *tiers* — vast, descending plateaus, each one miles wide, separated by cliff-faces hundreds of feet tall. There are dozens of these tiers, perhaps hundreds, stepping down and down and down into a brightness that becomes almost painful to look at, like staring into a green-gold furnace that has learned gentleness. The chasm runs to the horizon in both directions. It is not a crack in the earth. It is a continent turned inside out.

---

## The Green Architecture

The first thing you notice — after the light, after the sheer impossible scale — is that every surface is alive.

Not alive the way a meadow is alive, or a forest, where plants grow *on* something. Here, the plants *are* the something. The cliff faces between tiers are not rock covered in moss. They are moss. They are moss packed so densely, grown so thick over such impossible stretches of time, that the original stone has been absorbed, digested, replaced. What looks like a cliff of dark green is a wall of compacted vegetal matter — billions upon billions of tiny, cold-adapted bryophytes, interlocked root-threads forming a structural lattice harder than sandstone. When you press your hand against it, it gives slightly, like a firm mattress, and it is faintly warm — not from sunlight but from the slow metabolic heat of the living mass itself.

The tier floors are worse. Better. Stranger. Each plateau is a rolling expanse of green that shifts in character as you cross it — low, dense mats of needle-fine moss give way to thickets of something like horsetails, pale and ribbed and taller than a man, their stems so close together you must turn sideways to pass between them. Then the horsetails thin and you find yourself crossing a field of broad, leathery leaves that grow directly from the moss-floor, each one the size of a wagon, cupped upward, holding in its center a shallow pool of water so clear it looks like glass.

These cup-leaves — the Gatherers, as you will come to call them if you stay long enough — are everywhere. Thousands of them. Millions. Each one collecting the thin, slow rain that falls at irregular intervals, each one a tiny reservoir in a land where water is precious but not absent. The water in the Gatherers is always cold, always pure, and always tastes faintly of green — of chlorophyll, of growth, of the massive living body that produced it.

Between the Gatherers, the moss-floor bulges and swells into forms that look almost deliberate. Ridges that run in parallel, like plowed furrows. Mounds that rise in spirals. Channels that cut across the tier surface with the regularity of irrigation ditches. Whether these are the product of some subterranean root-logic or simply the emergent geometry of growth over millennia, you cannot tell. The effect is disorienting — you feel, constantly, that you are walking through a garden designed by an intelligence that does not think the way you think.

---

## The Light

The light is the great impossibility. You are below the surface of the earth, in a chasm whose ceiling — if it can be called a ceiling — is the frozen plateau far above, split only by the seam. The seam is narrow. In places, it closes entirely, and the chasm becomes a true cave, a hollow beneath the world. Yet the light does not diminish. If anything, it grows stronger the deeper you descend.

It comes from the plants.

Every living surface in the Veiled Chasm produces light. Not the faint blue-green shimmer of bioluminescent fungi — this is a full, warm, golden radiance, bright enough to read by, bright enough to cast shadows. The moss-cliffs glow from within, as if lit by a million hidden lamps. The horsetail thickets pulse with a slow, rhythmic brightening and dimming, like breathing. The Gatherer leaves are brightest of all, their cupped pools of water acting as lenses, focusing and amplifying the light into beams that crisscross the chasm at strange angles, illuminating the undersides of tiers above you in patterns that shift throughout the day.

If day exists here. The light does not follow a cycle you can detect. It fluctuates — brightening to near-white intensity, dimming to a deep amber glow — but the rhythm, if there is one, is too complex to map. Some travelers claim the light responds to movement below, brightening when something large shifts on a distant tier. Others insist the fluctuations are random, the vegetal equivalent of a mind dreaming.

What is certain is the cold. Despite the light — despite its warmth of color, its suggestion of summer — the air in the chasm is bitter. Your breath frosts. The water in the Gatherer leaves never freezes, but only because the leaves themselves generate just enough metabolic heat to keep it liquid. Touch the moss-wall of a cliff face and you feel warmth, yes, but pull your hand away and the cold reasserts itself instantly, like water closing over a stone. The plants have learned to keep themselves alive. They have not learned to keep you alive.

---

## The Turbulence

The chasm is not still. It is never still.

At first you think the movement is wind — a constant, low rustling that rises and falls, a sound like a million hands brushing against fabric. But there is no wind here, not below the third tier, where the seam above is too narrow to admit any real air current. The movement is the plants themselves.

They are growing. Constantly. Visibly. If you sit and watch a horsetail stem for an hour, you will see it extend — a fraction of an inch, perhaps, but real, undeniable, a slow and patient upward thrust. The Gatherer leaves tilt on their thick stems throughout the day, tracking light sources that shift as other plants grow and block or open channels of illumination. The moss-floor bulges and subsides in slow waves, like a sleeping animal's flank, as root-masses beneath expand and contract.

But this is the gentle turbulence, the everyday restlessness. The real turbulence is something else entirely.

Every few days — or every few hours; the timing is erratic — a tier will *surge*. Somewhere on its vast green surface, a section of growth will accelerate explosively. Stems that took weeks to reach waist-height will shoot to twenty feet in the span of minutes, groaning and cracking as they tear through their neighbors. Mounds of moss will erupt upward like slow-motion geysers, showering the surrounding area with clods of wet vegetal matter. Channels will split and re-form. The light will strobe wildly, surging to blinding intensity as new growth floods the area with fresh bioluminescent tissue, then dimming to near-darkness as the surge exhausts itself and the new tissue dies back, crumbling into the moss-floor to be reabsorbed.

These surges are not violent in the way of storms or earthquakes. They are too slow, too *organic*. But they are utterly unpredictable, and they reshape the landscape with a ferocity that makes any attempt at mapping futile. A path you walked yesterday may be buried under thirty feet of fresh horsetail growth today. A cliff you climbed last week may have been absorbed into a new moss-ridge, its face smoothed over, its holds obliterated. The chasm is a place that is always becoming, never finished. It is young — geologically, impossibly young — and it is growing the way young things grow: in fits and starts, in wild unpredictable spurts, with an energy that is both exhilarating and terrifying.

You understand, standing on a tier and watching a surge ripple across the distance — a wave of green rearing up, miles wide, reconfiguring the land like a shrug — that this place has not settled into itself yet. It may never settle. It may be, in some sense, still being born.

---

## The Emptiness

For all its vegetal abundance, the chasm is profoundly empty.

This seems like a contradiction. Every surface is covered in life. The air itself is thick with the green smell of growth, of photosynthesis, of a trillion tiny organisms metabolizing. Yet the *space* between things is enormous — the tiers are miles across, and the life on them, while continuous, is low. There are no trees here. Nothing rises more than twenty or thirty feet above the moss-floor, and most of the growth is far lower than that. The result is a landscape of immense horizontal openness — rolling green plains that stretch to cliff-edges that fall to more green plains, all of it under a ceiling of darkness or a slit of distant sky, all of it glowing, all of it cold.

You can stand on a tier and see for twenty miles and not see a single animal. Not a bird. Not an insect. Not a track in the moss. The emptiness is so complete that it becomes a physical presence, a weight on your chest. You are alone in a space the size of a small country, and the only living things are the plants, and the plants do not acknowledge you.

There are animals, but they are rare — so rare that each sighting feels like an event of significance, something to be recorded and remembered. A pale, blind salamander the length of your arm, found half-buried in a Gatherer pool, its skin translucent enough to show the slow pulse of its heart. A moth — if it is a moth — with wings like sheets of white paper, drifting between the horsetails on some unfelt current, its flight so slow and deliberate it seems less like flying and more like falling with intent. Once, on the eleventh tier, you find the remains of something larger: a skull, half-embedded in the moss, elongated and narrow, with eye sockets that face upward. The bone is green-stained, already being colonized by moss. Whatever it was, it has been dead long enough for the chasm to begin absorbing it.

These creatures are outliers. The chasm is not a place for animals. It is a place for plants, and the plants have filled it so completely that there is little room for anything else. The emptiness is not a lack. It is a consequence.

---

## The Quiet

The sound of the chasm is the sound of almost nothing.

The rustling — that constant, low, dry rustle of millions of stems and leaves in slow motion — is the baseline. It is always there, so constant that you stop hearing it within the first hour, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Beneath it, or through it, there are other sounds, but they are sparse and strange.

Water drips. Somewhere, always, water is dripping. From the edge of a Gatherer leaf onto the moss below. From the ceiling of the seam, condensation falling in slow, fat drops that take seconds to form and seconds to fall. The drips are irregular — no rhythm, no pattern — and they echo strangely in the vast space, sometimes arriving as sharp, clear *tocks* and sometimes as deep, resonant booms that seem to come from below rather than above.

The surges, when they happen, are accompanied by sound: a deep, fibrous groaning, like a ship's hull under strain, as stems thicken and split and force their way through packed growth. A crackling, like a distant fire, as dry outer layers of moss are torn apart by new growth from beneath. These sounds are alarming the first time you hear them. By the third or fourth surge, they become almost familiar — the chasm clearing its throat.

And there is one other sound, so faint you are never entirely sure you are hearing it: a low, sustained hum, felt more in the chest than in the ears, like the vibration of a very large, very slow machine. It may be the cumulative metabolic hum of the vegetal mass — a trillion organisms respiring in concert. It may be something else. It is always there, just at the edge of perception, and it is strangely comforting, like the hum of a house at night when everyone is asleep.

## The Marks of Hands

You are not the first to come here.

On the seventh tier, you find a wall.

It is not much — a rough line of stones, perhaps three feet high, running for fifty paces before dissolving into the moss. The stones are not from the chasm. They are grey, angular, clearly brought from the surface plateau above, carried down tier by tier and stacked with the kind of patient, inexpert care that suggests hands accustomed to simpler tools. No mortar. No cut faces. Just stones fitted together by eye and weight and the stubborn will of whoever placed them.

Inside the wall — for it does enclose a space, a rough rectangle the size of a large room — the moss has been cleared. Not recently. The clearing is old, and the moss has begun to reclaim it, sending runners across the bare ground in slow green fingers. But the clearing is still visible, and in its center is a fire-pit: a ring of smaller stones, blackened by ancient heat, containing a handful of charcoal fragments that crumble when you touch them.

Someone camped here. Someone built a wall, cleared the moss, lit a fire, and — what? Stayed for a night? A season? The wall suggests permanence, or at least the desire for it. The fire-pit suggests warmth, food, perhaps company. The chasm does not offer any of these things freely. Whoever came here brought them, made them, imposed them on a landscape that does not naturally accommodate human comfort.

There are other marks. On the twelfth tier, a series of horsetail stems have been cut — cleanly, with something sharp — and the cut ends have been woven together to form a kind of basket, now overgrown and fused into the living mat. On the twentieth tier, a Gatherer leaf has been deliberately punctured, its pool of water drained, the wound still visible as a dark, puckered scar. On the thirtieth tier, carved into the moss-wall of a cliff face — a process that must have been extraordinarily difficult, given the density and resilience of the compacted vegetal matter — is a symbol: a circle with a line through it, angled slightly to the left.

You do not know what the symbol means. You copy it into your journal. You will see it again, twice more, on deeper tiers, always carved at the same height — chest-height for a person of average stature — always with the same slight angle to the line. It is not a natural formation. It is not random. Someone was marking their descent, or marking their territory, or leaving a message for someone who might follow.

The artifacts are primitive — stone, cut stems, carved symbols. No metal. No glass. No synthetic materials. Whoever came here came with simple tools and simple means, and they left behind only what the chasm could not immediately absorb. Even so, the marks are fading. The wall is being swallowed by moss. The fire-pit is one good rain away from being washed clean. The basket is already indistinguishable from the living mat it was woven from. The chasm does not preserve. It grows over.

You understand that in fifty years, or a hundred, there will be no trace of these people at all. The chasm will have consumed their presence the way it consumes everything — slowly, greenly, without malice or intention. It is not hostile. It is simply, relentlessly, alive.

## The Deeper Tiers

You descend.

The light changes. Not in intensity — it remains bright, almost aggressively so — but in quality. The gold deepens. The green shifts toward something almost blue, though it never quite gets there, hovering in that liminal space between colors that makes your eyes ache if you stare too long. The shadows cast by the horsetails become sharper, more defined, as if the light is coming from fewer, more concentrated sources.

The plants change too. The horsetails give way to something new: thick, ropy vines that hang from the tier-edges above like green curtains, their surfaces ridged and segmented, pulsing with a slow peristaltic motion that suggests something is moving through them — sap, perhaps, or water, or something more complex. The vines are warm to the touch, noticeably warmer than the surrounding air, and they hum — that same low hum you have been feeling for days, but stronger now, more localized, as if the vines themselves are the source.

The Gatherer leaves grow larger. On the fortieth tier, you find one the size of a small house, its cupped center holding a pool of water that could swallow you whole. The water is perfectly still, perfectly clear, and at its center — at the bottom of the pool, visible through fathoms of green-gold light — something is glowing. Not the ambient bioluminescence of the chasm. Something else. Something concentrated, deliberate, a point of light so bright it hurts to look at, like a star fallen into the water.

You do not investigate. The pool is deep, the water is cold, and the chasm has taught you caution. But you mark the location in your journal, next to the circle-and-line symbol, and you note the date — or what you believe the date to be, since the light no longer follows any cycle you can use to track time — and you move on.

The surges are more frequent here. More violent. On the forty-third tier, you witness one that nearly kills you: a section of moss-floor the size of a village erupts without warning, sending a shockwave of displaced air and torn vegetal matter that knocks you off your feet and buries you to the waist in wet, steaming debris. You dig yourself out, shaking, and watch as the surge continues for another hour, reshaping the tier before your eyes, raising new ridges, opening new channels, burying old landmarks under tons of fresh, glowing growth.

When it is over, the tier is unrecognizable. Your path — the path you have been following for days, marked by the circle-and-line symbols — is gone. Buried. Erased. You are, for the first time since entering the chasm, truly lost.

---

## The Rules

The Veiled Chasm has rules. You have been learning them since you arrived, piece by piece, observation by observation, and they are not the rules of the world above.

**Water falls upward.** Not always. Not everywhere. But in certain places — usually near the tier-edges, where the moss-cliffs are thinnest and the light is strongest — water condenses on the underside of the tier above and falls *up*, rising in slow, silver droplets that drift toward the seam like inverted rain. You do not understand the mechanism. You suspect it has something to do with the light, with the heat generated by the plants, with some subtle inversion of gravity or thermodynamics that the chasm has devised for its own purposes. The upward rain is beautiful. It is also, somehow, deeply unsettling — a reminder that the physical laws you take for granted are negotiable here.

**The light is alive.** Not metaphorically. The bioluminescence of the chasm responds to stimuli — to movement, to sound, to the presence of living things. When you walk, the moss beneath your feet brightens slightly, as if curious. When you speak, the nearest horsetails pulse in rhythm with your voice. When you sleep — and you do sleep, eventually, in the shelter of a moss-ridge, wrapped in every layer you brought — the light around you dims to a soft, amber glow, like a bedside lamp turned low. You do not know if this is a conscious response or a simple biological reflex. You are not sure there is a difference, here.

**Nothing decays.** The chasm does not rot. Dead plant matter is not broken down by fungi or bacteria — it is *absorbed*, drawn back into the living mass with a speed and efficiency that is almost surgical. A fallen horsetail stem will be gone within hours, its substance redistributed to neighboring growth. A dead moth — you find one, once, its paper wings folded, its body still — is half-absorbed by the time you notice it, the moss already threading through its thorax, its legs already dissolving. There is no death in the chasm. There is only redistribution.

**The chasm is one thing.** This is the rule you arrive at last, and the one you are least certain of. But the evidence is there: the synchronized pulsing of the horsetails, the way the surges ripple across entire tiers as if driven by a single impulse, the way the light dims and brightens in patterns that span miles. The plants are not individuals. They are cells. The chasm is not a collection of organisms. It is an organism — a single, vast, vegetal entity, filling the crack in the earth like a body filling a wound, growing with the furious, undirected energy of something that has not yet decided what it wants to become.

## The Bottom (If There Is One)

You have been descending for — you do not know. Weeks, perhaps. The tiers continue. The light continues. The cold continues. The green continues, endless and absolute.

You have stopped counting the tiers. The numbers lost meaning somewhere past sixty, when the character of the chasm began to change again — the tiers becoming narrower, the cliff-faces steeper, the space between them compressing until the chasm feels less like a series of plateaus and more like a throat, a green, glowing throat, swallowing you downward.

The air is warmer here. Not warm — never warm — but warmer than above, as if the chasm's metabolic heat is concentrated at its core. The vines are thicker, more numerous, hanging in dense curtains that you must push through, their surfaces slick with condensation, their hum so strong now that you feel it in your teeth.

The sound has changed. The quiet is still there, but it is a different kind of quiet — not the empty, open silence of the upper tiers but a dense, muffled stillness, like being inside a very large, very soft room. The drips are fewer. The rustling is fainter. The hum is everything.

You find the last symbol on what you estimate to be the eightieth tier. The circle-and-line, carved into a vine rather than a moss-wall, the cut edges already healing over, the symbol distorted by the vine's growth. Below it, someone has added a second mark: a series of short, parallel lines, like tally marks. You count them. Twenty-three.

You do not know what they mean. You suspect they are a count of some kind — days, perhaps, or tiers, or surges survived. You add your own tally mark, using the small knife you carry, pressing it into the vine's tough skin until green sap wells up and fills the cut. The vine pulses beneath your hand, warm, alive, indifferent.

You continue down.

---

The chasm does not end. Or if it ends, you have not reached the end. The tiers continue, the light continues, the green continues. You are a small, warm-blooded thing descending through the body of something vast and cold and vegetable, and it does not notice you, or if it notices you it does not care, and this — this indifference, this absolute, green indifference — is the most frightening thing you have ever encountered, and the most beautiful.

You write in your journal: *The world is not what we thought. It is not rock and water and sky. It is a living thing, and we are inside it, and it is growing.*

You close the journal. You shoulder your pack. You descend.

The light is very bright now. The hum is very loud. The green is everywhere.

You are very small.

And the chasm is still being born.
