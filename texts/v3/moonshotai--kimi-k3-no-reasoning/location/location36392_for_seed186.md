# The Wending Steppe

## First Impression

At two miles of altitude, the air has the consistency of cold water. It resists your lungs. You breathe it the way you'd swallow a river — carefully, in gulps, knowing it doesn't want to be inside you.

The Wending Steppe is not a plateau. It's a slope that never commits to being a cliff. A tilted continental shoulder — grass, dark and dense, riding a gradient so long it takes three days to walk from the fold where the land bends upward to the fold where it bends away. Everywhere, the ground is steep enough that rain runs before it pools, and cold enough that it mostly doesn't rain at all. The moisture comes from within, or from below, or from the brief, shoulder-shrug of summer clouds that drag themselves over the ridgeline and shed a thin skin of drizzle before passing. The land survives on condensation. It is a place that drinks its own breath.

The light. There is almost none. The sun reaches this altitude at such a shallow angle, for so brief a window, that it functions less as illumination and more as rumor. The primary light source is the aurora — not the delicate green curtains of polar regions, but a sustained, structural architecture of magnetized luminance that hangs in the upper atmosphere like a low ceiling. It does not flicker. It *hum* — a vibration felt in the teeth, in the fillings, in the iron of your blood. It casts a light the color of deep ocean water, but dry. A light that makes the grass look blue-gray, that turns exposed rock to mercury, that makes every shadow a hole rather than an absence.

## The Grass

The grass is everything.

Not a meadow. Not a prairie. A *body*. The grass of the Wending Steppe grows in unified mats — rhizomal networks so dense and so interconnected that the surface of the land is effectively a single organism, kilometers across, growing on a slope. Each blade is short, waxy, dark enough to absorb what little light exists. Underneath, the root-mat is three feet thick. Walking on it is like walking on the hull of something alive — firm but not rigid, with a slow, almost imperceptible give that you feel in your ankles long after you've stopped moving.

The grass is cold-adapted in ways that are difficult to believe. Its cells contain a glycol-based antifreeze that, in winter, reaches concentrations that would pickle a mammal. In the brief thaw — a period that lasts perhaps six weeks, when the angle of the sun shifts and the aurora dims slightly and the ice in the soil cracks — the grass blooms. The bloom is not flowers. It is a foam. A biological event. The grass releases its stored energy as a dense, bioluminescent froth that coats the surface of the steppe in a thin, bright film — blue-green, visible from orbit, lasting three days. The event triggers everything else. The thaw, the bloom, the surge of life that follows. It is the steppe's heartbeat. One beat per year.

## The Machinery

There are engines here. Not human ones — not anymore. Or perhaps not ever, in the way we understand "human." The engines are *geological* in their patience and *biological* in their behavior, and they were built by something that considered mountains a reasonable construction material.

They sit in the landscape like boulders that have opinions.

You find them at intervals — irregular, unpredictable intervals that suggest a logic too old or too alien to parse. Each engine is a pod, roughly the size of a small house, made of something that looks like corroded bronze but behaves like living tissue. They have no visible seams. They have no exhaust. What they have is a *presence* — a low-frequency vibration, below the threshold of hearing, that you feel as pressure in your chest. They hum the way the aurora hums, but deeper. They are resonating with something. Each other, maybe. Or the steppe itself.

Their function is not immediately obvious. They do not produce anything visible. They do not consume anything visible. But where they sit, the grass grows denser. The condensation is heavier. The temperature is a degree or two warmer — just enough to matter at this altitude, just enough to shift the boundary of the permafrost, just enough to keep a pocket of soil soft where everything around it is stone-hard. They are *tending*. They are, in the most literal sense, gardening — maintaining microclimates on a mountainside, the way a careful hand maintains a bonsai. Except the bonsai is an ecosystem, and the hand is a machine the size of a room, and the timescale is measured in centuries.

They do not respond to approach. They do not respond to touch. They respond, over time, to *presence* — to the sustained occupancy of living things in their vicinity. Animals have learned this. The engines have become landmarks in the ecology of the steppe, centers of congregation, the way water holes are in a desert. But the engines are not water holes. They are more like churches — places where something is happening that you cannot fully perceive, that you attend anyway, because the alternative is absence.

## The Animals

Abundant is the right word, but it needs qualification. The steppe is empty in the way that a cathedral is empty — vast, echoing, the space between things more present than the things themselves. But what is here is *here*, fully and without apology.

The grazer is a thing called, in the closest approximation, a *felk*. It is built like a cross between a musk ox and a piece of furniture — low-slung, broad, with a coat so thick it looks upholstered. Its feet are wide and soft, adapted for the root-mat, and it moves with a slow, deliberate gait that suggests it has all the time in the world, which, on the Wending Steppe, it does. The felk eats the grass, but not the way a cow eats grass. It *harvests*. It selects specific patches — the ones nearest the engines, the ones where the microclimate is warmest — and it grazes in a pattern that is almost geometric. Spirals. Concentric arcs. The pattern is not random. It is *cultivated*. The felk are maintaining the grass the way the engines are maintaining the microclimate — a partnership so old it has ceased to be cooperation and become reflex.

The predator is harder to see. The steppe is dark, and the predator is adapted to darkness the way the grass is adapted to cold. It is called a *sennit* — a word that in the local grammar (if there is a local grammar, if there is anyone left to speak it) means something like *the shape of the space between two things*. The sennit is not invisible. It is *unnoticeable*. It is the same color as the aurora-light on the grass. It moves at the same speed as the wind. It is the exact temperature of the air. When it hunts, it does not chase. It *appears* — a coalescence of darkness into intention, a felk that was grazing and is now not, a silence that was ambient and is now loaded.

The sennit hunts the felk, but not often. It does not need to. Its metabolism is as slow as everything else here. It eats once a month. It can wait.

There are smaller things — a whole class of amphibious creatures that live in the condensation pockets, the temporary pools that form where the grass mat dips and the meltwater collects. They are called *wels*, and they look like a compromise between a newt and a length of rope. They are bioluminescent, the same blue-green as the grass-bloom, and during the thaw they migrate across the steppe in long, single-file lines — a procession of faint lights moving through the darkness, connecting pool to pool, a living constellation laid over the slope of the land. The wels are the steppe's circulatory system. They carry nutrients from pool to pool. They are eaten by everything. They are, in a sense, the only thing here that is truly *busy*.

## The Weather

The weather on the Wending Steppe is not weather as most places understand it. It is a negotiation between altitude and moisture, and the altitude always wins.

There are two seasons: the thaw and the lock. The thaw is the six-week window when the sun climbs high enough to matter, when the aurora thins, when the temperature rises to just above freezing and the grass blooms and the wels migrate and the felk calve and the sennit eats and the engines hum a little louder. It is the steppe's entire life compressed into a month and a half. It is intense. It is almost violent in its activity — a whole ecosystem rushing to complete its annual cycle in the time it takes most places to have a mild spring.

The lock is everything else. The lock is the rest of the year — forty-six weeks of cold so profound it has texture. The air is dry enough to crack your lips in an hour. The aurora is the only light. The grass goes dormant, its cells crystallizing into a state that is not quite frozen and not quite alive — a suspended animation that lasts until the next thaw. The felk huddle. The sennit sleeps. The wels burrow into the permafrost and wait. The engines continue to hum, but softer, as if they, too, are conserving energy.

During the lock, the steppe is almost silent. The wind is the only sound — a low, constant moan as it moves across the slope, a sound so regular it becomes part of the background, a sound you stop hearing after the first hour. The wind carries ice crystals. They are so small and so sharp that they abrade exposed skin. Over time — over *years* — they abrade rock. They have carved the steppe's surface into its current form: the long, smooth gradient, the occasional outcrop polished to a mirror finish, the shallow grooves that run perpendicular to the slope like comb-marks. The wind is the steppe's sculptor. It is patient. It has been working for a very long time.

## The Sound

Quiet, but not silent. The silence of the Wending Steppe is a *curated* silence — a silence that has been maintained, like a garden, by the absence of things that make noise. There are no birds. There are no insects. The felk are quiet. The sennit is quieter. The wels make no sound at all.

What you hear, if you stand still long enough, is the steppe's *internal* sound — the sound of processes too slow to be events. The root-mat creaking as it shifts on the slope. The permafrost cracking, deep below, as the temperature fluctuates by fractions of a degree. The engines humming. The aurora's electromagnetic whine, picked up by the iron in your blood, converted by your nervous system into a sound that isn't a sound — a pressure, a presence, a low-frequency insistence that the world is *happening*, even when it looks like nothing is.

And occasionally — very occasionally — you hear the felk. They make a sound during the thaw, a lowing, a call between individuals across the slope. It is not loud. It is not melodic. It is a sound that exists to confirm *presence* — I am here, you are there, the steppe is between us, and we are both part of it. It is the only sound on the steppe that is *intentional*. Everything else is process. The felk's call is communication. It is, in a sense, the only voice the steppe has.

## The Scale

You can stand on the Wending Steppe and see for miles. The slope is unobstructed. The aurora lights everything evenly. The grass mat extends in all directions — upslope to the ridgeline where the mountains finally commit to being mountains, downslope to the fold where the land bends away into a valley that is, if anything, even more desolate than the steppe itself.

But the scale is deceptive. The steppe *looks* open, but it is not traversable in the way openness usually implies. The slope is steep enough that walking is a negotiation with gravity. The cold is a constant tax on movement. The darkness — the thin, blue-gray light of the aurora — makes distance difficult to judge. A felk that looks like it's a hundred yards away might be a mile. The engines are invisible until you're almost on top of them. The sennit, of course, is invisible until it isn't.

The steppe is open the way a chessboard is open — a large, legible space with a finite set of rules, but the rules are strict, and the consequences of breaking them are immediate. You can go anywhere. But you can't go anywhere *carelessly*.

## What the Steppe Is

The Wending Steppe is a machine. Not the engines — the *steppe itself* is the machine. The engines are components. The grass is a component. The felk, the sennit, the wels — components. The whole system is running a program that was written so long ago, by something so patient, that the program has become indistinguishable from nature.

The program is simple: *maintain*. Keep the grass alive. Keep the microclimates stable. Keep the cycle of thaw and lock turning. Keep the predators and prey in balance. Keep the whole system running on the minimal energy budget that this altitude and this darkness allow.

The steppe is not a wilderness. It is a *garden* — a garden so old and so large that it has forgotten it was ever planted. The gardeners are gone, or they are the engines, or they are the felk, or they are the steppe itself, tending itself, in the dark, at the top of the world, on a slope that never ends, in a silence that is not empty but *full* — full of process, full of intention, full of the slow, patient work of keeping something alive that has no reason to be alive except that it *is*.

The aurora hums. The grass waits. The engines vibrate at a frequency you feel in your bones. The felk graze in spirals. The sennit is somewhere, being the space between two things. The wels are lights in the dark, connecting pools.

You are here. The steppe does not care. But it *notices* — the way a garden notices a new presence, the way a machine notices a new input. It will not change for you. But it will, over time, *incorporate* you — the way it has incorporated everything else that has ever stood on this slope and breathed this thin, cold air.

The Wending Steppe is patient. It has all the time in the world.

It *is* all the time in the world.
