There is a room, and the room is a mill, and the mill is the world.

No — start smaller. Start with your hand on the wall.

The wall is brick, but brick that has outlasted brick. The mortar joints have swollen with two hundred years of humidity until the courses read as one continuous skin, glazed in black-green biofilm, weeping from every pore. Your palm comes away wet. Everything here comes away wet. The walls sweat; the ceiling drips a slow, metronomic condensate that has dripped for so long it has drilled little cups into the iron floor plates, each cup the size of a fingertip, each filled with water that has sat so still it has gone faintly iridescent with iron bacteria — a thin gasoline sheen on water in a fingertip-sized well. That is the scale of this place. You could lie down in it, and your body would be a geography.

The room is perhaps eight paces across. It is not a natural chamber. Nothing in it is natural, and nothing in it has been natural for longer than the surrounding country remembers. The floor is cast iron in bolted plates, gone soft-edged with rust bloom, so the bolts sit in collars of orange velvet. The ceiling is a barrel vault of the same ancient brick, black with wet. There is one door, a hatch really, dogged shut with a wheel handle that will never turn again — the wheel's spindle has rusted into its gland and become one object, one continuous lump of orange geology where a mechanism used to be.

And the machine.

The machine fills the room the way a heart fills a chest. It is a single engine — one flywheel, one piston, one crank — but sized like a small house collapsed inward on itself. The flywheel alone is twice your height, a solid disk of iron webbed with spokes thick as your thigh, and it is turning. It has always been turning. This is the thing you understand within the first minute, the way you understand gravity: the wheel turns, the piston slides, the crank throws its endless slow circle, and none of this is new and none of it will stop. Not soon — *never*. The machine reached its final state sometime before language did. Every part has worn against every other part until the wearing stopped, until the tolerances became perfect the way river stones are perfect, until the whole mechanism settled into a groove so deep that motion and stillness became the same thing.

It is deafening.

The sound is not loud in the way a shout is loud. It is loud the way the sea is loud from inside a cave — a total, architectural, load-bearing loudness. The piston's stroke lands like a door slammed in the center of the earth, then the intake, a wet gasp the size of the whole room, then the door again. *Kshh-DOOM. Kshh-DOOM.* The flywheel hums a continuous low tone underneath it, a note so deep you hear it in your molars and your sternum rather than your ears. Water drips into its fingertip wells in counterpoint, tiny and bright and utterly swallowed. You could scream here and not hear yourself. People have. It changed nothing. The sound is not something happening in the room; the sound is a property of the room, the way its temperature is, the way its wetness is. It has been this loud for so long that the silence before it is not even a memory. It is a superstition.

And the water. The water is everywhere and goes nowhere.

It condenses on the vault overhead, runs down the brick in slow rivulets, sheets along the iron floor, and gathers in a shallow black sump beneath the machine's bed — a mirror that never ripples except when a drop lands, which is always, so it is always rippling and always still. The air itself is at the edge of rain. Breathing is like drinking through the lungs. Mildew, moss, and something greener and older have colonized every surface that holds still long enough, which is every surface except the piston rod and the wheel's rim — and even those wear a gloss of slime that the motion polishes but never removes. Ferns, impossibly, grow from the mortar joints near the ceiling, fed by nothing but condensation and the faint light, hanging down in limp dark-green fringes like the room is slowly growing hair. Algae paints the floor plates in slippery topographic maps. Where the dripping is heaviest, small pale things have built up — mineral accretions, lime scale grown into stubby stalagmites on the floor and stalactites on the pipework, geology happening at industrial speed, the room turning itself into a cave one drip at a time. In another ten thousand years the gap between floor and ceiling will close in stone. The machine will still be turning inside it.

The light is ordinary, and that is somehow the strangest thing.

It comes from wire-caged bulbs bolted along the wall, the old kind, the kind with filaments like glowing handwriting. They burn at a steady amber, no flicker, no dimming — they have burned at exactly this brightness for as long as the wheel has turned, fed by the same unknowable power. Their light is the color of weak tea, of old varnish. It makes the wet brick shine like the inside of a body. It makes the black sump water into a second, darker room hanging upside down beneath the first. There are no shadows with edges here; everything is glossed, everything doubles, everything reflects. You could stand in this room for an hour and see nothing change except the slow migration of a droplet down a pipe, and the light would be exactly as it was, and exactly as it will be.

Almost nothing lives here, and what lives here is barely alive.

No birds, no insects — the sound alone forbids them; anything with wings or ears fled or died long ago. What remains are things that do not hear. Once, sometimes, a single pale moth appears on a wall, wings folded, and sits for days without moving, and then is gone, and you cannot say where it came from, since the hatch is sealed and the intake grate is barred and rusted into one rusted mass. Blind white isopods wander the sump edge like slow punctuation. In the oldest dripping-wells, where the water is stillest, there are thin films of rot that might be colonies of something or might just be the water dreaming. That is all. The room does not need life. The room is performing something that life only imitates — repetition, endurance, self-maintenance — and it performs it better than anything with cells.

Here is what the machine does: nothing. Here is what the machine powers: itself.

Perhaps once it pumped water from a mine, or ground grain, or drove a loom or a dynamo or a bellows. Whatever shaft once carried its work out through the wall was cut away or rusted through so long ago that only a boss of metal remains, a sealed navel where purpose used to exit. The engine takes in steam or air or something through a pipe that disappears into the brick, and it exhales through another pipe that disappears into the brick, and between those two disappearances it turns its wheel, and the wheel turns nothing, and nothing is made, and nothing is moved, and the work of the machine is the machine. It is a closed liturgy. It has reached the final form of all engines: an engine that runs because it runs.

This is why the place feels so *old*, older than ruins, older than mountains nearly — not because its parts are ancient, though they are, but because it is finished. A ruin is still changing; a mountain is still rising or still falling. This room is done. It arrived at its end state — this exact wetness, this exact loudness, this exact amber light, this exact tempo — and it will now hold that state against the rest of time, dripping and booming and gleaming, sealed in its hidden eight paces of the world, a pocket of forever bolted together out of iron and condensation.

Outside — if there is an outside, and the sealed hatch insists there is — the world goes on: weathers turn, things grow and stop growing, seas argue with coasts. None of it enters here. The door will not open. The light will not flicker. The moth, if there is a moth, will fold its wings and wait.

*Kshh-DOOM. Kshh-DOOM.* A drop falls into a fingertip-sized well, and the rings spread, and close, and the water goes still again, and the wheel goes around.
