# The Underbloom

## Descent

You come to it from above, though "above" is a courtesy the world extends out of habit rather than accuracy. The surface — if you can call it that — is a thin mineral skin, brittle as old lacquer, cracked in polygonal plates that shift underfoot with the faintest exhalation of trapped air. Nothing grows here. Nothing could. The sky is a flat, colorless radiance, sunless but lit, the way an overcast afternoon is lit — sourceless, even, uncommitted. The light comes from everywhere and therefore from nowhere, and it is cool, and it has always been cool, and it will remain cool.

The plates underfoot are salt, but not the salt of oceans. This is mineral accretion, the dried residue of something that was never water. A chemistry that chose a different path long ago — or had the path chosen for it — and left behind these hexagonal crusts, pale gray, faintly bitter to the touch, humming at a frequency just below perception. When you step, they flex. When they flex, they breathe. The air that escapes is green-scented, humid despite the dryness, and warm compared to the surface chill. It smells like the inside of a stem.

Find a crack wide enough — there are several, spread across the plain at irregular intervals, some no wider than a door, some broad as boulevards — and look down.

The light does not diminish. This is the first surprise. Below the crust, the light changes quality but not quantity. It becomes *greener*, more diffuse, filtered through layered translucent membranes that hang like the walls of a vast, soft building. It takes your eyes a moment to understand what they're seeing, and when they do, the understanding arrives slowly, in pieces:

Walls. Corridors. Streets.

All of them alive.

## The City That Grew Instead of Being Built

The Underbloom occupies a region beneath the crust that extends — as far as anyone has mapped — across what would be, on the surface, a distance of perhaps forty kilometers in its longest dimension. It is not a cavern. It is not a tunnel system. It is a *space* — a vast, irregular, multi-leveled void between the mineral skin above and a denser, darker substrate below — and that space has been filled, almost but not entirely, by vegetation that has organized itself into something that functions like a city.

The walls are living tissue. Thick, corded vines — some as wide around as a sequoia trunk — run vertically and horizontally, branching and rebranching, fusing where they cross, forming structural lattices that hold the mineral ceiling up. Where the vines intersect, they swell into nodes — rounded, chambered, hollow — and these nodes serve as rooms. Some are small enough for a single occupant. Some are the size of cathedrals. Their walls are dense, fibrous, pale green, faintly translucent. Light passes through them and is altered — warmed, tinted, softened — so that each interior has its own weather of illumination.

Between the great vines, thinner growths weave. Moss-curtains. Fern-lattices. Hanging gardens of broad, waxy leaves that have oriented themselves in rows, in terraces, in stepped formations that mimic — or perhaps *are* — streets, plazas, stairways, bridges. A bridge of interwoven rootlets, dense as macramé, spans a gap between two vine-nodes, and it is strong enough to walk on, and it *grew that way*, self-directed, or directed by something, and it has small cup-shaped flowers along its railing-edge that open and close in rhythm with footsteps, as though acknowledging passage.

The city is under construction. This is evident everywhere. At its edges — and it has many edges, many fronts of expansion — the vines thin into exploratory tendrils, pale and almost translucent, reaching into the dark, unformed substrate. These growing tips exude a clear, viscous fluid that hardens on contact with the mineral walls into a kind of living mortar, anchoring new growth, preparing surfaces for the next wave of tissue. You can stand at one of these edges and watch the city being born. It is slow — a tendril might extend a centimeter in a day — but it is *visible*, and it is relentless, and the half-finished structures have a raw, skeletal beauty: lattices without walls, rooms without ceilings, streets that lead into solid rock and stop, waiting.

The older parts of the city are denser, darker, more layered. Growth upon growth. A wall that was once the exterior of a node has been overgrown, enclosed, incorporated into a larger structure, and then that structure was itself surrounded and absorbed. The deep districts have a geological quality — strata of tissue, each layer a generation, each generation thicker and more complex than the last. The oldest walls, in the lowest chambers, are almost wood-hard, dark green verging on black, and they carry a faint warmth, as though the accumulated mass of centuries of growth has generated its own mild heat against the cool of the depths.

## Industry Without Hands

There are machines here, or things that serve the function of machines, but they are not separate from the vegetation. They are *of* it. This is the strangest thing about the Underbloom, and it takes time to understand.

In a chamber the size of a factory floor, a series of bulbous, pod-like growths hang from the ceiling in precise rows. Each pod is perhaps two meters tall, leathery, veined with darker green. At their bases, they narrow into tubes that connect to a network of thicker conduits running along the floor. The pods *pulse*. Rhythmically. In sequence. A contraction travels down the row, one pod after another, and with each contraction, something moves through the floor-conduits — you can feel it, a surge, a peristaltic push. This is a pumping station. It is moving fluid — not water, but the clear, mineral-rich sap that the city uses the way a body uses blood — from one district to another. It does this constantly, without rest, and the sound it makes is a soft, wet, rhythmic *thump*, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Elsewhere: great fan-shaped leaves, broad as sails, mounted on flexible stalks that rise from the floor of a wide, low chamber. The leaves oscillate — not randomly, but in coordinated sweeps, first one bank, then another — and the air they move carries a faint mineral dust from one side of the chamber to the other, where it settles on a bed of dark, absorptive moss. This is a filtration system. It is cleaning the air. The leaves are the mechanism, and the moss is the filter medium, and both are alive, and neither was built by any hand.

In another district — a dense, multi-level tangle of growth that the inhabitants (there are inhabitants; I will come to them) call the Foundry — vast, hollow structures shaped roughly like kilns rise from the floor in clusters. They are plant-tissue, thick-walled, reinforced with mineral deposits absorbed from the substrate. Inside them, chemical reactions proceed at temperatures higher than the ambient cool — the tissue itself catalyzes these reactions, drawing energy from the sap-stream, channeling it into processes that produce... materials. Hard, lightweight plates. Fibrous cords. Translucent membranes. The products of the Foundry emerge from openings at the base of each kiln-structure, and they are carried away — by other plants, by mobile growths that serve as transport — to construction sites at the city's edges, where they are incorporated into new growth.

The city is building itself. The industry is biological. The machines are organs. The factories are fruit.

There are sounds here, but they are quiet sounds. The pulse of the pumping pods. The whisper of the filtration leaves. The low, creaking groan of structural vines adjusting their load — a sound like a ship's rigging in a gentle wind, organic and patient. Occasionally, a sharp *crack* as a new growth tip breaks through mineral resistance at the frontier, but this is rare and startling, and the silence closes over it quickly.

## The Inhabitants

There are creatures. Many creatures. They move through the city in the spaces between the growth — the corridors, the chambers, the bridges and terraces — and they are varied, and they are busy, and they seem, for the most part, unbothered by one another.

The most visible are the **trundlers** — low, wide-bodied animals, six-legged, perhaps a meter long, covered in overlapping plates that look like bark but feel like keratin. They move slowly and deliberately along the corridors, their broad, flat heads lowered, and they are eating. Not the living walls — never the structural tissue — but the thin, papery membrane that the city sheds continuously, like skin. The walls produce this membrane as a byproduct of growth, and it accumulates in drifts and sheets along the floors and ledges, and the trundlers consume it, and in consuming it they keep the corridors clear, and their waste is a dry, crumbly pellet that the moss-floors absorb with apparent eagerness. This is the waste-management system. It works beautifully.

Above, in the higher levels, **gliders** move through the open spaces between the vine-lattices. They are thin, membranous, winged — not with feathers or chitin but with something like the material of a leaf, broad and veined, stretched between elongated limbs. They do not flap. They ride the air currents generated by the filtration leaves, circling in long, slow spirals, and they feed on small, airborne seeds that the city releases in periodic puffs from specialized pods in the ceiling. The gliders are silent. Their wings make no sound. They pass overhead like thoughts.

In the deep districts, where the oldest growth is, there are things that are harder to see. They move in the narrow gaps between fused vine-walls, in the dark seams where one structure has grown over another. Shapes — low, many-limbed, quick — that appear at the edge of vision and are gone. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *present*, and they are part of the ecology, and their role is not clear, but the city does not exclude them, and the city, it seems, does not make mistakes about what it includes.

And there are the **tenders**. These are the creatures that seem most closely associated with the city's construction and maintenance. They are bipedal, roughly the size of a human child, with slender, articulated limbs and broad, flat hands that are less like hands and more like trowels — wide, thin, flexible. They move through the city with purpose, stopping at construction sites, at damaged walls, at nodes where new growth is beginning. They use their flat hands to shape the growing tissue — smoothing it, redirecting it, pressing it into forms. They do not speak, or if they do, it is at frequencies that blend into the ambient quiet. They are numerous but sparse — you might see one or two in an hour of walking, working alone, absorbed in their task, moving on when it is complete.

Are they inhabitants, or are they organs? Are they the city's hands, or are they gardeners who happen to live inside their garden? The distinction may not apply here. The Underbloom does not seem to recognize boundaries between *built* and *grown*, between *inhabitant* and *component*. Everything is of the same substance. Everything serves the same continuation.

## The Ecology of Dryness

There is no water. This is fundamental. The sap that moves through the city's conduits is not water-based. It is a mineral colloid — a suspension of dissolved silicates, metallic ions, and organic compounds in a medium that is closer to oil than to water, though it is neither. It is viscous. It moves slowly. It carries nutrients, signals, structural materials, and energy from the deepest roots of the system — which extend far below the city into the dark substrate, tapping mineral seams that have never seen light — to the furthest growing tips at the frontier.

The vegetation is not green because of chlorophyll. It is green because of a copper-based photosynthetic analogue that operates efficiently in the cool, diffuse light that filters through the ceiling membranes. This chemistry is slower than the water-and-chlorophyll chemistry of surface worlds, and it produces different byproducts — not oxygen but a faintly metallic vapor that hangs in the air and gives it its green scent. The fauna of the Underbloom breathe this vapor. They have adapted to it, or were born from it, and it sustains them the way air sustains surface creatures.

The cool temperature is a consequence of depth and chemistry. The mineral crust above insulates. The sap-stream carries heat away from the deep roots and distributes it evenly, preventing any single point from warming. The result is a stable, cool environment — never cold, never warm, always in that narrow band of temperature that feels like the inside of a cellar, or the space under a large tree on an overcast day. Comfortable, but with an edge. A temperature that reminds you that you are underground.

## The Rules of This World

The Underbloom operates under constraints that become apparent only after extended observation:

**Growth is law.** Everything here grows. Nothing is built in the sense of being assembled from inert materials. Even the products of the Foundry — the hard plates, the fibrous cords — are grown, catalyzed by living tissue, shaped by the tenders, incorporated into living structures. There is no dead matter in the city. Even the mineral crust above is slowly being colonized, its undersurface threaded with rootlets that are, molecule by molecule, converting it into something that participates in the living system.

**The city is not finished and may never be finished.** The frontier of growth is always active. The city is always becoming. There is no blueprint, no final plan — or if there is, it is encoded in the growth itself, in the chemical signals that tell one tendril to turn left and another to turn right, and it is not legible to any observer. The city may have an end-state. It may not. The tenders do not seem to know or care. They work on what is in front of them.

**Sparseness is structural.** For all its density of vegetation, the Underbloom is not crowded. The corridors are wide. The chambers are high-ceilinged. The spaces between the vine-lattices are generous. There is room — abundant room — and the room is not accidental. The city maintains open spaces the way a body maintains hollows — lungs, sinuses, the chambers of the heart. The emptiness is functional. It allows air to move, light to penetrate, creatures to pass. It is the space between notes that makes the music.

**Sound is minimal but meaningful.** The city is quiet, and the quiet is a feature, not an absence. The sounds that exist — the pump-pulse, the leaf-whisper, the creak of structural adjustment — are signals. They indicate that systems are functioning. A change in the rhythm of the pumping pods would be noticed. A silence in the filtration chamber would be an alarm. The creatures of the Underbloom are attuned to these sounds the way surface creatures are attuned to birdsong — as information, as ambient reassurance.

## The Frontier

At the edge of the city — pick an edge; there are many — the organized growth thins into something rawer, more tentative. The vines become tendrils. The tendrils become rootlets. The rootlets probe into the dark substrate, and behind them, the city consolidates: walls thicken, nodes swell into rooms, the tenders arrive to shape and guide.

The frontier is where the erosion — the *emerging* — is most visible. Not erosion as decay, but erosion as *formation*. The substrate here is being broken down, not by wind or water, but by the chemical action of the rootlets, which secrete compounds that dissolve mineral bonds and free the raw materials the city needs. It is a slow, patient demolition. The rock is not being destroyed. It is being *read*. Its components are being catalogued, absorbed, repurposed.

Standing at the frontier, you can see the city behind you — layered, complex, humming with quiet industry — and the unformed dark ahead. The boundary is not sharp. It is a gradient, a zone of increasing organization, and it moves — slowly, but it moves — always outward, always into the dark.

The air here smells strongest of green. The light is dimmest. The silence is deepest. And yet this is where the city is most alive — most active, most *becoming*. The tenders work here in greater numbers than anywhere else, their flat hands shaping the new growth, coaxing it into forms that will, in time, become corridors and chambers and bridges, and then become the framework for further growth, and then be absorbed into the deep structure, layer upon layer, until the frontier has moved so far beyond this point that what is now raw and new will be ancient and load-bearing, and the process will continue.

## The Deep Layers

Below the city — beneath the deepest inhabited levels, beneath the oldest growth — the root-system extends into the substrate in a vast, three-dimensional network that mirrors the city above in complexity. These roots are not visible. They are too deep, too fine, too intimately fused with the mineral matrix they penetrate. But their presence is felt. The floor of the deepest chambers is warm — warmer than the ambient cool — and it pulses, faintly, with the rhythm of the sap-stream. The city is connected to something below. Something that feeds it. Something that has been feeding it for as long as it has existed.

What is down there? The tenders do not go. The trundlers do not venture. The gliders never descend below the mid-levels. Whatever lies beneath the city — the source of the minerals, the origin of the sap, the foundation of the whole system — it is not accessible, and it may not be a *place* at all. It may be a process. A chemistry. A set of conditions that make the Underbloom possible and that the Underbloom, in turn, sustains and extends.

The city and the substrate are in conversation. The city grows into the substrate, and the substrate yields, and the yielding provides the materials for further growth, and the boundary between *city* and *not-city* shifts, and has always shifted, and the shifting is the point.

## Light

I have said the light is normal. It is. It is not dim. It is not dark. You can read by it. You can see clearly across a chamber, across a plaza, across the open space between vine-lattices. But it is a strange normality — a light without a source, or with too many sources, filtering through layers of translucent tissue from above, reflected and re-reflected from the pale green walls, diffused through the humid, mineral-scented air. It has no direction. It casts no sharp shadows. It is the light of a world that has never known a sun, and it is sufficient.

At certain times — the cycle is irregular, governed by the release of gases from the ceiling membranes, which thicken and thin in rhythms that correspond to the city's metabolic activity — the light shifts. It becomes greener, or bluer, or takes on a faint iridescence, as though the air itself has changed its optical properties. These shifts are subtle. They last for hours. They are not day and night. They are more like moods — the city's moods, if a city can have moods — and the creatures respond to them. The gliders become more active during the blue shifts. The trundlers slow during the green. The tenders work regardless.

## What It Feels Like

To stand in the Underbloom is to feel that you are inside something — not a building, not a cave, but a *being*. The walls are warm. The floor has give. The air is alive with chemistry. The sounds — the quiet, persistent sounds — are the sounds of function, of process, of a system that is working exactly as it should and has been working for a very long time.

It does not feel hostile. It does not feel welcoming. It feels *indifferent* in the way that large, complex systems are indifferent — not from malice or coldness but from scale. You are small. The city is large. It will continue whether you are here or not. This is not a threat. It is a fact, and there is a kind of peace in it.

The green light. The cool air. The slow pulse of the pumps. The distant creak of a vine adjusting to a new load. A glider passing overhead, silent, its leaf-wings catching the light. A tender at work, pressing its flat hands into new growth, shaping the future of the city one wall at a time.

The frontier, where the dark is being made into rooms.

The deep layers, where the roots go down and down.

The mineral crust above, thin and brittle, holding back the empty sky.

All of it alive. All of it growing. All of it unfinished.

---

*Somewhere in the mid-levels, in a chamber that was new within living memory, a pod on the ceiling swells and splits, releasing a cloud of seeds into the air. They drift. A glider banks toward them, wings wide. The seeds are not the city's seeds — they are something else, something the city produces for reasons that are its own, and the glider does not know why it eats them, only that it does, and that in eating them it carries some fraction of their chemistry to another part of the city, where it will land, and rest, and leave a trace of what it consumed on the surface of a wall that will absorb it and use it in its next phase of growth.*

*Nothing is wasted. Nothing is separate. The city is a loop, and the loop is tightening, and the tightening is growth, and growth is all there is.*

*The Underbloom continues.*
