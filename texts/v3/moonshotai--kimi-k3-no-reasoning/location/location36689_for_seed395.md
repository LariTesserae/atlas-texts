There is a vast, bone-dry basin continent stretching to every horizon under a pale, cloudless sky. No rivers, no rain, not even dew—everything here has forgotten moisture. The ground is a low, undulating plain of pale ochre dust and half-formed mineral ridges that are still raw, still pushing upward as if the land itself is being extruded fresh from below. 

This dryness should be a wasteland, yet it is not. The entire basin is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with life. A dense, low urban fabric grows directly out of the mineral substrate: countless biological structures, no taller than ten or fifteen meters, built from the living bodies of the vegetation itself. These are not constructed with tools or machines. They are the coalesced stems, roots, and woody tissues of a single dominant organism that behaves like both city and forest. 

The “buildings” are grown, not made. Thick, cellular trunks rise and fuse into walls. Branching canopies interlock overhead into vaulted roofs. Hollowed interiors form corridors, chambers, and layered terraces. Streets are narrow gaps between growing masses, paved with fallen seed casings and compressed root mats. Every surface is warm, slightly yielding, and faintly aromatic with dry resin and spice. The whole settlement is one continuous, breathing organism that has organized itself into urban density without any artificial technology.  

Light falls evenly, a steady golden daylight that never fully dims. The air is warm and still enough to carry almost no sound. There are no winds to speak of, no running water, no insect buzz or birdsong. Instead, the only noises are subtle: the slow creak and pop of expanding woody tissue, the soft friction of thousands of small bodies moving through the close quarters, the occasional dry rattle of seed pods detaching. Silence dominates, broken only by these intimate biological murmurs.  

Fauna crowd every available space. Flightless, six-limbed herbivores the size of dogs move in packed herds through the narrow passages, grazing on tender shoots that sprout from the walls. Smaller, scaled creatures cling to vertical surfaces, drinking metabolic moisture directly from the plant tissue. Predators—low-slung, warm-blooded hunters with overlapping plates—slither along the rooflines and through shadowed alleys, maintaining constant pressure on the herbivore masses. Nothing has room to spread out; every niche is fully occupied. Birth, growth, predation, and decay happen in immediate proximity, recycled back into the living architecture within hours.  

The vegetation that forms this city is lush in a dry-adapted way. Thick, succulent leaves cluster in rosettes; woody stems are swollen with stored starches rather than water. Colors are muted greens, deep burgundies, and dusty golds, all packed so tightly that the eye cannot find bare ground anywhere. New growth constantly pushes up from below—fresh mineral ridges crack open and are immediately colonized, walls thicken overnight, new chambers bud outward. The entire landscape feels embryonic, as though the city-forest is still deciding what it will become.  

Because the scale is continental, this single biological city has its own weather. Heat rises off the packed biomass in slow convection currents. Thin haze layers form from pollen and metabolic dust, drifting in vast, sluggish gyres that take days to cross the basin. In some regions the density is so extreme that the canopy knits into a continuous roof, creating dim, humid micro-climates underneath despite the dryness above. Elsewhere, growth fronts advance like slow glaciers, swallowing raw mineral ridges and converting them into new districts.  

The rules here are simple and absolute. Water is absent, so life has abandoned it entirely. All moisture is metabolic, extracted from minerals and recycled perfectly within the closed system. Death is immediate compost. Expansion is constant. Nothing is wasted. No tools, no fire, no metal—only biology pushed to architectural scale.  

Stand in the middle of it and you feel the press of existence. The ground thrums faintly with root pressure. The warm air tastes of dry spice and living dust. Around you the city-forest grows audibly, slowly, patiently filling every possible space. It is not silent in the sense of emptiness; it is silent in the sense of total saturation, a world so full of packed, active life that it has no need for loud voices.  

This is a landscape still being born, vast enough to generate its own climate, dense enough to feel like a single organism, and strange only in how completely ordinary it has made the impossible: a thriving, crowded, urban jungle on a world without a single drop of water.
