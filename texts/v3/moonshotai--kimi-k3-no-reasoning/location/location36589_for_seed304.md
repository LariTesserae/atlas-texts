The **Tumbledown Reservoirs**

The landscape is a single, dense catchment—a low, marshy basin carved by the fresh collapse of an ancient salt dome. The ground is new and still settling, a mosaic of black silt and pale, waterlogged clay that hasn't yet decided if it is solid earth or pond-bottom. It is **small**, a natural amphitheater no more than three miles across, walled in by low, chalky ridges that are still shedding rinds of stone.

This is a place of **immediate saturation**. Water is not a feature here; it is the medium. It lies just beneath the surface of every path, seeping up in grey beads that reflect the flat, ordinary light of a sky that is neither bright nor dark. It drips from the overhangs of buildings, pools in the ruts of cart-tracks, and stands in brackish, slow-moving channels that are too young to have dug proper banks. The air is perpetually damp, carrying the smell of wet ash, cold stone, and the faint, ozone tang of the newly exposed minerals.

The **settlement**—if you can call the dense warren of structures that—presses into every available inch of slightly raised ground. It is a town built entirely of **packed timber, wattle, and raw earth**, without metal nails or fired brick. Houses are low, hexagonal pods constructed from woven river-reeds lashed with rawhide and daubed with a grey, insulating mud mixed with crushed shells. They are built so close together that their thatched roofs touch, creating a continuous, dripping canopy. There are no streets, only narrow, waterlogged corridors of packed clay and stepping stones, slick with algae.

The town's layout follows the whims of the **fresh geology**. Some structures are perched on the sheer, unstable faces of recently calved mud-cliffs, braced with cedar poles. Others are half-sunk into the mire, their floors perpetually a few inches above a water table that refuses to drop. Everything is temporary, built to be moved or abandoned as the young land shifts. A path that was dry at dawn may be a stream by dusk.

**Vegetation is sparse and specialized**, clinging to life in the narrow margins. There are no trees, only thickets of **bone-white willow** that grow in dense, impenetrable copses, their roots drinking from the saturated soil. Patches of **purple saxifrage** and **glassywort** colonize the fresh sediment, their succulent leaves the only spots of color in a palette of greys, browns, and dull greens. The ground is otherwise bare, too recently exposed and too wet for a thick soil to have formed.

**Sound is a muffled, intimate thing**. The dense, wet earth and the close-packed, damp walls absorb noise. What you hear are the close, constant sounds of water: the *gluck-gluck* of a hidden siphon, the slow *drip-drip* from a roofline, the sucking sound of a boot pulling free of the mud. There are no birdsongs, only the occasional, startling shriek of a **mud-diver**—a small, elusive amphibious rodent that is one of the few creatures adapted to this half-drowned world. Human voices are low, carried on the damp air only a short distance before being swallowed.

The **unusualness** lies in the town's relationship with the water. It is not a barrier, but a resource and a tool. The inhabitants—who are rarely seen, moving like shadows in the mist—practice a form of **hydraulic masonry**. They have learned to channel the abundant water through sluices made of hollowed logs and packed clay, using its pressure to lift stone, its flow to power simple trip-hammers for shaping wood, and its currents to transport goods on reed rafts through the town's veins. The entire settlement is a low-tech, organic machine, humming with the quiet, purposeful energy of moving water. It feels less like a town built *on* the land, and more like a complex organ grown *within* it, breathing and functioning in the cool, wet, living mud.
