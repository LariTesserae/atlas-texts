Here, the horizon curves upward at its edges, subtly at first, then unmistakably, until the sky becomes a dome of water held above the world. This is the inverse ocean, a global shell of sea suspended miles overhead by technologies that predate the erosion of these stones. The surface you stand upon—high, warm, and spare—receives the sun’s full brightness without clouds to interrupt it, yet the light carries a prismatic quality, filtered through hundreds of feet of water. Shadows lie deep violet on the ground, and where the sun strikes obliquely, vast slow-moving caustics dance across the high desert, writing the wave-patterns of an invisible atmosphere onto the dust.

The terrain is old, settled into its own bones. Tepuis of porous tuff rise like petrified waves from a dry seabed, their flanks scored by the ghost-channels of water that vanished when the ocean was lifted. The rock has weathered into a geometry of absence: honeycomb hollows where salt once bloomed, arches polished by a wind that now rarely carries moisture, vast plates of sedimentary stone that ring like ceramic when struck. Between these mesas, the ground rolls in drifts of ochre silt and bleached gypsum, a sparse matrix holding scattered life.

The vegetation exists in islands of resilience. Sphere-trees—organisms that look like geological formations until they unfurl translucent fronds at dusk—dot the landscape at intervals of hundreds of yards. They gather what little moisture exists not from rain, for rain is a historical rumor, but from the condensation that beads on the undersides of rock overhangs during the rare thermal inversions when the warm air meets the cold shadow of the deep canyons. Their roots tap into brine pockets sealed in the stone, drawing up ancient water that has not seen light since the transformation. Between these solitary giants, the ground crusts with a lace of silver lichen and occasional stands of glass-grass—thin, hollow stalks that chime when the wind moves, contributing to the faint, high ringing that underlies the silence.

That silence is the landscape’s first gift. At this elevation, with the air thin and dry and the overhead ocean absorbing the planet’s ambient murmur, sound behaves differently. Footfalls do not echo; they fall dead into the dust. The human voice sounds wrong, flattened, as if spoken in a padded cell. Yet this absence creates a pressure that makes the smallest noises significant: the skitter of a chitin-shelled lizard across stone, the distant, almost infrasonic groan of the sky-sea settling on its supports, the faint hum of the built environment persisting in the rock.

The fauna has adapted to this hush and this glare. The world teems with life that moves between the sparse coverts, unburdened by the need to drink from pools. There are the balloon-floaters—creatures of translucent membrane and hydrogen-rich metabolism that drift above the scrub, photosynthesizing with fronds that mimic the sphere-trees, moving in slow herds that cast wandering shadows. Below them, in the shadowed labyrinths of the eroded stone, swarm the mechanics: arthropods the size of dogs that have incorporated the ancient nanotech into their exoskeletons, their carapaces etched with circuitry that glows faint blue in the dark. They process minerals, excreting pure metals that they stack into intricate, meaningless cairns—behavior that mimics the machines they consumed generations ago.

Predation here is an acoustic event. The long-limbed striders, tall as the tepuis’ shoulders, hunt by vibration, listening for the heartbeat of prey through the stone. When they strike, the impact is silent to the air but rings through the ground, a seismic punctuation felt in the teeth of observers miles away. The ecosystem moves in these slow, intense cycles: the migration of balloon-floaters following thermal currents up the mesa walls, the emergence of mechanics to bask in the intense midday light, the nocturnal blooming of carnivorous flora that unfolds luminescent petals to attract the drifting fauna.

The human marks are scattered and strange, integrated into the geology. A single road of fused glass runs for a hundred miles, cracked by thermal expansion into plates that shift underfoot, leading to nothing but a empty crater where a city once stood before being dismantled atom by atom. On a mesa top, a weather station persists—a single pole of memory-metal that has healed itself against erosion for millennia, still transmitting data to satellites that may or may not still listen. More common are the anchors: vast cylinders of unknown composite, half-buried in the stone, humming with a frequency that keeps the sky-sea aloft. They are warm to the touch, vibrating at a pitch just below hearing, and around them the vegetation grows slightly denser, feeding on the energy leakage.

The weirdness emerges in the boundary conditions. Near the anchors, water sometimes falls upward—slow spheres of liquid that bead on the stone and then rise, accelerating toward the ocean overhead, defying the local gravity. Dust devils spin with perfect geometric regularity, their paths describing fractal patterns that persist for days in the sand. The light itself occasionally stutters, not with darkness, but with a doubling—everything casting two shadows for a heartbeat before the sky-sea’s filtration systems correct.

As the sun declines, the temperature drops with high-desert abruptness, and the condensation begins. This is the active hour. The sphere-trees creak open, the mechanics emerge to drink from the sudden dampness, and the sky-sea above darkens to a profound indigo, through which the sun sets not below a horizon, but upward, into the water, becoming a diffuse red orb that slowly fades as if sinking into depths. The silence deepens, broken only by the crystalline chime of the glass-grass and the subsonic hum of the machines that hold the ocean above the dry, high world.
