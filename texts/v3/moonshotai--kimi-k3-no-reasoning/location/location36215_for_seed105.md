# The Silken Deeps

---

Cold, dark water fills everything — not as an absence of land, but as the medium of the world itself. The sea stretches across a broken basin the size of a small valley, enclosed on all sides by drowned ridgelines that rise and fall in slow, deliberate waves of stone. The peaks of these submerged mountains break the surface only rarely, and when they do, it is as black teeth scattered across a grey sky, catching the rain that falls in long, sustained walls. The rain never truly stops. It shifts in intensity — from a fine, drifting mist to a hammering downpour that turns the surface into a froth of white noise — but it is always there, and the sound of it striking water is the constant bass note beneath everything else.

Below the surface, the light dies quickly. The upper thirty feet hold a deep, bruised twilight, a blue-grey gloom thick with particulate matter: plant fragments, mineral dust, threads of organic silk drifting like underwater snow. Below that, darkness. Not the darkness of a room at night, but a darkness with texture — dense, cold, pressing in from all sides. The water has a faint mineral taste, sharp with iron and something else, something faintly sweet, as though the sea itself is slowly dissolving the remains of things that were once alive.

## The Terrain

The basin floor is not flat. It folds. Great ridges of dark basalt rise and fall like a rumpled blanket, and between them lie trenches where the water is colder still, where the pressure builds, where nothing grows. The rock is ancient — weathered, settled, worn smooth in places by currents that have followed the same paths for longer than memory. In other places the stone is jagged and fresh, split by some deep violence that predates everything else here. The old, settled erosion and the raw fractures coexist, and it is unclear which is winning.

On the shallower slopes — the submerged foothills and high plains — the vegetation takes hold.

## The Flora

It is lush in a way that seems impossible in this darkness, this cold, this depth. Great forests of ribbon-kelp rise from the rock, their fronds the colour of old bronze and deep bottle-green, some stretching forty feet toward the light they will never quite reach. These are not delicate things. They are muscular, dense, with blades as wide as a person's outstretched arms, and they move in the current like the breathing of a sleeping animal. Between the kelp, the rock is carpeted with thick, spongy growth — pale, translucent, faintly luminous. It glows with a cold light, not warm, not inviting, but present. Colonies of this luminescent mat form patterns across the stone: spirals, lattices, branching trees of pale blue-green light that pulse slowly, rhythmically, as though the entire basin floor is breathing in time with something immense and distant.

In the deeper trenches, where no light reaches at all, the vegetation changes character. Here grow things that might be plants or might be something else entirely — black, branching structures like coral made of ink, their surfaces covered in fine, hair-like filaments that tremble constantly. These filaments emit a low, sustained vibration. It is not sound exactly, not in the way we understand sound, but it is loud. The deeper trenches are full of this: a humming, resonant drone that builds and fades in long waves, that you feel in your chest before you hear it in your ears.

## The Silence and the Sound

This is the paradox of the place. There is no animal life. Nothing swims. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. The kelp forests are empty of fish. The luminous mats are untouched by grazers. The black, trembling growths in the trenches have no symbionts, no parasites, no predators. The ecosystem is entirely botanical — if these things are botanical — and yet it is in constant, violent motion.

The rain drives the surface into chaos. The currents, fed by the endless freshwater influx, churn through the basin in great, slow gyres, pulling fronds and detritus and dissolved minerals through channels carved over millennia. The luminous mats pulse. The black growths hum. The kelp sways. And the water itself, cold and heavy, presses and moves and carries sound with a clarity that makes every groan of shifting rock, every crack of splitting stone, every deep tremor from the earth below feel close, immediate, almost personal.

It is loud. The rain. The drone. The groaning. The constant, low-frequency hum of water moving through channels and around obstacles. If you were submerged in it, your body would feel like a tuning fork struck again and again.

## The Built World

Scattered across the basin — on the higher slopes, on the rare outcrops that break the surface, on flat shelves of rock where the current is gentler — are structures. Small ones. Built of stone, roughly fitted, without mortar or metal. They look like shelters, perhaps, or markers, or something in between. Most are partially collapsed, their walls slumped, their roofs long since dissolved or carried away by the current. Some are barely recognisable as structures at all: a circle of standing stones, a single wall jutting from a mound of silt, a doorway leading to nothing.

They are old. The erosion on them is uniform, patient, undramatic. The stone has been smoothed by water and time, the edges softened, the surfaces colonised by the same pale, luminous mat that covers everything else. In places, the mat grows so thickly over the ruins that the structures are visible only as faint, glowing outlines — architectural ghosts made of light.

There are also objects. Not many, and not large. A stone bowl on a shelf of rock. A length of rope, petrified or mineralised, coiled in a crevice. A flat disc of dark material, not stone, not metal, something else, half-buried in silt. These things are sparse. The emptiness between them is vast. You could travel for what feels like hours through the kelp forests and across the ridges and find nothing — no mark, no trace, no indication that anyone was ever here — and then stumble upon a single, small, weathered object sitting in a place that seems to have been chosen with enormous care.

Who built these things, and when, and why, the landscape does not say. The structures are too eroded to read clearly. They might be centuries old. They might be far older. The tools that made them were simple — stone on stone, hand-shaped, hand-placed. No metal. No machinery. The labour of bodies and time and patience.

## The Strange

There is a quality to the place that resists easy description. It is not threatening, exactly, but it is not safe. The darkness is not empty. The silence between sounds is not silence. The luminous mat, pulsing on the basin floor, forms patterns that shift over time — slowly, over days or weeks — as though responding to something. The black growths in the trenches change their pitch, their rhythm, in ways that seem coordinated, though there is no visible mechanism for coordination. And the kelp — the great, swaying, muscular kelp — moves in the current, yes, but sometimes it moves when there is no current. Sometimes a whole forest of it will lean in a single direction, all at once, and hold that position for a long moment, and then relax.

The water itself, at certain depths, in certain places, has a strange viscosity. It is thicker than it should be. It resists movement. It clings. In these pockets, the temperature drops further, and the darkness becomes absolute, and the drone of the black growths fades, and there is nothing — no sound, no light, no motion — as though the water has folded in on itself and created a space outside the world.

These pockets are rare, and they do not last. The current eventually breaks through, or the water thins, or the pocket simply dissolves. But while they exist, they are places of profound stillness in a landscape that is otherwise in constant, noisy, relentless motion.

## The Whole

The Silken Deeps are a drowned world, cold and dark and loud, where vegetation has achieved a kind of dominance that feels almost oppressive in its lushness, its density, its refusal to be diminished by the absence of light or warmth. The remnants of a built world — sparse, primitive, ancient — are scattered through it like seeds that never grew, or like the bones of something that died so long ago that the sea has almost finished digesting it. The rain falls. The currents turn. The luminous mats pulse. The black growths hum. And the kelp sways, always, in its patient, muscular rhythm, in the cold, dark water that fills everything.
