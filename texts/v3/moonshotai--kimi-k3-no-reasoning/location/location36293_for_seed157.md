# The Hollow of Standing Water

High on the mountain's shoulder, where the cold air sits thick and still, there is a bowl in the rock — no more than a stone's throw across in any direction — that has been filling with water for longer than anything living remembers.

The walls of the bowl are old granite, smooth as knuckles, worn into their final shape by centuries of seepage. Water does not pour down these walls. It *exudes*. Each face of stone is damp to the touch, filmed in a perpetually renewing skin of moisture that beads, merges, slides, and is replaced. The rock never dries. It has forgotten what dry is. Lichens — grey-green, almost black in the darkness — crust every inch in overlapping rosettes so dense they form a continuous living rind. You could not place a finger on bare stone anywhere in this hollow.

The floor is water. Or it is ground so saturated that the distinction has ceased to matter. Peat, black and dense as oil, has accumulated here in layer upon layer, each generation of moss and root pressing down on the last until the whole basin is a saturated sponge six feet deep. Where your foot lands, it does not splash. It *sinks*, slowly, with a sound like breath being drawn through clenched teeth, and the water rises around your ankle without a ripple — too thick, too cold, too patient for ripples.

Moss covers everything that is not already underwater, and much that is. Sphagnum in shades from corpse-pale to deep bottle-green forms hummocks and cushions, each one packed shoulder-to-shoulder against the next, a fabric of living tissue with no gaps. Sundews nestle in the creases between moss-mounds, their sticky red filaments glistening. Cranberry vines thread through everything, wiry and dark-leafed, binding the surface into a single continuous mat. Beneath this mat, the peat water is still. There is no stream feeding this hollow and no stream draining it. Rain arrives, condenses on every surface, soaks down, and stays. The water table is the ground.

Above, the sky is not visible. A canopy of dwarf birch and stunted rowan — no tree taller than twice a man's height — leans inward over the hollow, their branches interlaced so densely that the darkness below is nearly total. What light enters is a dim amber-green, filtered through layer upon layer of wet leaves, arriving exhausted. It is the kind of darkness that is not black but *thick*, a medium rather than an absence. Your eyes adjust, and the hollow reveals itself in tones of near-black: black-green, black-brown, the black-grey of wet stone, the near-black crimson of the sundews.

The cold is constant. Perhaps three or four degrees above freezing, never warmer, never colder. The water and the stone and the dense saturated air hold this temperature the way a held note persists in a stone room. There is no wind. The hollow is sheltered by its own walls and its own canopy, and the air inside is so humid it has weight. Breath condenses immediately. Every surface carries a film of cold water. Droplets form on the undersides of leaves and fall — one, then another, then a long pause, then three in quick succession — and this is the only weather the hollow has ever known.

The soundscape is built from this falling water. A drip lands on moss: absorbed, silent. A drip lands on open water: a small, round *plik*, deep and soft. A drip lands on stone: a click. These sounds arrive at long, irregular intervals, and between them the silence is so complete that you hear your own pulse in your ears. Occasionally — rarely — a larger sound: the slow, wet settling of the peat mat under some shifting weight, a groan like a sleeper turning over. The hollow is always adjusting itself, but so gradually that no single adjustment can be called an event.

The life here is overwhelming in its density and its stillness. This is not a place of visible animal drama. It is a place where every cubic inch of water, every fold of moss, every film of moisture on rock is *occupied*. The peat water, still and dark as tea, teems with crustaceans no larger than grains of rice — copepods, water fleas, translucent seed shrimp — packed so densely that a cupful of water is closer to a living slurry than a liquid. Larvae of midges and craneflies thread through the moss in uncountable numbers. Beetles — small, black, patient — cling to the undersides of every leaf. The wet stone walls host flatworms and snails and the slow, pale larvae of caddisflies armored in cases of glued-together moss fragments.

Amphibians are everywhere. Small dark frogs, their skin the exact color of wet peat, sit motionless on every hummock, so still and so perfectly camouflaged that you will not see them until one blinks. They do not hop away when approached. There is nowhere to hop to that is not already occupied by another frog. Salamanders, longer than your hand, lie half-submerged in the black water like drowned roots. Their numbers are difficult to comprehend because they do not move. They have no reason to move. The food — the larvae, the crustaceans, the small soft things — is already at their mouths. They have achieved an equilibrium so complete that predation here looks like stillness.

And there are the birds. Not many species, but those that are here are present in absurd density. Wrens — dozens of them — flick through the understory like nervous thoughts, their sharp *tik-tik* calls the loudest sounds in the hollow after the dripping water. Dippers stand on the wet rocks, bobbing, their white eyelids flashing in the gloom. They nest behind the seeping walls, in crevices kept perpetually wet, and their young fledge into a world where everything is saturated and there is no dry branch anywhere.

The whole system is in balance. Not a peaceful balance — a *saturated* balance, in which every niche is filled, every surface occupied, every resource claimed. Nothing is wasted because there is nothing left over to waste. The frogs eat the larvae that eat the detritus that falls from the canopy that is fed by the rain that condenses on the stone that releases the minerals that feed the moss that shelters the frogs. The loop is closed. It has been closed for so long that the hollow has stopped changing. A frog that dies sinks into the peat and becomes the peat and feeds the moss that another frog will sit upon. There is no succession, no colonization, no disturbance. The mountain does not shake. The climate does not shift. The water neither rises nor falls.

The effect of all this on a visitor is strange and total. The hollow is small enough to cross in thirty steps, yet it feels *complete*, a world entire. The darkness and the cold and the saturated air and the absolute density of life press inward on every sense. You are aware of being surrounded — not threateningly, but *thoroughly*, as though the life here has no room for you and no hostility toward you, only a perfect indifference born of perfect fullness. You are the only empty space in the hollow. Everything else is taken.

You stand in cold black water to your ankles. Moss cushions your feet. A frog watches you from a hummock and does not move. A droplet falls somewhere behind you — *plik* — and the silence resumes, and the darkness holds, and the water seeps, and the beetles cling, and the salamanders wait, and the peat deepens by the width of a hair each year, and nothing changes, and nothing has changed, and nothing will.
