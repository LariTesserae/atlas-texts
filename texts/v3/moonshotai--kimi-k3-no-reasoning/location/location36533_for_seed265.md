The darkness here is not empty. It is a pressure, thick and warm as blood, pressing against your skin with the weight of deep water. You stand in the Sump of Veyl, a basin that did not exist twelve years ago—a valley that unzipped itself from the continent’s belly in a slow-motion geological shrug, revealing fresh strata still raw and weeping. The erosion is young here, too young; the ground has not yet decided what shape it wants to hold. It slumps. It breathes.

Water rises. Not falling—rising. It condenses from the air itself in this heat, beading on the industrial carcasses scattered through the mire, dripping upward from fractured bedrock that hasn’t finished cooling from whatever cataclysm birthed this place. The moisture is dense, dense enough that you feel it in your lungs like swallowing wool. It pools in the shadowed hollows of recent faults, creating a marsh that exists in negative—black water in black air, visible only by the phosphorescent scum that rims each puddle like a diseased halo.

The sparse vegetation is wrong. Not scarce, but *cautious*. Blade-trees, thin as wire and tall as men, grow in scattered copses with too much space between them, as if each plant fears its neighbor. Their leaves are transparent membranes that catch the industrial runoff and glow faintly—sodium-vapor orange, mercury green—illuminating nothing but their own skeletal structure. When the warm wind moves through them, they do not rustle. They hum, a low frequency that vibrates in your molars.

Industry happened here, but recently, and badly. The structures are scattered with the random violence of a machine that vomited its guts across the valley floor. Half-built conveyor systems emerge from the black water like the spines of leviathans. A single smokestack stands perfectly vertical in a field of tilted bedrock, still radiating heat, still exhaling thin white vapor that dissipates into the greater dark. There are no lights from these machines—they were abandoned mid-operation, their power sources still humming somewhere beneath the water, sending up bubbles that break the surface with a sound like distant, slow applause.

The fauna is abundant, but you will not see it. You feel it. The density of life here is suffocating—too many presences pressing against the skin of reality. In the dark, something brushes your ankle, scaled and warm. The water around your boots writhes with eel-things that have evolved to live in the chemical heat. Above, in the lightless air, colonies of membrane-winged creatures navigate by the heat-signature of the buried machinery, their bodies translucent, their shrieks subsonic. They are numerous enough that their wingbeats create a pressure against your eardrums, a dense atmospheric quilt of life that never quite touches you.

And the weirdness. Reality here is thin, stretched over the recent wound of the land’s formation. In the darkness, distances collapse. You take three steps toward a glowing blade-tree and find yourself standing beside it, though it seemed a hundred meters distant. The water reflects stars that are not above you. Most disturbing are the overlaps—places where the young erosion has exposed not just new rock, but *other* rock, strata that shouldn’t exist in this timeline, glinting with metallic fossils that resemble circuit boards. You can reach into the dark in certain hollows and feel the texture of a different landscape entirely—dry desert, arctic ice, the inside of a living throat.

Time moves slowly. The changes here are geological and patient. The water rises by millimeters each year. The machines rust in centuries-long exhales. The blade-trees grow one transparent ring every decade. Even the abundant fauna moves with a sluggish inevitability, as if the heat and dark have slowed their metabolisms to match the land’s own ponderous emergence. Nothing here is urgent. The reality-breaks do not flash or scream; they simply persist, waiting for you to notice that you have been walking through the same hundred square meters for hours, or that your reflection in the black water is moving slightly out of sync.

The silence is heavy, broken only by the slow drip of condensation from the dead machinery, the occasional deep *thrum* of the earth still settling into its new configuration, and the soft, wet sounds of the abundant unseen life feeding in the dark. It is a landscape in the process of being born, or perhaps in the process of digesting something older, and you are standing in its open mouth, warm and wet and endlessly, densely dark.
