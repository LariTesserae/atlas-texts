The Burrow-Markets of the Under-Bark

The descent is not a ladder but a compromise. You do not climb down to the Burrow-Markets; you negotiate your way through the interface where the great taproot of the World-Oak meets the bedrock, slipping sideways between cork-layers that have grown around the rusted hull of a pre-Excavation ore-hauler. The air changes first—not colder, for this is a warm underworld, but thicker, carrying the humidity of condensation forests and the metallic taste of old combustion.

It is a single cavern, though “cavern” suggests stone. Here, the walls are a composite of living phloem and fossilized resin, bulging with the slow pressure of growth that has been diverted, channeled, and exploited for longer than the current tenants can calculate. The scale is intimate—hamlet-sized, a pocket-city tucked into the root-ball like an egg in a nest—yet the ceiling soars in cathedral arches where parasitic figs have strangled ancient support beams into lattices of wood and stone-dust.

Dimness prevails. Not darkness—the cavern is too crowded with life for true dark—but a low amber gloom, the color of weak tea, cast by bioluminescent bracket fungi that grow in great shelving tiers along the eastern wall. These are cultivated, not wild. The Light-Tenders, mole-like creatures with eyes that have receded to mere silvered pits, prune the fungal shelves with copper shears, harvesting the brighter caps for the street-lamps that line the root-causeways below. The light flickers with their feeding patterns, so the city breathes in shadow and gold, shadow and gold, a rhythm that has persisted long enough to become the local measure of time.

Vegetation is not merely present; it is the substrate, the architecture, the law. The streets are paved with compressed moss so dense and ancient it has taken on the consistency of leather, springy underfoot, releasing puffs of spore-laden mist when trodden. Buildings are not built but persuaded—hollowed tubers the size of grain silos, their walls dried and lacquered into waterproof shells, connected by bridges woven from living vines that adjust their tension hourly in response to the tremors of digging fauna. There is no stone here, no dead wood. Even the “metal” infrastructure—the steam-pipes that snake between the tuber-houses, the great pistons that pump air from the surface world—is sheathed in a creeping锈-lichen (rust-lichen) that feeds on oxidation, maintaining the machinery while slowly digesting it, a symbiosis that requires constant negotiation by the Pipe-Keepers.

The industry is bio-mechanical, loud, and strangely organic. In the central plaza—a space cleared only by the constant trampling of feet—stand the Fermentation Engines. These are not the clean factories of the surface, but great vats of translucent cellulose, grown rather than welded, where engineered yeast-beasts (blind, slug-like creatures the size of oxen) digest cellulose into alcohol and electrical current. The sound is immense: a wet, rhythmic churning, the gnashing of the yeast-beasts’ grinding plates, the hiss of pressure-release valves that vent not steam but clouds of nutrient-rich mist. This mist sustains the hanging gardens that dangle from the ceiling—orchids with glass-clear petals that chime like bells when the condensation drips through them.

Water arrives as a visitor, not a resident. It drips from the “Weep-Holes” in the ceiling—natural fissures in the limestone above that open only during the surface rains, creating temporary waterfalls that the citizens collect in great sponges of cultivated moss. Between rains, the air is merely damp, the walls sweating with the exhalations of the fauna. And the fauna is overwhelming.

They are everywhere, though spread thin in the dense architecture—a sparsity born of territorial instinct rather than lack of numbers. Blind cave-crickets the size of small dogs, their carapaces etched with phosphorescent patterns that spell out mating signals in the dark; they occupy the vertical spaces, leaping between fungal shelves with snapping sounds like breaking celery. Armored burrowers—descendants of badgers, perhaps, though they have grown scales and lost their eyes—move through the streets in single file, their claws clicking against the moss-pavement, carrying crops of tubers on their armored backs in a slow, domesticated migration. In the stagnant pools where the Weep-Holes overflow, translucent salamanders with external gills like red lace fans tend gardens of glow-worms, cultivating light as a crop.

The rules here are those of negotiated decay. Nothing is permanent, yet everything is established. The city has been in this state of balanced erosion for centuries—structures are constantly being eaten by the vegetation, but the growth is slow, predictable, and the citizens have learned to harvest the overgrowth for fuel and food. You do not fight the mold that eats your doorway; you time its consumption so that when the threshold finally crumbles, you have already grown a new door from a neighboring root.

The strange emerges in the gaps between species. There are the “Echo-Wrights,” creatures that appear to be birds but are actually mobile seed-pods, their wings made of layered bark, their songs the recorded and replayed sounds of the city from decades past—voices of dead citizens, the clatter of obsolete machinery, the sound of rain from a year when the surface burned. They perch in the high arches, replaying history at random, creating an auditory haunting that is considered good luck, or at least neutral.

Above all, the sound. It is a loud place, despite the dimness. The constant chittering of the cricket-swarms, the basso profundo of the Fermentation Engines, the chiming of the orchids, the splash and suck of the burrowers moving through wet moss, the sudden explosive pops of seed-pods bursting in the heat generated by the compost-floors. It is a cacophony that the citizens navigate by touch and vibration, their bare feet reading the Moss-code—patterns of vibration in the street-moss that function as signage for the eyeless.

When the surface storms come, the whole city vibrates. The roots tremble, the Weep-Holes open, and for a few hours, the Burrow-Markets become a place of rushing water and increased light (lightning channeled down through conductive root-fibers, flickering in green sheets along the walls). During these times, the predators emerge—fast-moving centipede-hounds that hunt by vibration, their legs drumming a terrifying tattoo on the mushroom-forests that line the drainage channels. The citizens retreat to the tuber-houses, sealing themselves in with membranes of living spider-silk, waiting for the waters to recede and the normal, established chaos to resume.

It is a place that feels simultaneously claustrophobic and infinite, industrial and wild, ancient and constantly becoming. The air tastes of spores and electricity, of wet earth and old iron. To live here is to accept that you are part of a digestive process—that you are being slowly broken down and repurposed by the world you inhabit, even as you break it down in turn. The Burrow-Markets persist not in defiance of entropy, but in partnership with it, a warm, dim, roaring pocket of life beneath the sleeping earth.
