**The Perch of Rootwater**

You arrive at Rootwater by climbing. There is no other way. The ascent begins in dry earth—cracked, ochre ground that gives way first to scattered stone, then to bare rock, and finally to the Root itself: a trunk the width of a small hill, rising vertical from the plain and disappearing upward through haze into light you cannot see from below.

This is the last tree. Not the last living thing—that would be impossible, for Rootwater teems—but the last tree *of consequence*, the last one tall enough to matter. It stands three thousand feet, perhaps more, though no one has ever climbed to the crown. What matters is not its height but its breadth: the trunk is thick as a fortress wall, bark folded into ridges deep enough to hold rainwater, if there were rain.

There is no rain. Rootwater is a desert of air. The tree draws moisture from deep stone through mechanisms no one living understands—ancient biology, chemical leaching, perhaps something older. What matters is that the tree is *wet* inside, and this wetness seeps. It beads on the bark. It drips. It collects in the crevices where the branches—also thick as roads, also ancient beyond memory—divide and redivide.

Here, at the middle elevations where the branches are broad enough to build upon, is the town.

## The Branches

Rootwater—the settlement, not the tree—spreads across nine major limbs, connected by rope bridges and carved wooden walkways worn smooth by generations of bare feet. The buildings are constructed from the tree itself, though not from cutting it. The tree sheds constantly: small branches, bark plates the size of doors, resin that hardens into amber-like slabs. The people of Rootwater gather these offerings and work them with stone tools, fire, and patience.

The architecture is dense, layered, vertical. Houses cling to the top of branches, hang beneath them, are carved into the sides where wood has grown soft with age. Space is precious. A family’s home might be a single room built into a hollow where two branches meet, accessible by ladder from below and by a small platform from above, the walls woven from flexible twigs and caulked with resin. There are perhaps two thousand people here, perhaps more—no census has ever been taken, and the boundaries of Rootwater blur into the tree itself.

The light is always dim. The canopy far above—still dense, still green despite the age—filters the sun into a greenish gloaming. What light reaches the town is amber-tinted, diffuse, as if always evening. In the crevices between branches, in the undersides of bridges, in the narrow alleys between structures, it is dark enough that lamps burn day and night. These are simple things: shallow clay bowls filled with rendered fat, wicks of twisted fiber. The smoke rises and joins the slow respiration of the tree.

## The Sounds

Rootwater is loud. This surprises visitors, who expect a tree-town to be quiet, contemplative, wind-whispered. Instead: constant noise. The branches themselves groan—not with movement, for there is little wind this high, but with the slow settling of ancient wood under enormous weight. It is a deep, subsonic moaning that you feel in your chest before you hear with your ears. The rope bridges creak. The resin caulking pops as it expands and contracts with the tree’s slow breathing.

And then there are the animals.

The tree is *full* of life. Birds—hundreds of species, maybe thousands, no one knows—nest in every available hollow. Their calls form a dawn-to-dusk cacophony, layered and overlapping: shrieks, trills, drums, whistles, the strange metallic songs of species found nowhere else. Insects drone and click. Tree-frogs, small and brightly colored, call from the water-filled crevices with voices like struck bells. At night, the mammals come out: gliding creatures that leap between branches with membranes stretched between their limbs, their landings audible *thumps*; slow-moving climbers with grasping hands that chatter and squabble in the darkness.

The people of Rootwater have learned to sleep through it. To them, the noise is the sound of safety. Silence would mean the tree has died.

## The Water

There is no standing water in Rootwater—no ponds, no streams. But water is everywhere. It seeps from the bark in slow beads. It pools in the deep crevices where branches divide. It condenses on the undersides of leaves in the canopy and falls as occasional, random drops. The people collect it with cloth: they tie woven pads of absorbent fiber to the bark, let them saturate, then wring them into clay jars. It is slow work. A single pad might yield a cup of water in a day.

The tree’s moisture is mineral-heavy, slightly bitter, safe to drink but never plentiful. The people ration it carefully. They have also learned to read the tree’s moods: when the beads of moisture grow larger, when the bark feels softer underfoot, they know the tree is drawing up more from the deep stone. These are times of relative plenty. When the bark dries and hardens, when the crevices stay empty, they know to conserve.

The animals have their own methods. The birds know where water collects. The insects lay eggs in the temporary pools. The tree-frogs have adapted to breed in the small, sheltered basins that form in the junctions of branches. There is an entire ecosystem here, dense and interdependent, all of it ultimately drawing from the tree’s slow, patient mining of the deep earth.

## The Age

No one knows how old the tree is. The people of Rootwater have no writing, but they have memory: oral histories that stretch back through dozens of generations, all of them set *here*, in the branches. The tree was already old when the first person climbed it. It was already alone—no other trees of its kind in the known world, no forest, only this singular vertical fact rising from the dry plain.

The wood is incredibly dense, almost stone-like in its hardness. The tools of Rootwater—stone adzes, bone drills, fire-hardened sticks—can barely work it. Most construction uses dead wood, shed branches, the softer interior wood that becomes accessible when a branch breaks and the people carefully, reverently, hollow it out. The tree seems to tolerate this. It has been shedding parts of itself for millennia. What is another branch, another bark plate?

But the tree is also *changing*, slowly. The oldest parts of the town, built generations ago, are being engulfed. The tree grows, but so slowly that no one notices within a single lifetime. A house built snug against the trunk might, after two hundred years, find the trunk pressing against its walls, the wood slowly flowing around the structure like very slow water. The people adapt. They rebuild. They carve away what they can and surrender what they cannot.

## The Rules

Rootwater has rules, though they are rarely spoken. You do not cut living wood. You do not build fires larger than a lamp. You do not waste water. You do not kill the tree-frogs, for they are the tree’s voice. You do not climb above the Ninth Branch, for the air is thinner there and the wood is older and the birds are strange.

The people live slowly. A single piece of construction—a house, a bridge—might take years. There is no hurry. The tree has been here for millennia. It will be here for millennia more. The people measure time in generations, in the slow growth of the branches, in the patient accumulation of resin and shed bark.

They are not primitive by choice but by circumstance. Metal is unknown—there is no ore in the tree, no way to smelt it this high. Stone for tools comes from below, carried up in long, dangerous climbs. Most work is done with wood, bone, fiber, fire, and patience. The technology is simple but exquisitely refined: rope bridges that flex with the tree’s subtle movements, water-collection systems that maximize every drop, structures that distribute weight so perfectly that they seem to float on the branches rather than press down on them.

## The Ecology

The tree is not alone. It is an ecosystem, dense and vertical. The bark hosts entire communities of insects, fungi, lichens—organisms found nowhere else. The water-filled crevices are miniature worlds: mosquito larvae, water beetles, tiny crustaceans that must have arrived as eggs stuck to birds’ feet generations ago. The canopy, far above, is rumored to be a forest in itself: epiphytes, mosses, smaller plants that grow in the accumulated debris of centuries.

The people of Rootwater are part of this ecology. They tend certain insect colonies for their resin. They encourage the growth of edible fungi in certain crevices. They have learned which birds’ eggs can be taken and which must be left. They scatter the seeds of the plants they eat—tough, slow-growing things that can survive in the bark crevices—and in doing so, they have shaped the tree’s ecosystem as much as it has shaped them.

## The Endurance

Rootwater endures. The tree endures. The people endure. The desert below does not encroach—it is already complete, already final. The tree does not grow taller—it has reached its limit. The town does not expand—it has filled the available space. Everything is in a state of dynamic equilibrium, change so slow it is almost imperceptible.

And yet it is not static. The tree breathes. The water seeps. The birds call. The people build and rebuild, adapting to the tree’s slow shifts. The resin accumulates. The bark sheds. The bridges are replaced. The stories are told and retold, each generation adding its own small variations.

This is Rootwater: a town in a tree, dense and vertical and loud, lit by dim amber light, sustained by patient water-collection and ancient biological processes no one fully understands. It is small—nine branches, two thousand people, a few acres of horizontal space spread across three dimensions. It is old beyond memory. It is alive in every sense: the tree, the people, the animals, the slow processes of growth and decay and renewal.

You could stay here. You could learn to climb, to build, to read the tree’s moods. You could become part of the ecosystem, another organism in the dense, layered community of the branches. The tree would tolerate you. It tolerates everything. It has been here too long to do otherwise.

And below, the desert waits, dry and patient, needing nothing.
