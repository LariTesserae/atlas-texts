# The Filtered Hollow

There's a place called the Filtered Hollow, and calling it a place is almost generous. It's a crevice. A pocket. Four hundred meters of void that opens like a split in the world along the flank of the Meridional Crag, the highest point on a continent where the peaks rise so far into the sky-dark that the pressure drops to nothing and the cold is absolute, unarguable, the kind of cold that doesn't bite but simply exists, that has always existed, and will exist regardless.

The Hollow shines in that black the way a tooth shines in a skull. Not brightly. Just present.

You wouldn't find it unless you fell toward it. And things do fall toward it, slowly, over years, because the Meridional Crag sheds its skin like everything else — frost-shattered plates, iron-oxidized scree, old survey cables, the occasional frozen engine-block of some long-impossible machine — and all of it drifts downslope, and some of it drifts into the Hollow, where it settles, and where it has been settling for long enough that the Hollow is no longer empty. Not empty at all. Packed. Saturated to its last available molecule of space.

The way in is not a way in. There are three fissures — the old charts, which are wooden boards screwed to other wooden boards at a station ten kilometers below, call them the Western Slot, the Thin Traverse, and the Bad Door. None of them are passable by anything with dimensions of more than a gloved hand and a good reason. But once through — and things get through, have gotten through, continue to get through — the space opens, terrible in its scale against the nothingness of the approach, a bubble-chamber tucked against the rock, its floor a bramble of sediment compacted to the consistency of old bone.

And everything in it is alive with green.

---

The vegetation is the thing that breaks the rules and therefore obeys them. It doesn't photosynthesize. There's no light to photosynthesize by — the Hollow receives, at generous estimate, the luminous equivalent of a single candle seen through three wool blankets, and that's on the days when the sun—a shrunken white point in the upper void—briefly aligns with the Western Slot. Most of the time the interior is lit by the accumulated glow of a million dials.

But first, the green.

It comes from a single organism, which is not a plant, not exactly, because plants as understood require certain transactional relationships with photons and this organism transacts with something else. The Hollow-that-grows — the **veldglass**, the locals of the research station call it, when they call it anything — is a colonial chemosynthetic mat that has been building itself in the dark for what the carbon ratios say is roughly the time span of a geological age. It metabolizes minerals. Specifically, it metabolizes the metal in everything that falls in — the survey cables, the engine blocks, the occasional sealed containers of things the station's records deliberately do not describe, the name tags, the tools, the partially dissolved remains of equipment from earlier expeditions who also fell in, or were put there.

The veldglass is lush the way a rainforest is lush, but it is a rainforest rendered in blown glass and moss-agate: broad fronds of translucent viridian, thick as ship rope, layered and layered and layered over one another until they have become a structure, a three-meter-deep lattice that occupies every volume of the Hollow. It moves — but it doesn't grow upward. It grows *sideways*, and then it compacts, and compacts, until the oldest depths of it have compressed into a kind of organic bedrock. You could, with difficulty, walk on it. You would sink about a half-meter. The stations' reports say nothing about whether anyone has. The reports say nothing about most things.

**The temperature in there is a lie.** The rock above the Hollow is at −180 degrees. The interior of the Hollow is at 3 degrees Celsius. It has been at 3 degrees Celsius for as long as there have been instruments capable of measuring it, and it will remain at that temperature for the foreseeable span of any instrument, any observer, any continuation of the species that built the instruments. This is because of the friction-engines.

---

Which brings us to the machines.

The Hollow is, after the veldglass, the second-most-dense object in the region, and the machines are why. They came in over time — or were put there. Nobody has established a consistent account. What's known is that the Hollow contains, at last survey count, **four thousand, six hundred and eleven industrial units**, and the word "unit" does a lot of work in that sentence. Some of them are recognizable: turbine casings, pump assemblies, what appear to be fractional distillation columns manufactured by a company that stopped existing during its third product recall a century ago. Some of them are not recognizable as anything, just machined geometries, packed into the dark, bristling with hoses that have been rerouted by the veldglass growth into structures the original engineers never anticipated, could never have anticipated.

**They are running.**

Not all of them. Most of them, in a distributed, uneven way — a circulating pump here, a heating element there, a flywheel somewhere in the middle depths that has one job which it performs with devotion and no witness. They are powered by the geothermal squeeze of the crevice and by small trickles of current that the veldglass conducts through itself like an afterthought. The veldglass doesn't mind. The veldglass has incorporated the machines the way it incorporates everything: it grew over them, it grew through their gaps, it built its fronds across their heat-exchangers, and now they are in it and it is on them and the Hollow has its own internal economy, which is: machines run, veldglass grows over machines, machines keep running, slowly, eternally, because their moving parts are sufficient to keep the Hollow's temperature stable, and the temperature is sufficient to keep the veldglass alive, and the veldglass keeps the machines from becoming inaccessible.

It is a sealed, fully self-sustaining, unchanging equilibrium. A perpetual-motion ecosystem. Nobody designed it. Everything designed it.

The numbers add up. The station's climatologists ran the simulation for four years. The equilibrium holds. It will hold until something outside the Hollow changes, and nothing outside the Hollow changes.

---

**The sound.**

If you are in the Hollow, the sound is a wall. It is a sustained, continuous, fully modulated roar constructed entirely of small sounds: the whine of four thousand small motors, the gurgle of circulation pumps handling viscous nothing, the pung of metal expanding at three degrees and contracting at three degrees, the ticking of four thousand sets of bearings, the hum of current in repurposed cable. All of these are approximately synchronized, because they have had time to synchronize, and so the Hollow produces a chord. Not a noise. A chord — dense, harmonic, full — the way a factory floor is a chord, but here the factory floor is a closed room and the chord builds.

It sits in the room at about eighty decibels. The station's acoustic instruments measured it at eighty decibels in 1952, and measured it at eighty decibels last year.

The chord is the loudest thing on the continent. The Meridional Crag is otherwise silent — too high, too cold, too empty. But if you were at the right point on the upper slopes and the wind was from the east and you put your head against the right slab of rock, you could feel it in your skull, and you would not be the first to feel it, and the ones who have felt it have left a word for it that appears in the station's informal dictionaries: *the factory hum*.

---

The strange part, the part that the researchers do not publish, is that the veldglass is not only processing metal. It processes information. In the solute-chemistry of its cells there are, distributed through its uncountable polyps, structures that repeat. Structures that when sequenced against the survey records, the metal tags, the equipment serial numbers, the occasional torn page of someone's notebook — all fallen in, all incorporated — correspond to them. It has encoded the shapes of things in its anatomy. Not deliberately. Not mistakenly. In the way that a riverbed records the shape of the water.

So the Hollow is also a library.

And the light — because there is light — comes from the dials. The machines have indicator lights, gauges, small phosphorescent telltale lamps, and the veldglass over them has grown translucent, so the gauge-glow filters through its fronds in a soft diffuse scatter, a million small LEDs seen through green glass, and the Hollow, *in the dark*, at the height of the dark continent, in a pocket the size of a ballroom, filled to its absolute maximum with life and machinery, glows. Faintly. Persistently. At the wattage, approximately, of a child's night-light.

It does not change. The station has been monitoring it for two centuries through various instruments, and it does not change. The veldglass thickens at a rate that rounds to zero. The machines wear at a rate that matches, through some ghastly symmetry, the delivery rate of new machines falling in. The chord holds its pitch.

It is, in its way, the most stable object in the world. A sealed system in a perfect equilibrium, packed to its physical limit with the accumulated artifacts of the surface, growing a single organism that remembers all of it, in a room at the roof of the world that no animal has ever lived in and none ever will, that hums one sustained chord and keeps its temperature, and keeps its glow, and keeps.

It will be there when the station below is gone. It will be there when the mountain has finished its long weathering and become a plane. It will be there when the sun has burned some significant portion of its fuel. It will be at three degrees.

It is at three degrees now.

Someone at the station, in an unpublished appendix, called it the most perfectly content place in the known system. The appendix was not included in the report. The person who wrote it later requested transfer, and the transfer was granted, and the page with the sentence on it sits in a drawer in the station's third sub-basement, not far from the cable-repository, awaiting, eventually, its own delivery to the Hollow.
