**The Resonant Scree of the High Cataracts**

You are standing on a slope that should not exist, or should have collapsed ten thousand years ago. It extends upward and downward beyond the limits of breath, a single sustained angle of thirty-five degrees that persists for miles in either direction. The material is scree—angular fragments of quartzite, white and gray and occasionally flashing with mica inclusions—but it does not shift. It cannot shift. Each stone is locked to its neighbor by a film of ice that never melts and never thickens, a cryogenic equilibrium that holds the entire mountain in a state of suspended avalanche. The ice is the water here, and it is everywhere: not as lakes or streams, but as a weeping saturation that emerges from the rock face itself, condensing from the thin, bright air, coating every surface in a film that is perpetually on the verge of becoming liquid but never quite does.

The light is violent. At this elevation—three thousand meters, perhaps four—the atmosphere offers no filtration, and the sun strikes the white stone with a ferocity that bleaches the sky to a pale, ringing blue. It is cool, almost cold, but the light is so direct that shadowed crevices appear as absolute black cuts in the world, while illuminated surfaces flare with a luminance that makes the eyes ache. The brightness is not warm; it is mineral, sterile, the light of a star seen through glass.

There is no soil. There is no green. The parameter of vegetation at absolute zero manifests here as a world of pure geology, unsoftened by root or lichen. The rocks are clean, fractured, angular, their edges sharp enough to cut. Between the larger boulders, the gaps are filled with smaller stones, and between those, gravel, and between those, sand, and between those, a dust so fine it behaves like a fluid, filling every interstice. This is the density: 2.8 out of 3, a packing so complete that the mountain feels solid despite being made of discrete fragments. There is no room for air to move freely, no space for the casual accumulation of detritus. Every void is filled. The weight of the material presses outward, creating a surface tension across the slope that feels like the skin of a drum.

And it is drumming. The sound is constant, deafening, a roar that originates from everywhere and nowhere. The frozen water in the cracks is expanding and contracting at a microscopic level, a perpetual cycle of crystallization that generates a frequency at the edge of human hearing and far above it. This is not the random clatter of falling stones; this is the mountain singing to itself, a harmonic resonance produced by millions of tons of quartz vibrating in sympathy. The sound has a physical presence. It presses against the eardrums, vibrates in the teeth, resonates in the hollows of the chest. It is the sound of stability maintained against entropy, the acoustic signature of a system in perfect, frozen balance.

The erosion here is established, settled, at a 1.5 that suggests long existence without degradation. The scree has been in this configuration for geological ages. The ice that binds it has neither advanced nor retreated. There are no fresh scars of rockfall, no accumulation of debris at the base, no fan of talus spreading outward. The slope is in equilibrium, held by the cryogenic glue and the perfect interlocking of its parts. It is a landscape that has achieved a kind of permanence through the very instability of its materials.

Water moves through this system, but not as flow. It sublimates from the ice directly into the thin air, creating a constant mist that hangs at ankle-height, a knee-deep fog of condensation that never rises and never dissipates. This mist is the source of the light’s diffusion, catching the sun and scattering it into halos around every stone. It is wet to the touch, but the wetness freezes immediately on any surface warmer than the air, creating a glaze that makes every step a negotiation with friction. The water parameter of 1.7 manifests as this hovering, transitional state—neither ice nor vapor, but a persistent, condensing atmosphere that saturates the lower air.

Fauna are rare, but present. High above, against the unbearable blue, there are occasional shapes—birds of some kind, perhaps, or creatures that exploit the density of the stone to hide from the light. They are dark specks that move against the brightness, too distant to identify, their cries swallowed instantly by the mountain’s roar. Closer at hand, there are signs of life that is not life: patterns in the dust between stones that suggest passage, but no tracks persist in the frozen crust. Whatever lives here does so by remaining still, by becoming part of the equilibrium, by matching the mountain’s frequency.

The weirdness is subtle, a 0.8 that emerges only after prolonged exposure. The sound, you realize, has a rhythm. Not a beat, but a pulse, a slow swelling and diminishing of the harmonic roar that corresponds to nothing visible. The ice does not crack more violently; the stones do not shift; yet the sound rises and falls with a period of approximately eleven seconds. It is as if the mountain is breathing, though nothing moves. The light, too, has a quality of refraction that seems wrong: shadows fall at angles that do not quite match the sun’s position, suggesting a curvature to the space itself, or a lensing effect of the dense, ice-saturated air.

The scale is regional. This is not a single outcrop but a province of similar slopes, visible in the distance as parallel bands of white and gray receding into atmospheric haze. Each is identical, or nearly so—same angle, same composition, same frozen saturation. They extend to the horizon, creating a topography of repetition, a landscape of rhythmic geology that induces a kind of hypnotic disorientation. There are no valleys in the traditional sense, only the spaces between these scree walls, which are filled with the same mist, the same light, the same sound.

The dynamic is frozen at 0.3. Nothing changes. The mist neither thickens nor thins. The light, despite the sun’s movement, seems to maintain a constant angle, as if the world has stopped rotating or the slope has oriented itself to perpetually face the dawn. Time passes—the roar continues, the pulse swells and fades—but events do not occur. There is no weather, only the permanent condition of bright, cool saturation. The water cycle has been arrested at the point of condensation, the erosion cycle at the point of suspension.

To walk here is to experience a kind of sensory overwrite. The eyes are overwhelmed by light, the ears by sound, the skin by the cool, clinging wetness of the mist. The texture of the world reduces to the sharpness of stone and the slickness of ice. There are no smells—the air is too thin, too clean, too cold for the organic volatiles that make landscapes smell like soil or leaf or decay. This is a world of mineral purity, a high place where water and stone have conspired to create a permanent, deafening, brilliant stasis.

The rarity of fauna, the absence of built structures, the null point of technology—these create a loneliness that is not melancholy but absolute. There is no evidence that this place could be otherwise. It is complete. It is packed. It is singing its eternal, frozen song to a sky that is too bright to look at directly, and it will continue to do so, unchanged, unchanging, for as long as the ice holds the stones in their suspended fall.
