## The Hollow Crown of Mount Cassia

The highest place on the world's edge, flattened by eight million years of patient grinding into a broad saddle of black basalt that gleams like old bone in starlight. Here the air thins until it feels like breathing through silk, yet warmth rises from deep fissures in the stone—thermal breath that keeps the summit in perpetual, humid summer.

Water weeps everywhere. Not falling from above, but condensing from the dense, warm atmosphere itself, beading on every surface and dripping in slow, irregular cadence. The rock is slick with it, carved into shallow basins that hold mirror-still pools a hand's breadth deep. These pools never freeze, never evaporate completely, and never grow larger than a dinner plate. They are simply *here*, as they have been here for longer than continents have had names.

The vegetation is a single species of impossible fern, grown to cathedral proportions in the absence of competition. Each plant is a solitary giant, rising twelve meters on a stem no thicker than a man's wrist, then exploding into a canopy of fronds so fine they seem to be made of green smoke. There are perhaps thirty of them across the entire saddle, each separated by a hundred meters of bare, wet stone. The space between them is the defining quality of the place—a cathedral with too few pillars, where darkness pools in the gaps like something physical.

The ferns are not rooted in soil. They grow directly from the basalt, their root-fibers threading into microscopic cracks and secreting acids that take centuries to dissolve a single grain of stone. They are eating the mountain, slowly, at a rate of one millimeter per thousand years. This is the slowest violence imaginable, and the mountain seems not to notice.

There are no stars overhead. The darkness is complete, absolute, and yet somehow not cold. The atmosphere here is thick with suspended moisture that catches and scatters what little light exists—bioluminescent motes shed by the ferns in slow, rhythmic pulses. These motes drift upward in lazy spirals, rise perhaps twenty meters, then gutter out like spent embers. For a moment after each pulse, the darkness is more complete than before.

The sound is the loudest thing here. Not wind—there is no wind. Not water—the dripping is too sparse. The sound is the mountain itself, groaning under its own weight. Basalt contracts as it cools from the day's warmth, and the contractions propagate through the stone as deep, subsonic booms that last minutes each. They come in irregular intervals, sometimes hours apart, sometimes in clusters that make the ground tremble beneath your feet. Each boom is followed by a silence so profound it feels like a physical pressure on the eardrums.

Scattered across the saddle are the remains of something that might have been a temple, or a machine, or both. They are not ruins in the traditional sense—they have not fallen. They are simply *incomplete*, as if their builders abandoned them mid-construction. Single columns of polished obsidian rise from the basalt at odd angles, their surfaces etched with patterns that seem to shift when you look away. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, as if they are drawing heat from somewhere deeper.

Between the columns are small, spherical objects made of a metal that does not reflect light. They are scattered like seeds, or like the droppings of some enormous creature. Each is exactly the size of a human eyeball, and each is warm. If you pick one up, it is heavier than it should be, and it vibrates faintly, at a frequency just below the threshold of hearing.

The fauna are almost entirely nocturnal and almost entirely blind. There are moths with wingspans of two meters, their scales so fine they feel like warm dust when they land on your skin. They navigate by the heat of the thermal vents, and by the slow pulses of the fern-motes. There are salamanders as long as your arm, their skin translucent and faintly phosphorescent, that live in the shallow pools and hunt the moths when they land to drink. And there are the spiders—colonies of them, each the size of a small dog, that build their webs between the obsidian columns. The webs are not sticky. They are made of a silk that conducts electricity, and the spiders generate small charges in their bodies to stun prey that touches the strands.

The most abundant creatures are the ones you cannot see. The air is thick with them—microscopic organisms that live in the suspended moisture, feeding on the minerals carried up from the thermal vents. They are so numerous that they create a faint, constant pressure against your skin, like the memory of a touch.

The technology here is not visible in the traditional sense. There are no wires, no circuits, no screens. But the obsidian columns are not natural. They are grown, not carved, from a single crystal lattice that extends kilometers deep into the mountain's core. They are antennas, or capacitors, or something else entirely—devices that draw power from the planet's rotation and store it in a form that current physics does not fully describe. The spheres are their output nodes, accumulating charge over centuries and releasing it in slow, controlled bursts that the spiders have learned to harvest.

The mountain is dying, but it is dying in the way that stars die—slowly, with immense reserves of energy that will take eons to exhaust. The ferns are eating it. The water is dissolving it. The columns are draining it. But these processes are so gradual that the mountain does not notice them as threats. It simply *is*, in its final form, settled into a state of near-perfect equilibrium.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute. Nothing grows quickly. Nothing moves without purpose. The darkness is not an absence of light, but a presence in itself, thick and warm and patient. The sound is not noise, but the mountain's own voice, speaking in a language of pressure and vibration that predates ears.

If you stand at the center of the saddle, between the two tallest columns, and wait for the next subsonic boom, you will feel the ground rise and fall beneath your feet like the chest of a sleeping animal. The ferns will pulse in sympathy, shedding a fresh cloud of motes that rise like slow green fireworks. The spiders will grow still, sensing the vibration through their webs. The salamanders will surface in their pools, their translucent skin glowing faintly as they wait for the moths to come down.

And for a moment, you will understand that this place is not waiting for anything. It is not dying, not decaying, not even enduring. It is simply *being*, in a state of perfection so complete that time itself has become irrelevant. The mountain will outlast the stars. The ferns will outlast the mountain. The darkness will outlast them all.
