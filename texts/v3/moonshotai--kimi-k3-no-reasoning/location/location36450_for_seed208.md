This is a valley that has been listening to itself for too long.

The water does not fall from above here; it seeps upward from the world’s core, meeting the cool air in a perpetual, breathless condensation. You stand in a landscape of weeping stone—vast, undulating plains of slick, black basalt that have been worn not into peaks, but into shallow, concave mirrors. It is a terrain of inverse mountains: depressions filled with still, tea-colored water that reflects a sky the color of old pewter. The elevation is low, a subtle sinking, and the erosion is absolute. These rocks have been polishing themselves against one another for eons until they achieved a terrible smoothness, a final, settled geometry that resists further change. It feels less like a place that was built and more like a place that has *finished*, a geological end-state where every surface has been rubbed to the texture of wet soap.

There is no green. Nothing photosynthetic survives in the dim, saturated light. Instead, the landscape is host to a mineral biology—crusts of electro-sensitive lichen that glow faintly amber when the atmospheric pressure shifts, and slow-moving colonies of silicate "moss" that creep across the wet stone, leaving trails of crystalline frost. The fauna, however, is abundant and deafening. 

They are the **Howlers**—pale, amphibious things the size of wolves, with no eyes and larynxes of hollow bone. They congregate at the edges of the black pools, filling the air with a cacophony that is not merely loud but *structural*. Their cries harmonize with the resonance of the eroded stone, creating standing waves of sound that make your teeth ache. It is a predator-prey dynamic played out in pure acoustics; the howls stun the smaller, translucent creatures that skim the water’s surface, and the noise is so constant, so layered, that silence itself becomes a forgotten concept.

Scattered across this open, sparse expanse—sometimes miles apart—are the remnants of those who tried to live here. Not ruins, exactly, but interruptions. A single standing stone, carved with a tool that left spiral grooves, marking the center of a pond. A primitive sluice gate made of lashed volcanic glass and braided gut, meant to divert the upward seepage, now collapsed and half-submerged. These artifacts speak of early-tool use, of hands that shaped flint and bone, but they are ancient, part of the same primordial flattening as the rocks. They have been here so long they have become geological features themselves.

The atmosphere is thick, cool, and vibrating. Mist does not drift here; it condenses directly onto your skin in fat, cold droplets. The light is a dim, directionless glow, as if the sun were a memory buried under miles of cloud. And the world is active, always active—water bubbling up through fissures, the Howlers shifting in their vast, scattered packs, the slow chemical weathering of stone into mineral soup. It is a valley that has become a single, sustained note: wet, ancient, loud, and empty of everything except the sound of its own endurance.
