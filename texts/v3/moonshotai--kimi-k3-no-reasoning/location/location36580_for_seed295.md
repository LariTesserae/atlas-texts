High up, where the air thins to a warm, silk-thin sweetness, lies a plateau that was never land to begin with—an ancient built expanse, maybe a colossal living roof or the hardened back of some vast biological shell, abandoned before memory but still functioning in its deep-cycled way. There is no rock or soil anywhere; everything is constructed, seamless, formed from once-living matter cured into honeycombed plates and amber‑flexed struts that breathe almost imperceptibly under the weight of millennia. Though it is structure, it behaves as ecosystem.

Darkness presses close here, softened only by a faint skyglow like dusk that never lifts. The darkness smells fertile. Sounds arrive long before shapes do: an overwhelming chorus of creatures layered upon creatures, buzzing, clicking, whistling, chewing, murmuring, low vibrating calls rolling through the hollow cavities of the built substrate. At times the plateau seems less a place than a resonating body. Some of the inhabitants cling beneath overhangs in clustered masses; others trail long threads of themselves over its edges into thinner air, glowing faintly only when disturbed.

Vegetation crowds every surface—dense, heaped, intertwined, a thick blanket of rooted climbers and heavy waxy leaves whose stems press into the seams of the structure as if it had grown for them. Moisture gathers in slow ways: dew condensed along the undersides of broad fronds, droplets migrating through microchannels in the built plates, pooling in pockets where the material dips. It never dries, but it never floods. Water here moves like breath.

The whole plateau breathes slowly, in and out over years. Old growth matures, folds down into the built substrate’s cavities, and is replaced by something similar but slightly shifted, a patient rotation of forms. Erosion is not destruction but settling—the structure smoothing itself, the organisms adjusting, the system holding itself in its own self-made balance.

Every so often a shadow moves overhead: some enormous winged shape, or a drifting cluster of spores. The sound changes when that happens—a muffling, a collective hush, then a renewed surge of noise, as if the fauna were reminding the dark that they are still many, still alive, still woven into this warm, high, humming world.
