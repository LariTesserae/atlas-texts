The high valley, though ancient and worn, remained trapped in a perpetual, deafening storm-state.

The mountains surrounding it were primordial, their peaks long since scoured to smooth, rounded domes of bedrock by millennia of relentless, hyperactive weather. They rose with abrupt, extreme elevation from the basin floor, creating a vast, open amphitheater that funneled the region’s turbulent energy. The erosion had settled into a final form here—not by calming, but by exhausting all loose material, leaving only the hardest stone to face the wind.

The air, consistently warm and thick with the damp of intermittent condensation and high-mist, carried an almost solid weight of sound. It was a ceaseless, chaotic rumble and shriek, a deafening symphony of wind tearing through rocky channels, the distant crash of water, and the unsettling, organic shifting of the ground itself.

This dynamism was the landscape’s true, strange nature. The valley floor was a churning mosaic of creation and destruction. Lush, vibrantly green moss and tenacious, low-slung ferns—vegetation adapted to instability—clung to the exposed stone, only to be ripped away in sudden, localized bursts of force, leaving behind stark, wet scars that would be reclaimed by new growth within hours. Pockets of dark, still water, no larger than a hand, would appear on flat rock, fed by unseen condensation, only to vanish as the stone beneath them subtly shifted or split.

The density was startlingly low. Despite the regional scale and the overwhelming noise, the valley felt hollow, almost empty. There was almost nothing per volume; no trees to speak of, no continuous soil, no dense thickets. The lushness was a thin, vibrant veneer over vast expanses of bare, ancient rock. The few animals—rare, chitinous, and swift—were seen only as momentary blurs, skittering between the unstable patches of green.

Scattered across this chaotic stage were faint marks of a primitive presence. Single, crudely stacked stone windbreaks, no higher than a knee, appeared near the more stable depressions. Occasional artifacts—shards of simple, unglazed pottery, or a single, hand-knapped stone tool—were half-embedded in the rock, looking less like refuse and more like geological inclusions, swallowed by the turbulent ground over eons. These structures offered no comfort; they were merely temporary anchors against the storm, built with early-tool-use simplicity and abandoned just as quickly.

The world operated on a rule of frantic, unstable equilibrium. The light was normal, filtering through a perpetually shifting, damp haze, illuminating a scene of profound, unsettling energy. The valley was not dying; it was simply too busy being born and destroyed simultaneously to ever settle into true peace. It was an ancient heart, beating with a deafening, chaotic rhythm.
