**The Plateau of Old Winds**

Beyond the last ridge of the world, where the air grows too thin for sound to carry and the stars hang low enough to touch, lies a vastness that has forgotten how to change. The Plateau of Old Winds spreads across the roof of a dying continent—an endless stretch of bone-white stone that was once seabed, then mountain, then seabed again, until finally the waters retreated forever and left it to the silence.

The stone here is old beyond measurement. Wind and frost have worked it for eons into a labyrinth of knife-edged fins and rounded domes, all worn to the same final geometry. Nothing erodes further. The rock has settled into its ultimate form: a frozen topography of shallow basins and sudden spires, connected by bridges of crumbling limestone that ring like bells when struck but never break. The plateau's surface is a mosaic of hexagonal tiles, each fitted perfectly to its neighbors, as if the land itself grew tired of chaos and chose crystalline order.

No water falls here. No rivers run. The only moisture exists as a thin, permanent rime of frost that coats every surface—not ice from cold, but a mineral exudation, the stone's own slow sweat. This frost never melts, never thickens. It simply *is*, a permanent second skin that catches the light of two small moons and casts the whole landscape in a pale, sourceless glow.

The vegetation is sparse in count but strange in form. Here and there, rooted in cracks between the stone tiles, grow the **glass-trees**—transparent, branching structures of silica and trapped air, their canopies spread wide to catch what little carbon dioxide still drifts up from the valleys far below. They do not grow. They do not die. They simply *persist*, each one a perfect, unchanging sculpture of itself. Some bear fruit: hollow orbs of brittle crystal that ring when the wind moves through them, though no creature eats them.

The fauna are everywhere. This is the plateau's great contradiction: a dead, dry, frozen place that teems with life beyond counting. They come in forms that defy easy categorization—

The **wind-hounds**, lean and long-limbed, their fur the color of old snow, run in packs of thousands across the stone. They make no sound. Their paws strike the rock and produce nothing, not even vibration. They hunt the **sky-eels**, ribbon-like creatures that swim through the thin air itself, feeding on something invisible. The eels number in the millions, their bodies catching the moonlight in flashes of silver and black, forming rivers of motion above the still land.

The **stone-borers** are everywhere—crustacean-like beings the size of a man's thumb, their shells fused with the rock itself. They do not move. They have not moved for millennia. Yet they are alive, their slow metabolisms drawing sustenance from the stone, their collective breathing producing the plateau's only sound: a faint, continuous *hiss*, like dust settling.

And the **apex things**—creatures the size of hills, slow-moving, many-legged, their bodies covered in symbiotic forests of glass-trees. They migrate in circles, following paths worn into the stone over geological ages, their passage marking the only visible change in the landscape. Yet even they are caught in stasis: each circuit takes ten thousand years, and the paths they follow were carved by their ancestors, identical in every detail.

The scattered structures are few and enigmatic. A single tower of stacked stones, too regular to be natural, stands at the plateau's center. No tool marks. No mortar. Simply fitted. Inside: nothing. A chamber empty save for a single crystal of impossible geometry that hums at a frequency below hearing.

Elsewhere: broken rings of standing stones, their purpose forgotten. A cave mouth that exhales warm air at regular intervals, though nothing lives within. A field of metal spheres, each the size of a head, half-buried in the stone, arranged in a spiral pattern that matches no constellation now visible.

The light is ordinary—sun by day, moons by night—but it behaves strangely. Shadows fall in directions that do not match the sun's position. Some areas of the plateau are perpetually brighter than others, though no source explains the difference. At certain times, the entire landscape seems to *flicker*, as if reality itself were a poorly maintained image.

The silence is profound. Even the wind, when it comes, makes no sound. It is a wind that moves without moving, a pressure without force. One feels it only as a faint resistance, a suggestion that the air itself has grown too old to bother with motion.

Time here is not stopped—it is *finished*. The plateau has reached its final configuration and sees no reason to continue. The creatures live but do not age. The stone stands but does not weather. The light shines but does not warm. It is a landscape at equilibrium, a world that has solved itself and now exists only as a monument to its own completion.

To walk here is to feel the weight of that finality. The air is thin and cold and tastes of metal. The stone beneath one's feet is smooth as glass, worn by the passage of countless feet that no longer exist. The wind-hounds watch from ridges, their eyes reflecting light that is not there. The sky-eels writhe overhead, silent as thought.

And sometimes, if one stands very still and listens with something other than ears, the plateau *speaks*—not in words, but in a sudden, overwhelming certainty that this is how all things end: not with fire or ice, but with perfect, eternal stillness.
