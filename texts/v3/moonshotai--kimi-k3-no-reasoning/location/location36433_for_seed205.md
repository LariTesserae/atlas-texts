The Hangings are called that because no one who named them could see the bottom.

From the ledge where you stand, the world drops away in terraces — but "drops" isn't quite right, because the drop is so thick with growth that it reads more as a *tilt*. A slope of green so dense it becomes architectural. The vegetation doesn't sit on the mountain; it is the mountain, or has replaced it. Somewhere beneath the canopy, stone exists — you know this because the streams emerge white from under root-mass and the occasional cliff-face shows black through the green — but stone is a substrate, not a feature. What you see is plant. Plant stacked on plant, plant growing from plant, plant as floor and wall and ceiling, plant in forms that have no name in any language because nothing outside this valley ever needed them.

The heat is the first thing that isn't green. It presses down with a weight that feels personal, deliberate. Not the dry heat of a desert — this is wet heat, heat that condenses on your skin and drips from leaves that are not raining but *exhaling*. The air is thick enough to taste: chlorophyll and rot and something sweet, overripe, the smell of life happening faster than it can be contained. You breathe and the breath is work. Your lungs labor. The moisture hangs in the air as a presence, not a weather event — it's simply *there*, a property of the atmosphere like gravity or light.

Water is scarce in a way that seems impossible, given the density of green. But the plants hoard it. They have evolved — or been shaped — into vessels. Every broad leaf curls at its edges into a trough. Vines swell at their nodes into translucent bladders that slosh when the wind moves them. The water is *inside* the vegetation, locked in cellulose and resin, cycling through a closed system that has almost no runoff. The streams that do emerge are rare and sudden: a crack in a root-mass, a spill from an over-full bromeliad the size of a house, and for an hour a waterfall exists, thundering and white, before the plants absorb the overflow and close the wound. The rain that falls is brief and warm, more a condensation event than a storm — the air gets too heavy, releases, and ten minutes later the canopy has drunk it all.

---

You walk. This is possible, but slow. The "ground" is not ground. It is a mat of interwoven root and rhizome, springy, sometimes hollow, sometimes giving way to pockets of hot dark water that smell of sulfur. Your feet sink. The vegetation resists, then yields, then closes behind you. You leave no trail that lasts.

The scale of it disorients. The valley — if it's a valley — extends beyond what your eyes can resolve. The far wall is a green haze that might be ten miles distant or a hundred. Weather forms *inside* the space: clouds condense beneath the canopy, trapped by the heat ceiling, and move sluggishly between the emergent trees like something swimming. You see rain falling a mile away, a gray curtain that doesn't reach the floor because the canopy intercepts it, drinks it, and the rain simply stops existing partway down.

The trees — calling them trees is a courtesy — rise on trunks that are less wood than *accumulated intention*. They are braided, fused, grown together from multiple seeds into columns that widen at the base into buttresses fifty feet across. Their bark is not bark but a kind of scaled skin, photosynthetic, green-black, sweating sap that hardens into amber crusts. The canopy they support is not a ceiling but a *terrain*: you could walk on it, if you could reach it, if it could hold your weight, which it might, because it is dense enough in places that birds nest on it rather than in it, and whole ecosystems exist up there that never touch the floor.

Light comes down in fragments. The canopy eats most of it. What reaches the floor is green-tinted, diffuse, directionless — a perpetual dusk that pulses when wind moves the leaves above. You lose track of time. The sun is a rumor. Shadows don't exist in the normal sense; instead there are *densities* of shadow, gradients of dark, and the deepest places are not black but a luminous green-brown where fungi glow faintly and the air is coolest.

---

The sound hits you in layers.

First: the insects. Not a hum — a *roar*. A broadband wall of stridulation so constant and so loud that you stop hearing it after a while, the way you stop feeling your clothes. It is the background condition of the world. When it changes — when it spikes or drops — you notice, and your body tenses, because the insects are telling you something even if you don't know what.

Second: the birds. Not song. Calls. Screams, hoots, rattles, sounds that seem mechanically impossible, sounds that make you turn your head even though you've learned that turning your head does nothing, the bird is always somewhere else, the canopy throws sound sideways and down. Some of the calls are so loud they hurt. One of them — a low, throbbing *whump* that you feel in your sternum — repeats every eleven seconds for hours and then stops, and the silence it leaves is worse.

Third: the movement. The vegetation is not still. Leaves turn, tracking light. Vines shift, adjusting tension. Somewhere to your left, something large moves through the undergrowth, and the plants *accommodate* it — bending, lifting, reshaping — and you hear the rustle and creak of a body passing through a medium that is more fluid than solid. You don't see it. You never see it. But the sound of its passage is a slow, deliberate wave, and it goes on for a long time.

Fourth: the water. When it comes — a spill, a release, a brief stream — the sound is shockingly loud in the wet-dense air, a white noise that cuts through everything. You learn to move toward it. Water means a break in the mat, a place to drink, a place where the green relents.

---

The fauna is overwhelming not in number but in *presence*. You are aware of being surrounded. Watched. The density of life is so high that the concept of "wildlife" breaks down; there is no *life* separate from the place, only the place, and the place is alive at every scale.

Things move in the corner of your eye. Small things — beetles the size of your fist, iridescent, armored, carrying scraps of leaf or each other. Lizards that are nearly plant, green and slow, their skin textured like bark, their eyes the only sign of animality. Frogs — you hear them more than see them, a chorus that rises at dusk into a shrieking wall — that live in the water-bladders of the vines and have never touched ground.

Larger things leave signs. A frond stripped to its spine. A depression in the root-mat, wide as a dinner table, still warm. A smell — musk and ammonia and something floral — that makes your hindbrain scream *predator* even though you don't know what predator looks like here. You find a feather, black, eighteen inches long, with a barbed tip that suggests the bird it came from is not a bird you want to meet.

Once, at dusk, you see eyes. Not a pair — a *cluster*, low to the ground, reflecting the fungal glow. Six, maybe eight. They blink in sequence. You don't move. The eyes don't move. After a minute, they simply go out, one by one, and you hear nothing, not even a footfall, and the absence of sound is somehow worse than the eyes were.

---

There are structures.

You almost miss them. They are so overgrown, so *absorbed*, that they read as natural formations until you see the angle — a straight line where no straight line should be, a corner, a plane. A wall. Or what was a wall. The stone is soft, weathered, carved with patterns that the moss has filled in and made decorative. A doorway, lintel intact, leading into a root-choked darkness that smells of cold and old water. You don't go in.

Farther on: a platform, circular, thirty feet across, made of fitted stones that the roots have prised apart and reassembled into something between ruin and topiary. In the center, a basin. The basin holds water — real water, open to the air — and the water is black and still and reflects the canopy. Around the rim, carvings: figures, elongated, with too many joints, doing something that might be dancing or might be dying. The stone is warm. You put your hand on it and feel a vibration, faint, rhythmic, that might be the roots growing or might be something else.

You find three more platforms over the next days, all similar, all placed at the same elevation, as if marking a contour line. And once, higher up, where the canopy breaks and you can see the sky: a tower. Or the stub of one. Stone again, but different stone — harder, darker, less weathered. It leans. The top is gone, sheared off, and the break is old, smoothed by centuries of rain and root. From its height, you can see the valley — or the world — stretching away, and you understand for the first time that the green doesn't stop. It goes to the horizon and beyond. The tower was built to see this. Whoever built it wanted to know how far the green went. They built, and looked, and — you assume — stopped building, because the tower is the tallest thing you've found and it is not very tall, and it is broken, and no one has repaired it.

The tools you find are simple. A stone blade, hafted with resin and fiber, the edge still sharp. A basket, tightly woven, lined with clay, sitting in a dry hollow as if its owner set it down a moment ago. A fire-pit, cold, the charcoal ancient and friable. These things are scattered — rare — spaced so far apart that each one feels like a message rather than a possession. Someone was here. Someone moved through this place, slowly, carefully, and left almost nothing behind.

---

The weirdness is quiet. It doesn't announce itself. It accumulates.

You notice, after a while, that the insects' roar has a rhythm. Not a beat — a *pattern*, a rise and fall that repeats over hours, synchronized across the whole valley. You notice that the birds' calls, chaotic as they seem, sometimes converge — a dozen species falling silent at once, then resuming, as if a signal passed through them. You notice that the vegetation's movement is not always random: some vines curl toward you as you approach, and some leaves fold shut when you touch them, and once — you are sure of this — a root shifted under your foot not because you stepped on it but because it *moved*.

The water in the basin on the platform does not evaporate. You check it over three days. It stays at the same level. It is not being replenished. It simply *stays*.

The tower's stone is warm at night. Not sun-warm — the nights are hot regardless — but *warm*, warmer than the air, as if it generates heat. You put your ear to it and hear nothing, but feel that vibration again, the slow pulse, and this time it feels like a heartbeat.

The eyes you saw — the cluster — you see them again, twice, always at dusk, always at the same distance. They don't come closer. They don't go away. They *accompany*.

---

You don't know how long you stay. Time is slippery here, measured in water-spills and light-changes and the slow arc of the canopy's breathing. You learn to drink from the vine-bladders, tapping them with the stone blade. You learn to avoid the hollows in the root-mat, because the water in them is hot enough to scald. You learn that the insects' rhythm speeds up before rain and slows before the movement of something large.

You don't find people. You find their traces — a path, overgrown but discernible, worn into the root-mat by generations of feet, leading from platform to platform, contour to contour, always at the same elevation. You follow it for two days. It leads to a cliff-face where a waterfall once ran — you can see the stain, the smooth channel — and there, carved into the stone behind the dry fall, is a chamber. Small. Dry. Empty except for a single object: a bowl, stone, black, polished, sitting in the center of the floor. The bowl is full of water. The water is still. It reflects the ceiling, which is carved with the same elongated figures, but here they are not dancing or dying. Here they are *growing* — sprouting, branching, becoming the elongated forms of the very trees that hold this cliff together.

You don't drink the water. You leave the bowl where it is. You walk back out into the green, and the sound closes over you, and the heat settles onto your shoulders like a hand, and the eyes — somewhere, at some distance — open and close and open, patient, unblinking, watching you go.

The Hangings don't end. They simply continue, and you are in them, and the green is the world, and the world is alive, and it knows you are there.
