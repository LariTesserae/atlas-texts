# The Rootsea

There is no ground. There has never been ground. What lies beneath the surface here — what *is* the surface, if you are standing on it, which you are not, because standing implies a floor and the floor is a rumor — is a vast, descending, warm, vegetal churn that fills a region roughly the size of a drainage basin, extending laterally beyond any sightline and downward into depths that generate their own weather. The Rootsea. The Undergrowth. The Throat. The people who live inside it, and things live inside it, call it all three names depending on which stratum they occupy and how recently someone they knew was absorbed.

Begin at the top, though "top" is generous. The upper boundary of this place is a translucent membrane of fused leaf-matter, a living canopy-ceiling stretched taut over everything below like the skin of a blister. Sunlight — normal, unremarkable, mid-spectrum daylight — filters through this ceiling and arrives below as a shifting, mottled, green-gold haze. The light is ordinary in quality but strange in behavior: it does not fall so much as percolate, seeping down through layered vegetation that drinks it, processes it, exhales it again as a humid luminosity that has less to do with photons than with the collective metabolism of everything glowing faintly as it grows. By the time light reaches the middle depths it has been handled, chemically altered, passed through a million stomata. It arrives tired. It arrives the color of old brass. It arrives warm.

Warmth is the medium. The ambient temperature hovers somewhere in the range that human bodies find oppressive but survivable — wet heat, lung-coating heat, the kind that makes sweat useless because the air is already saturated. Not with water. There is no water here, not in any phase. The saturation is sap. Vaporized sap, aerosolized resin, the gaseous exhalations of billions of organisms that do not distinguish between respiration and communication. The air is a nutrient broth. You can breathe it, but you are also, in a small way, being fed. And in a smaller way, being tasted.

## The Vegetal Architecture

The vegetation is not *on* anything. It is the thing. There is no soil, no bedrock visible at any level, no mineral substrate beneath the organic mass. The entire volume of the Rootsea — its walls, its columns, its vaults and bridges and chambers — is composed of living tissue. Root systems thick as cathedral columns twist through the middle depths, not anchored to anything but each other, forming a structural lattice so dense that traversing it requires either following the natural gaps (which migrate) or cutting through (which provokes a response). The tissue ranges in character from soft, pulpy, mushroom-adjacent flesh in the younger regions to something closer to hardwood — to something past hardwood, to something with the density and grain of petrified wood that is nonetheless warm to the touch and, if you press your ear to it, audibly active. Fluids move through it. Not water. Oils, latexes, something that behaves like lymph. You can hear the peristalsis of a root-trunk the diameter of a house, pushing its chemistry upward, downward, laterally, the roots communicating with each other through pressure-waves in their own vasculature. The sound is low and wet and constant.

The emerging quality of this place — its rawness, its under-construction-ness — is not metaphorical. The Rootsea is *growing into itself*. It is young. The entire structure, vast as it is, has the feel of a thing not yet finished becoming what it will be. Vast trunks end in blunt, actively dividing tips, meristematic nubs the size of plazas, soft and pale and glistening with growth-hormones, pushing outward into space that was empty yesterday and will be occupied tomorrow. You can watch it happen. A new branch-buttress extrudes from a mature trunk at a rate visible to the naked eye — slow, yes, but *visible*, the way a fingernail grows if you are patient enough, except these are the size of buildings and the patience required is measured in hours, not months. The Rootsea creaks with its own becoming. It groans under the weight of what it is adding to itself. It pops and cracks and shifts. Sections that were navigable last week have been overgrown, absorbed, remodeled. The geography is a rough draft, rewritten daily.

This instability is not merely structural. The entire mass *churns*. Not like water — there is no water to churn — but like something between a slow landslide and a digestive event. The middle depths are in constant, granular motion. Rootlets writhe against each other with a friction that generates both heat and sound. Vines cinch tighter around things — around other vines, around cavities, around organisms, around the artifacts of whoever last tried to build something permanent here. The whole volume flexes, subtly but relentlessly, a tectonics of pure biology. If you lie still on a stable surface (a relative term) and close your eyes, you can feel the place *moving* beneath you, the way you can feel a city through the ground — except this is not traffic. This is growth. This is appetite.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing that destroys you.

Not metaphorically. Arriving in the Rootsea without preparation — without the wax-and-resin earplugs the inhabitants make, without the gradual acclimation over days — results in disorientation, nausea, and eventually a kind of cognitive dissolution. The sound is not loud in the way of a single loud thing. It is loud in the way of a *billion* things, each producing its own contribution to a total acoustic environment so dense, so layered, so informationally saturated that the human brain cannot parse it as signal and cannot dismiss it as noise and simply begins to drown.

The base layer is the plants themselves: the groaning, creaking, popping of growth and movement described above. Layered over this is the friction-sound of rootlet against rootlet, a million tiny rasping whispers that merge into a hiss like static, like frying oil, like rainfall on a surface that is not rain and is not falling. Layered over *this* is the vascular pulse — the push and release of fluids through trunks and stems, a sub-bass throb that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears, a heartbeat the size of geography.

And then the fauna.

The fauna is overwhelming in the way that the vegetation is total, which is to say: completely, and without remainder. Every niche, every surface, every cavity and crevice and fold of the vegetal architecture is occupied. Not by a few species. By an ecology so dense and so vertically integrated that the concept of "species" begins to blur — everything here seems to grade into everything else, not through any failure of taxonomy but through sheer proximity and interdependence. Things live *on* things that live *in* things that live *inside* the plants that are the world. The fauna is not a layer on top of the vegetation. It is a second vegetation, a mobile one, interpenetrating the first so thoroughly that separating them is like separating the warp from the weft of a fabric.

Insects — or things that occupy the insect-niche, because the resemblance is structural rather than taxonomic — move through the Rootsea in aggregations that behave like weather systems. Swarms of thumb-sized, chitin-plated creatures with iridescent wing-casings pour through gaps in the root-lattice like wind, their wingbeats merging into a drone that cycles through frequencies, sometimes harmonizing with the vascular bass-pulse in a way that produces standing-wave patterns — zones where the sound doubles, trebles, becomes a physical pressure that can stagger you. These swarms do not fly so much as *boil*. They are moved by pheromone gradients, by light seepage from above, by the opening and closing of the plant-structure's own apertures (stomatal vents the size of doorways, which inhale and exhale on a roughly forty-minute cycle, sucking in clouds of insects and exhaling them, altered, elsewhere).

Larger things crawl the trunks. Things with too many legs and not enough eyes, or the reverse. Things that are clearly predators, though what they prey upon shifts as the swarms shift. A cat-sized, furred, vaguely mustelid creature that hunts by vibration, pressing its entire ventral surface against a root-trunk to feel the footfalls of prey on the other side, then punching through the intervening tissue with forelimbs adapted into living augers. A thing like a mantis crossed with a trumpet-flower, rooted (literally — it has roots) to a junction-node, its head a bell-shaped vocal apparatus that emits clicks and pops used for echolocation, its forelimbs striking at anything the sound reveals. These are not rare. They are everywhere. Every surface crawls. Every shadow contains motion. Every moment, something is eating something else within arm's reach, and the sound of this — the clicking, the chittering, the wet tearing, the wing-buzz, the alarm-calls, the mating-calls, the distress-screams of things being consumed while still partly alive — merges with the plant-noise and the pulse-noise into a totality that is less a soundscape and more an acoustic *substance*, something you move through the way you move through water, except there is no water, and you cannot stop moving through it, and it does not stop.

## The People

Things live here that make things. Whether they are people depends on your tolerance.

They are small — the largest barely reaching a human chest — and they are adapted to the heat in ways that make them look partially cooked. Their skin is a deep, lacquered reddish-brown, thick with a waxy secretion that prevents the sap-laden air from clogging their pores. Their eyes are disproportionately large, fitted with a nictitating membrane that filters the green-gold light into something their brains can process. Their hands and feet are similar in structure — four digits, all opposable, each tipped with a pad of roughened tissue that grips vegetal surfaces with the intimacy of a gecko's toe. They move through the Rootsea not by walking on surfaces but by *climbing through volume*, treating the entire three-dimensional space as navigable, which it is, if you are small and strong and unafraid of the things that share it with you.

Their technology is primitive by the standards of metal-smithing civilizations, but sophisticated in the ways that matter here. They do not work stone — there is no stone. They do not smelt ore — there is no ore. What they have is the plant-tissue itself, and they have learned, over however many generations they have been here (they do not count; time here is measured in growth-cycles, in the molting of specific swarms, in the extrusion of new trunk-buttresses), to shape it. Not by cutting. By *asking*. They have identified the chemical signals — the pheromones, the auxin-analogues, the growth-factors — that the Rootsea uses to direct its own development, and they have learned to synthesize crude versions of these from the sap of specific tissues. They paint these chemicals onto surfaces and the surface *responds*. A trunk grows a shelf where they need a shelf. A rootlet bridge extends across a gap where they need a crossing. A chamber-wall thickens into a partition. It takes days. Sometimes weeks. It is not reliable — the Rootsea has its own intentions, and they often override the intentions of the people — but it works often enough to constitute a technology, a craft, a relationship with the substrate that is closer to negotiation than to engineering.

Their structures are small, scattered, half-grown things. Not buildings. Not rooms. Grafted platforms of hardened sap, shaped by chemical persuasion, encrusted with the cast-off exoskeletons of insects they have domesticated (or that have domesticated them — the relationship is ambiguous). These platforms cling to trunks at various depths, connected by bridges that are half rootlet, half woven tendon (harvested from the larger fauna, cured, twisted into cables that hum in the vascular bass-pulse like guitar strings). Some platforms are abandoned — reclaimed by the growth, swallowed, visible now only as faint bulges in a trunk-wall where a floor used to be. Some are occupied, their inhabitants visible as small, reddish shapes moving quickly through the warm gloom, their voices (they have voices — high, rapid, tonal, a language that uses frequency-shifts the way other languages use consonants) adding yet another thread to the acoustic weave.

They make tools. Scrapers of insect-chitin. Blades of hardened leaf-margin, the edge of certain fronds growing sharp enough to shave tissue. Containers grown, not built — a gourd-like organ persuaded to form on a vine, hollowed, sealed with resin. They make music, if that is the word: they tap on trunks with chitin hammers, sending percussion-signals through the vascular system to other platforms, other groups, a drumming communication that is partially practical (warnings, summonses, the location of a new food-source) and partially something else, something that might be art, or ritual, or simply a way of adding one's own voice to the roar and saying: *I am also here. I also contribute to the sound.*

## The Chemistry of No-Water

The absence of water is not merely an environmental parameter. It is a foundational fact that shapes every chemistry, every biology, every interaction in the Rootsea. Where water would be, there is sap — a complex, lipid-based fluid that serves the same solvent-functions but behaves differently. It is viscous where water is fluid. It is flammable where water is not (and fire, here, is a terror beyond articulation — the entire world is fuel). It conducts sound differently, carrying the vascular pulse with greater efficiency than water would, which is part of why the place is so deafening — the sound propagates through the very substance of the world with minimal loss.

Fauna do not drink. They absorb. Through skin, through spiracles, through specialized membranes that filter the sap-vapor from the air. The people do the same, though they also consume concentrated sap-nodules — swollen organs on certain vines that store sugars and lipids in a gel that tastes, to the human palate (which has not evolved for this), like nothing describable, like the idea of sweetness translated into a language the tongue almost understands.

The air is not humid, because humidity implies water. It is *saturated* — thick with volatile organic compounds, terpenes, resin-vapors, the aerosolized byproducts of a billion metabolic processes. It has a taste. Breathing it is like inhaling a meal. Prolonged exposure alters the lung-lining, thickens it, changes its chemistry. Visitors who stay too long begin to find normal air thin, unsatisfying, unnourishing. They begin to miss the taste. This is the first step in a process that the inhabitants recognize and do not discuss: the process by which a visitor becomes a resident, and a resident becomes something else.

## The Depths

Below the middle depths — below the inhabited zone, below the layer where the light has faded to a bronze dusk — the Rootsea continues. Downward. The trunks thicken. The gaps between them narrow. The temperature rises, not from any geological heat-source but from the sheer metabolic output of the mass itself, billions of organisms respiring in proximity, generating warmth the way a compost pile generates warmth, except the compost pile is the size of a landscape and it is alive and it is *growing*. The sound changes character here — the higher frequencies attenuate, filtered out by the intervening tissue, and what remains is the deep pulse, the bass-throb of the vascular system, a sound so low and so powerful that it becomes indistinguishable from motion. You are not hearing the Rootsea at this depth. You are being vibrated by it.

Strange things happen at depth. The growth-tips — those pale, meristematic nubs — are more numerous here, and less directed. They do not grow toward light (there is none) or toward space (there is little). They grow toward each other, fusing, forming knots of tissue that serve no apparent structural function. These knots sometimes develop internal cavities. These cavities sometimes contain things. Not things that were placed there — things that *formed* there, the way a pearl forms in an oyster, except the oyster is the world and the pearl is a new organism, or a new structure, or a new organ whose function is not yet apparent and may never be. The Rootsea is experimenting with itself. It is generating novelties. Most of them fail — they are absorbed, dissolved, reabsorbed into the general metabolism. Some persist. Some, persisting, begin to grow.

The people do not go to these depths. They have stories about what is down there, and the stories do not agree, except in one respect: they all end with something *emerging*. Something new. Something the Rootsea made that it had not made before, and that the people had not anticipated, and that changed — is changing — will change — everything.

## The Turbulence

The dynamism of this place is not seasonal. It is not cyclical in any way that the inhabitants have mapped. It is constant, chaotic, and total. The Rootsea is in a state of permanent creative upheaval — new growth extruding into old growth, old structures collapsing under the weight of new additions, swarms of fauna re-routing through gaps that did not exist an hour ago, the sound-shapes shifting as the acoustic geometry of the space changes, the light-patterns rearranging as the canopy-ceiling thickens or thins or develops new apertures that send shafts of raw daylight lancing into depths that have not seen unfiltered light in weeks.

Standing in one place — on one of the grafted platforms, say, holding a tendon-cable with both hands while the platform shifts beneath you (it is always shifting; the trunk it is grafted to is always growing, always adjusting, always pulling the platform a centimeter this way, a centimeter that) — you can watch the change happen. A gap you used yesterday is closed today. A new one has opened. Something large moves through the new gap — a grazer, a thing the size of a draft horse, its hide plated with symbiotic moss (which is itself a miniature ecosystem), its breath a visible plume of processed sap-vapor, its passage sending shock-waves through the rootlets that travel outward in concentric rings, disturbing swarms, triggering alarm-calls, altering the local soundscape for minutes afterward. The people on the platform watch it pass without alarm. It is not dangerous. It is simply *present*, the way weather is present, the way geology is present, except geology does not usually have eyes.

The turbulence is not violence, though violence is common (everything here eats everything else, and the eating is constant and close and loud). The turbulence is *generativity*. The Rootsea is making itself, unmaking itself, remaking itself, at a pace and a scale that overwhelms any individual participant. It is an anthill the size of a landscape, except the ants are also the hill. It is a bazaar where every stall is also a customer and every customer is also a product. It is a conversation in which every speaker is also the language being spoken.

## The Feeling of the Place

To be in the Rootsea is to be *inside* something that is also inside you. The air you breathe is the exhalation of the world. The surface you stand on is the body of the world. The sound you hear is the voice of the world, and it is deafening, and it does not stop, and after a time — an hour, a day, a week — you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as something else, something closer to thought, something closer to the noise a mind makes when it is thinking too many things at once, when it is generating ideas faster than it can articulate them, when it is in the throes of a creative fever that will not break.

The warmth holds you. The sap-thick air feeds you. The light, tired and green and golden, settles on your skin like a hand. Something moves in the peripheral vision — always, something is always moving — and you turn, and it is a rootlet, growing, pushing through a gap, reaching for a surface it has not yet touched, and you watch it happen, and it is slow, and it is relentless, and it is the most ordinary thing in this place, and it is the strangest thing you have ever seen.

There is no silence here. There is no stillness. There is no water, no stone, no sky you can see without mediation. There is only the warm, living, growing, sounding mass of the Rootsea, and the things that live inside it, and the things it is becoming, and the things it is making, and the things it will make next, which no one — not the people, not the fauna, not the Rootsea itself, if the Rootsea has a self, which it might, which it probably does, which the people suspect and do not say — can predict.

It is not finished. It may never be finished. It is *becoming*, and the becoming is the loudest thing you have ever heard.
