The plateau rises too far for the air. Up here the atmosphere thins into a mineral exhalation — faintly blue, faintly metallic, cold enough that breath would freeze into beads if there were lungs. There are none.

Instead of water, the world exudes resin.

It climbs from the ground in slow threads: translucent filaments that thicken over centuries into trunks, ridges, bulbs. In the dim half-light (the sun is a pale seam along the horizon, a diluted glow that never truly rises), the vegetation has evolved to capture energy differently. Not leaves, but membranes — thin glassy sheets stretched between branching crystalline stems. They do not photosynthesize so much as refract: they split the scarce light into spectral tremors, storing some frequencies as internal lattice vibrations.

From a distance, the plateau looks like a valley of unfinished stained glass.

Up close, it is embryonic.

The ground is raw geology: basalts and pale salts still steaming from formation, ridges sharp as if extruded yesterday, though “yesterday” here means a million years. The high elevation is not a mountain — it is the crust lifting in a slow, continuous bloom. One can see it in the way the horizon bends upward, in the faint shear lines where strata are uncoiling like a muscle learning to move.

Everything is under construction. Even space.

The weirdness is not decorative. It is structural.

At certain elevations, the air folds. A step forward may carry you three meters sideways. Distances smear in dimness — two towers of resin that appear adjacent are separated by kilometers; a lone shard you could reach in minutes turns out to be a silhouette on another ridge entirely. The eye adjusts slowly, recalibrating depth.

Sound contradicts the sparse geometry.

It is deafening.

Not loud in the human sense — there are no animals to scream, no wind howling — but resonant. The crystalline vegetation vibrates at thresholds that hammer the inner ear even through vacuum-thin atmosphere. Harmonics stack. The plateau hums in sub-bass layers that rattle bone and enamel. Every few decades, a crystalline trunk completes a growth phase and snaps microscopically, sending a detonation across the valley — a sonic bloom that rolls and rolls and rolls, amplified by the curvature of rising land.

You do not hear with ears here. You hear through teeth. Through sternum. Through the soft tissue behind the eyes.

Structures exist — small, primitive, unmistakably intentional.

Low, circular foundations of fitted stone cling to sheltered alcoves. No metal beyond simple beaten alloys, dark and pitted. They were built by hands that understood compression and balance, not engines. Their walls are thick and inward-leaning, as if resisting pressure from within. Some are partially absorbed by resin growth — the vegetation threading through masonry, slowly dissolving mineral boundaries into translucent lattice.

No one remains.

Perhaps they left when the resonance intensified.

Perhaps they became part of the construction.

The fauna parameter reads zero, yet there are movements that suggest otherwise. Not bodies — but organized shifts. A membrane flexing in coordinated rhythm across an entire grove. A cluster of crystalline stems aligning simultaneously to catch the low seam of sunlight. No metabolism as you would recognize it. No chase. No predation.

Only alignment.

The place is sparse. You can walk for hours between clusters of growth. Between foundations. Between anomalies. The emptiness is deliberate, architectural. The plateau uses space as structure — the gaps amplify the sound; the distances bend the light.

Time moves slowly here, but not evenly.

Some resin trunks grow a handspan per century. Others erupt upward in a season when the crust flexes. Erosion is embryonic — wind barely exists, and there is no liquid to carve channels. Instead, decay occurs as vibration fatigue: crystal fracturing along invisible stress lines, collapsing into glittering powder that drifts in the thin air and settles into new embryonic seams.

The most disorienting feature is the horizon.

Because the land continues to rise, the horizon is not a line but a process. It climbs imperceptibly during your stay. Stars shift. The pale seam of sun slides sideways. You realize after days that the plateau is not a surface but a slow deformation of spacetime — a wrinkle being pulled upward through reality’s fabric.

At the highest point — if “highest” holds meaning — there is a single structure larger than the rest. Not a temple. Not a machine. A lattice frame of primitive stone and early alloy, arranged in concentric incomplete rings. The center is empty.

Yet standing within it, the deafening resonance resolves.

For one moment, the harmonics align.

And you understand that the entire plateau is an instrument.

Not finished.

Not tuned.

Still forming.

Still learning what kind of sound it is meant to make.
