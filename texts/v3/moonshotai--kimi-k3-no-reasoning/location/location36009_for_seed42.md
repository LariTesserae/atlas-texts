They call it **The Plummet**—though no one knows who gave it the name, only that the word tastes like copper and old breath.

You approach from above, first. From the broken spine of the continent, where the air is thin and cold enough to ache in the teeth. The altitude here is deceptive; you are already three thousand meters above the sea, but the landscape falls away into a wound that descends another two thousand more. This is not a valley. It is a **puncture**—a vast, vertical throat carved by nothing as simple as water or time, but by the slow, grinding insistence of roots and stone.

And it is full. Full to the brim with liquid, though not the kind you know.

The Plummet is a **cathedral of brine**. Imagine a single, immense chamber—roughly circular, perhaps eight kilometers across—whose walls are sheer cliffs of black basalt and weeping limestone. These walls do not rise into open air; they rise into a perpetual, vertical rain. Water falls from the fractured clouds above, but it falls *upward* as often as down, caught in thermal currents that spiral through the chasm like breath through a flute. The water never settles. It hangs in veils, in sheets, in sudden horizontal rivers that flow perpendicular to gravity, suspended by the updrafts. You could stand on the edge and watch rain fall sideways for an hour, or watch a droplet the size of a house drift upward, rotating slowly, catching the grey light.

But this is not empty weather. The water is thick with life.

The vegetation has not merely colonized the space; it has **become the medium**. The entire column of the Plummet—every cubic meter of that suspended brine—is infiltrated by *thalloid* masses: translucent, branching structures that are neither algae nor moss nor tree, but something that shares their hunger. They are pale, bioluminescent, the color of old bone and glacier ice. They grow not upward but *outward*, forming vast, floating continents of living tissue that drift in the updrafts. These are the **Gardens of the Fall**, and they are sparse—separated by kilometers of empty, watery void—but where they exist, they are total. There is no soil here, no rock that is not already coated in root. The plants have eaten the architecture of the place.

And architecture there is. This is a city, though built by hands that never knew iron.

Descending into the Plummet—down the treacherous, ice-slick stairs carved into the western wall—you come to **Hollow Tier**, the first of the settlements. It is built into the cliff face, yes, but also *into* the vegetation. The inhabitants—humanoid, wrapped in woven fibers that never seem wet—have domesticated the thalloids. They cultivate them, shape them, grow them into bridges that span between floating islands of denser plant-matter. Their buildings are not constructed so much as *persuaded*: hollowed-out knots in the living mass, lined with dried fronds, warmed by composting heat. The technology is stone-age, bone-age, fiber-age—obsidian blades, knotted ropes of incredible strength, pulleys made from the hollow bones of great birds. Yet the complexity is urban: aqueducts of woven reed channel the falling water into cisterns; hanging gardens of bioluminescent fungi illuminate the perpetual twilight; a hundred bridges of living root connect the scattered habitats.

The sound here is a physical force. The Plummet acts as a resonator. The water striking the plant-masses, the wind through the vertical shaft, the calls of the fauna—all of it is amplified, folded back on itself, until the air itself seems to shiver with a low, constant roar. It is not chaotic noise; it is **structured cacophony**. The falling water hits different densities of vegetation and produces notes—deep, percussive booms from the thick mats, high, hissing sprays from the fronds. The inhabitants have learned to navigate by this sound-map, to tell weather and time by the pitch of the roar. When the wind shifts, the entire chasm seems to inhale, and the volume doubles, triples, until your bones vibrate in sympathy.

The fauna are loud, and they are everywhere. **Glider-apes** with membranes of stretched skin leap between the floating islands, their calls sharp as cracking ice. **Filter-whales**—vast, slow beasts the size of cottages—drift through the suspended water, mouths open, sieving the brine for plankton, their songs long and mournful and felt in the chest rather than heard. In the deeper, darker reaches where the light fails, **pale eels** with external gills like red feathers hunt in packs, their shrieks piercing through the liquid. And everywhere, **moss-skaters**: small, six-legged creatures that walk on the surface tension of the falling droplets, their feet creating ripples that sing at frequencies just below hearing.

The light is normal—grey, diffuse, neither bright nor dark. It filters down through the water-veils and the floating gardens, refracted into shifting caustics that paint everything in moving patterns. It is cool light, the color of deep winter, though the temperature is merely chilly, not freezing. The cold here is a constant, a presence, seeping into the stone and the bone.

Time moves slowly here. The erosion is ancient, established. The walls of the Plummet have been worn smooth by millennia of this vertical rain, carved into scalloped patterns that look like the folds of a giant’s brain. Nothing changes quickly. The gardens drift in cycles that take decades to complete. The settlements grow by accretion, layer upon layer, each generation adding a new chamber, a new bridge, until the structures look like geological formations rather than buildings. A rope bridge might be a century old, its fibers replaced strand by strand by careful hands, until not one original thread remains, yet the bridge persists, continuous, patient.

Weirdness permeates the place, subtle but undeniable. The water falls up. The plants glow with a light that has no source. Sometimes, if you stand on the lowest tier and look up, you can see the sky reflected in the pooled water of a floating garden—a perfect mirror of clouds and light—except that the reflection moves slightly *before* the real sky does, as if the water is remembering the future.

The rules of this world are simple and rigid:

1. **Nothing heavy stays aloft.** If it is not buoyed by gas, by root, by the updraft, it falls. And falling here is not a clean drop; it is a slow, tumbling descent through layers of vegetation and water, often taking hours, sometimes days, before the object reaches the unseen bottom.

2. **Everything is connected.** The thalloid masses, though separate, share a single root system that spans the entire chasm. Damage one, and the others feel it. The inhabitants know this. They harvest with reverence, with ritual, cutting only what is already dying.

3. **Sound is currency.** To move through the Plummet, you must speak it. The inhabitants use complex whistled languages that carry through the roar, and they build instruments—great horns of hollowed wood, drums of stretched hide—that resonate with the natural frequencies of the chasm. A wrong note can cause a cascade, a sympathetic vibration that shakes loose debris or panics the fauna.

This is a place of **saturated emptiness**. Despite the abundance of life, there is space—vast, echoing space—between the islands of matter. You can float in the center of the Plummet, suspended in a bubble of updraft, and see nothing but grey light and falling water in every direction, for kilometers. The settlements are sparse archipelagos in this liquid sky, each one a node of warmth and noise and smell in the vast, cool silence.

The people of Hollow Tier and the deeper settlements—**The Root-Clutch**, **The Ninth Veil**, **The Salting**—live lives of vertical intimacy. They are born to the sound of the fall, and they die to it. They farm the glider-apes, hunt the filter-whales, harvest the bioluminescent fungi for light that never burns. They do not dream of the surface, of the dry land above, because for them, there is only the Plummet. The world is a throat, and they are the voice it makes.

And the Plummet speaks. Constantly. Loudly. In a language of water and stone and living tissue, it says: *I am here. I am deep. I am full. I am falling. I am falling. I am falling.*
