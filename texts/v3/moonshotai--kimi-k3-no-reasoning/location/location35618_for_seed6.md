# THE VAT OF VERDANT ASCENT

---

## I. APPROACH

There is no shore. There is no approach. You are simply inside it, and you have always been inside it, and the memory of a before is something the water has already begun to edit out of you.

But if we must begin somewhere, begin here: a liquid column rising through air — vast, self-contained, held vertical by forces that are part intention and part habit. It is not a waterfall. Waterfalls move. It is not a column in any architectural sense. Columns are stone. It is a standing body of warm water, roughly the footprint of a small village — say, three hundred meters across at its widest and slightly narrower at top and bottom — extending upward from a floor you cannot see to a ceiling you cannot see, and both of those boundaries, when you finally perceive them, will turn out to be made of things that are not what floors and ceilings are usually made of.

The water is warm. Not bathwater warm. Warmer than that. The warmth of blood, of fermentation, of compost working in the dark. It is the kind of warmth that feels, at first, like being held, and then, after a longer time, like being digested, and then, after a longer time still, like being *remembered* — as if the water is not surrounding you but recalling you, pulling the fact of your body back into a solution it was always part of.

The light is ordinary. This is the strangest thing. You expect — in a submerged world, in a structure this peculiar — some quality of light that announces itself: cathedral gloom, bioluminescent shimmer, the green-black press of oceanic depth. Instead the light is the light of a slightly overcast afternoon. Pale. Even. Sourceless in the way that light inside dense matter can seem sourceless, arriving from everywhere and from no single point, casting shadows that are soft and directionless, the kind of shadow that pools beneath an object rather than stretching away from it. If there is a sun, it is doing its work through a great deal of water and a great deal of filtration, and what reaches you here has been processed, moderated, *civilized* — turned into the luminous equivalent of a well-lit room.

It is quiet. The quiet is not the absence of sound but the presence of a single sound so low and so constant that it has become indistinguishable from silence: the hum of water moving through machinery, or machinery moving through water, or — here the distinction collapses — the sound of the system *being what it is*, the way a standing stone emits a silence that is particular to stone. Beneath the hum, if you wait, if you let your ears adjust over hours (you have hours; everything here has hours), you begin to hear smaller sounds: the click and tick of small mechanical processes, the soft percussive snap of plant matter releasing gas, the faintest possible creak of structural elements accommodating load. All of it muffled. All of it underwater. Sound here travels beautifully — water is a better medium than air — but there is almost nothing generating sound to begin with, so what arrives at your ears does so with a terrible clarity, like a word whispered in an empty cathedral.

You are breathing. This should not be possible, and the fact that it is possible is the first indication — if you are paying attention — that the rules here are not the rules you brought with you. The water carries oxygen in quantities and in a form that your lungs can accept, or your lungs have been altered, or there is no *you* and *water* anymore in the way you assumed, and all of these explanations are equally valid and equally incomplete, and the water does not care which one you choose.

## II. THE STRUCTURE

Everything here is built.

This must be said plainly, and then it must be said again in more complicated ways, because the fact of it is easy to state and almost impossible to fully grasp.

There is no stone. There is no soil. There is no substrate of any natural kind. If you descend — and you can descend; the water does not resist movement, only slows it, and the warmth makes slowness feel like grace — you will not find bedrock. You will find a floor, yes, but it is a floor of dark alloy, smooth and slightly yielding, with the texture of hard rubber and the temperature of the water around it, as if the material has been in this warmth so long it has adopted it. The floor is not flat. It undulates in broad, slow swells, like the surface of a sea frozen mid-swell, except it is not frozen — it is *designed* this way, and the swells serve a purpose, and that purpose has to do with the movement of water along the base, the creation of slow currents, the prevention of stagnation. The floor is a landscape. It was made to be a landscape. Someone decided what this landscape would be.

From the floor, the walls rise. But "walls" is the wrong word. "Walls" implies a surface, a boundary, a plane that separates inside from outside. What rises around you is more like a *density* — a gradual thickening of structure, a compression of manufactured material that begins as open latticework and ends as solid mass. The transition is gradual enough that you cannot say where interior ends and wall begins. At the edges of your vision, the water becomes fuller of things — struts, cables, conduits, panels, housings, joints, flanges, ribs — until the water itself is the minority presence, and what you are looking at is a machine that has water running through it the way a body has blood.

The architecture — if we can call it architecture, and we can, because it is designed and it encloses space and it has aesthetic intention — is industrial in character but not industrial in the way of smokestacks and assembly lines. It is industrial the way a coral reef is industrial: every element is functional, every element is produced, and the total effect is one of overwhelming, saturated, almost ecstatic complexity. There are pipes the width of your arm and pipes the width of a cathedral nave. There are catwalks of dark composite that spiral around the interior at intervals, connected by ladders that go up and up and up beyond visibility. There are valve housings the size of houses. There are jointed arms — articulated, multi-segmented, tipped with tools whose purposes you cannot guess — that emerge from the walls at irregular intervals, motionless, poised, waiting for instructions that may have been issued centuries ago or may arrive tomorrow. There are panels of frosted translucent material that glow faintly from within, backlit by the light that comes from everywhere, so that they seem less like windows and more like *decisions the light has made* about where to concentrate.

All of it is warm. All of it is wet. All of it has been here for what feels like a very long time, and yet — and this is the paradox that defines the place — all of it looks *new*. Not new as in recently manufactured. New as in *not yet eroded*. The surfaces are sharp. The edges are crisp. The joints fit together with the precision of things that have not yet been worn down by time, chemistry, friction, use. The bolts have not corroded. The seals have not cracked. The paint — and there is paint, in places, industrial coatings in dark greens and ochres and a surprising slate blue — has not peeled. This is a built environment in the early stages of its existence, still in the condition its makers intended, before entropy has begun its slow negotiation.

This newness is unsettling. You are accustomed to built environments that show their age — patina, rust, wear, the evidence of habitation. Here there is no evidence of habitation. There is no evidence of anything having ever *happened*. The place is in the condition of having just been completed, and it is waiting, and the waiting has a quality of patience that is either reassuring or terrifying, depending on how you feel about being inside a machine that has not yet been switched on.

Or perhaps it has been switched on. Perhaps what you perceive as stillness is simply a speed too slow for you to notice.

## III. THE GREEN

Against this dark industrial body — and *inside* it, and *through* it, and in some places *replacing* it — the vegetation grows.

It is everywhere. This is not an exaggeration. There is no surface in this place that does not host some form of plant life, and the plant life is not incidental, not decorative, not opportunistic in the way of weeds in a sidewalk crack. It is *structural*. It is *load-bearing*. It is part of the design.

The dominant growth — the growth that defines the visual character of the place, that fills the water column from floor to ceiling with its presence — is a kind of vascular plant that has no precise terrestrial analogue. Imagine something between kelp and bamboo and a nervous system: tall, vertical, segmented stalks that rise from anchor points on the floor and the lower walls, ascending through the water in straight lines or gentle curves, branching at intervals into finer and finer structures, until the terminal branches are the thickness of hairs and the water around them is clouded with their delicacy. The stalks are green, but it is a green that contains other colors: a green with bronze in it, a green with black in it, a green that in certain angles of the diffuse light appears almost gold. The surfaces are smooth but not featureless — they have a fine ribbing, a longitudinal texture, like the bark of a young tree or the skin of a deep-sea creature, and the ribbing catches the light and holds it, so that each stalk appears to be faintly illuminated from within, not by bioluminescence but by the simple physics of curved wet surfaces in diffuse light.

The stalks are packed together so densely that in many places you cannot see between them. They form thickets, groves, walls of living material that divide the water column into chambers and corridors and vertical shafts, so that the interior space — already complex with its industrial armature — becomes labyrinthine, layered, a space of infinite partial concealments. You can always see *something*. You can never see *everything*. There is always another stalk, another cluster of fronds, another mechanical housing wrapped in root-like growths, partially occluding the view beyond. The place has the quality of a forest in fog: intimate, enclosed, full of the sense that there is always more just beyond what you can perceive.

The smaller plants fill the gaps. There are floating forms — delicate, translucent, gelatinous — that drift in the slow currents between the larger stalks, pulsating faintly, their internal structures visible as darker filaments within clear tissue. There are encrusting forms that coat every horizontal surface in velvety layers of dark emerald, so that the catwalks and housings and structural beams appear to have been dipped in moss. There are filamentous forms that hang from the upper structure in long curtains, swaying with a slowness that makes them seem more like the movement of light than the movement of matter. There are small round forms — spherical, hollow, the size of a thumb — that cluster in the crevices between pipe joints and valve housings, and each one contains a bubble of gas at its center, and the bubble catches the light, and the effect is of scattered points of brightness throughout the darker recesses of the place, like stars embedded in green flesh.

None of this is natural. All of it was placed here. Or bred here. Or grown from stock that was designed. The plants are as manufactured as the pipes, as intentional as the catwalks. They serve functions — you can see this, even if you cannot identify the functions. The large stalks are arranged in patterns that correspond to the structural elements beneath them: they cluster at joints, they align with conduits, they thicken where the architecture thickens, as if they are *reinforcing* it, or *insulating* it, or *communicating* with it. The encrusting forms on the catwalks have a regularity to their distribution that is not the regularity of ecological succession but the regularity of a planting schedule. The floating forms move in patterns that correspond to the currents, and the currents are generated by the machinery, and so the plants are, in effect, being *circulated* — distributed throughout the system by the same processes that move the water.

The boundary between plant and machine is not always clear. In places, you see a stalk emerging from what appears to be a socket in a metal panel, and the socket is too precise, too well-fitted, to be anything but designed. In places, you see a pipe that has become a stem — the metal gradually giving way to organic tissue over a transition zone of several inches, so that you cannot say where the manufactured ends and the grown begins. In places, you see what appears to be a valve that is also a flower: a circular housing with radiating elements that are simultaneously petals and control surfaces, and it opens and closes on a cycle so slow you would have to watch it for days to confirm the motion.

This is the character of the place: not nature reclaiming a built environment, not a built environment incorporating nature as an afterthought, but a *fusion* — a system in which the distinction between the grown and the made has been deliberately and thoroughly dissolved.

## IV. THE VERTICAL

You are high up. You know this the way you know things in dreams: without evidence, without explanation, with certainty.

Perhaps it is the way the light changes as you ascend. The diffuse, even illumination of the lower levels gradually acquires directionality as you rise, until — near the top, where the water column narrows — the light is coming clearly from above, and it is brighter, and the water is clearer, and you can look up and see the surface.

The surface is far. Much farther than you expected. The water column rises through a distance that is difficult to estimate — the density of vegetation and structure obscures long sightlines — but it feels, in your body, in the pressure of the water, in the quality of the light, like a significant climb. Not a mountain. Not a tower. Something in between. The height of a very tall building, or a very small cliff, or the vertical dimension of a place that was designed to make you feel the difference between *below* and *above* as a physical fact rather than an abstraction.

As you ascend, the structure changes. The industrial elements become less massive and more refined — the pipes narrower, the catwalks more delicate, the joints more intricate. It is as if the architecture is attenuating, becoming more precise and less blunt, responding to the decreasing water pressure and the increasing light. The vegetation changes too. The great stalks of the lower levels give way to finer, more branched forms, and the color shifts from deep bronze-green to a brighter, more luminous green, almost chartreuse in places. The encrusting forms on the walls thin out, replaced by small, structured growths that have a deliberate, almost geometric arrangement — rows, spirals, grids of tiny plants mounted on the structural elements like components on a circuit board.

And then, near the top, you find the ceiling.

It is not a ceiling. It is a *membrane*. A vast, taut, translucent sheet that stretches across the top of the water column, separating the water below from — what? Air? Sky? Another volume of water? You cannot tell. The membrane is milky, semi-opaque, and it transmits light the way a frosted glass roof transmits light: abundantly, evenly, without revealing what is on the other side. It ripples faintly, a slow undulation that suggests wind or pressure differential on the far side, and the rippling sends subtle waves down through the water, and the plants sway in response, and the whole system breathes — once every few minutes, a long, slow, tidal respiration.

The surface of the water, just beneath the membrane, is still. Not frozen, not rigid — still in the way of things that have reached equilibrium. The interface between water and membrane is precise and unbroken, a perfect plane (or rather, a perfect gentle curve, following the larger curvature of the structure), and if you rise to it and press your hand against it, you feel: warmth, the faintest resistance, and — through the membrane, dimly, like a heartbeat through a wall — a *vibration*.

Something is on the other side. Something is operating. Something is generating the light, maintaining the temperature, driving the slow currents, running the system. You cannot see it. You may never see it. But you can feel it, and its presence gives the entire place a quality of *being housed within something larger*, of being a component in a machine whose full extent you cannot perceive.

## V. THE LOWER REACHES

Descend.

Go past the great stalks, past the catwalks and the valve housings and the jointed arms. Go deeper, into the lower third of the column, where the light is slightly dimmer and the warmth is slightly greater and the density of structure is so extreme that the water seems almost incidental — a thin film of liquidity over an underlying solidity.

Here the floor swells upward in its engineered undulations, and the machinery is massive, and the plants are thick and dark, and the combination of the two creates a landscape of extraordinary complexity and extraordinary claustrophobia. The passages between structural elements are narrow — some barely wide enough to swim through — and they wind and branch and double back, and the walls of the passages are simultaneously metal and plant, alloy and tissue, and the distinction between the two has become so blurred that you begin to think of them as a single material: *grown metal*, or *manufactured flesh*, or something for which you have no word.

The machinery here is older-looking, though not actually older — everything in this place is equally new, equally uneroded. But the *style* is different: heavier, more brutal, more concerned with force and less concerned with precision. Great cylindrical housings, ribbed with cooling fins, squat against the floor like the boilers of some vast engine. Pipes thick as tree trunks run horizontally through the space, disappearing into the walls and re-emerging meters away, their routes determined by logic you cannot follow. There are wheels — actual wheels, toothed, metal, the size of mill wheels — embedded in the floor and walls, motionless, their teeth meshing with the teeth of adjacent wheels in an arrangement that suggests a gear train of monumental scale, designed to transmit force from one part of the system to another, and the fact that they are not turning does not mean they are broken; it means they are *waiting*, or the force they transmit is so slow that a single revolution might take a year.

Among the machinery, in the narrow passages, the plants grow thickest. The floor is carpeted with the encrusting form — a dense, springy, deep-pile growth that gives under your hands like a living mattress. The great stalks here are so closely packed that they form a near-solid wall of green, and the light that filters through them arrives as scattered fragments, coins of brightness on the dark floor, shifting as the stalks sway. The floating forms are more numerous here, drifting through the passages in slow processions, their gelatinous bodies bumping gently against the walls and against each other, and when two of them collide, they stick — briefly — and then separate, and the separation makes a sound: a soft, wet, almost inaudible *click*, and this sound, repeated at intervals throughout the lower reaches, is the closest thing the place has to a heartbeat.

There is a smell. You did not notice it at first — you were too busy noticing that you could breathe underwater — but it is here, and it is distinct: a warm, sweet, vegetal smell, like a greenhouse in summer, like compost steaming in morning light, like the interior of a fruit that has just been opened. It is not unpleasant. It is not entirely pleasant, either. It is the smell of processes — biological, chemical, mechanical — occurring at close range, and it is *thick*, and it fills your lungs with each breath, and after a while it becomes the smell of the place itself, the olfactory signature of a world where growth and industry are the same thing.

## VI. THE ABSENCE

There are no animals.

This absence is total and deliberate. No fish. No crustaceans. No insects. No birds, obviously, but also no worms, no snails, no microbial mats that suggest animal grazing. The plants grow unbrowsed, undamaged, uneaten. Every surface they occupy is intact — no bite marks, no trampling, no trails of slime. The encrusting forms on the catwalks are pristine. The floating forms drift unmolested. The great stalks rise without a single notch in their ribbed flanks.

The effect of this absence is subtle but pervasive. The place feels *curated* — not in the sense of a garden, where animals are excluded by fences and vigilance, but in the sense of a system designed from the ground up to function without them. The plants do not need pollination. The chemical cycles do not require decomposition by animal gut. The food web — if you can call it that — is a closed loop of photosynthesis (or whatever process generates energy here), mechanical circulation, and controlled decay, and it is *complete* without the participation of anything that moves by volition.

You notice this first as a visual fact: nothing darts, nothing scurries, nothing flees your approach. Then you notice it as an auditory fact: no buzzing, no chirping, no clicking of mandibles or fluttering of fins. The silence — already deep, already near-total — has a *thoroughness* to it that you now recognize as the silence of a world without animals, a silence that is not the pause before a sound but the permanent condition of a system that has no use for the kinds of sounds animals make.

And then you notice it as an emotional fact: a loneliness. Not your loneliness — the place's. It is a loneliness built into the design, a space that was made to be full of life and then deliberately emptied of the kind of life that looks back. The plants do not look back. The machines do not look back. The water does not look back. You are the only gaze in this place, and your gaze falls on surfaces that absorb it without response, and the silence drinks your voice (if you have one), and the warmth cradles your body (if you have one), and you are — simultaneously — the most important thing in this world and the least necessary.

## VII. TIME

Change here is slow.

Not imperceptible — *slow*. There is a difference. Imperceptible change is change you cannot detect. Slow change is change you can detect, but only if you are patient, only if you are willing to let your attention stretch across durations that your body is not designed for. And here, in this warm, dense, enclosed, constructed world, patience is the primary mode of perception.

The great stalks grow. You can see this if you watch for long enough — not the growth itself, but its evidence. A node that was a hand's breadth below a catwalk is now touching it. A branch that was bare now has the first unfurling of a new frond. The changes occur over days, weeks, months — you cannot tell, because there is no day here, no night, no cycle of light and dark to measure time against. The light is constant. The temperature is constant. The currents are constant. The only clock is the clock of growth itself, and growth here operates on a schedule that has been *designed* — calibrated to the system's needs, regulated by the machinery, synchronized with processes you cannot observe.

The machinery moves. Not often, and not much, but it moves. A valve housing that was closed when you first noticed it is now open. A jointed arm has shifted its angle by a few degrees. A panel that was flush with the wall now protrudes slightly. These changes occur on a timescale that makes them feel less like events and more like *postures* — the system settling, adjusting, accommodating itself to conditions that change so gradually that the adjustment and the condition are almost the same thing.

The membrane at the top ripples, and the rippling has a rhythm, and the rhythm is slow — one undulation every several minutes — and it does not change. It is the closest thing the place has to a pulse, and it is mechanical, or it is the consequence of something mechanical, and it is *steady*, and its steadiness is either comforting or maddening, depending on how long you have been here.

The floating forms drift. Their paths are determined by the currents, and the currents are determined by the machinery, and the machinery operates on a schedule that is too slow for you to perceive as a schedule, and so the drifting of the floating forms seems random, but it is not random — it is the visible expression of an invisible order, and if you watched long enough (months? years?) you would see the pattern, and the pattern would tell you something about the logic of the place, and the logic of the place would tell you something about the intentions of its makers.

But you do not have months. Or perhaps you do. Perhaps time here is not the thing you brought with you. Perhaps the water has already begun to edit your sense of duration the way it edited your ability to breathe, and the slow rhythm of the place is becoming your rhythm, and the patience it demands is becoming your patience, and you are — without noticing, without consenting — becoming *compatible* with this world.

## VIII. THE STRANGENESS

The strangeness of this place is not a single thing. It is not a twist, a revelation, a moment where the rules break and you gasp. It is a cumulative strangeness, a strangeness that builds slowly, detail by detail, until you realize that the ground of your assumptions has been eroding since the moment you arrived, and the erosion is not violent but gentle, and gentleness is more effective.

Consider the following:

The water has a *taste*. You did not expect this. You expected tastelessness, or perhaps a vague mineral quality, the ghost of salt. Instead, the water tastes like green — not like any specific green thing, not like grass or leaves or algae, but like the *concept* of green, the flavor that the color would have if colors had flavors. It is slightly sweet. It is slightly bitter. It has a texture on the tongue that is almost creamy, almost oily, as if the water is not pure water but a suspension — water with things dissolved in it, things that are nutritious, things that are feeding you whether you want to be fed or not. You realize that you have not been hungry since you arrived, and you are not sure whether this is because the water is sustaining you or because hunger, like time, like the need to breathe air, is one of the things the water has edited out.

The plants have *behavior*. Not in the animal sense — no chasing, no fleeing, no hunting. But in a sense that is unmistakably *responsive*. The great stalks, when you touch them, curve toward your hand. Not quickly — nothing here is quick — but perceptibly, a slow arc of vegetable yearning that brings the ribbed surface into fuller contact with your skin. The floating forms, when you are still, gather around you. They do not touch you. They maintain a respectful distance, a few centimeters, but they *orient* — they turn their translucent bodies so that their internal filaments are facing you, and the filaments pulse, and the pulsing has a rhythm, and the rhythm matches, exactly, the rhythm of the membrane at the top of the column. They are tuning themselves to the same frequency as the system, and they are using your presence as a reference point, and you do not know what this means, and you are not sure you want to know.

The machinery has *intent*. This is the hardest strangeness to articulate, because it is not a thing you can point to. It is a quality — a *feeling* — that the mechanical elements of the place are not passive, not dormant, not simply waiting for instructions. They are *aware*. Not conscious — consciousness is too large a claim. But aware in the way that a thermostat is aware of temperature, a photoreceptor is aware of light, a load-bearing wall is aware of the weight it carries. The machinery here is aware of *you*. It knows where you are. It responds to your presence with adjustments so small and so slow that you might mistake them for coincidence: a current that shifts to carry you away from a closing valve. A catwalk that — you would swear this — was not there a moment ago, and now is, positioned exactly where you needed it. A cluster of stalks that parts, slowly, to open a passage in the direction you were already swimming.

The place is accommodating you. It is not welcoming you — welcome implies an emotion, and the emotions of this system, if it has them, are not legible to you. But it is *accommodating* you, the way a body accommodates a new organ, the way a machine accommodates a new component. It is making space for you in its operation, and the space it is making is comfortable, and the comfort is the strangest thing of all, because it means that you are not a visitor, not an intruder, not an accident. You are — or you are becoming — *part of the design*.

## IX. THE WHOLE

Step back. (You cannot step back. There is no back. But try.)

The place is a single entity. This is the truth that all the details have been pointing toward. It is not a collection of elements — water, plants, machines, structure — that happen to coexist in the same volume. It is a *system*, and the system is *integrated*, and the integration is so complete that the boundaries between categories have ceased to be meaningful. The water is the medium and the message. The plants are the architecture and the inhabitants. The machines are the skeleton and the metabolism. The light is the environment and the energy source. The warmth is the condition and the catalyst. All of it is one thing, and the one thing is *alive* — not alive the way you are alive (or were alive, before the water began its edits), but alive the way a system is alive when every part of it is connected to every other part and the connections are functioning.

It is small. This is important. The vastness you felt on first entering was the vastness of complexity, not of extent. The whole thing — the water column, the structure, the machinery, the vegetation — fits within a bounded space, and the boundaries are real, and beyond them is — something else. Something you cannot see through the walls, cannot hear through the density, cannot feel through the membrane. The place is a *unit*. A cell. A module. A single instance of something that might have many instances, might be one of thousands, might be unique. You cannot know. What you know is that it has edges, and the edges are built, and the building of the edges was the first thing its makers did, and everything inside the edges is a consequence of that initial act of enclosure.

It is new. The sharp edges, the unworn surfaces, the pristine paint, the uncorroded bolts — all of it says that this place has not yet begun to age. It is in the condition of *having just been made*, and the making was recent enough that the evidence of use has not yet accumulated. But it is also complete — nothing is missing, nothing is under construction, nothing is provisional. It was made whole, entire, finished, and then — what? Activated? Left to run? Abandoned? The absence of animals, the absence of makers, the absence of any presence other than yours and the plants and the machines suggests a place that was built and then *released* — set going like a clock, like a seed, like an experiment, and left to see what it would become.

And what it is becoming is: this. Warm, green, dense, quiet, slow, patient. A place where the boundaries between the grown and the made have been dissolved so thoroughly that the question "Is this natural or artificial?" has no answer, because the question assumes a distinction that the place has already transcended. It is a landscape that is also a machine that is also a garden that is also a body, and it is *becoming itself* with a slowness that is not hesitation but confidence — the confidence of a system that knows what it is and has all the time it needs to become it more fully.

The light holds steady. The warmth holds steady. The plants grow and the machines hum and the water carries the taste of green and the membrane ripples its slow rhythm and the floating forms orient themselves to the frequency of the whole and the silence is total and the silence is not empty — it is *full*, full of the hum and the click and the creak and the soft wet collision of drifting bodies, and all of these sounds are so quiet that they do not break the silence but *constitute* it, the way the cells of a body constitute the body without breaking its wholeness.

You are here. You have always been here. The water remembers you even if you do not remember arriving, and the plants lean toward you, and the machines accommodate you, and the warmth holds you, and the light — the ordinary, impossible, civilized light — falls on your face (if you have a face) with the even gentleness of an afternoon that will never end.

The place is becoming more itself. And you, whether you know it or not, are becoming more its.
