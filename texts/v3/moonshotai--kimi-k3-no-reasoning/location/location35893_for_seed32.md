# The Murmuring Womb

Beneath the surface — far beneath — there is a room.

Not a room in the sense of four walls and a door. More in the sense that a seed has a room inside it: a cavity shaped to hold one thing perfectly. This room is about the size of a chapel. Its walls, floor, and ceiling are indistinguishable from one another — they are all the same material, a pale ceramic substance, bone-white and faintly warm to the touch, that has been cast and re-cast so many times it no longer has edges. Corners have been eroded by centuries of vibration into smooth transitions. Wall becomes ceiling becomes floor without seam. The entire interior is one continuous surface, curved like the inside of an egg.

It was made. Everything here was made. But it was made so long ago, and remade so often by the slow physics of its own existence, that its made-ness has become geological. You could chip a flake from the wall and find no tool marks, only the layered patina of time — strata of molecular compression, the material slowly densifying under the pressure of what fills the room.

What fills the room is the sound.

---

The sound comes from the walls. Or rather, it comes from what grows in the walls — the only living thing here, a pale lichenous organism that has colonized the ceramic substrate with absolute thoroughness. Every square centimeter of the interior surface is covered. The organism forms a thin, leathery crust — cream-colored, almost the same tone as the substrate beneath it — with a texture like overlapping fish scales, each scale no larger than a thumbnail, pressed tight against its neighbors in a tessellation so complete that not a molecule of bare substrate is exposed.

This organism is the room's sole vegetation, and it is also the source of the sound.

The scales vibrate. Constantly. They have always vibrated. It is not a behavior — it is a structural property, the way that certain crystals vibrate when you flex them. The scales are piezoelectric. The warmth of the room — which is steady, cool, unvarying, perhaps fifteen degrees — creates a constant thermal flux between the organism's interior and the room's atmosphere, and this flux flexes the scales, and the flexing makes them resonate.

Multiply one scale's faint hum by two hundred million scales.

The room **roars**.

Not with volume alone — though it is loud, louder than any natural enclosed space should be, loud enough that you would feel it in your sternum before you heard it with your ears — but with density of texture. Each scale resonates at a slightly different frequency determined by its size, its age, its thickness. The result is a sound that is less like noise and more like *matter* — a wall of interlocking frequencies so tightly packed that the air itself seems to have texture, to have grain, to be a solid thing you could run your fingers along.

There are no pauses. No rhythm. No crescendo. The sound is absolutely, cosmically stable. It has been identical — mathematically identical, every frequency, every amplitude — for longer than the surface world above has had names for itself. The room has achieved an acoustic equilibrium so perfect that it has become, effectively, a frozen chord. A single unresolvable note held forever.

---

The light is the other thing that will undo you.

It comes from everywhere. The lichen-scales are faintly bioluminescent — not in pulses or patterns, but with a steady, saturated radiance, a pale gold that is the color of late afternoon distilled to its purest frequency. Every scale emits the same quality and quantity of light as every other scale, and because the scales cover every surface without gap or variation, the room has no shadows. None. A hand held up before your face would be lit identically from all sides, every fingerprint and crease illuminated with the same gentle, total attention.

This light, combined with the sound, creates an atmosphere that is almost aggressive in its fullness. There is no darkness to rest in. There is no silence to think in. The room offers no refuge from itself.

The air is bone-dry. There has never been water here — not a droplet, not a molecule of humidity. The ceramic substrate was fired in the absence of water; the lichen derives all its chemistry from the mineral content of the walls and the trace gases that percolate down through kilometers of overlying rock. The air tastes of static and old paper. Your lips would crack within hours.

Nothing breathes. Nothing moves. Nothing is born and nothing dies — or rather, the lichen dies and is reborn in continuous, microscopic, perfectly balanced turnover: individual scales reach the end of their lifespan and are immediately replaced by new growth from beneath, the old material absorbed and recycled with no waste, no residue, no *change*. The total biomass of the room has not varied by a gram in millennia.

---

The space itself is small — you could walk its longest axis in twenty steps — but it does not feel small. The absence of any visual or acoustic reference point, of any corner or edge or shadow or silence, dissolves the sense of boundary. You might be in a chapel. You might be in a planet. The uniform golden light and the uniform roaring sound eliminate perspective the way fog eliminates a horizon. There is only *near* — the patch of wall close enough to touch — and *far*, which is everything else, which is identical to near.

The temperature does not vary. The pressure does not vary. The light does not vary. The sound does not vary.

If you could sit in this room long enough — long past the point where the sound stopped being sound and became simply the condition of existence, long past the point where the light stopped being light and became simply what the world looks like — you might begin to notice the room's single irregularity. It is not a feature so much as a *tendency*. If you hold very still and extend your awareness outward through the saturated air, you may feel that the room has a center — a point, roughly at the geometric middle of the space, where the sound is infinitesimally louder. Where the frequencies align with a precision that is not present anywhere else.

At that point, there is nothing. No object. No structure. Just a location in the air where the sound is *more itself*.

This is what the room was built to hold: not a thing, but a coordinate. A place where sound achieves its maximum density and stays there, forever, vibrating in perfect equilibrium, the way a heart holds a beat it never stops and never finishes.

---

No animal has ever been here. No tool has ever been here. The lichen built the room around itself, or the room grew the lichen within itself, or both are true — the distinction collapsed so long ago that the room no longer remembers which came first. It is a closed system. A sealed, self-maintaining, self-sounding instrument tuned to a single eternal note and left to play itself in the dark earth, lit by its own gold light, packed so full of sound that there is no room for anything else.

It does not need anything.

It does not want anything.

It simply *is*, at the top of its voice, forever.
