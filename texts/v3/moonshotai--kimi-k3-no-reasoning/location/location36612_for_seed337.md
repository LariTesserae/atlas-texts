The **Under-City of Kharos**

---

**I. The Descent**

You arrive not by road but by shaft—stone stairs worn concave by centuries of feet, descending past the last sliver of natural sky into a geography that refuses to stay still. Kharos is not a ruin. It is an *unfinished* city, a settlement carved into the throat of the earth before its walls were complete, before its ceilings were sealed, before the stone had settled into permanence. It exists in a state of perpetual becoming, a civic embryo suspended in limestone.

The air hits you first: cool, the temperature of deep cellars and shaded streams, carrying the smell of wet chalk and oxidizing iron. Water is not abundant here—no rivers rage through the dark—but it is *present*. It beads on the curved ceilings of the great halls, gathers in natural basins that the inhabitants have widened into reservoirs, drips with the patience of geological time from stalactites that have grown only millimeters in the lifetime of the city. The moisture is intermittent, unreliable, a visitor rather than a resident. When the rains come to the surface world above, Kharos knows it three days later, when the seepage finally filters through the overburden and the temporary rivulets sing in the drainage channels.

**II. The Architecture of Compression**

The city is packed. Not merely dense, but *saturated*—a honeycomb of dwelling-hollows carved into the living rock, floor stacked upon floor with no clear distinction between natural cave and constructed chamber. The builders of Kharos, working with bronze picks and fire-setting techniques, followed the bedding planes of the limestone. They did not conquer the stone; they negotiated with it. Corridors widen into plazas where the rock allowed, narrow into crawl-spaces where fault lines barred the way. There is no wasted volume. Alcoves serve as storage niches, sleeping platforms, or shrines. Ventilation shafts double as light-wells, polished bronze mirrors angled to catch the sun from the surface and throw it, diminished but warm, into the third and fourth sublevels.

The construction is layered in time. The oldest chambers, near the water table, are rough-hewn, their walls bearing the scalloped marks of primitive tools. Above them, the architecture grows more confident—arched doorways, corbelled vaults, sophisticated drainage. But the newest sections, at the excavation frontier, are raw again: the raw geology of active expansion, where the city is literally eating its way deeper into the mountain. Here, the walls weep continuously, fresh stone exposed to air for the first time in millennia, and the sound of hammering never ceases. The city grows downward and outward simultaneously, an organism digesting rock.

**III. The Economy of Damp**

Life in Kharos revolves around the management of moisture. The sparse vegetation—pale ferns, ghostly liverworts, and a species of translucent cave-cress that grows only in the drip zones—is cultivated with the intensity of desert agriculture. Every drop is harvested. Condensation collectors of woven reed line the ceilings of the great halls, funneling atmospheric water into cisterns lined with waterproof clay. The few animals here are rare and precious: blind cave fish in the deep reservoirs, pale crickets that chirp only when the humidity spikes, and the domesticated *khos*-lizards that hunt them, their scales kept oiled against the chill.

The people move through their packed world with a quiet urgency. Sound is muffled by the close walls and the absorbent stone; voices drop to murmurs, and the dominant sounds are the *tick* of condensation, the scrape of a bronze shovel, the distant crack of expanding rock. Yet the city is active. It breathes. Fires in the great kilns drive air currents through the packed corridors. The excavation front advances daily. Markets bustle in the Mirror Hall, where the reflected sunlight dances on wet walls and illuminates trade in salt, fungal bread, and worked stone.

**IV. The Rules of the Under-World**

Kharos operates by strict hydrological law. Water rights are measured in *drops-per-hour*, and the theft of condensation is punishable by exile to the Dry Galleries—unworked cave systems where the air is still and dead. The city's density creates a social pressure as tangible as the physical; privacy is unknown, and the inhabitants have developed a culture of intense interiority, their lives conducted in whispers and their emotional lives kept as carefully contained as their water.

The light is normal but not constant. It comes in scheduled doses—mirror-shifts that rotate with the surface sun, lamplight fueled by rendered fat from the rare fauna, and the phosphorescent glow of cultivated fungi that the citizens encourage in the older, abandoned galleries. In the deep markets, you can tell time by the quality of light: the blue hour when the mirrors catch morning, the amber hour of afternoon, the green hour when the lamps are lit and the city retreats into bioluminescence.

**V. The Unfinished Nature**

What makes Kharos strange is not magic—it is almost aggressively normal, following the logic of geology and primitive engineering—but its *potential*. This is a city that has not decided what it will be. The erosion parameter is zero not because the stone is pristine, but because the city is still in its constructive phase, not yet subject to the gentle wearing of abandonment. It is embryonic. The streets end in raw rock faces. The drainage systems terminate in sumps that have not yet found their outlet. The city is a question being asked of the mountain, and the mountain is still answering.

Standing in the newest excavation, where the air tastes of crushed stone and possibility, you can feel the pressure of the place—the density of habitation pressing against the unknown voids ahead, the cool damp promising water that has not yet arrived, the light of mirrors illuminating a civilization that is building its own ground as it walks. It is a landscape of anticipation, packed tight with human presence yet expanding always into the silent, waiting dark.
