# The Hollowing

---

It began, they say, as a valley. Or perhaps it is still becoming one — the distinction matters little here, where *began* and *is becoming* describe the same held breath. The land does not change. It has not changed. It was made young and it has stayed young, the way certain metals stay molten not because heat is being applied but because cooling has simply not occurred to them yet.

You stand at the threshold of something continental. There is no horizon — not because the land curves away or the atmosphere thickens, but because there is no light to reach one. Darkness here is not the absence of day. Darkness is the medium. Darkness is what the air is made of. You do not see through it; you *are in it*, the way a fish is in water, and it presses against the skin with a warmth that is neither comfort nor threat but simply fact — the ambient temperature of a world that has never been cold, has never needed to be cold, keeps its heat the way the earth's core keeps its heat: because there is nowhere for it to go.

---

## The Substrate

The ground is not ground. The ground is plant.

This is not a forest in any sense that word has been used before. There are no trees with trunks and canopies, no understory, no floor. The distinction between *vegetation* and *terrain* collapsed so long ago — or so recently, time being what it is here — that the question of which came first has no meaning. The land is a single contiguous living mass, a vegetal body of continental extent, and everything you might call a feature — a rise, a hollow, a ridge, a channel — is the body doing something. Bulging. Folding. Reaching.

It is warm to the touch. Not warm like sun-heated stone — warm like a wrist. Warm like something with a pulse, though whether it has one is unclear, because a pulse implies rhythm and rhythm implies change and this place does not change. Perhaps it is better to say: the warmth is constant, and the warmth is the temperature of the plant-mass, and the plant-mass is the temperature of the world, and the world is warm, and that is all.

The surface textures vary. Near the threshold — if thresholds exist in a place with no geography — the plant-body lies in broad, overlapping plates, like the leaves of some enormous succulent compressed under their own weight into geological strata. They are thick as mattresses, cool-slick on top, warm beneath. When you press your hand into one, it yields slowly and then stops, and you feel the resistance of the layer beneath, which is also plant, and the layer beneath that. There is no soil. There has never been soil. The plant-mass goes down and down, and what it rests on — if it rests on anything — is a question the place does not answer.

Further in, the texture changes. The plates give way to something denser, more vertical: columns of braided vegetal tissue, each as wide as a cathedral nave, rising into the darkness overhead. They are not trees. They have no bark, no branches, no canopy. They are more like the structural members of the plant-body itself — load-bearing growth, the architecture of a living continent holding itself upright against its own gravity. Between the columns, the space is filled. Not empty air. Never empty air. Finer growths fill every gap: mosses the size of rooms, hanging in sheets; filamentous curtains that brush your shoulders as you pass; cushions of compacted cellular material that spring back when you push through them, slowly, the way deep water springs back.

---

## The Water

Water is here, but it is not a river. It is not a lake. It is not even, quite, weather.

The plant-body sweats.

Moisture condenses on every surface — not in droplets that fall, but in films that persist. The broad plates near the threshold are glazed with water, always, the way a cold glass glazes in humid air, except the glass is the size of a football pitch and the humidity is the atmosphere itself. Between the columns, where the density increases, the moisture thickens into something between mist and liquid — a suspended saturation, water that has not decided whether to be air or fluid and so remains both. Breathing it feels like drinking. Your lungs adjust, or they don't.

In the lowest places — and "lowest" here means shallow depressions in the plant-body, gentle bowls where the vegetal surface dips and holds — the water gathers into stillness. Not ponds. Not pools. Gatherings. The water is the color of nothing because there is no light to give it color, but it is warm, and it is full, and the plant-mass beneath it is softer there, more pliant, as though the water has soaked into the living tissue and made it tender.

The water does not flow. There are no currents, no gradients, no downhill. The landscape is too flat for flow — not flat in the sense of a plain, but flat in the sense of *equilibrium*. The water sits where it sits because where it sits is where it is, and there is no force to move it elsewhere. Evaporation happens, presumably — the air is saturated, after all — but the condensation equals it, perfectly, and so the system holds. The water cycle here is a circle with no circumference, a process that occurs entirely in place, going nowhere, arriving nowhere, sustaining itself through the sheer refusal to change.

If you listen — and listening is something you learn to do here, in the absence of light — you can hear the water. Not dripping. Not running. *Present*. A faint, continuous, almost-subsonic hum, like the sound of wetness itself, the sound that moisture makes when it is not falling or flowing or drying but simply *existing* on a surface. It is the quietest sound you have ever heard, and it is everywhere.

## The Engines

Somewhere in the dark — and *somewhere* is doing heavy work in that phrase, because distance here is unmeasurable, unmappable, a concept that dissolves in the absence of sight — there are machines.

They were not built. That is the first thing to understand. No hands made them. No tools shaped them. They *grew*, or they *occurred*, or they *precipitated* out of the same processes that produced the plant-mass and the water and the warm dark, the way crystals precipitate out of a supersaturated solution. They are industrial in character — pistons, turbines, great slow-turning wheels, arrays of piping that vanish into the vegetal substrate and re-emerge elsewhere, carrying something that might be sap, might be water, might be something else entirely. They are industrial in *scale* — some of the pistons are the size of buildings, rising and falling with a rhythm so slow it cannot be perceived, only inferred: you stand beside one for what feels like hours and realize that the great dark cylinder above you has descended the width of your hand.

But they are not machines in the way a factory is a machine. They do not produce anything. They do not process anything. They are not part of a system that has an output. They are more like *gestures* — the plant-body's gestures, performed in metal and motion instead of growth. A piston rises. A turbine turns. A wheel rotates. The motions are continuous and unvarying, and they accomplish nothing, and they have always been happening, and they will always happen, and they are as much a part of this landscape as the moss-sheets and the water-films and the warmth.

They are quiet. This is perhaps the strangest thing about them. A piston the size of a building, moving through its stroke, should make a sound like thunder — the hiss of pressure, the clang of contact, the groan of material under stress. These do not. They move in near-silence, and the sounds they do make are so low, so slow, so thoroughly woven into the ambient hum of the water that separating them requires a kind of attention that borders on meditation. You place your hand on the housing of a great turbine and feel the rotation as a vibration in your bones — a frequency too low for ears, a music for the skeleton.

The machines are warm. Warmer than the plant-mass, warmer than the air. Heat radiates from them in slow, even waves, and where they pierce the vegetal substrate, the surrounding growth is denser, more elaborate, as though the plant-body has responded to the warmth by flourishing — or as though the machines are not separate from the plant at all but are the plant's way of *being warm*, concentrated, the way a body concentrates its warmth in its core.

## The Silence

The silence here is not an absence. It is a *presence*.

In most landscapes, silence is what remains when sound is removed — a negative space, a gap. Here, silence is what the space is *filled with*. It has texture. It has weight. Sitting in the warm dark with your eyes open or closed (it makes no difference), you feel the silence pressing against your eardrums from the outside, a gentle, constant, warm pressure, like lying at the bottom of a pool and feeling the water's weight.

The few sounds that exist — the water-hum, the machine-vibration, the faint rustle of the plant-mass adjusting itself in ways too slow to see — do not break the silence. They *are* the silence, in the same way that the plants *are* the ground. The silence is made of these small sounds, the way a wall is made of bricks. Remove them and there would be nothing left to hear. Keep them and what you hear is silence, built up, layer by layer, into something solid.

There are no voices. There are no calls. There are no footsteps except your own, and even those are muffled by the soft, giving surface of the plant-body, which absorbs sound the way fresh snow absorbs sound, turning each footfall into a suggestion of a footfall, a rumor of movement.

---

## The Ecology

Nothing lives here, if by "lives" you mean the things that animals do — move, hunt, flee, mate, die. There are no insects in the moss-sheets. There are no fish in the water-gatherings. There are no birds overhead, because there is no *overhead* in any sense that a bird could use. The darkness is not a sky. It is not even an absence of sky. It is simply what is above, and it goes up and up, and nothing flies in it.

But the plant-mass is alive, and its aliveness is so total, so all-encompassing, that it fills the niche that animals would occupy in another world. The plant-body *moves* — slowly, imperceptibly, on timescales that make the machines look frenetic. It shifts. It adjusts. Columns widen. Plates tilt. New growths emerge from the substrate and old ones are reabsorbed, not through decay but through a kind of smooth, continuous reincorporation, as though the plant-body is constantly digesting itself and regrowing, not in cycles but in a single sustained gesture of self-renewal that never quite changes anything.

This is the ecology: one organism, continent-scale, eating itself and growing from its own substance, warmed by its own machinery, watered by its own condensation, existing in a darkness that is not night but *nature*. No predator. No prey. No competition. No death, because death implies an ending, and nothing here ends. The plant-body does not die because it has never not been alive. The machines do not stop because they have never not been running. The water does not dry because it has never not been wet.

## The Scale

You cannot walk across it. This is not a figure of speech.

The plant-body extends in every direction to distances that exceed the useful range of the word "distance." It is not that the landscape is big in the way a desert is big — a long walk, a hard journey, many days. It is that the landscape is big in the way a *planet* is big, or bigger. The curvature of the ground, where you can feel it, suggests a radius so vast that the word "continent" becomes quaint. You are not on a continent. You are on a *surface*, and the surface goes on, and the going-on is not a journey but a condition.

The darkness compounds the scale. In a lit landscape, vastness is visible — you see the horizon, the layering of far hills, the atmospheric perspective that tells your brain *this is far*. Here, the vastness is invisible, and so it is felt instead: in the way sound behaves (nothing echoes; the space is too large for reflections to return), in the way the air moves (it doesn't, or it does so on scales too grand to perceive), in the way the warmth remains constant no matter how far you walk (you are not moving through a climate; you are inside a *system* that maintains its own temperature, the way a body maintains 37 degrees, and you are smaller than a cell in that body).

There may be weather here — the scale suggests it, the moisture suggests it — but if weather exists, it operates on a scale that makes it indistinguishable from climate, from geology, from the permanent features of the world. A storm here might last a thousand years. A shift in the humidity might take a century to complete. You would not notice. You are too brief, too small, too local. The weather is not for you. The weather is for the plant-body, and the plant-body experiences it the way you experience a heartbeat: constantly, unconsciously, as the background condition of being alive.

## The Light

There is none.

This is worth dwelling on, because the absence of light here is not the absence of light in a cave, or at night, or in a room with the curtains drawn. In those places, darkness is temporary — a condition that will end when the sun rises, when the door opens, when the match is struck. The darkness is *waiting*, and you know it, and the knowing shapes your experience of it.

Here, the darkness does not wait. It is not the darkness *before* anything. It is not the darkness *after* anything. It is not a phase, a cycle, a transition. It is the medium in which the landscape exists, and it has always been the medium, and the suggestion that it might change — that light might arrive, that day might break — is not frightening or hopeful but simply *incoherent*, like suggesting that water might become dry or that silence might become loud.

Your eyes adapt, after a fashion, but there is nothing to adapt *to*. You develop other senses, or other ways of sensing. The warmth gives you information — warmer near the machines, cooler (by a degree, half a degree) in the water-gatherings. The textures tell you where you are — plates here, columns there, moss-sheets somewhere else. The sounds, quiet as they are, orient you in the way that the hum of a house orients you in a dark room: not by showing you anything but by confirming that you are *somewhere*, that the somewhere is consistent, that it holds.

---

## The Stillness

Nothing changes.

This is the deepest fact of the place, and the hardest to hold in the mind, because the mind is a changing thing and it wants to project change onto whatever it encounters. You look for cycles — day and night, growth and decay, seasons, tides — and you find none. The warmth does not fluctuate. The moisture does not increase or decrease. The machines do not speed up or slow down. The plant-body does not bloom or wilt. The darkness does not deepen or lift.

You might walk for what feels like days and return to where you started — or think you have, because every place is like every other place, not in the way that dunes in a desert are alike (each shaped by wind, each subtly different) but in the way that two points on a sphere are alike: identical not by coincidence but by *principle*, because the system that produced them does not differentiate.

The stillness is not frozen. Frozen implies that something was once liquid and has hardened, that motion was arrested, that time was stopped. This stillness was never arrested because it was never in motion. It is not a *stopped* thing. It is a thing for which *stopping* and *starting* are equally meaningless, a thing that exists in a state so complete, so perfectly self-consistent, that change would require the introduction of an entirely new category of being — and there is no room for new categories here, because the existing ones fill every available space the way the plant-mass fills every available surface.

---

## What It Feels Like

It feels like being inside something that loves you but does not know you exist.

The warmth is not for you. The moisture is not for you. The slow, steady, silent work of the machines is not for you. The plant-body does not acknowledge your presence — does not recoil from your footstep, does not reach toward your warmth, does not notice the small compression your body makes in its surface. You are irrelevant to it, and this irrelevance is not hostile or cold. It is *total*, and in its totality it becomes a kind of peace.

You lie down on a plate of warm vegetal tissue, and the plate is soft, and the moisture on its surface touches your cheek, and the darkness is above you and around you and inside your lungs, and the hum of the water and the vibration of the machines are in your bones, and the silence presses gently against your ears, and you understand — not with your mind, which still wants cycles and horizons and dawn, but with something older — that this place is not waiting for anything.

It is not becoming. It is not ending. It is not resting between states. It *is*, in the fullest and most literal sense, and the *is* is so complete that it leaves no room for anything else — no room for change, no room for time, no room for you, ultimately, because you are a creature of change and time and you cannot stay here, not really, not in the way the plant-body stays, not in the way the machines run, not in the way the darkness persists.

But for the duration of your presence — your brief, warm, wet, silent, dark, utterly still presence — you are *in* it, and it is in you, and the boundary between the two is as unclear as the boundary between the plant and the ground, the water and the air, the machine and the organism, the silence and the sound.

---

*Somewhere in the dark, a piston the size of a building completes its stroke. It has been completing this stroke forever. The vibration moves through the plant-mass, through the water-films, through the warm saturated air, and arrives at your body as a feeling that is almost like a word — a single, long, low word, spoken in a language with only one syllable, and the syllable means:*

*here.*
