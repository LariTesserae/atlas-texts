# The Hollow of Mercy's Drum

There is a chamber inside the world's spine where nothing grows, nothing drinks, and nothing stands still for more than the width of a thought.

The elevator cage that takes you down there is an afterthought bolted to a much older machine. It descends a shaft that was once a magma conduit — the walls still carry that glassy, rope-swirl texture, frozen mid-flow — and drops you at a depth where the rock above your head is measured in kilometres, not metres. The cage judders. The chains sweat metallic oil. Then the floor opens up in a way that no floor should open, because it isn't a floor. It's a ledge.

You stand on the rim of a bowl roughly four hundred metres across. Inside it, someone built a city.

---

## The Bowl

The bowl is not natural, but it isn't entirely made, either. Some early civilisation — or the planet itself, if you believe the geomancers on Level Seven — scooped the hollow into the interior of a mountain's root. The walls are concave, curving inward slightly at the top, so the whole space feels like the inside of a bell that has been rung once and is still deciding whether the sound matters. The curvature gives you vertigo if you look up too long: the ceiling is a dome of basalt and brass trusswork, strung with what look like stars but are, on closer inspection, rivets the size of your head, holding plates of tectonic armour in place. The dome groans. Always groaning. A low, stony complaint, barely audible, felt in the sternum more than the ears.

The rock is young. That's the strange thing. Despite the depth, despite the weight of everything above, the stone here has the sharp-edged, unweathered quality of something freshly extruded — as if the mountain gave birth to this cavity recently and hasn't yet learned how to smooth it down. Fractures run through the basalt in clean, geometric lines. Some of them glow faintly, a dull amber, like cooling filaments. Geologists who visit call this "residual thermal memory." Locals call it "the wound still being open."

The city occupies the bowl's floor and climbs its lower walls in stacked tiers, like a barnacle colony given architectural ambition. Buildings are packed shoulder to shoulder — dense beyond comfort, dense beyond logic — pressed into every available plane of the concave surface. They don't spread outward because there is no outward. The bowl contains them, and so they grow *inward*, toward each other, overlapping, interpenetrating, sharing walls and roofs and plumbing in ways that make surveyors weep.

---

## The City Itself

No green anywhere. Not a single blade, not a moss stain, not even the ghost of lichen on old stone. This is a mineral place. The buildings are made of the mountain's own substance: basalt blocks, iron-veined granite, slag poured into moulds and left to cool into walls that have a faint rainbow sheen, like oil on water, except there is no water. Windows are mica sheets. Doors are cast bronze. Staircases spiral up through shafts drilled by steam-boring machines that still operate somewhere in the city's underworks — you can hear them, distantly, a rhythmic chuffing like an animal breathing in its sleep.

The buildings lean. Not from neglect — from density. They press against each other the way passengers in a crowded tram press, and over decades the pressure becomes structural. Roofs interlock. Balconies from one building pierce the wall of the next. In the oldest districts, near the bowl's centre, it is genuinely impossible to tell where one structure ends and another begins. The whole mass has fused into a single architectural organism, porous but solid, a hive of corridors and chambers and stairwells that have never seen sky.

And there is no sky. The dome closes overhead. What light exists comes from a grid of carbide lamps strung along cables that sag between the highest towers — a permanent artificial twilight, dim and slightly green-tinged, as if you are looking at the world through old bottle glass. The lamps flicker in sequence. Nobody has figured out why. The electricians maintain them faithfully, replacing filaments, rewiring junctions, but the flickering follows its own pattern, sweeping across the dome ceiling in slow waves, like something passing overhead on the other side.

Shadows here are thick and textured. They pool in alleys like sediment. The dimness is not uniform — it has grain, it has depth, it shifts as the lamps pulse. You learn to read it the way sailors read water.

---

## No Water

There is no water. This cannot be overstated. The bowl is sealed from the water table by a shell of anhydrous rock, and the air is so dry it pulls moisture from your mouth within minutes. Residents drink from recycled vapour — condensation scraped from cooling coils, distilled urine, the breath-harvest of thousands of lungs, run through charcoal filters and copper stills until it is technically potable and spiritually grim. They call it "return." Every drop is accounted for. There are return-merchants who walk the tiers with brass flasks on their hips, selling measured mouthfuls like pharmacists dispensing medicine.

The absence of water shapes everything. Buildings are never washed; they accumulate dust and grime in layered strata, like geology. Clothes are cleaned with abrasive powder. Fires are catastrophic and rare, fought with chemical foam and, once, with a controlled cave-in that buried three city blocks under rubble to starve the flames of air. The fire district is still a raw crater in the urban fabric, un-rebuilt, its edges sharp as broken pottery. Children play on the rubble. The rock is young even where it's broken.

---

## Engines

The city runs on engines. Not clean, humming electronics — real engines: reciprocating pistons, steam turbines, diesel dynamos, flywheels the size of houses spinning in sub-basements. The industrial base is dense and total. Foundries occupy entire tiers. Machine shops nest between residential blocks. Conveyor systems carry ore and castings through tunnels bored into the bowl's walls, and the clatter of their chains is the city's baseline rhythm, under everything, always.

Power comes from geothermal taps — boreholes sunk deeper into the mountain's root, drawing heat from rock that still remembers being molten. The taps hiss and shudder. Sometimes they surge, and the lights flare bright for a few seconds, and everyone stops and looks up, and the dome groans a little louder, and then it settles back. These surges are called "the mountain breathing in." Nobody likes them.

The air tastes of machine oil, hot metal, ozone, and something underneath that is harder to name — a mineral sharpness, almost like the smell of a struck match, but drier, older. You breathe it and your throat tightens. Old-timers breathe it like incense.

## What Lives Here

The fauna is extraordinary. Not in variety — in *density*.

The bowl supports a biome that exists nowhere else, a dense weave of creatures adapted to darkness, dryness, heat, and the constant tremor of machinery. They are not large. Nothing here is large. But they are *everywhere*.

**Tremor-moths** are the most visible — palm-sized insects with wings of translucent chitin that catch the lamplight and scatter it into brief, cold rainbows. They swarm in the thousands, drawn to vibration. When a flywheel spins up, the moths arrive in clouds, settling on every surface, fanning their wings in synchrony with the machine's frequency. The effect is beautiful and unsettling: a whole wall covered in pulsing, iridescent wings, beating in time to a dynamo. Workers call them "the governor's choir." They eat metallic oxides — rust, patina, corrosion — and are tolerated, even encouraged, because they keep the ironwork clean. Their droppings are a fine copper-coloured dust that accumulates in drifts on rooftops and window ledges.

**Basalt crawlers** — segmented, eyeless things the length of a forearm, armoured in overlapping plates that look like chips of the mountain itself — move through the city's interstitial spaces: wall cavities, pipe runs, the gaps between fused buildings. They are slow and deliberate and absolutely silent. You find them in places you didn't think anything could reach. They feed on mineral deposits, leaching salt and silica from the rock with rasping mouthparts. They are harmless, but finding one in your bed is an experience you do not forget. Their shells, when they moult, are collected by children and traded like currency. A good crawler shell, intact, with all segments, is worth a week's water ration.

**Wire-eels** live in the electrical conduits. They are long, thin, cartilaginous, and conductive — their bodies carry a faint charge, and touching one bare-handed will make your arm go numb to the elbow. They nest in junction boxes. They travel along live wires the way fish swim in streams. Electricians have a complicated relationship with them: the eels degrade insulation, but they also eat the tremor-moth larvae that clog cooling vents, so the balance of tolerance shifts depending on which problem is worse this month. Some old electricians keep them as pets, feeding them copper filings by hand. This is considered eccentric but not insane.

And then there are the **drum-rats**. Not rats — the name is a misnomer carried down from some earlier settlement's vocabulary. They are small, warm-bodied, quick, covered in a dense felt of silvery fur that insulates against both the cold rock and the hot machines. They live in colonies of hundreds, packed into the warm spaces around engine housings, turbine casings, anywhere heat accumulates. They communicate by drumming — rapid, complex patterns tapped out on metal surfaces with their forepaws — and at shift-change, when the engines throttle down and the city's hum drops by a few decibels, you can hear them: a patter of polyrhythmic tapping, like rain on a tin roof, except there is no rain, and there is no roof, and the sound is coming from inside the walls.

The drum-rats are intelligent. Not tool-using, not language-having, but *intelligent* in the way that crows are intelligent, or octopuses — a quick, sideways intelligence that watches you and calculates. They steal. They trade. They have been observed leaving small offerings — a bright bolt, a polished washer — near the food scraps that workers set out for them. Whether this is reciprocity or coincidence is a subject of ongoing debate in the city's single, underfunded natural philosophy society.

## The Turbulence

Nothing here stays the same. This is the defining quality of the Hollow, the thing that makes it more than a strange city in a strange place.

The bowl is geologically active. Not in the way of surface earthquakes — slower, deeper, more structural. The dome shifts. The walls flex. New fissures open. Old ones close. The mountain is still forming, still settling, and the city inside it is built on a substrate that refuses to hold still.

Buildings crack. Sometimes they fall. The city's charter includes a standing order — Article One, actually, predating all other laws — that mandates continuous structural monitoring. Every building carries a network of tension wires and mercury gauges, checked daily by guild-licensed surveyors who walk the tiers with brass instruments and chalk, marking stress points, flagging walls for reinforcement, condemning structures that have moved beyond tolerance. The condemned buildings are not demolished — there is no room for demolition, no place to put the rubble — they are *absorbed*: filled with concrete and slag, sealed up, incorporated into the foundations of whatever is built next to them. The city grows around its own dead like coral around old skeletons.

This constant flux gives the place its energy. The population — a few thousand, crammed into a space that should hold half that number — lives with a restless, adaptive intensity. People here are good at moving. They are good at rebuilding. They are good at letting go of things that the mountain has decided to take back. There is a fatalism to the culture, but it is not passive — it is the fatalism of sailors, of miners, of people who live inside a force they cannot control and have made a home there anyway.

The turbulence is not just geological. The city's economy is a churn. Workshops open and close within weeks. Trade routes through the interstitial corridors shift as passages collapse and new ones are cut. The water market fluctuates wildly — a cracked condenser coil can spike the price of return by forty percent in an afternoon. There are no stable institutions except the guilds, and even they are fractious, territorial, prone to sudden schisms and mergers. The whole social fabric vibrates at the same frequency as the stone.

And the drum-rats drum on.

---

## The Quiet

For all its machinery and movement, the Hollow is a quiet place.

The bowl's acoustics are strange. The concave walls absorb sound rather than reflecting it — something about the curvature and the density of the basalt. Voices don't carry. The clatter of industry is muffled to a background murmur, present but not oppressive. Footsteps land softly. Even the engines, massive as they are, produce more vibration than noise — you feel them in the floor, in the handrail, in your teeth, but the *sound* of them is low and distant, like a heartbeat through a mattress.

This quietness shapes behaviour. People speak in low voices. Arguments happen in whispers. Music is percussion — drum-rat rhythms echoed by human hands on metal railings, on pipe casings, on the stretched membrane of salvaged oil drums. The city has no bells, no horns, no loudspeakers. Announcements are made by runners, or by coded patterns tapped into the steam pipes, a civic morse that every child learns before they can read.

In the deepest hours — there are no days and nights, only shifts, but the deepest hours are when the third shift is asleep and the first hasn't yet risen — the city goes almost silent. The engines idle. The lamps dim to their lowest setting, a pale green dusk. The moths settle. The crawlers rest in their cavities. Even the drum-rats soften their tapping to a slow, sparse patter, like a heartbeat winding down.

And in that silence, you can hear the mountain. Not the groan of the dome — that's always there. Something else. A sound that might be the settling of stone, or might be the distant pulse of the geothermal taps, or might be something else entirely, something that lives in the rock and has always lived in the rock and will outlast every engine and every moth and every human being who ever descended that shaft in a rattling cage.

It sounds like breathing.

---

## The Quality of Being There

To stand on a balcony in the Hollow of Mercy's Drum — the name's origin is lost; there are at least eleven competing etymologies, each defended with tribal ferocity — is to feel contained in a way that is both claustrophobic and oddly comforting. The world is small. The world is *this*: this bowl, this city, this dim green lamplight, this dry air, these machines, these creatures, these people pressing close together in the rock. Above you is a kilometre of stone. Below you is heat and darkness. Around you is a community of the stubborn, the adaptable, the ones who looked at a cavity in the deep earth and said: *here*.

It is not a kind place. The dryness cracks your skin. The dimness strains your eyes. The noise, though soft, never fully stops. The buildings lean on each other and on you. The mountain shifts and things fall. Water is precious and tastes of copper. The moths get into everything.

But in the early hours, when the lamps are low and the drum-rats are tapping out their patient mathematics and the dome groans overhead like a ship's hull in deep water, and you are standing on a balcony three tiers above the nearest neighbour, looking out at a city that glitters faintly with copper dust and condensation — a city that should not exist, in a place that should not hold it, built by people who refused to leave —

it is beautiful.

Not despite everything.

Because of everything.
