# The Hanging Terraces of Velka's Hollow

---

## First Impression

You come upon it from below, climbing a path that is less a path than a negotiation with wet stone, and what you see first is not the settlement but the *sound* — or rather, the absence of it, broken by a single dropped note: water falling from some ledge far above, taking so long to reach the ground that the drop becomes a note, a thin silver strike against rock, and then silence again for a long time, and then another note. The rhythm is irregular. It depends on the wind.

Then the mist parts — it does this in sheets, not wisps, entire curtains of cool vapour sliding laterally across your vision as though the mountain is breathing — and you see it: a shelf of rock, perhaps two hundred paces wide, jutting from a cliff face that rises beyond sight above you and drops beyond sight below, and on that shelf, a town. Small. Dense with green. Lit by nothing obvious. Humming with something that is not sound but the pressure of many living things in a small space.

This is Velka's Hollow. It is new. It is strange. It is very, very full of eyes.

---

## Terrain and Structure

The cliff is young — geologically infant, barely stabilized. The rock face is sharp-edged, dark basalt shot through with veins of something pale and faintly phosphorescent, a mineral that glows dimly in the blue-green spectrum, barely enough to read by if you hold the page close. This is the primary light source. There is no sun here, or rather, the sun exists somewhere above the canopy of mist but reaches this shelf only as a diffuse, directionless gloaming — a perpetual hour just before dawn, or just after dusk, depending on your mood. The light is cool, blue-grey, and even. Shadows are soft, long, and unanchored; they do not point anywhere because the light comes from everywhere and nowhere.

The shelf itself is not natural. Or not entirely natural. Something — and this is one of the strangenesses — something cut this ledge, or grew it, or convinced the mountain to offer it. The basalt at the shelf's edge is too clean, too deliberate, scored with parallel grooves that run the full width of the settlement like corduroy. The grooves channel condensation — the mist condenses constantly on every surface — into thin rivulets that feed the gardens. This is the entire water system. There is no river, no spring. There is only the mountain breathing mist and the town catching it.

Below the shelf, the cliff drops into a gorge that has not finished forming. You can hear it forming — distantly, a crack, a rumble, a fresh seam splitting open somewhere in the dark below, and then a rain of small stones that takes seven or eight seconds to finish falling. The gorge is perhaps three hundred feet deep, perhaps more; no one has measured. The bottom is filled with a slow, pale river — glacial melt, milky with mineral silt — and the sound it makes is so low and so constant that you stop hearing it after the first hour. It becomes part of your body. You notice it only when it shifts.

The rock is friable in places. Fresh. You can break pieces off with your fingers. Fossils — recent ones, still articulated, still fleshy in the deep rock — surface occasionally when a face crumbles. The people of the Hollow find bones in their gardens sometimes. Bones of things that are not yet extinct, which is another way of saying that this place has a complicated relationship with time.

---

## The Settlement

Velka's Hollow holds perhaps sixty structures, though "structure" is generous. The architecture is wattle-and-daub raised to a kind of desperate elegance: woven branches of the local whipwood — a plant that grows in dense, springy thickets on every horizontal surface — lashed with strips of bark and daubed with a mud-and-moss mixture that dries to a hard, faintly luminous grey-green. The moss is alive. The walls are alive. The buildings glow very faintly at night, a cold breath of light, and in certain conditions — before rain, or when the mist thickens — they pulse.

This pulsing is one of the strangenesses. It corresponds to nothing anyone has identified. It is not seasonal, not tidal, not correlated with any celestial movement anyone can track. It simply happens, and the people have learned to read it the way you might read the mood of a large animal you live beside: by instinct, by accumulation of small observations, by the tension in your shoulders when you wake.

The buildings are small — single rooms, mostly, low-ceilinged, hunched close to the rock as though sheltering from something overhead. They cluster along the back of the shelf, against the cliff face, leaving the front two-thirds of the ledge open: a broad, sloping apron of cultivated ground, terraced in rough steps held by dry-laid stone walls. The terraces are the town's life. They are also, increasingly, its problem.

Paths between buildings are paved with flat stones scavenged from rockfalls — sharp-edged, unweathered, sometimes still warm from the friction of their descent. They are laid without mortar, and they shift. The whole shelf shifts. The town creaks at night, a deep, slow groaning as the ledge settles into the cliff, finding a rest it has not yet found. This is a young place. It has not stopped being born.

There is a central structure — larger than the rest, though still modest: a hall, roughly hexagonal, its walls woven so tightly they are almost solid, its roof layered with broad, waxy leaves that overlap like scales. Inside, a fire pit. This is the only fire in the settlement. Wood is too precious, too scarce, too needed for building and tools to burn casually. The fire is kept low — a bed of coals, mostly, fed with dried whipwood prunings and the dung of the climbing beasts. It is a communal fire. Meals are cooked here, together, in clay pots that are passed hand to hand. The firelight, warm and orange against the cool blue-green of everything else, feels almost transgressive — a pocket of another world's weather smuggled into this one.

The tools here are stone, bone, and plant fibre. Obsidian — the cliff provides it in small, sharp nodules — is knapped into blades and scrapers. Bones of the larger fauna are carved into needles, fishhooks (though there are no fish), digging points. There is no metal. There is a rumour of metal — a story the elders tell about a place below the gorge where the river has exposed a seam of something red and heavy that can be beaten flat — but no one has gone to look, because going below the shelf means descending into the gorge, and the gorge is where the fauna are thickest.

## Vegetation

The green here is aggressive. It is not the lushness of a warm jungle, not the riot of wet heat and decay, but a cool, tenacious, muscular green that grows the way frost forms — slowly, then all at once, then everywhere.

The dominant plant is the whipwood: a low, dense shrub with pale bark and long, flexible branches that it uses — and this is not metaphor — to strike. The branches are loaded with some hydraulic tension; when disturbed, they whip back with enough force to welt skin. The people harvest them carefully, at dawn, when the plant is sluggish with cold. The wood is strong, light, and slightly water-repellent. It is the town's skeleton.

Between the whipwood thickets, the terraces grow crops: a starchy, purple-fleshed root called *velka* (the settlement is named for it, or it for the settlement — no one agrees), a broad-leafed green that tastes of iron and rain, and a climbing bean that produces pods only in the dimmest corners, as though light is something it needs to hide from. The bean plants are trained up the cliff face itself, on trellises of woven whipwood, and they have begun to escape. Vines reach thirty, forty feet up the basalt, anchoring in cracks, and the cliff above the town is slowly turning green.

The moss is everywhere. It is not one moss but dozens — a taxonomy of mosses, each with its own texture, its own shade of luminescence, its own behaviour. Some are soft as breath. Some are rough as tongue. Some release spores at certain hours that drift through the settlement like slow snow, catching the faint light, and for those minutes the whole town is filled with drifting points of green-blue radiance, and the people stop what they are doing and watch, because it is beautiful and because it means the air is still enough for the spores to settle, and stillness is rare.

Ferns grow in the terrace walls — not between the stones but *in* them, rooted in cracks too thin to see, their fronds emerging flat against the rock face like pressed specimens in a book. They are epiphytes of stone. Their roots exude a mild acid that is slowly, very slowly, softening the basalt. In a few hundred years, the terrace walls will crumble. The people know this. They replant the ferns anyway, because the ferns' fronds are edible, and because a few hundred years is not a problem that belongs to anyone living.

## Fauna

This is where the world tips.

The Hollow is not large — a shelf, a town, a cliff face, a gorge. You could walk its full extent in twenty minutes. And yet it is *filled* with living things, packed so densely and so variously that the boundary between "settlement" and "ecosystem" dissolves entirely. The fauna do not stay in the gorge. They do not respect the terraces. They are not afraid.

**The lantern-moths** are the most visible. Hand-sized, broad-winged, with wings of translucent membrane stretched over dark frames, they emerge at what passes for dusk — the dimming of the already-dim light, a subtle shift in the quality of the gloaming — and they fill the air. Hundreds. Thousands. Their bodies carry a bioluminescent organ at the thorax, a warm amber point, and when they swarm, the town is lit by drifting, pulsing constellations. They are drawn to the fire pit but not harmed by it; they orbit the hall in lazy spirals, and their combined glow through the woven walls turns the interior into a paper lantern.

The people do not eat them. This is not sentiment. The moths, when crushed or cooked, release a toxin that causes vivid, prolonged hallucinations — not unpleasant, apparently, but incapacitating. One of the elders, in her youth, ate three on a dare and spoke in a language no one recognized for two days. She remembers none of it. The moths are left alone.

**The cliff-hounds** are less benign. They are small — terrier-sized, long-bodied, six-legged, with gripping pads on each foot and a pelt of dense, oily fur that sheds the constant moisture. They hunt in packs of five to eight, moving across the cliff face with the easy verticality of geckos. They are fast, coordinated, and curious. They have learned that the terraces contain food — the purple roots, the beans, and occasionally, if someone is careless, a child who has wandered too close to the shelf's edge.

They have not yet killed anyone. But they have tried. The town's single defensive structure is a whipwood palisade along the shelf's edge — more a suggestion than a wall, chest-high and full of gaps. It does not stop the hounds. It slows them. It gives the people time to reach the hall, to light brands from the communal fire, to make noise. The hounds dislike noise. The town, normally so quiet, becomes briefly, desperately loud.

This happens perhaps once a month. It is not a catastrophe. It is a rhythm. The hounds probe, the people repel, the hounds retreat to the gorge, and for a few weeks the town is left alone. The people do not hate the hounds. They watch them with a kind of wary professional respect, the way farmers watch weather.

**The glass-eels** live in the condensation channels. They are thin as twine, translucent, and they move through the rivulets that crisscross the terraces in slow, deliberate undulations. They eat algae. They are harmless. But at certain times — and no one has determined what triggers it — they mass. Hundreds of them braid together into thick, ropy columns that climb out of the channels and move across the ground with a terrible, fluid purpose, seeking — what? Water, perhaps. Mates. Each other. They do not attack, but they will crawl over anything in their path, including sleeping people, and the sensation of a cold, living rope passing over your face in the dark is, by all accounts, something you do not forget.

**The birds** — if they are birds — are the strangest thing here, and they are never fully seen. They live in the mist above the shelf. You hear them: a clicking, like stones tapped together, and a low, resonant hum that vibrates in your sternum. Occasionally, when the mist thins, you see a shadow — large, slow-moving, with a wingspan that seems too wide for the body it carries. They do not descend. They do not interact. They are simply *there*, above, and their presence exerts a pressure on everything below, a kind of ecological gravity. The cliff-hounds will not hunt while the humming is audible. The lantern-moths cluster tighter. The moss dims.

The people call them *the auditors*. No one remembers who first used the word. It is the right word.

## Atmosphere and Sound

The dominant sound is silence, but it is a populated silence, a silence with texture. It is the kind of quiet that exists in a room where many people are holding their breath.

Water drips. Constantly, from everywhere — the cliff face, the building eaves, the bean trellises, the edge of every fern frond. Each drop is a small event. The rhythm is never the same twice. In certain states of attention — half-asleep, feverish, meditative — the dripping begins to sound like speech. This is reported too often to be dismissed. The people do not talk about it much.

The gorge rumbles. The river whispers. The cliff creaks. The wind moves through the whipwood thickets with a sound like a bow drawn across very low strings — a sustained, moaning drone that rises and falls over minutes. When the wind is strong, the entire shelf hums, and the mist streams horizontally past the settlement, and the lantern-moths retreat into crevices, and the people tie everything down and sit inside and listen to their world vibrate.

The loudest sound, on most days, is human: someone singing while they work the terraces. The songs are simple, repetitive, tuneless almost — more chant than melody. They serve a function. The vibration of the voice, carried through the ground, deters the glass-eels from massing. The people learned this by accident. They do not understand the mechanism. They sing because it works, and because singing in the dim, cool, mist-wrapped silence is one of the few things that makes the Hollow feel like theirs.

## Rules of the World

The mist has a schedule. It thickens at what corresponds to midday (the light brightens marginally, the mist condenses more heavily) and thins at what corresponds to midnight (the light dims to near-darkness, the mist pulls back). This is the town's clock. There are no stars. The mist never fully clears. No one in the Hollow has ever seen the sky.

The rock glows brighter when wet. After rain — and rain comes in sudden, cold, brief bursts, as though the mist occasionally loses patience with its own subtlety — the entire cliff face lights up, a dim but unmistakable radiance, and the town is bathed in blue-green light for an hour or more. This is the brightest it ever gets. The people call this *the unveiling*, and they use the time to do work that requires precision: mending tools, repairing walls, inspecting the terraces for damage. Children are told to look at the gorge during unveilings, because the river at the bottom lights up too, and for a few minutes you can see the full depth of the world you live on the edge of.

Nothing decays quickly. The cool, dim, moist environment is not kind to rot. Dead things — plants, animals, people — persist. They dry, slowly, or they are colonized by moss and become part of the green architecture. The people bury their dead in the terrace walls, laid horizontally between courses of stone, and over time the moss grows over them, and the wall becomes a kind of genealogy — a layered record of the settlement, written in bodies and moss and stone. The oldest walls are the thickest. The town is literally built on its dead, and this is not mournful. It is structural. It is how things hold together here.

The strange pulse of the moss-light — the unaccountable brightening and dimming — has begun, in the last few years, to synchronize with the dripping. The people have noticed. When the moss pulses, the water drops fall in rhythm. When the dripping syncopates, the moss flares. Two systems that should have nothing to do with each other are beginning to speak. The elders watch this with an expression that is not quite fear and not quite hope. It is the expression of people who suspect that their world is about to tell them something, and who are not sure they are ready to hear it.

## A Moment

It is the dim hour. The mist is thick. You are standing at the edge of the shelf, behind the palisade, looking down into the gorge. You cannot see the bottom. The mist is a white sea below you, and from it rises the hum of the river, the click-click of the unseen auditors above, the drip of water from the bean vines behind you.

A lantern-moth lands on the palisade rail, arm's length away. Its amber light pulses — once, twice, a pause, once. The moss on the rail beside it pulses in answer — a different colour, a different rhythm, but unmistakably responsive. A glass-eel slides past your foot, cool and purposeful, heading for the terrace channel. Above you, the humming shifts in pitch, and for a moment — just a moment — the mist above you thins, and you see, or think you see, a vast, slow shape pass overhead, and the shape is not a bird, and it is not a cloud, and it is not anything you have a word for.

The mist closes. The moth lifts away. The eel is gone. The silence returns.

You are standing in a small, cool, green, dim, damp, strange, teeming, quietly alive place that is still deciding what it is going to be.

It has not finished.

Neither have you.
