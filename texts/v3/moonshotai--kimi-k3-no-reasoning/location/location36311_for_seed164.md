The sky here is a high, thin ceiling of hammered tin, a color that suggests the sun is always somewhere else—behind a ridge, behind a cloud, behind the vastness itself—yet the light manages a kind of flat, grey-gold permeation, diffuse and even, touching every wet surface with a matte, industrial sheen. It is cold, bone-deep and unwavering, a stillness of the atmosphere that suggests winter not as a season but as a permanent condition, a baseline state of matter. The air bites, dry despite the omnipresence of water, because the water is rarely in the air; it is in the ground, the stone, the iron, weeping from pores in the earth as if the planet itself is sweating in its sleep.

You stand at an elevation that strips the breath from your lungs, on a plateau that might be a continent or might be the only world, so wide that the horizon curves away into a haze not of distance but of scale. Below and above and around, the land is a fresh wound in the cosmos—geologically infantile, raw, unblended. The stone is sharp, crystalline, black basalt and grey granite thrust upward in angular plates that have not yet learned to crumble. The edges are so new they glint, razor-faced and geometric, slicing through the thin soil where it has managed to accumulate in sheltered cracks. This is terrain that has been recently *made*, extruded or lifted or cooled from a violent birth, and it wears its youth in the severity of its angles, the lack of rounded shoulders, the pristine brittleness of its cliffs.

Water moves through this young stone not in rivers—there is no time yet for the patient carving of channels—but in sheets and seeps, a constant, pressurized weeping from the rock itself. The ground is saturated, a sponge of moss and black mud and shallow, mirror-calm pools that reflect the tin sky with a metallic sheen. The water is everywhere near the surface, condensing on the undersides of overhangs, dripping from the eaves of the city in a slow, relentless percussion, pooling in the grates of the streets. It is a marsh without the softness, a wetland at altitude, where the cold keeps the surface film of the ponds from ever quite freezing solid but chills it to a viscous, slate-grey slowness.

And there is a city. Or rather, the city is the landscape, and the landscape is the city, for they grew together in the same sudden moment. It sprawls across the high plateau in a grid that seems to obey no human geometry, vast beyond comprehension, its avenues wide enough to be canyons, its blocks measuring miles on a side. The architecture is industrial, mechanized, born of the same recent emergence as the stone: towers of cast iron and riveted steel, their surfaces already streaked with rust-orange tears from the constant condensation, their windows dark and empty as eye sockets. Factories rise like geological formations—smokestacks that vent not smoke but a steady, white plume of steam that freezes instantly in the air and falls as a fine, dry snow, dusting the streets with a powder that never melts. Pipelines, thick as sequoia trunks, run in elevated trellises above the ground, humming with the movement of unseen fluids, their joints weeping condensation in rhythmic drips.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost violent, a superabundance of green that has colonized the city with the speed of an explosion. It is not the riotous diversity of a jungle but a monochrome, obsessive growth: mosses so thick they form carpets three feet deep, swallowing entire ground floors of buildings; ferns with fronds the size of ship sails, their edges perpetually beaded with water, growing in the crotches of girders and the beds of abandoned freight cars; a kind of black, rubbery grass that sprouts from every crack in the pavement, waving in a wind that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. It is lush because it is empty—there is nothing to eat it, nothing to trample it, no competition from animal life. The green has the city to itself, and it grows with a silent, deafening intensity, a slow-motion conquest that has already draped the industrial skeleton in shrouds of dripping emerald.

The emptiness is profound. Vast distances separate the structures, and within them, only more space, more corridors of rusted iron and dripping stone leading to atriums large enough to hold weather systems. There are no birds, no insects, no rustle of small bodies in the undergrowth. The fauna is zero, null, a void. The only movement is the water, the steam, the slow creep of the moss, and the machines.

Ah, the machines. They are the inhabitants, and they are loud. The sound is a constant, physical pressure, a subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow of your bones and the fillings of your teeth. It comes from everywhere: the grinding of great gears somewhere deep in the earth, the rhythmic piston-thud of engines that never sleep, the high, whining screech of metal on metal from automated maintenance trams that glide on frozen tracks, empty, from nowhere to nowhere. The factories operate themselves, assembly lines clattering in the darkness, stamping out parts for other machines that stamp out parts for other machines, a closed loop of industrial purpose with no product, no consumer, no end. The noise is the sound of metabolism without life, of work without worker, a symphony of mechanical function echoing through the wet, cold emptiness.

The strangeness settles in slowly. You notice it in the way the water sometimes flows uphill, seeping from lower pools to higher ones in defiance of gravity, as if the land is still deciding which way is down. You notice it in the frost that forms not on surfaces but in mid-air, suspended in the shape of ghosts of structures that were never built—frost outlining phantom towers that hover a foot off the ground, visible only in the right light, the flat, normal light that somehow reveals these absences. The rules here are negotiable, or perhaps they were never fully written. A drop of water might fall from a leaf and take an hour to reach the ground, drifting like a bubble. The moss might glow with a soft, bioluminescent green when the factories are running at peak output, as if photosynthesizing the noise itself.

And yet, nothing changes. The dynamic is locked, a frozen equilibrium where the output of the machines equals the decay of the rust, where the growth of the vegetation exactly matches the subsidence of the stone, where the water that evaporates is instantly replaced by the weeping of the rock. Time does not pass here; it accumulates, layer upon layer of identical moments, each as cold and loud and wet and empty as the last. The weather does not turn. The light does not shift. The factories do not age, nor do they improve. The city is a engine idling in a void, vast enough to have its own gravity, its own weather of steam and iron, and it will run forever at this pitch, this temperature, this exact, unwavering state of becoming.

You walk through streets where the water is up to your ankles, black and still, reflecting the endless repetition of the iron towers. The moss cushions your steps. The noise is a blanket. You are the only living thing in a world that has forgotten life is necessary, and the cold, wet, vast, mechanical silence—loud as a thousand engines—welcomes you into its permanent, changeless embrace.
