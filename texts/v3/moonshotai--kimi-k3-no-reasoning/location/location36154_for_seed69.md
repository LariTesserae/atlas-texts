# The Vastening

---

## The Descent Inward

The first thing to understand about this place is that you arrive from above, and arriving from above means falling for a very long time through darkness that has texture. Not the texture of cloud — there is no cloud layer as such — but the texture of *accumulation*. Warm water condensing against your face in drifts and currents, each current carrying its own smell: wet iron, crushed green, something sweet-and-electric that you will later learn is the breath of the great filtration stacks, still hundreds of miles below. You fall through strata of warmth. You fall through strata of noise. The noise arrives before the ground does — a pressure that builds against the eardrums slowly, then all at once, as if the atmosphere itself were a throat and the land beneath were speaking through it, continuously, in a language made of engine drone and insect shriek and the deep infrasonic groan of things moving through water at scales that do not belong to water.

And then the canopy.

Not a canopy in the sense of a single ceiling of leaves, but a *stack* — seven, eight, sometimes twelve distinct layers of vegetal architecture, each one adapted to a different quality of darkness, each one filtering the light from above until, by the time you reach the ground, there is nothing left that could be called light at all. Only the memory of light, carried downward in the bioluminescent chemistry of the plants themselves, the faint blue-green smolder of bacterial colonies on bark, the hot orange flicker of the machine-lights scattered through the understory like candles in a cathedral the size of a continent.

This is the Vastening. It has been here for a very long time.

---

## Terrain and Water

The land itself is old — not ancient in the way of mountains, which stand defiant against time, but ancient in the way of a body that has been eroded into acceptance of its own shape. Whatever peaks this region once had were worn down long before the current ecology took hold. What remains is a landscape of **immense, shallow undulations** — rises and basins that span tens of miles, their slopes so gradual that walking them feels less like climbing and more like the world slowly changing its mind about its own elevation.

The high ground — the rises — is where the rock shows through. Not bare, never bare, but *close to the surface*: dark, iron-rich stone that weeps constantly, its pores exhaling water that has been filtering through the body of the land for decades. The weeping is not metaphorical. Every rock face glistens. Every outcrop has its own vocabulary of drips and seeps and thin vertical streams that gather into rills, and the rills into runnels, and the runnels into the dark arteries of the lowland waterways.

The basins are where the water lives.

Call them marshes and you have described one percent of what they are. They are **water-bodies** — shallow, warm, slow-moving systems that occupy the low ground between rises, interconnected by channels both natural and artificial, their depths ranging from ankle-deep blackwater to sinkholes that have never been plumbed. The water is not still. It *breathes* — rising and falling on a cycle governed partly by rainfall (which is frequent and warm and comes not in storms but in hours-long condensations, as if the sky were sweating) and partly by the rhythm of the machines.

The water is dark. Not dirty — *dark*. Stained by centuries of dissolved organic matter, by tannins leached from root systems older than recorded history, by the mineral exhalations of the weeping rock. In daylight (if daylight ever came), it would be the color of strong tea. In darkness, it is simply black, a mirror for whatever light finds it — the blue-green bioluminescence of the canopy organisms, the sodium-orange lamps of the industrial platforms, the brief violent white of electrical discharge from the storm-collectors on the high rises.

## The Vegetal Stack

The vegetation is not lush in the way a garden is lush. It is lush in the way a *crowd* is lush — a density of competing, cooperating, overlapping lives so intense that the boundary between one organism and the next becomes difficult to locate.

The stack begins with the **emergents** — trees of extraordinary height and relatively sparse distribution, their trunks bare and gray-white for the first hundred feet, rising through the darkness like the columns of a flooded basilica. These are the *lightseekers*, evolved over millennia to breach whatever ceiling exists above them, and what they breach here is not forest canopy but the lower boundary of the perpetual cloud-sweat that hangs over the Vastening. Their crowns exist in a different world — dimmer than daylight but brighter than anything below, shrouded in warm mist, home to entire ecosystems of epiphytes and canopy-dwellers that never touch the ground.

Below the emergents: the **strata trees**. These are the bulk of the stack — layer upon layer of broad-leaved, waxed, dark-pigmented organisms that have carved out their niches in the absence of light. Their leaves are enormous — some as wide as a person is tall — and dark, nearly black, tuned to extract energy not from sunlight (which never reaches them) but from the bioluminescent emissions of the layer above. It is a *cascade economy of light*, each tier living on the luminous waste of the tier above it, the energy diminishing with depth until, at ground level, the plants barely photosynthesize at all and have turned instead to **chemosynthesis** — drawing energy from the mineral-rich water, from the decay of the fallen, from the warm chemical exhalations of the industrial processes that thread through the understory.

The understory — the ground level — is where the density becomes almost unbearable. Not just plants but **growths**: fungal mats that span acres, their surfaces convoluted like brain tissue, exuding moisture and faint amber light. Root systems that have fused with neighboring root systems into communal networks — not symbiosis but *merger*, individual organisms surrendering their boundaries to become parts of a distributed whole that spans entire basins. Vines that are less like vines and more like **arteries** — thick, pulsing, warm to the touch, carrying water and nutrients and (some claim) something like information between the strata.

The air at ground level is thick enough to taste. Water vapor, volatile organic compounds released by the vegetation, spores, pheromones, the ozone-sharp exhalations of machinery — all of it suspended in warmth that never drops below what a human body would find uncomfortable. Breathing here is an act of intimacy with the landscape. Every breath is full of other lives.

## The Machines

The industrial presence in the Vastening is not new. It is not old, either, in the way the land is old. It occupies a middle age — decades, perhaps centuries, long enough for the land to have absorbed it into its own processes, not long enough to have erased the distinction between built and grown.

The machines are scattered but not sparse. They appear as **platforms** — elevated structures of dark metal and composite materials, rising above the water on pilings that have been colonized by root systems and fungal mats until they are more grown than built. The platforms vary in size from a single room on stilts to complexes that span hundreds of acres, interconnected by elevated walkways and pipelines that sag between support towers like the vines they coexist with.

What the platforms do is **filtration and processing**. The water of the Vastening is rich — rich in dissolved organics, in minerals, in the chemical byproducts of the canopy's cascade economy. The machines extract value from this richness. They pump water through filtration membranes, they harvest the biological material that accumulates in the mats and root networks, they process the mineral exhalations of the weeping rock into usable compounds. The products are shipped out along the pipeline network to destinations unknown — or, rather, destinations that exist beyond the boundaries of this description, in whatever world surrounds the Vastening.

The machines are **loud**. This is their most immediate and most pervasive quality. They generate a continuous soundscape — a layered drone of pumps, compressors, turbines, and the rhythmic hammering of extraction pistons — that permeates every level of the stack. The sound is not uniform. It has geography. Near a processing complex, it is a physical force, a vibration felt in the sternum and the roots of the teeth. A mile away, it is a background hum that blends with the drone of insects and the low-frequency vocalizations of the larger fauna. In the deep basins, far from any platform, it is still present — a subsonic tremor carried through the water and the saturated ground, felt rather than heard.

The platforms are lit. This matters enormously in a world of darkness. Each platform carries its own lighting system — sodium lamps that cast pools of amber light onto the black water, blue-white work lights that cut through the understory in hard shafts, the pulsing red of warning beacons on the tallest stacks. These lights have become **ecological features**. Species have evolved to navigate by them. Certain plants have oriented their growth toward them, supplementing their meager bioluminescent income with the steady artificial glow. Swarms of insects congregate around them in dense, living clouds, and the predators that feed on the insects congregate in turn, creating hot-spots of biological activity in the amber circles, tiny ecosystems organized around human light in a world that has none of its own.

Some platforms are **abandoned**. Not many — the machines are maintained, by whom and for what purpose is not immediately apparent to an observer within the landscape — but enough. An abandoned platform is a strange and powerful thing here. The lights go out. The pumps fall silent. And the land closes over the structure with a speed that is almost aggressive, root systems threading through vent grilles, fungal mats swallowing walkways, vines enveloping staircases. Within a decade, an abandoned platform is a ruin. Within a century, it is a **hill** — a slight rise in the understory, distinguished from natural rises only by the angularity of its silhouette and the occasional glint of corroded metal through the growth.

## The Fauna

Abundant is an understatement. The Vastening **teems**.

The insects come first — they are the baseline, the substrate of animal life, present at densities that make the air itself feel populated. They are large, many of them, larger than insects have any right to be: beetles the size of a fist with carapaces that reflect the machine-lights in oily rainbows; moths with wingspans like dinner plates, their wings patterned with bioluminescent eyespots that pulse in slow, hypnotic rhythms; swarming things that move through the understory in coordinated masses, their individual bodies small but their collective presence vast, darkening the air like smoke.

The insects are loud. A single cicada-analog in the Vastening produces a call that can be felt as vibration through the soles of the feet. Thousands of them, calling in overlapping waves, create a wall of sound that rises and falls on cycles that seem almost tidal — loud enough, at peak, to drown out the machine drone, to make conversation impossible, to fill the skull with a ringing pressure that persists for minutes after the wave subsides.

Above the insects: the **amphibians and reptiles** of the water-basins. These are numerous and varied and, in the darkness, largely unseen — detected by sound (the slap of a body entering water, the bellow of a territorial call that echoes across a basin for miles, the rustle of scaled bodies moving through the understory mat) and by the brief flashes of movement in the edges of machine-light. Some are enormous. The basins conceal bodies of considerable size — things that move through the dark water with the slow, deliberate power of creatures that have never needed to hurry. Their eyes, when caught in the light of a platform lamp, are the size of saucers, and they shine with a dull, patient gold.

The **birds** — if birds is the right word for the flying vertebrates of the canopy — are a presence felt mainly in the upper strata. Their calls descend through the layers of vegetation like something falling: high, piercing, often beautiful, sometimes deeply wrong in ways that are difficult to articulate. A call that starts as music and ends as something else. A call that seems to come from everywhere at once. A call that mimics the machine-sounds so precisely that the boundary between biological and mechanical noise dissolves entirely.

The **mammals** — and there are mammals, or things close enough to be called by that name — occupy the middle strata, the space between the water-surface and the lowest canopy layer. They are climbers and leapers, adapted to a three-dimensional world of vines and branches and the horizontal pipes of the machine-network. They are dark-furred, many of them, and their eyes are large and reflective. They are **social**, living in groups that communicate through a combination of vocalization and bioluminescent display — patches of skin that flush with faint light in patterns that carry meaning.

And then there are the things that are harder to classify.

## The Strange

The weirdness of the Vastening is not overt. It does not announce itself. It accumulates — in small wrongnesses, in details that resist explanation, in the slow realization that the rules of this place are not quite the rules of anywhere else.

**The water rises at night.** Not tidally — there is no visible moon, and the concept of "night" is largely theoretical in a world of permanent darkness — but on a cycle that corresponds to something. The water level in the basins rises by several inches over a period of hours, then recedes. The cycle is regular but not predictable by any obvious mechanism. The machines account for it; the platforms are built to accommodate the fluctuation. The plants and animals account for it. But its cause is not apparent.

**The bioluminescence is coordinated.** Not in the way of fireflies synchronizing their flashes — that would be remarkable but explicable. In the Vastening, the coordination operates at landscape scale. Across an entire basin — miles of terrain — the bioluminescent organisms (plants, fungi, insects, bacterial colonies) will simultaneously shift their emission color, transitioning over the course of minutes from blue-green to amber to a deep, pulsing red. The transitions are smooth, wave-like, propagating across the landscape as if driven by a signal traveling through the water or the root networks or the air. What the signal is, and what it means, is unknown. It happens several times a day. The machines seem to track it; the platform lights dim in response, as if in acknowledgment.

**Some of the machines are not machines.** This is difficult to verify and impossible to dismiss. Among the industrial platforms and pipelines, there are structures that look mechanical — that have the right silhouette, the right materials, the right apparent function — but that, on closer inspection, reveal themselves to be **grown**. Not grown in the way of a vine wrapping a pipe, but grown in the way of a tree — organic structures that have assumed the form of industrial equipment, complete with what appear to be intake valves, exhaust stacks, and processing chambers, all made of living tissue. Whether these are organisms that have evolved to mimic the machines, or machines that have been colonized and transformed by the landscape's aggressive biology, is a question that the Vastening does not answer.

**The darkness has weight.** This is subjective, but it is also consistent enough across observers to qualify as a feature of the landscape rather than a quirk of perception. The darkness in the deep understory, far from any machine-light, feels *substantial* — as if the absence of light were not an absence at all but a **presence**, a medium with its own density and texture. People who spend time in the deep dark report a sense of being *observed* — not by any creature, not by any intelligence, but by the darkness itself, as if the darkness were a condition of the land's attention.

## Sound

The soundscape of the Vastening is its most overwhelming quality, and its most defining. It operates at multiple scales simultaneously.

At the lowest frequency: the **infrasonic pulse** of the landscape itself. This is below the threshold of hearing — felt as pressure, as a slow oscillation in the chest, as a sensation of the ground breathing. Its source is unclear. Some combination of the machines' deep-cycle pumping, the movement of water through underground channels, and the collective respiration of the vegetal stack. It never stops. It is the heartbeat of the place.

At the middle frequencies: the **machine drone** — the layered, continuous hum of pumps, turbines, compressors, and electrical systems, blending with the **insect drone** — the layered, continuous shriek, buzz, and chirr of the swarming masses — into a composite noise that is more than the sum of its parts. This middle layer of sound is the **texture of the air**. It is present always. It varies in intensity with proximity to platforms and with the tidal cycles of insect activity, but it never falls below a certain floor. Silence, in the Vastening, does not exist.

At the highest frequencies: the **events**. Sudden, sharp, transient sounds that punctuate the drone — the crack of a branch under the weight of a climbing mammal, the splash of something entering the water, the alarm call of a bird-thing in the canopy, the hiss-clang of a pressure valve releasing steam, the explosive pop of a seed pod, the deep, resonant bellow of a basin-dweller asserting its territory. These events are random and constant, and they give the soundscape its **aliveness** — the sense that the landscape is not a recording but a performance, happening in real time, responsive and unpredictable.

The total effect is **loud** — not painfully so, in most places, but *presently* so, a wall of sound that requires a period of adjustment for newcomers and becomes, for those who stay, a kind of **silence of its own** — a background so constant and so complex that the brain stops parsing it as noise and begins to hear it as the sound of the world being itself.

## The Light

There is no sunlight. This bears repeating because it is so fundamental. The Vastening exists in permanent darkness. Not the darkness of night, which implies a coming day, but the darkness of a place where day is not a concept. The cloud-sweat that hangs above the emergents is thick enough to block any celestial light source; the canopy layers below ensure that even the diffuse, filtered glow that penetrates the cloud never reaches the ground.

Light, in the Vastening, is **manufactured**. It comes from three sources:

**Bioluminescence** — the blue-green smolder of the bacterial colonies, the amber glow of the fungal mats, the pulsing displays of the insects and the social mammals, the faint self-illumination of certain strata-tree leaves. This light is soft, organic, omnipresent but dim. It is enough to navigate by, once the eyes adjust. It is not enough to read by.

**Machine light** — the sodium lamps, work lights, warning beacons, and status indicators of the industrial platforms. This light is hard, artificial, localized. It creates pools and shafts of illumination in the darkness, each one a small world of visibility surrounded by black. The contrast between machine-lit and unlit space is absolute — there is no twilight in the Vastening, no gradient. You are in the light or you are in the dark.

**Electrical discharge** — the occasional, unpredictable, violent flash of lightning from the storm-collectors on the high rises, or from the natural electrical activity that builds in the warm, saturated atmosphere. These flashes are brief and total — for a fraction of a second, the entire landscape is visible: the stacked canopy, the black water, the platforms, the weeping rock, the swarming air. Then darkness reasserts itself, and the afterimage burns on the retina like a memory of another world.

The darkness shapes everything. The ecology is built around it. The machines are built to pierce it. The inhabitants — biological and mechanical alike — navigate by sound and chemical signal and bioluminescent display, and vision is a secondary sense, useful only in the amber circles around the platforms. The Vastening is a **tactile world**, a world experienced through the skin and the ears and the nose more than through the eyes.

## The Built Traces

Beyond the platforms — beyond the active industrial infrastructure — the Vastening is scattered with the **remnants of other presences**. These are few and faint and old.

A **stone wall**, half-swallowed by a root network, its surface carved with patterns that have been worn to near-illegibility by centuries of warm rain. The carvings depict something — figures, perhaps, or maps, or instructions — but the erosion has reduced them to suggestions, and the root system that has engulfed the wall has added its own organic geometry to the design, making interpretation impossible.

A **rail line** — a single track, elevated on crumbling concrete pilings, that runs for a few miles through a basin before disappearing into the water. The rails are corroded to the point of fragility; the pilings are colonized by the same fungal mats that cover everything else. No rolling stock remains. The line's destination, like the products of the active platforms, is unknown.

A **clearing** — a circular area, perhaps fifty yards across, where the vegetal stack is thinner, the ground is bare, and the bioluminescence is absent. In the center of the clearing, a **concrete pad** — flat, featureless, unmarked. Nothing grows on the pad. The edges of the clearing are sharp, as if the vegetation respects a boundary that is not visible. The air in the clearing is noticeably quieter — the insect drone is reduced, the infrasonic pulse is fainter. Whether the clearing is a landing site, a burial, a sealed system, or something else entirely is not determinable from within the landscape.

These traces are scattered at low density across the Vastening — encountered rarely, remembered long. They suggest that the current regime of machines and vegetation is not the first to occupy this land, and that the land has absorbed previous occupations with the same patient thoroughness it applies to everything.

## The Rules of the World

The Vastening operates on principles that emerge from its parameters like water emerging from saturated ground:

**Everything is wet.** This is the first rule. There is no dry surface, no dry air, no dry season. The water is not a feature of the landscape — it is the medium of the landscape. The land is water that has temporarily assumed solid form.

**Nothing is still.** The water moves, the plants grow and decay, the machines pump and process, the animals hunt and flee and mate and die. The bioluminescence shifts. The water level rises and falls. The sound builds and subsides. The Vastening is a system in constant motion, and the motion is not chaotic but **rhythmic** — cyclical, patterned, driven by the interaction of biological, mechanical, and geological processes that have been co-evolving for long enough to have reached a dynamic equilibrium.

**The boundary between natural and artificial is negotiable.** The machines are colonized by plants. The plants assume the form of machines. The animals navigate by artificial light. The fungi process industrial effluent. The landscape has been **hybrid** for so long that the distinction is maintained only as a matter of habit.

**Scale is deceptive.** The Vastening feels vast from within — the basins stretch to the limits of sound, the rises are long and gradual, the canopy is a sky of its own. But the vastness is **layered** — each stratum of the stack contains its own complete world, and the total volume of life is the product of these layers, a density that makes the place feel larger than its physical dimensions. You can walk for a day and pass through a dozen distinct ecological zones, each one a world unto itself, each one connected to the others by the water and the roots and the sound.

**Darkness is the default.** Light is an event, an intrusion, a gift. The landscape exists in darkness the way a fish exists in water — not as a limitation but as a **condition of being**. The creatures of the Vastening do not miss the sun. They have never seen it. The bioluminescent glow of a fungal mat, the amber circle of a platform lamp, the brief white violence of an electrical storm — these are their stars, their seasons, their weather.

## The Feeling of the Place

To stand in the Vastening — at ground level, in the deep understory, far from any platform — is to be **enveloped**. The warmth presses against the skin. The wet air fills the lungs. The sound fills the ears. The darkness fills the eyes. The ground beneath the feet is soft, saturated, alive with the slow movement of roots and the quick movement of small creatures. The nearest tree trunk is close enough to touch, and its bark is warm and slightly damp, and the bacterial colonies on its surface pulse with a faint blue-green light that is just bright enough to suggest the shape of things without revealing them.

The infrasonic pulse is in the chest. The machine drone is in the bones. The insects are in the ears. The smell — wet iron, crushed green, ozone, decay, sweetness — is in the throat.

Something large moves through the water in the nearest basin. You hear it — a slow, deliberate displacement, a ripple that reaches the shore and touches your boots. You do not see it. You will not see it. But you feel the water it has moved, and the sound it has made, and the shift in the insect drone as the swarms adjust their patterns in response to the predator's passage.

The bioluminescence shifts. Across the basin, across the miles, the light changes from blue-green to amber, and the wave of color moves through the darkness like a slow sunrise that belongs to no sun. The machine-lights dim in response. The canopy breathes. The water rises a fraction of an inch.

And the Vastening continues.
