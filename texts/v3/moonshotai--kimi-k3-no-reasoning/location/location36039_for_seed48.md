The city sits in the mouth of the estuary like a tooth rooted in gray water. It is old—settled into its own geometry for centuries now, long past the era of expansion. The concrete quays have gone soft at the edges, rounded by the constant lapping of brackish water that rises and falls with the tides, never quite draining away. Everywhere, moisture beads on the ironwork, condenses on the copper pipes, drips from the undersides of the endless, identical bridges that arch over the canals. The air itself is a wet blanket pressed against the skin, heavy with the heat that radiates from the brick facades and the black basalt cobbles of the streets. It is a low place, a depression barely above sea level, and the heat pools here, trapped by the surrounding hills that you can just barely see on the horizon—distant smudges of hazy blue-gray.

There are no trees. There are no grasses, no mosses, nothing green or photosynthetic. The city is a mineral arrangement: rust-colored iron, pitted steel, weathered limestone that has fissured into plates like the skin of an elephant. The buildings are industrial in their bones—foundries with their smokestacks dormant but intact, warehouses with rail lines running directly into their bellies, tenements built from the same poured concrete as the factories. Everything is electrified, humming faintly with the current that runs through the grid, but the light is dim, always dim. The sun is a pale disc behind a permanent haze of humidity and coal-smoke that never quite clears, casting a perpetual twilight over the narrow streets and the wide, empty plazas.

In this emptiness, the fauna moves.

They are everywhere, and they are not insects, though they chitter. They are not birds, though they occupy the vertical spaces. They are creatures of the heat and the wet, scaled and furred in patches, metabolisms running at a fever pitch that matches the ambient temperature. They have filled the ecological vacuum left by the absence of plants, becoming scavengers and predators and grazers of the city's mineral crust. They number in the millions, perhaps the billions, yet the city feels empty because they are small, or fast, or hidden.

You see them in the margins: a flash of iridescent chitin as something darts into a storm drain; the brief silhouette of a leathery wing against a attic window. They have adapted to the industrial landscape—some drink the condensation that drips from the cooling towers, others nest in the warm housings of decommissioned dynamos. They move in waves through the streets at dawn and dusk, a rustling tide that flows over the cobblestones and vanishes into the sewers. Their sounds are quiet—clicking mandibles, the whisper of scales on brick, the occasional wet thud of something falling from a height—creating a texture of near-silence that is somehow more unnerving than noise would be.

The water connects everything. It is not just in the canals but in the walls, rising by capillary action to paint dark stains up to the second floors of the oldest buildings. It collects in the basements, in the subway tunnels that are now half-flooded and inhabited by pale, eyeless things that have returned to the dark. The city breathes through this water, evaporating and condensing in a slow, hot cycle. The air tastes of salt and ozone and the metallic tang of the creatures themselves.

Despite the overwhelming presence of life, the human scale feels sparse, abandoned, or perhaps simply outnumbered. The infrastructure persists—trams still run on their lines, though the cars are often empty; the gas lamps still sputter to life at twilight, though there are few to light the way for. The city has achieved a balance, an erosion of purpose into function. It is a machine that runs itself now, inhabited by the swarm, cooled by the water, heated by its own trapped breath. It is a low, hot, wet place where the stone has settled into its final shape, and the only movement is the quick, quiet darting of the animals that have inherited the empty rooms.
