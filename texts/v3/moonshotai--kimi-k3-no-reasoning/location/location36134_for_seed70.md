The water is warm as breath.

It fills the high basin to its lip, a bowl of liquid sky cradled between ridges so young their flanks still gleam. Nothing built. Nothing made. Only the slow work of water on stone, and the slower work of green returning.

The place is steep and wet, thrust upward in some recent agony of the earth, and the water followed—chasing the uplift, pooling in every declivity, spilling down channels it has not yet had time to deepen. Streams move with the deliberation of thought. They have nowhere urgent to be. The basin holds them.

Vegetation crowds the margins and pushes into the shallows with an almost aggressive vitality. Not trees—nothing so structured—but a dense, layered mat of things that photosynthesize in whatever way serves them best. Some stretch tall as spears, hollow and ribbed, nodding in air that carries the weight of permanent humidity. Others spread flat against the surface of the water, leaves overlapping like scales, like the armored back of some sleeping thing. Beneath them, the light changes quality. It becomes green, then amber, then something closer to darkness.

The water itself is not transparent. Too many lives suspended in it—microscopic, drifting, feeding on light and on each other. The basin is thick with presence. Density is the rule. Every cubic meter contains multitudes: the rooted, the floating, the swimming, the sessile things that wait with their mouths open.

Warmth is the other rule. The water holds heat like a memory. It never chills, not even at the margins where night might be expected to cool it. Steam rises in the early hours, not from any geothermal violence but simply from the temperature differential, the warm water meeting the slightly cooler air. It hangs. It does not dissipate quickly.

The young ridges surrounding the basin are raw. No soil yet, not properly. What passes for ground is a thin skin of moss and creeping rhizome over naked stone. The stone itself is strange—layered, yes, but in a way that suggests it was once liquid, once flowed. Ripples frozen mid-motion. The geologic process that made this place has only recently stopped, or paused, and the marks of its passage are everywhere.

Sound is sparse. The water laps, but gently. Wind moves through the spear-plants with a low, fluting tone—hollow stems acting as accidental instruments. Occasionally a splash, but these are rare. Fauna here are few, and they move slowly. Something large grazes the submerged meadows in the deep part of the basin, but it surfaces only at long intervals, and when it does, it makes no spectacle of itself. A broad back. A slow exhalation. Then gone again.

The light is ordinary, almost disappointingly so. Sunlight. Daylight. The kind that falls anywhere. But the water and the steam and the density of growing things conspire to change it. It reaches the depths filtered and transformed. Colors shift. What appears brown at the surface reveals itself as crimson, as gold, as something iridescent when seen from below.

Nothing here is static, but nothing moves quickly either. The basin is filling—has been filling for some time—and the vegetation is keeping pace, migrating upward, outward, colonizing new shallows as they form. The process is slow enough to watch, if you had the patience. A particular bank might change over the course of a season. A channel might shift. A new pool might appear where none was before.

The strangeness of the place is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It accumulates. The ripples in the stone that suggest the earth once flowed like water. The way certain plants, when they die, do not decay but mineralize, becoming small stony replicas of themselves that litter the basin floor like a garden of statues. The fish—there are fish, a few species, small and silver—that have begun to develop auxiliary breathing structures, as if preparing for a future where the water might not always be here. Or might be more than here.

The water has a taste. Not salt. Not mineral. Something else. Something alive.

At the center of the basin, where the water is deepest, the vegetation gives way. The bottom drops. The light fails. Something down there is warm—warmer than the rest—and occasionally a bubble rises from that darkness, slow and large, taking its time to reach the surface. When it does, it releases a smell: not unpleasant, but unfamiliar. Organic. Rich. Like the smell of soil after rain, but intensified, concentrated, as if the concept of soil-after-rain had been distilled into a single breath.

The basin has no name. Nothing here names it. Nothing here needs to.
