Here is a landscape where the ground remembers being made and is still deciding whether to become soil.

The **Sintering Flats** stretch beyond every horizon, a plain so vast that curvature matters. Stand anywhere and you can see the world bend, see storms stacking themselves like bricks along the far edge before they choose a direction. It is low country—barely a breath above the water table, though water itself is a rumor here, a seasonal argument. The heat presses down with the weight of a hand, a dry hammering that never quite stops, that makes the air shimmer not with mirage but with the simple fact of being baked.

The place is under construction in the most literal sense. There is no bedrock here, no ancient geology to dig through. Instead, the earth is made of **extrusions**—endless ribbons of slumped, sun-fired clay, still soft in their cores despite the heat. The ground is a grid of half-completed berms, some ten meters tall, others just beginning as cracks in the dust that will, given centuries, grow into walls. These are not ruins; they are **pre-ruins**, structures in the act of becoming before they have ever been inhabited. The erosion here works backwards. Where other worlds have wind that wears things down, this wind deposits, carries grains of fired clay that stick to the berms like mortar, building them higher, smoothing their edges into aerodynamic curves. The landscape is accreting, embryonic, raw with its own making.

Everything is built, yet nothing is artificial in the way a city is artificial. There are no straight lines that stay straight. The berms wander, collapse, reform. The material is the same throughout—a gray-pink silt that hardens into ceramic under the relentless sun—but it behaves as though it were alive, or at least ambitious. It has no machines, no tools, no hands. It simply **grows upward** in response to the heat, which triggers a slow chemical swelling in the clay, a thermal expansion that continues through the cool nights, making the ground tick and groan like a settling house.

Water comes as **condensation** more than rain. In the lee of the highest berms, where the wind creates pockets of stillness, the humidity gathers and weeps. Droplets form on the ceramic surfaces not because they are cold, but because the clay is thirsty, pulling moisture from the air through some mineral capillary action. These damp zones are rare—pockets the size of a house scattered across a continent of dryness. Where they persist, a sparse vegetation takes hold: **siliceous crusts**, organisms that are neither plant nor coral, spreading in fractal patches of gray and rust-red. They photosynthesize the brutal light, exhaling a fine dust that contributes to the construction. They are the only green, if you can call it green—more a verdigris, a corrosion that lives.

The scale allows for weather that acts like architecture. The heat builds pressure cells so large they become permanent features. There is the **Long Swirl**, a cyclone three hundred kilometers wide that has been spinning for decades, moving so slowly across the flats that it functions as a traveling season. When it approaches, the sky turns the color of old bone. It brings no rain, only wind that sculpts, that deposits new layers on the berms, that whistles through the gaps with a sound like breath through broken teeth. Other systems exist: the **Heat Shamals**, thermal updrafts that lift dust to the stratosphere, creating temporary clouds of fired clay that rain back down as pellets, adding mass to the growing ground.

Silence is the rule. There is no animal life to break it—no insects, no birds, nothing that scurries. The sound is the sound of expansion: the tick of cooling clay at dusk, the deep bass groan of a berm shifting its weight, the occasional sharp crack when a section of ground decides to split and begin a new ridge. These sounds are events, spaced minutes or hours apart, leaving the rest of the world in a hush so complete you can hear the blood in your own ears.

The light is ordinary—yellow, midday, dependable—which makes the landscape stranger. There are no dramatic shadows, no alien suns. It looks like Earth, like a desert should look, until you realize the horizon is too far away, and the ground beneath your feet is warm not from the sun but from its own internal chemistry, and the "hill" you are climbing did not exist a decade ago and will be twice as tall in a decade more.

Ecology here is a slow violence. The crusts grow, are buried by the wind-driven deposition of new clay, and then push up through the fresh layer, leaving ghostly strata of their former selves. The berms compete for material, stealing dust from one another through complex wind-shadow effects. Some grow tall and sharp; others slump and spread, becoming plateaus. The whole plain is a construction site where the workers are the laws of physics themselves, busy building a world that may never be finished, that may simply continue to sinter and swell under the hot light until it achieves some critical density and becomes something else—a continent of ceramic, a fired brick the size of a moon.

You walk here and feel the emptiness as a presence. The distance between features—a single damp pocket, then nothing but cracking clay for fifty kilometers—creates a rhythm of anticipation and loss. The heat makes you hallucinate slight movement in the corner of your eye, but it is only the ground breathing, expanding, becoming.

It is a world still in the kiln, unfinished and burning.
