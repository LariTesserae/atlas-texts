There is a city down here, and you have never heard anything so loud.

Not loud like a machine is loud, not loud like a crowd — loud like the city is a single continuous scream that has been happening for so long that the water has learned to carry it the way air carries heat. It vibrates through your ribs before it reaches your ears. It gets into the spaces between your bones and hums there. You do not hear this place so much as become part of its frequency.

The water is warm. That's the first thing you notice after the sound — the water sits on your skin like a held breath, like the inside of a mouth, and it is everywhere. There is no surface. There is no floor you can reach. The water is the medium through which everything exists, and everything exists suspended in it, strung between it, built into it. The city does not sit on the ground. It is embedded in the water column itself, a dense knot of structure hanging in the warm dark like a cluster of eggs in an oviduct, and the water presses in on it from all sides with the patience of something that has been pressing for a very long time.

The city is small. That's the thing that undoes you — for all its infrastructure, all its layered construction and organized mechanical density, it occupies a volume you could almost cup in two hands if your hands were the size of a cathedral. A pocket. A single contained node of civilization adrift in an enormous warm void of water that stretches outward in every direction with nothing in it. Nothing. The emptiness around the city is not a backdrop — it is a presence, a vast warm nothing that presses against the city's outer hulls and conduits and makes the whole thing feel less like a place and more like a held note.

---

Inside, the city is dense the way a machine is dense — every cubic meter accounted for, every surface doing at least two jobs. Corridors are also conduits. Walls are also filtration membranes. Floors are also heat exchangers. There is no wasted space because wasted space is wasted water displacement, and water displacement is energy, and energy is the only currency that matters down here. The architecture is industrial in the deepest sense — not industrial as an aesthetic but industrial as a metabolism. This city is not decorated with machinery. It is machinery, and the people — if there are still people — are somewhere inside it the way neurons are somewhere inside a brain, embedded, functional, inseparable from the system that produces them.

The light is dim. Orange, mostly, or a deep red-amber that feels less like illumination and more like the visible edge of heat. Light sources are small and specific — a lamp over a valve, a strip of bioluminescent tape along a corridor junction, the dull glow of a pressure gauge — and between them, darkness pools thick enough to feel like a second liquid. The water inside the city is clearer than the water outside, filtered and cycled and re-filtered, but it is not clean. It carries a faint mineral haze, suspended particulates that catch the amber light and drift in slow constellations, and when you exhale, your breath rises in a column of silver spheres that wobble and merge and break apart against the ceiling grates.

There are no plants. Nothing green, nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that grows toward light. The concept of growing toward light is foreign here. What exists instead is mineral — accretions of calcium and silica that build up on the city’s outer surfaces like slow coral, except they are not alive. They are chemistry happening in place, deposits left by the water’s warm mineral load as it slides across the hull. Some of them are beautiful — translucent amber fans, needles of white aragonite, layered crusts of iron oxide that look like rusted lace — but they are not ecology. They are sediment. They are the water’s handwriting on the city’s skin, and the city’s maintenance systems scrape them away and they grow back and the cycle continues and it means nothing.

---

The sound.

You have to understand the sound. It is not one sound. It is all the sounds a city makes when it is sealed inside a pressure vessel at the bottom of a warm, lightless ocean, and every one of those sounds is trapped with you. There is nowhere for sound to go. It bounces off the hull, off the water, off the mineral accretions, off your body, and comes back. The city’s machinery — the pumps, the turbines, the filtration arrays, the thermal exchange systems, the pressure regulators, the thousand small motors that open valves and close hatches and cycle water through scrubbers — all of it runs constantly, because if any of it stops, the city stops, and if the city stops, the water comes in. So the machines run, and they roar, and the roar is not a noise you hear but a condition you exist inside of.

There are rhythms in it, if you listen long enough. A deep bass pulse that you feel in your sternum — the main circulation pumps, pushing water through the city's arterial system. A higher whine, almost electrical — the power distribution grid, converting thermal energy from the deep vents into current. A percussion of clanks and hisses that seems random until you realize it is the pressure regulation system, constantly adjusting, constantly compensating for the minute shifts in the water column outside. And underneath all of it, a sound that is almost not a sound — a subsonic throb that might be the city itself, its structural members flexing under the weight of the water, groaning in a frequency too low for your ears but just right for your skeleton.

The sound has weathered the city. Not physically — the city is maintained, repaired, patched, its corrosion scraped and its seals replaced — but acoustically. The spaces inside the city have been shaped by sound the way a canyon is shaped by water. Hard surfaces are angled to deflect the worst of the noise into dampening chambers. Soft surfaces — insulated panels, foam-lined ducts, hanging baffles of woven polymer — absorb what they can. But the city is old in its own way, settled into its own state, and the sound has found every resonance, every harmonic, every frequency at which a pipe will sing or a plate will shiver, and it has made the city an instrument. A deafening, continuous, self-playing instrument that has been performing the same piece since before anyone can remember.

---

There is almost nothing alive here.

Not nothing — almost nothing. Rare things. Things that drift in from the surrounding void and attach themselves to the city's warmth like burrs to fabric. A pale, translucent worm, no longer than your finger, that feeds on the mineral accretions and leaves behind a trail of scraped-clean hull. A cluster of sessile organisms — not plants, not animals, something simpler, something that barely qualifies as biology — that form gelatinous mats in the corners of low-traffic corridors, feeding on the dissolved organic compounds in the recycled water. They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are present, the way mold is present in a damp basement — an inevitability, a consequence of warmth and moisture and time.

And once, you saw something larger. Moving through the water outside the city, beyond the reach of the amber lights. A shape — long, slow, undulating — that passed through the peripheral murk and was gone before you could decide if it was a single organism or a chain of them, linked end to end, drifting through the warm dark like a sentence in a language you don't speak. It did not approach the city. It did not avoid the city. It simply was, briefly, in the same volume of water, and then it was elsewhere, and the void closed over the space where it had been.

---

The water outside the city is not still. It moves — not in currents you can feel from inside, but in slow, massive convulsions that shift the pressure on the hull and set the whole city groaning. These are not tides. They are not storms. They are something between convection and mood — the warm water rising from deep thermal sources, cooling as it climbs, sinking as it cools, rising again. A planetary circulation so slow and so vast that from inside the city it registers only as a periodic change in the pitch of the ambient sound, a deepening of the subsonic throb, a subtle shift in the angle at which particles drift through the corridor water.

But sometimes — sometimes — the circulation accelerates. Something deep below the city, something in the thermal vent field that the city taps for power, hiccups. Or exhales. Or shifts. And the water column convulses, and the city shakes, and the sound goes from deafening to apocalyptic, and every piece of machinery screams at once, and the mineral accretions flake off the outer hull in a slow-motion blizzard of white and amber and rust, and the few living things that cling to the city's surfaces flatten themselves and hold on, and inside, if you are inside, you press your hands against the nearest wall and feel the entire structure flex around you like a lung, like a throat, like something trying very hard not to come apart.

These episodes last minutes. Or hours. There is no way to predict them. They are the city's weather — not atmospheric weather, not surface weather, but the deep, thermal, tectonic weather of a world that is all water and all warmth and all pressure, and the city rides them out the way it rides out everything: by being built to endure, by being maintained by systems that do not sleep, by accepting that its existence is a continuous act of resistance against a medium that wants to flatten it, fill it, and silence it.

After an episode, the city settles. The sound drops back to its baseline roar. The particles in the water resume their slow drift. The worms return to their scraping. The mineral fans begin, molecule by molecule, to rebuild.

---

The strangest thing about this place is not the sound or the dark or the isolation or the fact that it is a city suspended in warm water with no sky and no floor and no horizon. The strangest thing is that it is, in its own way, calm. Not quiet — never quiet — but settled. Established. It has been here long enough that its turbulence has become a kind of stillness, the way a waterfall is still if you look at it from far enough away. The machinery's roar is constant enough to become a kind of silence. The pressure is constant enough to become a kind of weightlessness. The dim amber light is constant enough to become a kind of clarity.

You could live here. People might have lived here, might still live here, might have been born here and never known anything else. They would have bodies adapted to the pressure and the warmth and the sound — bones denser than yours, eardrums thickened to leather, skin slick with a biofilm that keeps the water from leaching minerals out of their flesh. They would move through the corridors with the ease of things that have never known dry land, never known open sky, never known a world where sound dissipates instead of accumulating. They would speak, if they speak, in a language of gesture and vibration — hands on walls, fingers on pipes, reading the city's machinery the way you read a face.

They would find the emptiness outside the city normal. They would find the absence of plants unremarkable. They would find the sound — the terrible, continuous, enveloping sound — to be simply what the world sounds like. They would be right.

The city hangs in the warm dark. It roars. The water presses in. The minerals accrete. The worms scrape. The circulation rises and falls. The light stays dim and amber. The void stretches outward in every direction, warm and empty and patient, and the city endures inside it like a held note, like a clenched fist, like a heart that refuses to stop beating because stopping is the only thing it has never done.
