# The Under-Commons

The city lies inside the earth, but not in darkness — this is the first wrongness, and everything else follows from it.

Light comes from the stone itself. The cavern walls — the vault of it, the floor, the pillars and buttresses and the distant, impossible ceiling — all of it glows with a steady, sourceless amber-white radiance, like old bone held to a lamp. It is not warm light. It illuminates without heat, without flicker, without any discernible mechanism. The stone simply *is* bright, the way certain minerals simply *are* purple or simply *are* magnetic. If you press your palm to a wall, it is cool — cool like cellar air, cool like water drawn from deep wells — and it glows against your skin, shining through the thin gaps between your fingers.

The space itself is enormous and irregular. This is not a single cavern but a linked system — chambers opening into chambers, connected by archways and corridors and great sloping ramps, some of them shaped by water long gone, some of them shaped by hands long gone, and increasingly, over the millennia, shaped by both in such tangled succession that no one can tell where nature ended and craft began. The total span is perhaps four or five miles across at the widest, though no one has ever measured it precisely, and the ceiling varies from claustrophobic crawls to vaults so high and so luminous that they read as sky — a low, white, evenly lit sky, like perpetual overcast noon, pressing down with the weight of miles of rock above it that you cannot see and must not think about.

## The Stone

The dominant material is a pale, dense mineral — not quite limestone, not quite marble, not quite anything with a name in surface languages. It is called *halik* by the inhabitants, and it is everywhere: in the walls, the floor, the buildings, the tools, the furniture, the beads they wear, the cups they drink from. It carves well when fresh-cut, taking edges cleanly, but over centuries it flows — not melting, not dissolving, but *creeping*, the way glass creeps in old windows, the way mountains creep over geological time, except here it happens on the scale of decades. Doorways sag. Steps become ramps. Carved faces soften into ovals. A lintel installed straight will, in a human lifetime, bow downward like a tired shoulder. Nothing stays square. Nothing stays sharp. The entire city is slowly, patiently relaxing into smoothness, as though the stone is exhaling after some ancient, tectonic breath held too long.

This gives the architecture a quality that is difficult to name. It is old — genuinely, incomprehensibly old, layer upon layer upon layer — but it does not look ruined. It looks *melted*. Buildings slump into each other like wax figures left in the sun. Walls lean and fuse. Streets that were once gridded now wander. Columns bulge at their bases, spread like candle stumps, and their capitals — once ornate, perhaps — are now smooth mushroom-rounds of pale stone. The oldest structures, the ones at the city's heart, have lost almost all definition. They are hills. They are domes. They are great soft boulders with doorways cut into them, and even the doorways are going round at the edges.

And yet people live here. They have always lived here. They cut new doorways when the old ones close. They shore up ceilings with timber — no, not timber, there is no wood — with props of bundled reed-fiber and compressed fungal mat, the closest things to structural material they have. They carve channels in the floor to redirect the water that seeps and drips and occasionally, in season, flows. They adapt. They have been adapting for so long that adaptation *is* the culture.

## Water

The water comes from above. It is always coming from above.

Not in rivers — not usually — but in seeps, in sweats, in slow beads that form on the ceiling like the cavern is a great cold forehead. In certain seasons (and the seasons are marked by water, not by light, since light never changes) the seeping intensifies into dripping, and the dripping into thin, glassy streams that run down walls and pool in the lowest chambers, filling cisterns that have been carved and re-carved and re-carved again as the stone flows shut around them. The people call this *the breathing*, and they can read its rhythms the way surface dwellers read weather — the particular mineral tang of the water, the rate of the drip, the temperature of the pools, the specific chambers that flood first and the ones that stay dry longest.

The water is clean. Shockingly clean. It has been filtered through miles of stone, and it tastes of nothing but cold. They store it in halik basins, in great communal reservoirs, in small private jars sealed with plugs of compressed fungus. They do not waste it. Wasting water is the one unforgivable act in the Under-Commons — worse than violence, worse than theft. There are stories, very old ones, about the Drowning Times, when the breathing came too hard and too fast, and whole districts filled like cups, and what happened then is spoken of only in the vaguest terms.

## The Living Things

There is no green here. No chlorophyll, no photosynthesis, nothing that would be recognized as a plant. The light does not feed anything; it simply exists, indifferent, a property of the stone rather than a source of energy.

What lives here instead are two kingdoms, entangled.

The first is fungal. Not mushrooms as you might picture them — no cute caps on stems — but vast, creeping, mostly invisible mats of mycelium that thread through the stone itself, through the cracks and the seep-channels, surfacing only in certain conditions to produce structures: shelf-like plates of dense, leathery tissue; pale branching corals of finger-thick fungal fingers; great loose pillows of spongy gray mass that sag from ceilings and walls. Some of these are cultivated — the people farm specific strains, encouraging them with water and with the chemical-rich waste of the city's other kingdom. The fungal plates are their bread, their leather, their rope, their paper. The thick sheets can be peeled, dried, and worked into fabric-like material, soft and pale and faintly luminous where thin.

The second kingdom is animal, and it is everywhere.

They are called *keth* — a name that covers dozens of species, perhaps hundreds, all of them variations on a single body plan: something between a centipede and a hand. Long, segmented, many-legged, ranging in size from a fingernail to a man's forearm, with smooth pale carapaces and clusters of simple eyes that reflect the ambient light as bright points. They swarm. They are the motion of the city, the turbulence of it — the constant, skittering, chitinous undercurrent of activity that never stops, never sleeps, is never entirely absent from any space. They eat the fungi. They eat each other. They eat the dead. They are eaten, in turn, by the people — roasted on hot stones, ground into pastes, fermented into something that serves the role of both wine and cheese.

The keth are not domesticated. They are *managed*, the way one manages weather. Certain districts have higher keth density than others, and this is a function of fungal cultivation, water availability, temperature gradients, and factors no one fully understands. In the deep heart of the city, where the buildings are oldest and most melted, the keth form something like a living floor — a shifting, clicking carpet that moves in patterns the people read for omens, though whether the omens are real or imposed is a question that occupies their philosophers.

And then there are the larger things.

The *vanduul* — a word that means something like *patient ones* — are the keth's cousins at a different scale: dog-sized, slow, armored like tanks, with the same many legs and the same pale carapaces but a deliberateness of movement that makes them seem almost ceremonial. They live in the deep cisterns and the flooded lower chambers, surfacing rarely, and their appearance is always noted, always discussed. They eat the keth, which keeps the swarms from becoming catastrophic. The people do not hunt them. The people do not interfere with them. The vanduul are, in some sense that no one articulates precisely, *the city's* — not the inhabitants', not anyone's, but belonging to the place itself, as much a part of the Under-Commons as the glowing stone or the seeping water.

## Sound

The city is quiet. Not silent — silence is rare and frightening — but quiet in a way that becomes its own texture. The keth click and rustle. Water drips, and the dripping has rhythm, and the rhythm changes with the breathing, and you can tell the season by listening. Voices are kept low, not by rule but by acoustics: the cavern carries sound strangely, sometimes swallowing it whole, sometimes bouncing a whisper across a quarter-mile of open chamber to land in an unintended ear. So the people speak softly, and gesture a great deal, and their language has developed a parallel sign-system that operates alongside speech, so that a conversation might shift seamlessly from voice to hands and back, depending on who might be listening, or where.

Music exists, but it is percussion — tapping on halik surfaces of different thicknesses, each producing a different tone, a different resonance. The best musicians are also the best stoneworkers, because they understand the material's hidden densities, the places where it rings and the places where it thuds. A skilled player can make a wall *sing* — a low, sustained, bell-like hum that fills a chamber and vibrates in the chest — and these performances are rare and treasured, because the stone changes, creeps, alters its density over time, and the notes are never quite the same twice.

## Rules of the World

The stone glows. The stone creeps. Water comes from above. The keth swarm. The vanduul watch. These are the constants.

The turbulence — the thing that makes this place feel less like a tomb and more like an ant-hill, a bazaar, a churning engine of slow, persistent life — comes from the interplay between stability and change. The stone's creep means the city is never finished. Buildings must be maintained, re-cut, re-carved, shored up or abandoned. Districts flood and are evacuated, then dry and are reoccupied, then flood again. The fungal strains shift, hybridize, fail, surge. The keth populations boom and crash. Nothing is static except the light, and the light's constancy only makes the rest of the change more visible, more pressing.

The people themselves — they are not quite human, though they are human-like. Long generations in the Under-Commons have made them pale, large-eyed, hairless or nearly so. They are strong-handed and soft-footed. They navigate by touch and sound as much as by sight, though their eyes are well-adapted to the even, shadowless glow. They do not dream of the surface. They do not know there is a surface. The world is the cavern; the cavern is the world; the light is the light; and above is only more stone, forever, pressing down with a weight that they feel in their bones without ever naming it.

They build with stone and fungus and keth-shell. Their tools are halik blades and chisels, hafted with mycelial cord. They write by pressing into soft fungal sheets, producing a braille-like script of bumps and ridges that is read by fingertip. Their oldest texts — preserved in sealed chambers where the stone's creep has been slowest — speak of a time before the city, when the cavern was empty and the light was already there, and something — someone — first cut a doorway into the glowing wall and said: *here*. Here we will be. Here we will remain.

The oldest building in the city — if it can still be called a building — is called the First Cut. It is a dome, almost perfectly smooth, with a single round opening that has been re-carved so many times that the opening is now wider than the wall it pierces. Inside is a single room. Inside the room is nothing — no furniture, no artifacts, no inscriptions. Just the glowing stone, and the cool air, and the faint sound of dripping water, and the feeling — unavoidable, undeniable — that this is where it started, whatever *it* is, and that it has not finished yet.
