They built the Arteries where the mountain had not yet decided to become a mountain. It was a plateau caught in the slow-motion violence of birth, a high-altitude tableland where tectonic forces still kneaded granite like dough, pushing up ridges that cracked as they cooled. The air was thin and sharp, a cold that felt less like temperature and more like the absence of anything holding heat in. It smelled of wet iron and ozone, the scent of the atmosphere itself being split and re-stitched by the grid.

This was a city that acted more like a planetary organ. Across the vast, raw expanse of the emerging rock, the infrastructure had been poured in wide, sweeping arcs of carbon-lattice and self-repairing ceramic. It did not look like a metropolis; it looked like a nervous system. High-altitude conduits, shimmering with a faint internal luminescence, bridged canyons that hadn't yet finished carving themselves. These were the main lines, humming with a low-frequency resonance that vibrated in the marrow of anyone who stood too close, a sound that was felt rather than heard, a thrumming counterpoint to the wind.

The water was everywhere, but it was shy. It didn't gather in grand lakes or raging rivers. Instead, it wept from the fresh-cut faces of the stone. It hung in the air as a persistent, clinging mist that condensed on the cold, metallic skins of the transit towers. It gathered in the shallow, silver scars of the basins—pockets of freezing, mineral-heavy fluid that reflected the bruised, violet light of the sun. These pools were the only places where the ground wasn't naked rock or polished alloy. 

Here, the vegetation was a stubborn, dark carpet of moss and cold-tolerant lichen, engineered to survive the high UV and the erratic moisture. It didn't grow tall; it spread wide, gripping the stones, pulling the meager minerals from the grit. Between these patches of green, the land remained an open, architectural void. There was space here—vast, echoing distances where the wind had room to build its own weather systems. You could stand on a ridge and watch a squall line, a mile-long wall of freezing vapor, roll across the plain, colliding with the energy-dampening fields of the city-grid in a shower of static discharge that danced across the rocks.

The fauna had adapted to the architecture. They weren't the loud, chaotic beasts of a lush jungle; they were the quiet, efficient inhabitants of the margins. Large, thermal-furred grazers moved in slow herds across the high meadows, their breath pluming in the cold, their movements heavy and deliberate. They fed on the engineered lichens, their metabolisms synced to the rhythmic pulses of the city’s power lines, which they seemed to find comforting. Overhead, raptors with wingspans that defied standard aerodynamics—assisted by sub-dermal lift-assist implants—circled the thermal vents, their eyes tracking the small, scurrying creatures that lived in the vents’ warmth.

The rules here were simple: everything was moving, and nothing was settled. The city-tech monitored the planet’s growth, adjusting the pressure on the rising crust to prevent catastrophic seismic events. It was a titanic act of gardening. If the sensors detected a spike in magma pressure, the grid would bleed off the energy, sending a pulse of heat into the atmosphere that triggered a localized, artificial lightning storm—a brief, violent illumination that turned the mist into a strobe-lit theater of white and gold.

To walk through the Arteries was to feel profoundly small. The horizon didn't just curve; it seemed to stretch away into an eternity of rising stone and glowing wire. The silence was heavy, punctuated only by the occasional crack of a rock splitting under the pressure of its own creation or the distant, rhythmic thrum of a transport vessel sliding through the magnetic levitation tracks that hung suspended over the abyss. It was a place of cold, clean, terrifying potential—a world being built in real-time, where the inhabitants lived in the pockets of stability carved out of the raw, cold, and beautiful chaos.
