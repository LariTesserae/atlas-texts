# The Churning Canopy

High on the face of a mountain that has barely finished being born, there is a gorge. It is narrow — a wound the earth opened and has not yet learned to close. The walls are fresh basalt, black and glassy, their fracture-planes still sharp enough to catch. Every ledge is crowded with green.

The mountain breathes water. Not rivers — not yet — but a constant seeping, a weeping from every crack in the new stone. Condensation gathers on the underside of overhangs and falls in irregular percussion. Rivulets thread down the walls, braiding and rebraiding, finding no settled course because the mountain keeps rearranging itself. Every few hours, some part of the gorge shifts: a slab calves off the east wall and thunders to the floor, a fissure widens, a shelf collapses into the churning froth at the base. The whole place is in a state of geological adolescence — violent, rapid, unfinished.

And yet the vegetation is overwhelming. Mosses so thick they hang like pelt, three layers deep, each stratum a different green — the oldest almost black, the newest a screaming lime. Ferns crowd every horizontal surface, their fronds interlocking so densely that light cannot pass between them. Not that there is much light to begin with.

The darkness here is not the darkness of night. It is the darkness of depth and density. The gorge is a slot, and what sky is visible from the floor is a pale grey seam, mostly obscured by the overlapping canopy that bridges from wall to wall. Plants that should need sun have found a way without it, or have simply not yet learned that they do. They grow toward the moisture, toward the vibration, toward the sound.

The sound is enormous.

At the base of the gorge, where the water has pooled and found a crack to drain through, the mountain has inadvertently built an engine. The water drives downward through a narrow vent in the basalt, and the vent acts as a resonating chamber — a natural piston system, water and air compressing and releasing in a cycle that produces a deep, rhythmic *whump-whump-whump* that shakes the walls. It is loud enough to feel in the sternum. The ferns tremble with each pulse. Loose stones dance on ledges. The mosses have grown their root-structures in patterns that match the frequency — concentric whorls, ripple-formed, as though the sound itself is a gardener.

This is the only machine here, and the mountain built it by accident. But it is industrial in character — repetitive, relentless, productive. What it produces is more mountain. Each pulse drives water deeper into the stone, and the stone cracks, and the cracks become channels, and the channels become new chambers, and the chambers add new voices to the rhythm. The gorge is manufacturing itself. It is a factory whose product is its own expansion, whose workers are physics and time.

The vegetation responds to this as though it understands. Root systems penetrate the fresh cracks within days of their opening. Spores ride the pressure-bursts from the vent, carried upward in explosive exhalations that coat every surface in a fine, fertile mist. The growth rate is absurd — a fern uncurled in the morning may be knee-high by the next. Everything is racing. Everything is urgent. The plants grow so fast they sometimes split their own skins, and the sap that weeps from these growth-wounds feeds the mosses, which thicken, which crowd, which press against each other in a vertical wall of green competition so dense it has the texture of solid matter.

There are no animals. Nothing moves here except plant and stone and water. The absence is not noticed — it is not an absence. The gorge is full. There is no room in the acoustic and physical chaos for the deliberation of animal life. A creature would need stillness to navigate, silence to listen, patience to hunt or graze. The gorge permits none of these. It is too loud, too fast, too crowded with growth and collapse.

The air is cool — not cold, but the cool of wet stone and shade and altitude. Breath does not fog, but it comes shorter than it should. The elevation thins the atmosphere, and the density of vegetation, for all its green abundance, does not compensate. The air tastes of iron and chlorophyll and the particular mineral sharpness of freshly broken rock.

The turbulence is constant and multi-layered. On the scale of seconds: the pulse of the water-engine, the drip and spatter of condensation, the rustle of ferns responding to vibration. On the scale of minutes: stones shifting, small avalanches of loose scree, the sudden crack-and-fall of a root-bound boulder losing its grip. On the scale of hours: the larger calving events, slabs of basalt the size of doors shearing away from walls and crashing through the canopy, leaving temporary holes in the green that admit brief, shocking shafts of grey light before the plants close over them again, voracious, within a day.

On the scale of days — if anyone were here to observe on the scale of days — the gorge visibly changes shape. The walls are moving apart, slowly, driven by the hydraulic wedging of water in the cracks. The floor is rising, built up by debris. The vent at the base migrates. The rhythm of the engine shifts key. What was a narrow pool becomes a broad one. What was a waterfall becomes a seep. The entire system is in flux, and the flux is the norm. There is no stable state. There is only the current configuration, which is already becoming the next one.

At the gorge's narrowest point — where the walls are perhaps three meters apart — the vegetation has achieved something remarkable. The ferns and mosses from opposing walls have met and interlocked, forming a living bridge, dense and fibrous, thick enough to bear weight. This bridge is not intentional. It is the inevitable result of too much growth in too little space. But it has consequences: below the bridge, the gorge is a sealed chamber, a green room with stone walls and a thundering heart. Above it, the gorge continues, but differently — airier, more exposed, the vegetation thinner because the moisture is less trapped.

The chamber below the bridge is the gorge's inner sanctum. It is here that the darkness is most complete, the sound most overwhelming, the growth most frenzied. The walls are upholstered in moss to a depth of half a meter. Ferns grow not from the floor — there is no floor, only churning water — but from the walls themselves, horizontal, their fronds weaving into the moss to form a continuous living surface that shudders with each pulse of the engine. The water in the chamber is white with turbulence, aerated to the consistency of foam, and the foam rises and falls with the piston-cycle like a chest breathing.

Nothing here was made by hands. There are no tools, no marks, no paths. But the gorge has the feeling of a built thing — of architecture — because the forces at work are so specific, so repetitive, so structured. The vent is a machine part. The canopy-bridge is a roof. The moss-walls are insulation. The whole system processes water and stone and time and produces more of itself. It is a self-building engine, and it has not finished.

The scale is intimate despite the violence. You could stand in the gorge — if you could find footing, if you could bear the noise, if you did not mind the falling stones — and touch both walls in some places. It is not vast. It is concentrated. All of this energy, this growth, this geological fury, compressed into a space the size of a cathedral nave. The compression is the point. The narrowness forces the interaction. The walls force the plants to compete. The competition forces the density. The density forces the darkness. The darkness forces the plants to adapt or die, and they adapt, and the adaptation produces more growth, and the growth produces more density.

It is a feedback loop wearing the costume of a landscape.

The young stone gleams where water runs over it — obsidian-bright, mirror-slick. In the brief moments when the grey seam of sky aligns with a gap in the canopy, thin light enters and the wet rock flashes like a signal. But the gaps close fast. The green is always closing in.

At the top of the gorge, where the slot opens onto the mountain's shoulder, the world changes abruptly. The vegetation thins to alpine scrub. The sound fades to a muffled throb, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. The wind — absent in the gorge, where the walls block it — returns with sudden cold force. Looking back down into the slot, you see only green, a dense and churning green that moves without wind, stirred from below by the machine-breath of the mountain, trembling and growing and tearing itself apart and growing again.

The gorge is perhaps a century old. It may not survive another. The same forces that built it are dismantling it, and the same forces dismantling it are building what comes next. It does not care. It is not a thing that persists. It is a thing that *happens*, continuously, loudly, in the dark, crowded with life that has never seen the sun and does not miss it.

The pulse continues. *Whump. Whump. Whump.*

The moss grows another fraction of a millimeter. A stone falls. The water finds a new path. The mountain turns in its sleep, and the gorge dreams with it — a green dream, a loud dream, a dream of becoming.
