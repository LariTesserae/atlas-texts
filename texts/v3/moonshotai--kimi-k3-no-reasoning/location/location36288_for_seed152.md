# The Morning That Is Still Being Born

You arrive at it by crouching. The entrance is a seam between two slabs of fresh basalt, still warm from the interior, and the seam opens downward into a space no larger than a chapel's side-room — perhaps forty paces across at its widest, though the dimensions shift depending on where you stand, because the walls themselves have not yet decided to be walls. They are mid-becoming. Stone in a state of recent arrival: crusts of dark glass where lava cooled last season, last month, perhaps last night, and alongside these the raw gray crumble of rock that has barely had time to remember it is rock. Nothing here has been worn smooth. Everything is edge, angle, roughness — a geometry without precedent. The floor is littered with stones that have not yet been weathered into roundness, and they sit at every possible tilt, as though the earth coughed them up and is still deciding whether to take them back.

## The Water

It is damp, always damp, but never wet in the way of rivers or floods. Moisture here is a conversation rather than a statement. It beads on the undersides of rock overhangs — slow accumulations of condensation that gather over hours until the drop grows heavy enough to let go, falling a hand's width or a body's length onto the floor below, where it strikes with a small, precise *tock* that is, for long stretches, the only sound in the place. These falls are irregular. You might wait half a minute, or five, before the next. The rhythm cannot be learned. Each drop lands in a shallow depression it has not yet carved — because nothing here has had time to carve anything — and the water sits there, or doesn't, or seeps into a crack and vanishes, feeding something below that you cannot see but can almost sense, a root or a mycelial thread, reaching.

Pockets of water collect in the natural basins of the uneven floor: some no wider than a cupped palm, others broad enough to reflect the dim ceiling. They are warm — body-warm, or warmer — because the stone beneath is warm, and the air above them carries a faint mineral breath, not unpleasant, like the smell of a struck match long after the flame has gone out, or of rain on hot pavement though no rain falls here. The dampness is internal. This place sweats.

## The Light

The light is dim — not the dimness of night or of a windowless room, but the dimness of something filtered through layers of not-quite-opacity. There are gaps overhead, where the vault of new stone has not fully sealed itself: hairline fractures, a fissure no wider than two fingers, through which light enters in thin, angled columns that the moisture-heavy air makes visible. These columns shift slowly as the sun moves — or as whatever light source exists above moves — and where they strike the floor they illuminate small islands of gold against a general blue-gray gloom. The effect is of a cathedral under construction, with the roof not yet finished, light arriving as scaffolding rather than architecture.

In the dimmer corners, away from the shafts, a faint bioluminescence does some of the work. Not dramatic — no glowing forests — but a soft, almost subliminal green-blue that emanates from patches of moss and from the undersides of certain fungi, so faint that you notice it only when your eyes have fully adjusted, and then you wonder how long it has been there and whether it has been getting brighter or whether you are simply learning to see it.

## The Settlement

There is a settlement here, and it is one of the strangest things about this place — not because it is grand or elaborate, but because it exists at all in a landscape so young. It is not a city, not even a town by any ordinary measure, but it has the *intention* of one. Structures — if you can call them that — rise from the uneven floor in a loose arrangement that suggests organization, pathways, districts, a center. But the builders are not human, and the materials are not quarried or cut. The structures are biological: towers of compacted mineral saliva, ribbed and spiraling, built by something like termites — though larger, slower, and with a more patient aesthetic. Each tower is perhaps knee-height to a person, or chest-height, and they are spaced with a kind of deliberateness, connected by low arches and bridges of the same hardened secretion, forming a lattice that follows no grid but has a clear internal logic.

The towers are warm. You can feel the heat radiating from them if you hold your hand close — the metabolic warmth of the colony inside, thousands of bodies in slow, coordinated labor. They hum faintly, not audibly but in a frequency you feel in your sternum if you stand near enough. Some towers are old — by the standards of this young place, meaning they were started perhaps a season ago — and these are darker, denser, their surfaces hardened to a ceramic-like shell. Others are new: pale, damp, still soft enough that you could press a thumbprint into them, though you would not, because the builders respond to disturbance with a quiet, orderly alarm that manifests as a shift in the humming, a tightening of the frequency, and a slow emergence of small, dark, glistening workers from hidden openings — not aggressive, but present, watching, waiting.

The settlement occupies perhaps a third of the chamber. The rest is left open. Whether this is by design or by the simple fact that the colony has not yet expanded into it, you cannot tell. There is a sense of deliberate restraint — as if the builders understand that this place is still forming, that the walls may shift, that the floor may crack, and that it would be unwise to overcommit.

## The Vegetation

Lush is not quite the right word — lushness usually implies abundance of growth, profusion, greenness. What grows here is lush in its density and ambition but strange in its palette and its structure. The dominant growth is a kind of moss — but calling it moss is like calling a forest "some trees." It blankets every surface that has remained stable for more than a few days: walls, floor, the lower reaches of the settlement towers, the rims of the water pools. It is thick — fingers-deep in places — and it is not green but a deep, warm coppery-red, the color of old pennies, with fronds so fine and densely packed that the surface appears almost velvet. When the condensation drops fall from above and strike this moss, the impact is absorbed silently, and the moss darkens briefly at the point of contact, drinking, then slowly returns to its resting hue as the moisture distributes through its body.

Emerging from the moss — sparse, scattered, each one alone with much empty space around it — are what you might call plants, though they are unlike any you have seen. They are tall and thin, reaching toward the light shafts with a single unbranched stem, translucent, almost glass-like, through which you can see a slow, rhythmic pulse of darker fluid rising and falling — not photosynthesis exactly, but something analogous, something that feeds on the warmth from below as much as on the light from above. Each stem is topped with a structure like an open hand, fingers spread, and the fingers are coated with a fine, sticky secretion that traps — what? Dust, spores, the minute airborne life of this sealed world. The hands are slightly cupped, as though waiting to receive something, and when a drop of condensation falls from the ceiling and happens to land in one, the fingers close slowly — very slowly, over the course of an hour — and hold the water until it is absorbed.

There are not many of these plants. Perhaps a dozen in the entire chamber. Each one stands alone, several paces from its nearest neighbor, and the moss between them is undisturbed, so the effect is of a sparse garden — or a congregation — solitary figures in quiet, perpetual prayer.

## The Fauna

Life moves here, though quietly. The termite-like builders are the most organized presence, but they are not the only ones. Small, pale creatures — somewhere between salamander and crustacean — navigate the pools and the damp moss on six jointed legs, their bodies translucent enough that you can see the dark line of their gut, the faint pulse of whatever passes for a heart. They are slow, deliberate, unhurried. They have no eyes — no need for them in this dimness — but they navigate by touch and by sensitivity to temperature gradients, and they are drawn to the warmest spots: the base of the settlement towers, the edges of the hottest pools.

Something lives in the water pockets. You see it only as movement — a flicker, a darting shadow beneath the surface of a pool no deeper than your thumb. It might be a fish, might be an insect larva, might be something with no analogue in any taxonomy you know. It is fast, and it is shy, and it vanishes when you lean close, leaving only a ripple that spreads slowly across the tiny surface and is absorbed at the edges by the moss.

Overhead, in the fissures where the light enters, something else lives — you hear it rarely, a faint, papery rustling, like wings folding. You never see it clearly. It occupies the boundary between this enclosed world and whatever lies above, and it comes and goes through the cracks, carrying with it — you imagine — spores, dust, news of the outside. It is the only thing here that seems to move between worlds, and its presence gives the enclosed space a sense of permeability, of connection to a larger system you cannot see.

There is a predator here, too, though you might not recognize it as one. It is a slow, flat organism — like a slug crossed with a lichen — that adheres to the ceiling and walls, moving so gradually that its motion is invisible in real time. But over hours, over days, it repositions itself, and where it has been, the moss is gone — consumed — leaving a clean, pale trail on the rock. The six-legged creatures avoid these trails. The glass-plants, rooted and helpless, can only lean. The predator is not fast, not fierce, not dramatic. It simply persists, and everything else in this small world makes quiet, constant accommodation for its presence.

## The Sound

Silence, or near-silence, is the ground state. The occasional *tock* of a condensation drop. The almost-inaudible hum of the settlement towers, felt more than heard. The faint, wet sound of something moving through moss — soft, like a finger drawn across damp cloth. The rustle of the unseen thing in the ceiling fissures, brief and infrequent. And beneath all of this, so low it might be your own body you are hearing: the slow, deep ticking of the rock itself, cooling, settling, contracting, adjusting to its new existence as solid matter. The sound of geology happening in real time. The sound of a world being born, and finding the process slightly uncomfortable.

## The Rules of This World

This is a young place, and its youth is its defining characteristic. Everything here is provisional. The walls have not finished cooling. The floor has not finished settling. The water has not yet carved channels or pools with defined edges — it simply sits where it falls, in the shallow accidents of the terrain, and waits for time to give those accidents the appearance of intention. The vegetation has not yet competed, has not yet sorted itself into winners and losers — it grows where it can, in the tentative, exploratory way of a first draft. The settlement is mid-construction, and will always be mid-construction, because the builders build not toward completion but toward continuation, adding and adjusting and repairing in response to the shifting ground, the changing moisture, the slow evolution of the chamber they inhabit.

Nothing here has a history. Everything here has a future. And the difference between those two states is the thin, warm, damp, dim, quiet, trembling present in which you stand — a single moment in the life of a world that is still, very literally, deciding what it will become.

The air is warm and close. Your breath fogs slightly, not from cold but from the humidity, and your sweat does not evaporate. You are aware of your own body as a foreign object here — too large, too loud, too dry. The place tolerates you the way it tolerates the predator on the ceiling: without alarm, without welcome, with the vast, slow patience of something that was here before you arrived and will be here — changed, always changing — long after you have gone.
