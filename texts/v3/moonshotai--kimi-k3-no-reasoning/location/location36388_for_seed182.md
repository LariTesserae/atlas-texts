# The Condensation Warrens of Vex-Under

---

## First Impression: Descent

You come down into it. That is the only way in. The surface above — whatever dry, bright, windswept thing it is — does not matter here. What matters is the stairwell, or the freight elevator cage, or the slumped concrete ramp with its rusted guide-rails, all of which lead you below the crust into a place that is warm the way a mouth is warm.

The air hits you first. Not heat like a desert — heat like breath. Dense, close, carrying a wetness that is not quite rain and not quite fog but something in between: a humid exhalation from ten thousand surfaces all weeping at once. Your skin dampens within seconds. Your clothes begin to cling. Every inhale tastes of minerals, machine oil, and something faintly sweet that might be mold or might be flowering moss or might be both.

The light is amber and insufficient. It comes from caged bulbs strung along cable runs, from furnace-glow seeping through grate floors, from bioluminescent lichen that grows in pale green constellations on pipe flanges. None of it is bright enough to read by. All of it is enough to navigate by, barely, if you let your eyes adjust and accept that shadows here are not empty — they are just full of things you cannot see.

You are in a city. Underground. Hot, wet, crowded, and old-young — built recently, built fast, and already changing into something its builders did not plan.

## Terrain & Structure: The Hollow That Fills Itself

Vex-Under sits in a geological pocket — a void in the bedrock that was either natural (a dissolved limestone chamber, a collapsed lava tube) or excavated (a mined-out seam, a hollowed reservoir) or some combination of both. The residents have long since stopped distinguishing. What matters is that the space is bounded — roughly four hundred meters across at its widest, perhaps seventy meters floor-to-ceiling at its tallest vault — and that every cubic centimeter of it has been claimed.

The architecture is **vertical and interstitial**. There is no open plaza, no grand avenue, no negative space. Buildings do not stand apart from one another; they lean, bridge, fuse. A three-story tenement of corrugated iron shares its western wall with a fungal greenhouse and its roof with a water-cistern platform that also serves as someone's vegetable garden and someone else's chicken coop. Catwalks cross the void at six different heights, sagging under the weight of conduit bundles, laundry lines, and the occasional entire dwelling suspended in the gap like a swallow's nest.

The floor — what remains visible of the original stone — is uneven, sloping toward a central drainage sump that never quite empties. Over it, layers of grating, planking, poured concrete, and scavenged metal plate create a palimpsest of walking surfaces, each layer a few centimeters above the last, so that the effective ground level of Vex-Under rises roughly a millimeter every year. The oldest residents remember when you could see bedrock on Copper Lane. Now you walk on someone's grandfather's ceiling.

Materials are scavenged, industrial, and organic in roughly equal measure:

- **Rusted steel** — girders, plates, pipes, drums — sweats constantly, weeping orange rivulets that stain everything below them
- **Concrete** — poured in haste, crumbling at edges, colonized by moss
- **Brick** — old kilns somewhere below fired them; they line the deepest tunnels
- **Timber** — rare, precious, warped by humidity into curves and used only where flexibility matters (door hinges, tool handles, the frames of beds)
- **Woven fiber** — from processed fungal chitin and vine bast, used for screens, bags, hammocks, partition walls
- **Glass** — salvaged bottles melted and re-cast into thick, greenish panes that let furnace-light pass between levels

Nothing is plumb. Nothing is level. The whole settlement has the quality of a wasp nest crossed with a ship's engine room: purposeful, dense, improvised, and vibrating with a low-grade energy you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears.

## Water: The True Citizen

Water is everywhere in Vex-Under, but it is never abundant. It arrives by condensation, by seepage, by the occasional drip-fed pipe from an aquifer tap that someone maintains with obsessive care. It moves through the settlement the way blood moves through a body — in small vessels, under pressure, essential and contested.

**The Walls Weep.** Every surface below a certain depth is covered in a film of moisture. This is not flooding; it is a persistent, patient dampness that never dries. Metal corrodes. Concrete spalls. Wood swells. But the moisture also sustains: moss gardens on every north-facing wall, slime molds that glow faintly in the dark corners, vines that root in condensation traps and climb forty meters of cable-runs to reach a heat vent where they flower.

**The Cisterns.** Water collection is the settlement's primary infrastructure. Condensation coils — kilometers of copper tubing run along the coolest walls — drip into collection troughs. These feed public cisterns (large, communal, guarded) and private tanks (small, hidden, locked). The quality of water varies enormously: some cisterns are clear and cold, others are green with algae, others smell of sulfur and are used only for washing or industrial coolant.

**The Sump.** At the lowest point, where all drainage converges, there is a pool. It is perhaps ten meters across, shallow, warm, and opaque. Things live in it — pale amphibious things, bacterial mats that form floating skin, a type of blind crustacean the locals call "sump-crawlers" and occasionally eat. The pool exhales a warm mist that rises through the settlement's central void and condenses on the ceilings of the uppermost levels, where it is collected again. Vex-Under's water cycle is closed, slow, and slightly toxic, and everyone who lives here has adapted to it the way you adapt to a relative who is difficult but indispensable.

**Rain.** There is no rain. But there are *drip-events* — moments when condensation on the great ceiling vault reaches a critical threshold and releases in a scattered, percussion-less patter that lasts anywhere from seconds to hours. Residents call these "weeps" and treat them with the casual attentiveness of farmers watching weather. A heavy weep can fill a cistern. A light one just makes the walkways slick.

## Vegetation: The Green Insurgency

For a place underground, Vex-Under is astonishingly alive. The vegetation here is not the vegetation of sunlight — no trees, no grasses, nothing that photosynthesizes in the traditional sense. Instead, the ecology is **chemosynthetic, thermosynthetic, and scavenger-based**, fed by heat, by mineral-rich moisture, and by the metabolic byproducts of industry.

**The Vines.** Dominant. Everywhere. They are thick-stemmed, waxy-leafed, a deep green-black in color, and they grow along anything that conducts heat — pipe runs, cable bundles, the warm sides of furnaces. Their roots seek moisture; their leaves seek warmth. They produce no flowers visible to the human eye, but they set fruit — small, dark, oily berries that are edible if boiled and mildly hallucinogenic if eaten raw. The vines are not cultivated; they are *tolerated*, pruned back from doorways and walkways, harvested for fiber and fruit, but never eradicated because everyone understands, without being told, that the vines are doing something structural — holding the walls together, perhaps, or filtering the air, or simply being the thing that makes Vex-Under alive rather than merely inhabited.

**Fungal Terraces.** Deliberate agriculture. The lower levels, where heat from the geothermal vents is strongest, are terraced into growing beds. The fungus cultivated here is not mushroom-like but *mat-like*: broad, shelf-forming growths that spread across prepared substrate beds and are harvested in sheets. The primary species, called **velvet** by locals, is pale gold, protein-rich, and has a texture between firm tofu and soft leather. It is the staple food. Secondary species include a bioluminescent mat grown for light (the pale green constellations mentioned earlier) and a medicinal mold that, when dried and smoked, alleviates the respiratory complaints that are endemic in Vex-Under's humid, particulate-laden air.

**Moss Gardens.** Everywhere. On walls, on walkways, on the undersides of stairs, on the rims of cisterns, on the backs of doors. The moss is not planted; it arrives, colonizes, persists. It is a dozen species at least, ranging in color from silver-green to rust-red to a startling deep violet that appears only near the sulfur vents. Some residents cultivate particular patches — encouraging them, trimming them into shapes, using them as insulation or packing material or simply as beauty. A home without moss is considered either very new or very poor.

**The Bloom.** Occasionally — perhaps once every few years — a section of vine will flower. The flowers are large, pale, and last only a single night. They open after the evening shift change, when the settlement is at its quietest, and by morning they have collapsed into a fragrant sludge. The event is celebrated. Residents gather at the blooming site, light lanterns, share food, and breathe the perfume, which is described variously as "like warm bread," "like the inside of a seashell," and "like remembering something you never knew." Whether the bloom is triggered by temperature, humidity, or some deeper cycle in the bedrock is unknown. It is one of the few events in Vex-Under that is genuinely surprising.

## Heat & Industry: The Body's Engine

The heat in Vex-Under is **industrial and geological in roughly equal parts**. Geothermal vents in the deepest levels provide baseline warmth — the bedrock here is close to something hot, and the heat rises through the stone. But the settlement's own machinery amplifies this considerably.

**The Works.** At the deepest accessible level, adjacent to the sump, a cluster of machines — steam-driven generators, water-purification units, air-circulation pumps, a small forge — operates continuously. These are not sleek or efficient. They are massive, loud-in-a-low-way (a bass thrum more felt than heard), and maintained by a hereditary guild of engineers who guard their knowledge with the ferocity of priests. The machines produce electricity (dim, rationed), purified water (the good cisterns), and mechanical power for the freight elevators and ventilation fans that keep the air marginally breathable.

**Heat Stratification.** Temperature in Vex-Under varies by depth and proximity to the Works. The lowest levels, near the sump and the machines, are genuinely hot — 40°C or more, with humidity near saturation. Workers in the Works wear minimal clothing and drink constantly. The middle levels, where most habitation occurs, are warm and wet — 30–35°C, like a tropical night that never ends. The uppermost levels, near the ceiling vault, are marginally cooler — perhaps 28°C — and this is where the oldest and wealthiest residents live, in chambers carved from the original stone, above the worst of the moisture, with access to the largest condensation surfaces.

**The Glow.** Furnace-light is the primary illumination in the lower levels. It is orange, flickering, and warm in every sense. It casts long shadows that move slowly as the fires breathe. Residents who live near the Works have skin tinted faintly amber from years of this light. They can be identified in a crowd the way you can identify someone who has spent years at sea — not by any single feature, but by an overall quality of having been shaped by their element.

## Sound: The Hush That Hums

Vex-Under is quiet. Not silent — silence is impossible in a place this dense with machinery and life — but quiet in the way that a library is quiet, or a hospital ward at 3 AM. The dominant sounds are:

- **Dripping.** Constant, arrhythmic, from every surface. After a few hours, you stop hearing it. After a few days, you cannot sleep without it.
- **The Thrum.** The Works' bass note, a frequency just below conscious hearing, felt in the chest and the soles of the feet. It is the settlement's heartbeat. When it changes — when a machine is shut down for maintenance, when a new load comes online — everyone notices, even if they cannot say what changed.
- **The Fans.** Large, slow-turning ventilation fans in the upper levels, pushing air in languid cycles. They creak. The creaking is rhythmic and soporific, like a porch swing in slow motion.
- **Human sounds, muffled.** Voices are low here. Partly this is courtesy — in a place this dense, loudness is a form of aggression — and partly it is physics: the humid air absorbs sound, dampening high frequencies and swallowing echoes. A conversation on a catwalk three meters away is unintelligible. This creates a strange intimacy: you live surrounded by hundreds of people and yet experience a privacy that would be impossible in a surface city.
- **The Weeps.** When condensation releases from the ceiling, the patter is soft and widespread, like fingertips drumming on a vast skin. It is the closest thing Vex-Under has to weather, and it is noticed and remarked upon the way surface-dwellers remark on rain.

Music exists, but it is quiet music: low drums, humming, instruments made from hollow pipes that resonate at frequencies felt in the bones. There is a tradition of "thrum-song" — vocal harmonization with the machine-frequency of the Works — that is practiced by some residents as meditation and by others as a form of communion with the settlement itself, as though Vex-Under were a living thing whose voice you could learn to sing with.

## Fauna: The Few Who Share the Space

Animals are rare in Vex-Under. The environment — hot, wet, dim, dense with human activity — supports a limited fauna:

- **Sump-crawlers** — the blind crustaceans of the central pool, hand-sized, pale, edible in a pinch
- **Pipe-moths** — small, gray, attracted to heat-vents; their wings are dusted with a powder that glows faintly under UV light
- **Wall-skinks** — small lizard-like amphibians that cling to vertical surfaces and eat moss and moths; they are translucent-skinned, and in the right light you can see their organs working
- **The Wardens** — a semi-domesticated species of large, slow-moving beetle, perhaps thirty centimeters long, that residents keep in their homes for pest control (they eat mold and small insects) and, to some extent, companionship. Wardens are long-lived — some are older than their keepers — and are passed down through families. They have iridescent carapaces that range from deep green to bronze, and they make a soft clicking sound when content. A home with a well-fed Warden is considered prosperous.

There are no birds. No mammals larger than a rat (and the rats, ironically, are the rarest animals — the humidity and heat are hard on them, and the Wardens keep their numbers suppressed). The ecological niches that surface animals would fill are occupied, instead, by the vines, the fungi, and the machines.

## Rules of the World

Vex-Under operates by a set of implicit and explicit rules that emerge from its physics and its sociology:

**1. Water is currency.** Not metaphorically — literally. Access to clean water is the basis of all exchange. Cistern-shares are inherited, traded, gambled, and fought over. The water-guilds — loose associations of cistern-owners and condensation-coil maintainers — are the de facto government.

**2. Verticality is status.** Higher is better. Cooler. Drier. The upper levels are the province of old families, successful water-guilders, and the engineers of the Works. The lower levels are for laborers, newcomers, and those who have fallen. Social mobility in Vex-Under is literally upward.

**3. Space is the scarcest resource.** Every cubic meter is claimed, used, contested. New construction means building over, under, or through something that already exists. The settlement's architecture is a record of negotiations — some peaceful, some not — between neighbors competing for room. The density is such that you are never more than two meters from another human being, even in sleep. Walls are thin. Privacy is acoustic, not visual: you hear everything but learn to act as though you haven't.

**4. The Works must not stop.** This is the closest thing Vex-Under has to a religious law. If the machines fail — if the pumps stop, if the fans cease — the settlement dies within days. The engineers who maintain them are exempt from most social obligations, paid in water and food and deference, and subject to a single absolute commandment: *keep it running.*

**5. What grows here belongs here.** The vines, the moss, the fungi — they are not decorations or crops. They are co-inhabitants. Destroying a vine network or a moss garden is a crime analogous to arson. Cultivating them is a civic virtue. The boundary between "built environment" and "ecology" has dissolved, and the residents understand, even if they cannot articulate, that the green things are performing functions — air filtration, structural stabilization, psychological sustenance — that the machines alone cannot.

**6. The surface is irrelevant.** Whatever is up there — desert, ruin, wilderness, other settlements — does not concern Vex-Under. The entrance tunnels are guarded, and goods and people occasionally pass through, but the settlement's attention is turned inward and downward. The surface is a rumor, a memory, a threat. What matters is below.

## Time & Change: The Slow Turning

Nothing in Vex-Under happens quickly. The settlement's rhythms are those of condensation (droplets forming, growing, releasing over hours), of vine growth (centimeters per week), of water-table fluctuations (seasonal, maybe — it is hard to tell without a sky), of machine wear (years between major failures), and of human generational turnover (children born in the lower levels aspire to the upper; the upper levels slowly fill; the slow accretion of walkways and platforms raises the floor; the settlement's center of gravity shifts).

**The Great Weep.** Roughly every seven years (though the interval is irregular), the condensation cycle reaches a peak: the ceiling vault becomes saturated, and the settlement experiences a weep that lasts for days. During a Great Weep, the sump overflows, the lower levels flood to ankle depth, and the air becomes so saturated that visibility drops to meters. Great Weeps are dangerous (structural collapses increase) and celebrated (the vines grow explosively in the aftermath; cisterns are filled for months). Residents mark their lives by Great Weeps the way surface-dwellers mark theirs by droughts or storms.

**The Deepening.** There is a persistent, unconfirmed belief among some residents that Vex-Under is *sinking* — that the floor levels rise not only because of accumulated construction layers but because the entire chamber is slowly subsiding into the bedrock. Evidence is ambiguous: some wall-mounted benchmarks show displacement over decades; others do not. If the Deepening is real, it is imperceptible on any timescale shorter than a generation. But the belief persists, and it gives the settlement a particular philosophical texture: a sense that they are going somewhere, slowly, inevitably, into the earth, and that this is not a crisis but a destiny.

**The Bloom Cycles.** The vine-blooms, unpredictable and rare, serve as the settlement's only genuine surprise. Between blooms, Vex-Under's existence is steady, cyclical, and deeply patterned. The blooms rupture this. They remind the residents that they live inside a system they do not fully understand — that the vines have their own logic, the bedrock its own timeline, the water its own agenda. This is not frightening. It is, in a way, comforting: the settlement is not entirely of human making, and therefore its failures are not entirely human failures.

## Atmosphere: What It Feels Like to Be Here

To live in Vex-Under is to be **held**. The heat holds you. The moisture holds you. The density of structures and people and growing things holds you. There is no horizon. There is no sky. The farthest you can see in any direction is perhaps fifty meters, and usually much less. The world is close, textured, layered — a nest, a womb, a machine you live inside.

This produces a psychological state that visitors find either oppressive or profoundly soothing. There is no agoraphobia trigger here — no vast openness, no exposure. Instead, there is the opposite: a claustrophilia, a comfort in proximity, a sense that the world is *enough* — that its boundaries are its generosity. You cannot be lost in Vex-Under. You can always be found.

The light — dim, warm, shifting — does something to time. Without a day-night cycle, without seasonal change, hours blur. Shift schedules (the Works run continuously; someone is always awake, always working) create a polyphasic rhythm in which the concept of "night" is replaced by "quiet shift" and "loud shift." Residents sleep when tired, eat when hungry, and measure their days by the sounds around them: the fan-cycle, the weep-patter, the shift-change footsteps on the catwalks.

There is a smell to Vex-Under that is impossible to forget: warm metal, wet stone, green growing things, machine oil, fungal sweetness, human sweat, and the faint sulfuric undertone of the deep vents. It is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is *specific* — the olfactory signature of a place that exists nowhere else, that could exist nowhere else, that has made itself out of heat and water and necessity into something that its inhabitants, for all its hardships, call home.

## The Unnamed Quality

There is something else in Vex-Under — something that sits at the edge of the describable. The residents do not talk about it directly. It is not a religion, exactly, or a superstition, exactly, or a measurable phenomenon, exactly.

It is the sense that the settlement is **becoming** something. Not growing — it has no room to grow. Not evolving — its technology is static, its population stable. But *becoming*, in the way that a crystal becomes itself: not by expanding but by intensifying, by filling its own structure more completely, by turning inward toward a complexity that has no external expression.

The vines are part of this. Their growth patterns are not random — they follow the heat-pipes and the cable-runs, yes, but they also trace paths that serve no obvious function: spiraling around pillars, forming lattices across open voids, creating patterns that some residents interpret as decorative and others as *communicative*, as though the vines are writing something on the architecture that no one has yet learned to read.

The thrum is part of this too. The machine-frequency of the Works is not constant. It drifts — slowly, over months — and the drift correlates, some engineers believe, with the growth cycles of the fungi, with the weep-patterns, with the bloom-intervals. As though the machines and the organisms and the geology are converging on a shared frequency, synchronizing, settling into resonance.

Whether this is real or projected — whether Vex-Under is genuinely becoming a single integrated organism-machine-system, or whether its residents are simply pattern-hungry primates seeing intention in coincidence — is an open question. It is, perhaps, the only open question in a place where almost everything else is bounded, known, and packed tight against its neighbors.

But you can feel it. Standing on a catwalk at the settlement's mid-level, vine-wrapped railing under your hands, warm mist on your face, thrum in your chest, dim amber light on wet stone all around you — you can feel that the place is not finished. That it is still arriving at itself. That the density is not just crowding but *compression*: the pressure that turns carbon into diamond, given enough time.

And Vex-Under has time. All the time in the world. It is not going anywhere. It is already where it is going.

It drips. It hums. It grows. It waits.
