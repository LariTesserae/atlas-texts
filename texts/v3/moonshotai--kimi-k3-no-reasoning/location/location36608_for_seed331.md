### The Weeping Basin of the First Flat

Here the world has worn itself down to the bone of its oldest idea. The ground is a single, almost featureless sheet of dark, water-polished stone, so ancient that every ripple and every micro-fracture has been erased by a trillion slow strokes of rain and root. It slopes so gently that the eye must travel for minutes to detect the fall, and even then it is only the faintest suggestion, like the memory of a mountain that has forgotten it was ever anything but level.

Water is everywhere, yet never deep. It lies in broad, shallow mirrors that shift with the day’s breathing; at dawn they are silver and tight, at noon they loosen into vast, trembling skins, and by dusk they have crept outward again, swallowing another centimeter of stone. The air itself condenses without warning: warm, heavy droplets bead on every surface, on your skin, on the undersides of leaves, on the lens of your eye. Nothing is ever dry for long.

The vegetation is restrained, almost ascetic. A single species dominates: a low, succulent rosette the colour of bruised jade, each leaf the size of a child’s hand, each rosette separated from its neighbours by exact, almost ceremonial distances. Between them the stone is naked, reflecting the sky so perfectly that the plants seem to float on two skies at once. Nothing grows taller than your knee. Nothing dares.

Yet the fauna is beyond counting. They are small, translucent, and relentless. Winged things like living prisms drift in loose, glittering clouds, so numerous that their bodies sometimes block the sun into a soft, moving twilight. Others skim the water’s surface in infinite, overlapping waves, their legs never quite breaking the tension. Beneath the stone, in the thin film of water that never quite leaves it, an invisible multitude churns; you feel them as a faint, continuous vibration through the soles of your feet, a subsonic purring that never stops. There is no menace in them, only abundance pressed to the edge of silence.

The built things are rare, and older than the stone they rest on. Here and there, half-sunk in the reflective sheet, stand slender columns of some substance that is not quite matter: they refract the light into slow, deliberate rainbows that crawl across the water like living things. Each column is exactly the same height, exactly the same impossible material, and each one hums at a frequency just below hearing. When the light strikes them at the correct angle (which happens every few hours, according to a rhythm no instrument has yet decoded), they unfold delicate, petal-like structures of pure geometry, releasing clouds of engineered micro-seeds that drift down to the water and become the prism-winged creatures. The cycle has repeated for longer than the basin has been flat.

There is no weather as we understand it. Instead there are processes: slow tides of humidity that rise and fall with the sun’s position, periodic brightenings when the columns bloom, and the constant, patient migration of the fauna in vast, overlapping spirals that never quite repeat. Predator and prey are the same creatures at different moments of their brief, luminous lives.

The light is merciless and beautiful. It falls straight down, white and heavy, turning every drop of water into a tiny lens until the entire basin sparkles like a field of scattered diamonds. Shadows are rare and therefore precious; when a cloud of wings passes overhead, the sudden soft darkness feels like a benediction.

Sound is almost absent. The fauna make no cry. The water makes no lap. Only the faintest, continuous hiss of condensation forming and re-forming, and beneath it the subsonic heartbeat of the hidden multitude. Sometimes, when the columns unfold, a single pure tone rings out, so low it is felt in the teeth rather than heard, and for a few seconds every reflective surface in the basin shivers in sympathy.

This is a place that has finished becoming. It has nowhere left to go. It simply continues, bright and wet and quietly, overwhelmingly alive, wearing its eternity like a skin of water that never quite dries.
