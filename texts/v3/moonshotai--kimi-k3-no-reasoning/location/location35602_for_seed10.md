**The Weeping Terraces of Mount Karth**

The first thing you notice is that the mountain is sweating.

Cold, viscous water beads on every surface of the ancient basalt—on the stepped terraces that climb toward the cloudline, on the hanging gardens of carnivorous moss, on the obsidian flanks of the great engines left by the Precursors. It condenses on your eyelashes. It runs down your collar. It tastes of iron and something older, something that suggests the mountain itself is breathing out through a million tiny pores.

This is a place where water has won. Not through violence, but through patience. Over ten thousand years, it has seeped into every fracture, every seam, every memory of solid stone, and turned the high places into a vast, vertical marsh. The terraces—some natural, some carved by hands that forgot they were hands long ago—hold shallow pools of black water that never freeze despite the cold, never quite drain despite the slope. They are connected by weeping walls, by cascades of fog, by rivulets that run upward when the wind changes.

The vegetation here is not so much growing as **occupying**. It has become the architecture. Towering stands of blood-bamboo, thirty feet tall and hollow as organ pipes, form colonnades that hold up the sky. Their roots do not anchor in soil—there is no soil, only stone and water—but wrap around the mountain’s bones like fingers. Between them, vast mats of *luminous sphagnum* glow with a faint, cold phosphorescence, providing the only reliable light in a landscape shrouded in perpetual dusk. The moss is deep enough to swallow a man to the waist, and it moves. Slowly. Breathing. When you step on it, it exhales a mist of spores that smell of ozone and cinnamon.

But the true strangeness of Mount Karth is the **mechanics**.

The ancient erosion that should have flattened this place has instead revealed its skeleton: a sprawling, incomprehensible infrastructure of rusted iron and ceramic conduits that honeycomb the mountain. They emerge from the rock like veins, then dive back in. They are not maintained. They are not understood. But they are *active*. Pistons the size of tree trunks rise and fall in the mist with the regularity of a heartbeat, pumping water from the deep aquifers to the surface. Great flywheels, half-encased in ice and ferns, turn slowly in the gloom, powering nothing, connected to nothing, simply refusing to stop.

The sound is tremendous. It is not the roar of industry—there are no factories here, no production—but the groan and shriek of a billion moving parts operating in defiance of entropy. The mountain **hums**, a bass note that vibrates in your teeth, punctuated by the rhythmic *clang-hiss* of steam valves releasing pressure, the *thwack* of bamboo striking bamboo in the wind, the wet slap of unseen creatures moving through the shallows.

And there are creatures. Abundant, impossible fauna that have adapted to a world that is breaking down.

The **glass eels** swim in the air. Or seem to. They are translucent, ribbon-like, and they move through the thick atmosphere by undulating against currents of moisture that are visible as faint, shimmering distortions in the dim light. They feed on the spores, and where they pass, they leave trails of frost that sparkle briefly before sublimating into nothing. On the higher terraces, **rust-hounds** prowl—six-legged mammals the size of wolves, their fur the color of old iron, their eyes glowing with the same phosphorescence as the moss. They hunt the eels, leaping improbably high to snatch them from the air, and when they land, the impact sends shockwaves through the saturated ground that cause the pools to ripple in perfect concentric circles.

But reality is thin here. The weirdness is not subtle.

The dynamic instability of the place means that the mountain is constantly **revising itself**. You will watch a stand of bamboo grow thirty feet in an hour, only to collapse into black sludge moments later, feeding a sudden bloom of bioluminescent fungi that devour the nutrients and die in turn. Pools form in depressions that were solid stone minutes before, then drain away through cracks that seal themselves behind the water. The Precursor engines, those ancient mechanisms, occasionally **shift**—grinding against their stone housings, reconfiguring their geometry in ways that hurt to observe. Yesterday, a conduit that had been dry for centuries began spewing not water, but a slow, viscous light that pooled on the ground like oil and evaporated into colors that have no names.

The density is wrong. Despite the abundance of life and machinery, there is too much space. The mountain is vast—open, regional in scale—and the presences within it are scattered. You might walk for an hour through corridors of weeping stone and see nothing but the occasional flicker of a glass eel, then stumble into a hollow where a hundred rust-hounds have gathered to sleep, their bodies piled together for warmth in the cold, their breathing synchronized with the mountain’s heartbeat.

The cold is not a simple cold. It is a **damp, penetrating cold** that carries the sound of the engines for miles. It keeps the vegetation brittle despite the wetness, creates ice that is not ice—clear, flexible membranes that form over the pools and shatter like glass when touched, only to reform within seconds.

At the summit, where the elevation touches the cloudline and the air is thin, there is a place where the erosion has exposed a chamber. It is not built—there are no walls, no tools, no artifacts—but it is *arranged*. The Precursor conduits converge there in a spiral pattern that seems to suggest intention. In the center, a pool of water that is perfectly still, perfectly black, and perfectly deep. It reflects nothing. When you look into it, you do not see yourself. You see the mountain as it was ten thousand years ago, or as it will be in ten thousand more—a vision of the place before the engines, or after they have finally stopped.

The turbulence is increasing. The water is rising. The engines are running faster, though their purpose remains opaque. The bamboo forests are migrating—slowly, imperceptibly, but measurably—downslope, retreating from something that is emerging from the deep stone. The rust-hounds have begun to howl at night, a sound that is not quite sound but a vibration in the bones, a warning that the thinness of reality here is about to tear.

You do not stay long on Mount Karth. No one does. It is not a place for habitation, only for passage. But you carry it with you—the taste of iron on your tongue, the image of light pooling like liquid, the sound of a mountain that refuses to die, even as it transforms into something that is no longer a mountain, but a machine, or a body, or a thought that the world is having about itself.
