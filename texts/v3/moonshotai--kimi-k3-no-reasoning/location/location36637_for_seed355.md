**The Drowned City of the Second Moon**

Heat radiates up from the paving stones as though the earth has a fever. The air is so wet it is barely air at all; it is a suspension, a warm soup that condenses on skin and stone alike, running in rivulets down walls that sweat more profusely than any mammal could. This is a city built not on a riverbank or a coast, but in a shallow, steaming basin where the water table has risen to meet the foundations, submerging streets knee-deep in a tea-colored brine that smells of iron and ancient decay. The water is everywhere, and it is warm—geothermally warm, or perhaps warm from some other source, some metabolic heat generated by the city itself as it slowly digests its own substance.

The buildings rise from this shallow, sun-warmed sea like the ribs of some vast beached creature. They are tall, narrow structures of a pale, salt-crusted stone that weeps continuously, their facades streaked with black algae and minerals deposited by centuries of evaporation. There is no glass in the windows—there never was, or it has long since surrendered to the humidity—and the doorways gape like toothless mouths, exhaling breaths of cooler, damper air. The architecture is dense, labyrinthine, a settlement of thousands of structures packed into a basin perhaps five miles across, connected by bridges of corroded metal and pontoons of a black, rubbery material that might be petrified wood or might be something else entirely. Yet for all this density, there is a quality of sparseness, of vast intervals. The plazas are empty, wide enough to hold crowds that never come. The streets are broad canals where the water sits still and reflective, broken only by the occasional pillar or the collapsed span of an arch. It is a city built for multitudes, now holding only space and heat and the slow drip of water.

Above, the sky is a darkness that is not quite night. It is a permanent eclipse, perhaps, or a veil of ash high in the stratosphere, or simply a wrongness in the way light behaves here. A single, swollen celestial body hangs low on the horizon—too large to be the moon, too dim to be the sun, a bruised, reddish orb that provides a heatless, colorless illumination, just enough to silhouette the towers against the void. This is the Second Moon, and it casts no shadows, or rather, it casts shadows that fall in the wrong directions, multiplying and overlapping in defiance of geometry. The darkness is not total; it is a deep, penetrating gloom that allows vision but denies clarity, a world seen through smoked glass.

There is no life here. No birds cry. No insects whine. No fish move in the warm shallows. The water, though it touches everything, is sterile, or perhaps occupied by microorganisms too strange and simple to be called fauna. The only vegetation is sparse and architectural: great, leafless pillars of a calcified fungus or mineralizing plant that mimic the built environment so closely it is impossible to tell where construction ends and growth begins. They rise like pale towers, hollow and resonant, and when the wind passes through them—which is rare, for the air is usually still—they produce a low, mournful humming, a sound so quiet it is felt in the teeth rather than heard.

This is the primary sound: the hum. Otherwise, silence reigns, broken only by the drip of condensation falling from ledge to water, a rhythmic, arrhythmic percussion that echoes in the vast emptiness. Occasionally, there is a deeper, more structural groan—the sound of stone settling, of reality adjusting itself.

For reality here is thin. The erosion is not the gentle wearing of wind and rain, but a slippage of substance. Buildings that appear solid from one angle reveal themselves, when viewed from another, to be facades only an inch thick, behind which yawns an absolute darkness that does not correspond to any interior space. Staircases ascend to platforms that overlook courtyards which could not possibly exist given the building’s exterior dimensions. The water, when disturbed, sometimes reflects not the dark sky and the Second Moon, but a blazing, impossible blue firmament and a sun that has never shone here. These are not hallucinations; they are features of the landscape, inconsistencies in the world’s rendering, gaps where the underlying structure of place shows through.

The heat makes the air shimmer, and in these shimmers, the city doubles. Ghost-images of towers overlay the real ones, slightly out of phase, creating a visual stutter, a sense that one is seeing the city’s past or future superimposed upon its present. Walking here requires navigating not just the physical water and stone, but these temporal refractions—patches of ground that are simultaneously solid pavement and open void, depending on which layer of reality one’s foot touches.

And the city is active. Despite the absence of inhabitants, of fauna, of anything that could be called a population, the place moves. The water rises and falls in a tidal pattern that does not correspond to the Second Moon’s pull, flooding certain districts at irregular intervals, then receding to reveal salt-crusted plazas that steam in the heat. The calcified towers grow, visibly, adding millimeters of new substance in the course of an hour, their surfaces crackling softly as they crystallize. The bridges shift, realigning themselves into new configurations when unobserved, altering the city’s geography in a slow, patient dance. New archways appear where none were; old streets vanish beneath mirror-calm water that does not ripple when it swallows the stone.

It is a machine without operators, a settlement without settlers, a wet, hot engine of urbanism running on some fuel of pure existence. The darkness presses down, and the heat presses up, and between them, the city continues its slow, reality-fraying work of being, its empty plazas echoing with the near-silent drip of water and the low, biological hum of stone that refuses to stop growing, even in the absence of light, even in the absence of life, even as the fabric of what is possible here wears thin enough to see through to the void beyond.
