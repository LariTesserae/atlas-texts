The city breathes.

Not metaphorically. The city is a single lung, or rather, a single vast vascular system—a cathedral of xylem and phloem built upon the bones of something older, something stone and grid-straight that the green has not so much swallowed as *replaced*. You stand at the perimeter where the ground drops away into a warm, shallow basin, and before you rises the Infrastructure: walls not of brick but of braided lianas thick as bridge cables, streets that are hollowed tubes of living wood, and towers—oh, the towers—great columns of fungal-stem and flowering bark that spiral upward not to catch light (though there is light, normal daylight filtering through the canopy) but to channel the Sound.

The Sound comes first, always. It is not noise; it is *density*, a physical pressure against the eardrums. It is the song of ten thousand winged things—not birds, not insects, but something between, with membranous sails and resonating chambers in their thoraxes—that circle the spires in swirling, predatory flocks. It is the percussive *thrum* of the city’s heartbeat, a hydraulic pulse that moves water and nutrient through the transparent streets. You can see the fluid moving beneath your feet: golden, slow, carrying bioluminescent spores. The Sound vibrates in your teeth. It is the population speaking, feeding, mating, dying. It is deafening.

Move inward, if you can find the gaps. The city is built at the scale of rooms, of intimate chambers, yet it contains multitudes of emptiness. Turn a corner—duck under a low-hanging bladder-fruit that pulses with warm rain—and you enter a nave of space so vast and vacant it feels like a held breath. Here, the vegetation forms the architecture: great arches of strangler fig that have long since consumed their hosts, leaving hollow, ribbed tunnels. The floors are not stone but compacted root-mass, springy, exhaling the scent of wet earth and ozone. These are the Empty Places, the city’s lungs, where nothing grows and nothing builds, where the fauna do not enter. Here the Sound falls away to a subsonic vibration, and you can hear your own blood.

But these voids are small. The city is dense with life elsewhere—life that is green, photosynthetic, predatory. The walls drip with condensation, warm as breath. Rain falls not from clouds but from the canopy above: a constant, intermittent precipitation squeezed from the air by the city’s own metabolism. It collects in pools that are not still but *active*, harboring eel-like amphibians with transparent flesh and the slow, patient jaws of things that wait.

Reality is thin here. The city has been growing so long, in such equilibrium, that its biology has begun to ignore the old laws. Walk down a boulevard of flowering bark and you will find that the perspective does not resolve. A tower may be closer than it appears, or farther; a doorway may open into a chamber larger than the structure that contains it. The fauna know this. The six-legged grazers—soft, mammalian things with eyes like dew—step *between* the cracks in space, appearing and disappearing as they browse on the phosphorescent moss that coats the vertical surfaces. They do not build. Nothing here builds with tools. The city is grown, secreted, expressed from the genetic memory of the green.

Yet it is urban. There is organization, terrible and organic. There are districts: the Processing Wards, where giant pitcher-plants the size of houses digest the dead, their lids opening and closing with hydraulic sighs; the Residential Canopies, where the winged fauna roost in woven nests of living vine; the Arteries, where the fluid flows fastest, where the current of warm, golden sap carries nutrients and information. The infrastructure is dense—layer upon layer of growth, old wood petrifying into a kind of stone, new shoots erupting through the cracks.

The light is ordinary, a dappled gold that suggests midday, yet it illuminates impossible colors: leaves that are simultaneously violet and green, bark that shifts in texture when unobserved. The boundaries of the city are precise. You know when you have left because the temperature drops—not cold, but *less* warm—and the Sound cuts off as if a door has closed, leaving a silence so complete it rings.

Inside, the processes never stop. Predator and prey are indistinguishable here; a flowering vine may snap shut on a grazer, digesting it over weeks, while the grazer’s offspring feed on the vine’s own tender shoots. The erosion is ancient, settled—the city has been in its current state for millennia, neither growing nor shrinking, maintaining its boundaries against the dry lands beyond. It is a hamlet-sized organism pretending to be a metropolis, or a metropolis that is actually a single creature, breathing and singing and breaking the rules of geometry in its sleep.

If you stand in the center—there is a center, a plaza of bare, woven roots where the fauna do not go—you can feel the warmth rising from below. The city is thinking. The city is dreaming. And the Sound, the terrible, beautiful Sound, is the only language it knows.
