**The Canopy Sea of Keth-Moran**

You stand on the edge of the world, though the world here is not earth but vegetation—a green ocean that extends from your feet to the horizon in every direction, filling a caldera the size of a small sea. The air is warm and carries the humidity of a living lung. This is a place that has finished becoming; it is the end-state of ten million years of growth, a stable configuration where stone and sky have been replaced by cellulose and chlorophyll.

**Terrain and Architecture**

The elevation is vertiginous. You are somewhere high—cloud-high—yet the caldera walls rise another thousand feet above you, terraced with root-systems that hold the thin soil like fingers clutching dust. The floor of the caldera is not visible. Instead, the vegetation has grown up to meet you, forming a canopy so dense it functions as ground. 

Beneath your feet is not soil but a lattice of branches, each as thick as a city block, woven together by the slow negotiation of light-seeking growth. These are the Keth trees—ancient, deciduous giants whose bark has mineralized into something like gray stone, though it flexes slightly underfoot. The trees grow so close that their canopies have fused into a single, continuous surface. There is no "forest floor" in the traditional sense; there is only the Upper Canopy, a landscape of hollows and ridges formed by the architecture of the trees themselves.

The erosion here is not the wearing down of mountains but the settling of biomass. This ecosystem has reached its final form. The trees no longer compete for height—they have all reached the altitude where the air grows too thin for their vascular systems, creating a plateau of green. What erosion occurs is internal: the slow collapse of ancient branches into the hollows below, creating sinkholes that drop hundreds of feet into shadowed galleries where the air is still and cold. These gaps are rare, however, and the canopy mostly presents a unified front to the sky.

**Water and Atmosphere**

The dampness is a specific quality, not a weather pattern. Rain comes perhaps once every ten days, brief and warm, but the true moisture is endogenous. The trees transpire. The canopy breathes out water vapor that condenses on the undersides of leaves and in the crooks of branches, creating a perpetual mist that never quite falls as rain but never dissipates. 

There are no rivers here, only "veins"—channels where water collects in the depressions between major branch-unions, flowing slowly along the architectural contours of the canopy. These streams are transparent, filled with the larvae of insects and the pale roots of epiphytes that dangle into the flow. The water tastes of green, of crushed leaves, of the slight alkalinity of wood-ash.

**The Sound**

The sound hits you first—a physical pressure against the eardrums. It is not the sound of wind, though there is wind. It is the sound of consumption. The fauna here is overwhelming in its density, and every creature is eating, calling, or moving through the foliage.

The noise is stratified. At the highest register, the shrieking of the canopy-dwellers—primates with fur like oxidized copper, birds with vocal sacs that inflate to the size of melons—creates a continuous, shimmering wall of high-frequency sound. Below this, the mid-range is occupied by the buzzing of insects so numerous they form clouds that darken patches of the canopy like passing shadows. The bass note comes from the movement of the megafauna: the slow, grinding passage of the Great Sloths, creatures the size of whales that move through the upper branches on limbs adapted into broad, flat platforms, their weight causing the entire canopy to sway and creak like a ship in heavy seas.

There is no silence here. Even in the rare moments when the diurnal creatures rest, the night-shift emerges—moths with wings like sheets of parchment that flutter with a sound like tearing paper, frogs that produce calls at frequencies that make the water in the leaf-axils ripple visibly.

**Ecology and Movement**

The density is paradoxical. While the vegetation is total—there is no bare rock, no open water, no sky visible from within the canopy—the large fauna is sparse, distributed thinly across the vast scale of the caldera. You might walk for hours across the green plateau and encounter nothing larger than a beetle, then suddenly find yourself in the territory of a Great Sloth, its presence announced by the deep groaning of stressed wood and the sudden, panicked flight of smaller creatures.

The ecosystem is in constant motion, but it is the motion of a closed system. Predator and prey have achieved a balance so ancient it appears static, though every square meter is a battlefield of consumption. The trees themselves are active participants, their leaves closing when touched, their sap flowing to seal wounds within seconds. There are plants here that move visibly—vines that creep across the canopy surface at the pace of a minute hand, seeking new hosts, new light.

**The Built Environment**

Scattered across this green expanse are the remains of the Keth, the people who once lived here. Their structures are primitive by any standard—platforms of woven branches, stone tools of flaked obsidian, the occasional circle of standing stones that mark some long-forgotten territorial boundary. These artifacts are not ruins so much as they are part of the substrate now. The trees have grown through and over them, incorporating the worked stone into their root systems, lifting the platforms into the air on pillars of new growth.

You find a basket, perhaps, woven from reeds that no longer grow here, preserved in the dry hollow of a branch. Or a fire-circle, the stones blackened by ancient carbon, now sitting thirty feet above the actual ground level of the caldera, lifted by the slow accumulation of centuries of leaf-litter and growth.

**Light and Space**

The light is normal—sunlight filtered through a atmosphere free of pollution, bright and direct. But because the canopy is so dense, the light arrives in specific geometries. It falls through gaps in shafts that illuminate the mist like solid bars. It reflects off the waxy upper surfaces of leaves, creating a glare that makes the canopy seem to shimmer like water. At midday, the light is almost white; at dawn and dusk, it turns the green to gold and then to a deep, blue-shadowed emerald.

The scale is regional. From the caldera rim, you can see the curvature of the earth, the way the canopy dips into the vast bowl of the crater and rises up the opposite side. The nearest mountain range is a blue line on the horizon, weeks of travel away across the green sea. The emptiness is vertical as much as horizontal—there are vast spaces between the major tree-trunks, cathedral-like voids where the light falls unimpeded to the dark, mysterious lower levels where few creatures venture and where the air is cold enough to see your breath, despite the warmth above.

**Rules of the World**

Here, the primary rule is that *up* is life and *down* is death, or at least mystery. The canopy is the only habitable zone; below lies the "Understory," a lightless realm of decay and pressure where the weight of the living mass above compresses the fallen material into a peat-like substance that occasionally ignites into slow, smoldering fires that burn for years.

Another rule: nothing is still. Even the stone-like bark of the ancient trees is in slow motion, expanding, contracting, healing. The world is growing, always, and the sound of that growth—the cellular division, the stretching of fiber, the flow of sap—forms a subsonic hum beneath the cacophony of the fauna, a bass note so low you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it.

You walk carefully here. The canopy is strong, but it is alive, and it responds to pressure. A misplaced step might trigger a defensive reaction—leaves closing, thorns extending, sap flowing. The Keth knew how to move here, how to read the flexibility of a branch before trusting it. You must learn this too, or find another way across the green ocean, high above the world, in the warm, deafening, living air.
