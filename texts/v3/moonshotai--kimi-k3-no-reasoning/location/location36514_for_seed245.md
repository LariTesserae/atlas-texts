# The Undertaking

Imagine standing somewhere low. Lower than low. A place so close to the planet's molten attention that the floor has opinions about whether you should be there.

The surface beneath your feet is not ground. It is *inventory*.

This is a world where someone — something — began making everything and has not yet finished. Or has finished many times and keeps starting over. There is no natural substrate. There was, perhaps, once — a stone, a magma, an original thing — but it has been covered, converted, re-rendered so many times that the word "bedrock" has become an archaeological rumor. Everything you can see, touch, stand on, or fall through is made. Not made well. Not made permanently. Made *ongoingly*.

The scale of it defeats your eyes first. You are standing on something flat-ish that extends to a horizon so far away it should be beyond sight, but the dim light does something strange at distance — it doesn't fade, it *repeats*. The horizon stutters. You see the same distant structure — a low wall, a stack of slab-forms — three, four, five times, each iteration slightly offset, as if the world is a document being photocopied by a machine that is running out of toner and conviction. Beyond that stuttering repetition there is more. Always more. The Undertaking is continental. It may be planetary. It generates its own weather not because it is large enough to have an atmosphere doing atmospheric things, but because the *process of its own making* produces exhaust — heat, steam, particulate failure — and this exhaust has nowhere to go, so it has become a climate.

---

## The Material

What is it made of? This is the wrong question and also the only question.

The dominant material is something like fired clay, or something like cast metal, or something like the residue left behind when a very large thought is forgotten too quickly. It has properties of all three. It rings when struck — a low, flat, disinterested tone, like a bell that has given up. It is warm. Not warm from the sun — the light here is too weak for that — but warm from within, as though the material itself is a slow exothermic reaction still in progress. It was poured, or extruded, or *decided*, and it has not finished cooling, and it may never finish cooling because the cooling keeps being interrupted by revision.

The surfaces are not smooth. They are *almost* smooth. They are smooth the way a first draft is smooth — the intention is there, the execution keeps failing at the millimeter scale. Run your hand across a wall and you feel micro-stutters, places where the fabrication process hiccupped, repeated a layer, skipped a step. These imperfections are not decorative. They are not weathering. They are *process artifacts* — the physical evidence of a making that is happening too fast or too carelessly or too *many times simultaneously* to achieve consistency.

The color is dim. Not dark — dim. There is a difference. Dark is an absence. Dim is a *restraint*. The palette is ochre and bone-grey and a brown so deep it borders on maroon, all of it matte, all of it absorbing what little light exists and returning very little. Occasionally — rarely, and becoming rarer — you find a surface that was made with a slight glaze, a sheen, and these surfaces catch the dim light and hold it with a kind of astonishment, as if they are surprised to be visible at all.

---

## The Water

Water is here. Not abundantly. Not as rivers or lakes or seas. Water here is *present* the way regret is present — it seeps. It condenses. It weeps from joints in the fabricated walls, pools in the shallow basins of incomplete floors, drips from overhangs with a rhythm that suggests schedule rather than accident. The water is warm. Always warm. It does not refresh. It does not cleanse. It arrives at body temperature and sits in its shallow pools like a confession no one asked for, flat and still and faintly mineral, tasting — if you were desperate enough to taste it — of iron and something sweeter underneath, something almost organic, something that makes you think of the inside of a mouth.

The water has an unusual relationship with the fabricated surfaces. It does not erode them — nothing here erodes; the world is too young, too raw, too *still-being-made* for erosion to have gained a foothold. Instead, the water *activates* the surfaces. Where a pool sits for long enough — an hour? a day? time here is difficult — the material beneath it begins to soften, to reconsider its shape, to slump and bulge and occasionally to *sprout* new provisional structures: low ridges, nub-like protrusions, half-formed archways that rise a few centimeters from the softened floor and then stop, as if the fabrication impulse driving them lost interest or received a contradictory instruction.

These proto-structures are everywhere. They give the landscape its texture of permanent incipience. Everything is almost something. Nothing has committed.

## The Light

Dim, as established. But the dimness has a quality that requires elaboration.

The light source is not a sun. There is no single point of illumination. The light is *ambient* — it comes from the sky the way sound comes from a large room: directionless, suffused, equidistant from everywhere. It is the color of late evening on an overcast day, a grey-amber, a bruised gold, and it does not change. There is no dawn here. No dusk. The light simply *persists*, and its persistence is one of the few stable things about the Undertaking, and you come to rely on it the way you rely on a heartbeat — not because it is comforting, but because its absence would mean something has gone very wrong.

Shadows are long but soft. They pool rather than fall. A vertical structure — and there are many vertical structures, walls and columns and stacked forms rising from the flat fabricated plain — will cast a shadow that does not quite match its own geometry. The shadow will be slightly wider than it should be, or slightly shorter, or it will bend at a point where nothing bends it. These discrepancies are subtle. You notice them only after you have been here long enough to stop being overwhelmed, and once you notice them, you cannot stop noticing them, and they produce a low-grade perceptual nausea, a sense that the relationship between object and shadow is *negotiated* rather than physical, and that the negotiation is not always successful.

---

## The Dynamics

This is where the Undertaking becomes difficult to describe, because description implies a stable subject, and the Undertaking is not stable.

It is not stable in a specific way. It is not chaotic in the sense of random violence. It is chaotic in the sense of *simultaneous incompatible orders*. Imagine a construction site where every worker has a different blueprint, all the blueprints are for the same building, and no one can stop working. The result is not destruction — it is *contested creation*. Sections of the world are continuously being made, unmade, and remade according to principles that shift without warning.

A wall rises. You watch it rise — it extrudes from the floor the way clay rises from a potter's wheel, wet and warm and *intentional*. It reaches a height of perhaps three meters. It develops a window. The window is in the wrong place — not structurally, but *narratively*; you can feel that the window is wrong, that it contradicts something the wall was trying to be. The wall pauses. The window fills itself in. The wall resumes rising. It reaches four meters and then stops entirely, and over the course of the next hour it slowly settles, slumps, returns to the floor, not as rubble but as *material reclaimed* — the wall simply becomes floor again, its specific identity absorbed back into the general inventory of the world's substance.

This happens everywhere, all the time, at scales ranging from centimeters to kilometers. On the far horizon — the stuttering, repeating horizon — you can sometimes see a structure rising that must be enormous, a tower or a ridge or something for which you have no word, and it rises with agonizing slowness over the course of what might be days, and just as it begins to achieve a form you could almost name, it collapses — not downward but *inward*, folding into itself like a closing fist, and the horizon stutters and the structure is gone and something else has already begun.

The sound of this is quieter than you expect. A rising wall makes a sound like bread baking — a low, internal crackling, a settling. A collapsing structure makes almost no sound at all, which is worse. The silence of un-making is one of the worst sounds here.

## The Ecology

There is no ecology. This requires more explanation than you might think.

There are no plants. There are no animals. There is nothing that grows, hunts, grazes, migrates, mates, or dies. The water supports no algae. The warm clay-metal surfaces host no lichen. The air — warm, humid, faintly metallic — carries no spores, no insects, no birdsong.

And yet. And yet.

The *process* behaves like an ecology. The rising and falling structures interact. A wall rising in one location seems to *deplete* the material available nearby, causing a neighboring proto-structure to stall, to starve, to sink back into the floor. Two walls rising simultaneously will sometimes *compete*, racing upward, each growing faster as the other grows, until one exhausts the local supply and fails, and the winner stands alone for a moment — a *moment* — before it too begins its slow reabsorption. There is something that functions like predation: occasionally a large structure will incorporate a smaller one, absorbing its material mid-growth, and the smaller structure's half-formed window or archway will appear, briefly, on the surface of the larger structure, like a fossil, like a memory, before being smoothed over.

There is something that functions like succession. In areas where the fabrication activity has been quiet for a long time — where nothing has risen or fallen — the water pools grow still and the surfaces harden and the color shifts from ochre to grey, and these grey areas feel *established*, feel *old*, feel like they have achieved a kind of tenure. And then something stirs. A ridge rises in the middle of the grey stillness, and the grey cracks, and the ochre bleeds through, and the process reclaims the silence.

If there are agents here — builders, workers, entities — you do not see them. The fabrication appears to be *the world making itself*, autonomously, without hands. But the results are not organic. They are architectural. They are *designed*. Every rising wall has the proportions of intention. Every collapsing tower falls with a kind of grammar. Something is deciding. Something is building. You cannot find it. It may be distributed — a property of the material itself, a mineral intentionality, a clay that dreams of becoming a city and keeps dreaming and keeps waking up and starting over.

## The Weirdness

It intensifies the longer you stay.

At first, the strangeness is contained in the things I have described — the stuttering horizon, the mismatched shadows, the autonomously rising structures. These are odd, but they are *consistent* in their oddity, and the mind adapts.

Then the deeper strangeness begins.

You notice that distances are not stable. A structure that appeared to be an hour's walk away can be reached in minutes. A pool of water that you stepped over this morning is now a hundred meters wide, not because it grew but because the space around it *revised itself*. The Undertaking is not fixed in its geometry. It is being made, and the making includes the space between things, and the space between things is as provisional as the things themselves.

You notice that the repetition at the horizon is not confined to the horizon. It happens close-up. You watch a wall rise, and as it rises, you see the same wall rising behind it, offset by a few meters, and another behind that — a sequence of identical walls, each slightly less resolved than the one before, like a sentence being translated into progressively simpler languages until only the grammar remains. When the first wall collapses, they all collapse, in sequence, front to back, a cascade of unmaking that produces a sound like a long exhale.

You notice — and this is the worst noticing — that *you* are subject to the process. Not physically. You do not rise. You do not collapse. But your *location* within the Undertaking is not fixed. You fall asleep — if you can sleep, in the warm dim damp unending twilight — and when you wake, the structures around you are different, and this could be explained by the constant making and unmaking, but the structures are not just different, they are *impossibly* different — you went to sleep in an open plain and you have woken in a corridor, and the corridor extends in both directions farther than you can see, and the walls are the same warm clay-metal and they are wet and they are close, and you understand that the world has not just changed around you, it has *recomposed* around you, and you are not sure if you have been moved or if the world has been rearranged or if these are the same thing here.

## The Sound

Mostly quiet. As established. But the quiet has a texture.

The dominant sound is the dripping. The weeping of water from joints and overhangs, irregular but constant, a slow syncopation without rhythm, without pattern, without meaning. It is the closest thing the Undertaking has to a voice, and it is saying nothing.

Beneath the dripping, there is a hum. Very low. Below the threshold of music, below the threshold of tone, more a *pressure* than a sound — the hum of a process running, of material being moved at scales too large or too slow to see. It is in the floor. It is in the walls. It is in your sternum. You feel it more than you hear it, and after a while you realize it is not constant — it *fluctuates*. When a large structure rises, the hum deepens. When something collapses, the hum pauses, briefly, and the silence in that pause is absolute and terrible, and then the hum resumes and the dripping resumes and you resume, because you must.

Very rarely — once every few days, perhaps — there is a *sound-event*. Something loud. A crack, a boom, a deep resonant tone as if the world has been struck like a bell. These events correspond to nothing visible. No structure falls. No new structure rises. The sound simply *occurs*, fills the dim air for a moment, and is gone, and the hum and the dripping close over it like water over a stone.

## The Meaning of It

You will search for one. You will not find one, but the search will teach you things about the Undertaking that the Undertaking itself cannot say.

It is a world that is being built by something that does not know what it is building. It is not a construction site — a construction site has a plan, a completion date, an intended use. The Undertaking has none of these. It has only the *impulse to make*, and this impulse is enormous, planetary, tireless, and utterly without direction. It builds because building is what it does. It builds walls because walls are a thing that can be built. It builds windows because walls sometimes have windows. It does not know what windows are for. It has never seen anything through a window. It fills them in.

It is a mind — or the residue of a mind, or the habit of a mind — that has forgotten everything except the *gesture* of creation. The muscle memory of making. It builds and destroys and builds again not because it is dissatisfied but because it has no mechanism for satisfaction. There is no moment at which the Undertaking could say *this is finished* and stop, because "finished" is a concept that requires an endpoint, and the Undertaking has no endpoints, only iterations, only revisions, only the warm wet endless present tense of *making*.

The water is its sweat. The dim light is its attention — diffuse, unfocused, perennial. The hum is its pulse. The rising and falling structures are its thoughts, half-formed, abandoned, replaced. The stuttering horizon is its vision — myopic, repeating, unsure. The mismatched shadows are its doubt.

You walk through it. You are the only living thing. Your footsteps make sounds that the world does not make — sharp, deliberate, *temporal* sounds, sounds with a before and after. The dripping pauses when you pass, or seems to. The proto-structures near your path rise slightly, as if responding to your warmth, your motion, your *aliveness* — the one input the Undertaking has never encountered, the one variable it has never been asked to incorporate.

A wall rises beside you. It rises quickly, eagerly, faster than any wall you have seen. It reaches your shoulder height. It develops a window. The window is at the height of your eyes. You look through it.

On the other side, you see the same plain. The same dim light. The same ochre-grey expanse of almost-smooth surfaces and weeping joints and shallow warm pools. The same stuttering horizon.

But in the middle distance, there is a structure you have not seen before. It is low and wide and symmetrical, and it is not rising and it is not falling. It is simply *there*. It looks like a shelter. It looks like a home. It looks like the first thing the Undertaking has ever made that is finished.

You walk toward it. The distance is wrong — it takes longer than it should, then less time, then longer again. The wall with the window collapses behind you, silently. You do not turn around.

The structure does not grow as you approach. It does not change. It waits.

You arrive. It is the size of a single room. The walls are smooth — truly smooth, not almost-smooth — and dry. The floor is level. There is a doorway. There is no door. The interior is dim and warm and quiet, and the hum of the world is muted here, and the dripping cannot be heard, and for the first time since you arrived in the Undertaking, you are in a space that is not in the process of becoming something else.

You sit down. You lean against the wall. The wall is warm, but it is a different warmth — not the exothermic warmth of ongoing making, but the retained warmth of something that was made and has held its warmth the way a memory holds its emotion.

Outside, the world continues. Walls rise and fall. Water weeps. The horizon stutters. The great meaningless beautiful process churns on, making and unmaking, building its endless draft of a city that no one will ever live in, a world that no one will ever finish.

But here, in this one small room, something has been completed.

You do not know if it was made for you. You suspect it was not — that it is simply an accident, a moment where the process stumbled into completion the way a monkey at a typewriter stumbles into a word. But the room does not feel accidental. It feels *earned*. It feels like the one true sentence in a lifetime of false starts.

You stay. The dim light persists. The warm water drips beyond the walls. The Undertaking continues.

And the room holds.
