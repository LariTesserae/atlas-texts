# The Sump-Borough of Vess

## Descent

You reach Vess by going down. This is the first thing, the thing that organizes everything else. The surface above — whatever town or moor or forest once stood there — is a rumor, a thickness of earth and fractured shale between you and a sky you have stopped expecting. The passage down is a stair of poured concrete, four hundred and eleven steps, old enough that the center of each tread has been worn into a shallow trough by the passage of feet that no longer come. The walls weep. They have always wept. Water moves through the rock above with a patience that predates the borough, and it arrives at the interior surfaces as a slow beading, a gathering, an eventual release — droplets the size of fingernails sliding down to the stair-edge and falling into darkness with a sound like a small tongue clicking.

The stair descends into a cavity. Calling it a cavern implies geology; calling it a chamber implies design. Vess is both — a natural void in deep sedimentary rock, perhaps a dissolved fault-void, perhaps something stranger, that has been widened, squared, buttressed, and occupied. The ceiling is fifty meters overhead in the highest vault, dropping to twelve in the narrow galleries. You cannot see the ceiling from the floor. The air is too thick with what the air here carries.

## Light and Air

The light in Vess is the color of weak tea and comes from nowhere in particular. This is not metaphor. There are fixtures — thousands of them, mounted on every wall and post and pipe-bracket, small caged bulbs of a design abandoned on the surface a century ago, each one burning at a fraction of its rated wattage. They are fed by a power system that still functions, dimly, and their combined effect is not illumination but the *idea* of illumination — a pervasive amber gloaming in which shapes are present but not sharp, in which you navigate by familiarity and by the slight variance in darkness between a wall and a doorway. Nothing here casts a clean shadow. Shadows are diffuse, sympathetic, pooling in corners like sediment.

The air itself is a participant. It is saturated — beyond humid, approaching a state where the boundary between breathing and drinking becomes theoretical. Moisture condenses on every surface continuously: on the iron railings, on the brick faces of the buildings, on your skin, on the leaves of the pale plants that grow in the gaps. It does not drip so much as *sheathe*. Every surface in Vess wears a film of water, and the water wears a film of light, and the light wears a film of something else — a faint iridescence, an oil-slick shimmer that suggests the water is not merely water. It smells of limestone and machine oil and something faintly sweet, like rotting pears. The residents do not notice this smell. You will stop noticing it too, in time.

The temperature is constant: cool enough that you want a second layer, not cold enough to see your breath. It does not vary by more than a degree between what might be called day and what might be called night, though those terms have only administrative meaning here. The deep rock holds its temperature the way it holds everything — stubbornly, patiently, without concern for preference.

## The Borough

Vess is a settlement — was a settlement — *is* a settlement, though the number of human residents has fallen below what the infrastructure was built to serve, and continues to fall at a rate too slow to alarm anyone in any given decade. At its height, perhaps three thousand people lived here, in a cluster of buildings organized around the central sump with the unplanned density of a thing that grew rather than was designed. Now perhaps two hundred remain, though census-taking has become one of those activities that everyone agrees should happen and no one performs.

The buildings are brick and cast iron and poured concrete, three to five stories tall, pressed close together along narrow lanes that follow the contours of the rock floor. They are industrial buildings, or were — machine works, pumping stations, ore-dressing floors, chemical precipitators — that have been converted to housing with varying degrees of thoroughness. Some have proper windows, glazed and curtained. Others have openings that were once vents or material-hatches, now fitted with shutters or simply left open to the amber air. Clotheslines cross the lanes between buildings, and the clothes hung on them never quite dry. They reach a state of damp equilibrium and remain there, sagging slightly, moving in air currents too slow to feel.

The architecture is layered in the way of things built over long periods without master plan. The oldest structures — at the borough's center, nearest the sump — are stone, almost geological in their massiveness, their corners rounded by centuries of moisture and touch. Around them, brick buildings of the early industrial period, their facades blackened not by soot (there is no combustion here, or very little) but by a slow chemical patina, a reaction between the brick's clay and the air's chemistry. Then concrete — mid-period, functional, pouring-platform construction with embedded pipe-runs and conduit channels. And finally, most recently, structures of corrugated steel and salvaged plate, bolted together, already rusting, already sagging, already being absorbed into the borough's slow metabolism.

Nothing here is new. The most recent construction anyone can point to is forty years old — a footbridge across the eastern channel, assembled from parts stripped from a decommissioned pump house. It is already indistinguishable in texture and color from everything around it. Vess does not preserve its buildings; it *digests* them, slowly, incorporating them into a single continuous surface of wet mineral and corroding metal that has more in common with a reef than a city.

## The Sump

At the center of Vess is the water. It is not a lake or a pool or a reservoir, though it has aspects of all three. The sump is a body of black water, roughly circular, perhaps eighty meters across, that occupies the lowest point of the cavity floor. It is fed by the ceaseless weeping of the walls and ceiling, by the condensation that sheets down every surface and finds its way through channels and gutters and simple gravity to this central point. It is drained by — this is where understanding becomes approximate. There are pumps. There were always pumps. The pumps still run, powered by the same dim electrical system that feeds the lights, and they move water out of the sump through pipes that go somewhere — deeper, presumably, or laterally through the rock to some distant outlet. The pumps' capacity is fixed. The inflow varies. The level of the sump rises and falls across weeks and months in a cycle too slow to track without instruments, and the instruments are old, and the people who maintained them are mostly gone.

The water in the sump is not clean and not dirty. It is *processed* — by the rock it has passed through, by the chemistry of the borough's industries (still active in their residuals, still leaching into the groundwater traces of compounds manufactured a century ago), and by the things that live in it. It is warm compared to the air — a degree or two warmer, enough that a faint vapor rises from its surface on the rare occasions when the air currents shift. This vapor catches the amber light and makes the sump visible from a distance as a brighter patch of gloom, a luminous absence at the borough's heart.

The buildings nearest the sump are flooded to their ground floors, permanently. They are not abandoned. They are inhabited in their upper stories, reached by wooden walkways and iron ladders that connect them to higher ground. The people who live in them are called sump-side residents, and they are understood to be a particular kind of person — patient, unhurried, comfortable with dampness as a primary condition of existence. Their floors are wet. Their walls are wet. They have stopped distinguishing between the water below and the water in the air and the water in the walls. It is all the same water. It is all the sump, expressing itself at different elevations.

## Machinery

The industrial infrastructure of Vess is old, vast in proportion to the current population, and partially functional in the way of a body whose organs are failing at different rates. The main systems are these:

**The pumps** — already mentioned. Seven of them, housed in a pump hall on the sump's western shore, though only four are operational at any given time and no one is certain which four. They are piston pumps, driven by electric motors the size of wine barrels, and their operation is the loudest sound in Vess — a slow, arrhythmic thumping that resonates through the rock floor and can be felt in the soles of your feet anywhere in the borough. When a pump fails, the change is noticed not as silence but as a subtle shift in the borough's pulse, a missing beat that the residents feel rather than hear. Someone — it is never entirely clear who — will eventually descend into the pump hall and do what can be done. Usually something can be done. The pumps are old but they were built to be repaired, and the parts that fail are parts that can be fabricated in the borough's workshops, or salvaged from the pumps that are beyond repair.

**The ventilation system** — a network of ducts and fans that once moved fresh air from the surface through the borough and back. The fans still turn, slowly, powered by the same electrical grid. Whether they are actually moving air from the surface or simply recirculating the borough's internal atmosphere is a question that has been debated for decades without resolution. The ducts are large enough to walk through, and some residents claim to have walked them and reached a point where the air grew noticeably fresher. Others claim the ducts are blocked by cave-ins, by accumulated mineral deposits, by growths of the pale vegetation that colonizes every surface here. No consensus exists. The fans turn. The air is breathable. The question of where it comes from has become philosophical.

**The electrical grid** — the source of the amber light. It is fed by a generator in a sealed room below the pump hall, a room that no current resident has entered. The generator runs. It has always run. What fuels it is unknown. The cables that carry its output are thick as a man's wrist, jacketed in cracked rubber, running along the ceilings of every lane and through the walls of every building. They hum, faintly, at a frequency just below hearing — a subsonic presence that you feel as a pressure in your teeth. The bulbs they feed burn at perhaps a quarter of their intended brightness, and have burned at this level for as long as anyone can remember. No bulb has been observed to fail. This is either a testament to the quality of their manufacture or evidence that they are operating so far below capacity that failure is statistically improbable. Either way, the light does not change. It has not changed. It will not change.

**The workshops** — the productive heart of Vess, insofar as Vess has production. Three large buildings on the borough's eastern edge, equipped with lathes, milling machines, forges that burn a gas tapped from somewhere in the rock (it smells of sulfur and burns with a low blue flame), and racks of hand tools accumulated over generations. The workshops are not busy. They are used when something breaks, which happens at the slow pace at which everything happens here. A machinist — there are perhaps six people in Vess who could accurately be called machinists — will spend a week fabricating a replacement part for a pump, and this will be the primary industrial output of the borough for that month. The workshops also produce small goods for daily life: cookware, tools, hinges, brackets. These are made well, without hurry, and are expected to last.

## Vegetation

What grows in Vess grows slowly, palely, and without enthusiasm. There is no photosynthesis here — the amber light is too dim, too spectrally narrow. What grows instead are things that feed on chemistry: on the minerals in the water, on the compounds that leach from the industrial residue, on the slow exhalations of the rock itself.

The most common growth is a film — a thin, gray-green biological layer that coats every surface not regularly disturbed by human contact. It is not quite moss, not quite algae, not quite fungus, though it shares characteristics with all three. The residents call it *the sheen*. It is slippery underfoot. It grows on walls, on ceilings, on the undersides of walkways, on the cables of the electrical grid, on the caged bulbs themselves (contributing to the dimness, perhaps, though cleaning the bulbs does not brighten them — the sheen regrows within days). It is edible, technically. It tastes of nothing. Some residents cultivate it deliberately on flat stones, harvesting it in thin sheets that they dry over the gas forges and grind into a flour-like powder used as a thickener in soups. This is done less from necessity than from the deep human impulse to make use of what is available.

Beyond the sheen, there are larger growths — scattered, sparse, each one an event in the landscape rather than a feature of it. A cluster of pale, branching stalks growing from a crack in the lane near the eastern workshops, waist-high, translucent, swaying in air currents imperceptible to human skin. A broad, flat shelf of something leathery and gray growing from the wall of the pump hall, three meters across, thick as a mattress, its underside beaded with moisture that falls in slow, regular drops. A single vine-like growth that has colonized the interior of a disused stairwell, spiraling up through five stories of darkness, its tendrils pale as candle wax, tipped with small, luminescent nodules that provide the only genuinely bright light in Vess — a faint blue-green glow, cool and steady, like a pilot light.

These growths are old. The shelf on the pump hall wall has been there for at least sixty years, based on the testimony of the eldest residents. It has grown, but so slowly that its growth is measured in decades. It is not dying. It is not thriving. It exists, in equilibrium with its environment, in the same way that Vess exists.

## Fauna

The animals of Vess are numerous and quiet. They are, with few exceptions, small. And they are — this is the strangeness that the residents accept as ordinary — *not quite right*. Not monstrous, not threatening, but divergent in ways that suggest the biology here has been following its own logic for a long time.

**The drip-rats** are the most visible. Rodent-like, cat-sized, covered in a fur so fine and dense that water beads on it without penetrating, giving them a perpetually dewed appearance, as if they have just emerged from the sump. They are everywhere — in the buildings, on the walkways, in the ducts, on the pipes. They are unafraid. They will sit on a windowsill and watch you with eyes that are slightly too large, slightly too forward-facing for a rodent, with an attentiveness that feels evaluative. They eat the sheen. They eat the pale stalks. They eat each other, occasionally, without apparent distress. Their vocalizations are rare and unsettling — a low, thrumming purr that resonates in the chest cavity, felt more than heard, like the pumps' subsonic pulse given animal form.

**The ceiling fish** — this is the name the residents use, though the things are not fish and do not live on the ceiling. They are amphibious, eel-like, between thirty centimeters and a meter in length, and they live in the water that coats the walls and ceiling — the film, the sheen of moisture that covers every surface. They move through this film as if it were deep water, swimming along vertical walls and across the undersides of ledges, their bodies undulating in the thin liquid layer, held there by surface tension or something else. They are translucent, their internal organs visible as faint shadows within their bodies. They eat the microbial life in the water film, filtering it through feathery structures around their mouths. They are harmless. They are also, when you first see one — a pale, sinuous shape gliding across the ceiling above your bed — profoundly disorienting. The residents do not notice them. You will stop noticing them too.

**The moths** — large, palm-sized, their wings the exact color of the amber light, which means they are effectively invisible in flight and visible only when at rest on a surface darker than themselves. They congregate around the caged bulbs, though what draws them is unclear — the bulbs emit almost no heat and a spectrum of light that should not attract any phototactic insect. They rest on the cages with their wings folded, motionless, sometimes dozens of them, and when disturbed they rise in a slow, silent cloud that is felt as a gentle displacement of air before it is seen. Their caterpillars — pale, hairless, the size of a little finger — feed on the sheen, leaving clean trails through the gray-green film, and these trails are one of the few dynamic visual elements in Vess, slowly extending, day by day, like handwriting in an unknown language.

**The sump things** — what lives in the central water is known only partially. Shapes move beneath the surface. Occasionally something breaks the surface — a back, a fin, a limb — too briefly to identify. The residents fish the sump from the walkways, using lines and nets, and they catch things: pale, soft-bodied creatures with too many fins, or too few; crustaceans with asymmetric claws; fish that are normal except for their eyes, which are large and reflective and seem to gather the amber light and hold it, glowing faintly for minutes after the fish is removed from the water. These catches are eaten. They taste of minerals and faint sweetness. No one has become ill from eating them, or no one has become ill in a way that could be definitively attributed to the eating. The sump gives. The sump takes. The balance is old and stable.

## Sound

Vess is quiet in a way that is not silence but *subtraction*. The deep rock absorbs sound. The moisture-laden air muffles it. The result is an acoustic environment in which every sound is intimate — close, small, personal — and in which the absence of sound is not empty but full, a dense, textured quietness that has weight.

The sounds that exist:

The pumps. Their slow, arrhythmic thumping, felt through the floor, a heartbeat at geological tempo. This is the baseline, the metronome against which all other sounds are measured.

The water. Not running — running water is rare here, the flow too slow for that. Instead: dripping, the constant, distributed dripping of condensation falling from ceilings and ledges and cables and the undersides of walkways. Each drip is a distinct event, a small impact on stone or metal or water, and together they create a random, patient percussion, like a very slow rain that has been falling for centuries and will fall for centuries more. The pitch of each drip depends on what it strikes — the high, tight *tik* of water on metal, the soft *thup* of water on the sheen, the deep, resonant *plock* of water striking the sump's surface. Attentive listeners — and in Vess, everyone is an attentive listener, there being little else to attend to — can locate themselves within the borough by the specific composition of the dripping around them, the unique rhythm and timbre of their lane, their building, their room.

The fans. A distant, low rushing, like wind through a narrow passage, so constant and so quiet that it is only noticed when it changes. It changes rarely.

Human sounds. Voices, when they occur, are low. People in Vess speak quietly not from secrecy but from the knowledge that sound carries oddly here — a normal speaking voice will echo off the wet stone and arrive at unintended ears distorted but intelligible. Footsteps are soft on the sheen. Doors are few, and those that exist are rarely closed. The most human sound in Vess is the clink of tools in the workshops, a sound that occurs perhaps a few hours each day, and that carries through the lanes with a clarity that makes the work feel communal even when the worker is alone.

The drip-rats' purr. Felt in the sternum. Unsettling until it becomes familiar. Then, somehow, reassuring — a living vibration in a place of stone and water and slow decay.

## Time and Change

Nothing in Vess happens quickly. This is not a poetic observation; it is a structural feature of the place. The water moves slowly. The plants grow slowly. The machines operate slowly. The erosion of the buildings proceeds at a pace that can be measured only across generations. The population declines slowly. The level of the sump rises and falls on a cycle measured in months. Even the light, which is electrical and therefore theoretically instantaneous, has a quality of slowness — perhaps because it is dim, perhaps because the bulbs take a perceptible fraction of a second to respond when a switch is thrown (though switches are rarely thrown; the lights are simply left on, always, at their quarter-power amber burn).

The result is a place where the concept of urgency has no traction. Residents of Vess do not hurry. They do not schedule. They do not plan in units smaller than weeks. A conversation may span days, with long silences between exchanges that would be considered rude on the surface but here are simply the natural tempo of communication. A repair to a building may take a year, not because the work is difficult but because there is no reason to compress it. The building has stood for a century. It will stand for another. The repair can proceed at the pace at which repairs wish to proceed.

This is not laziness or depression or the result of some chemical influence of the air or water — or if it is, it is also something else, something that arises from the deep structure of the place. Vess is a landscape that operates on geological time, and its inhabitants, human and otherwise, have adapted to that tempo. To live in Vess is to slow down. Not to stop — things do happen, change does occur — but to move at the pace of the rock and the water and the amber light, a pace so slow that from outside it might appear motionless but from within feels exactly right, feels like the speed at which things are supposed to happen.

## The Strangeness

Beyond the ceiling fish and the reflective-eyed sump catches and the lights that never fail and the generator that runs on unknown fuel, there are aspects of Vess that resist categorization, that the residents have stopped trying to explain and have incorporated into their understanding of the world as simply *the way things are here*.

The light, for instance. It is not uniform. In certain areas of the borough — the deep lanes near the sump, the flooded ground floors of the sump-side buildings, the upper reaches of the stairwell where the vine grows — the light has a quality that is difficult to describe. It is thicker. Not brighter or dimmer but *denser*, as if the light itself has viscosity, pooling in these areas like liquid. Objects in these zones appear slightly displaced, their edges uncertain, as if viewed through a layer of clear, slow-moving fluid. The residents call these areas *the thick light*, and they walk through them without comment, though newcomers often stop, disoriented, feeling as though they are wading through something they cannot see.

The reflections in the sump are another. On the rare occasions when the sump's surface is perfectly still — no drips, no movement of fish, no vibration from the pumps — the reflection it shows is not the ceiling above but something else. A different ceiling. A different arrangement of vaults and buttresses, similar to Vess but not identical, as if the sump is reflecting a version of the borough that exists somewhere else, or at some other time, or in some other configuration of the rock. The differences are subtle — a pillar where no pillar stands, a bridge spanning a width where no bridge exists — but they are consistent. The same wrong reflection, every time. The residents have stopped looking at the sump's surface when it is still. Not from fear, exactly. From a sense that the reflection is not meant for them, that it is a view into something that does not concern them, and that looking at it is a kind of trespass.

And there are the sounds that are not sounds. In the deepest parts of the borough — the flooded basements, the lower reaches of the ventilation ducts, the spaces behind the pump hall where the pipes descend into the rock — there is a perception, at the edge of hearing or below it, of *structure*. Not music, not rhythm, but a sense of organized vibration, as if the rock itself is humming a tune too low and too slow for human perception, and what you feel is the uppermost edge of it, the highest frequency component of a sound whose fundamental is measured in hours or days. The drip-rats respond to it. They orient toward the deepest pipes, sit motionless with their noses pressed to the metal, purring in what may be sympathy or may be communication.

## The Rules

Vess operates under rules that are not written and not enforced but are understood, the way a body understands its own limits:

Do not drink from the sump directly. The water that condenses on clean surfaces — the undersides of metal walkways, the faces of glass windows — is safe. The sump water is processed, but only after filtration through the ceramic filters that every household maintains.

Do not enter the generator room. This rule is easy to follow, as no one knows where the entrance is. It is mentioned in old documents, in the administrative records that are kept in a moldering archive in one of the sump-side buildings, but the entrance has been sealed — or built over, or forgotten — for at least a century. The generator runs. That is sufficient.

Do not remove the bulbs. Not because they are needed for light (there are more than enough) but because removing a bulb creates an absence in the circuit, a gap in the dim electrical web, and the residents believe — without evidence, but with the deep conviction of people who have lived in a place long enough to develop instincts about it — that the circuit needs to be complete. That the grid is not just a power system but something more like a nervous system, and that interrupting it would cause a response. What kind of response is not specified. No one has tested the belief. No one intends to.

Do not count the drip-rats. This rule is newer, and its origin is obscure, but it is followed with the same unthinking compliance as the others. The drip-rats are numerous. They are, if anything, increasing. But no one counts them. Estimates are permitted. Approximations are acceptable. An exact census is not attempted.

## What It Feels Like

To stand in Vess, in one of its narrow lanes, in the amber dimness, with the wet walls close on either side and the drip of condensation marking slow time and the subsonic pulse of the pumps in your feet and a ceiling fish gliding along the wall above you and a drip-rat watching from a windowsill with its too-knowing eyes — to stand there is to feel that you are inside something. Not a building, not a cavern, but a *system*, a living arrangement of rock and water and metal and biology that has achieved a state of equilibrium so complete and so old that it has begun to develop the qualities of an organism. You are not a resident of Vess. You are a component of it. Your breathing adds moisture to the air. Your footsteps wear the stone. Your body heat contributes, negligibly, to the thermal budget. You are absorbed, gently, without malice or welcome, into the slow metabolism of the place.

This is not unpleasant. It is not pleasant. It is a state of being that exists outside the binary of pleasure and discomfort, in the way that Vess exists outside the binary of natural and artificial, of surface and depth, of living and built. It is a place that has found its own category and settled into it with the patience of water finding its level.

The light does not change. The pumps beat their slow pulse. The water rises and falls across its months-long cycle. The sheen grows and is eaten and grows again. The drip-rats purr. The moths gather on the bulb-cages. The ceiling fish glide along the walls. The vine's luminescent nodules glow in the stairwell like a constellation in a sky that no one here has ever seen.

And the sump, at the center, holds its dark water, and in its stillness shows a ceiling that is not the ceiling above it, and the residents do not look, and the water waits with the patience of a thing that has been here longer than the borough, longer than the people, longer perhaps than the rock itself, and will be here when all of it — the brick and the iron and the pale plants and the amber light — has been reabsorbed into the slow, wet, patient dark from which it came.
