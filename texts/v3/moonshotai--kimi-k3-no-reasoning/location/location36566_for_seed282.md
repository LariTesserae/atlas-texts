# The Cistern That Dreams of Itself

---

There is a place beneath the world — not a cave, not a cellar, but a room that was never finished being built. Or perhaps it was finished once, long ago, and then something began rebuilding it from the inside, adding to it, layer by layer, the way a wasp adds paper to a nest, endlessly, without blueprint, without foreman, without end.

No one has found the door. The room has doors — hundreds of them — but they open onto other parts of the room. The geometry folds. Corridors loop. Staircases descend and arrive at the ceiling of where you were. The whole structure is contained within something — embedded, pocketed — but no wall is an outer wall. Every surface faces inward. There is no outside. There is only the Cistern and the things the Cistern is making.

---

## The Construction

Everything is built. Every surface, every grain. There is no bedrock here, no soil, no geological substrate. The walls are concrete — but concrete that has been poured over concrete that was poured over brick that was laid over iron plate that was riveted over tile that was glazed over more concrete, and the strata go down (or out, or through) for what feels like geological depth, except it is all *made*, every layer a decision someone or something once enacted, and the deeper layers are stranger — not older exactly, but less resolved, as though the earliest construction crews were working from instructions they only half understood.

Near the "bottom" (a relative term; gravity here is mostly reliable but has moods), the walls are raw: uncured cement that never hardened, still wet after however long, grey and soft as potter's clay. If you press your hand into it — and you should not — the surface yields, and the imprint remains, and slowly, over hours, the wall seems to *lean toward* the imprint, as though considering it, as though the room is trying to understand what a hand is by growing toward the shape one left behind.

Steel reinforcement bars protrude from unfinished edges like bones from a wound. They are cold — everything is cold, a deep still cold that lives in the materials themselves, not the air — and they hum at a frequency just below hearing, a subsonic murmur that you feel in your molars and the backs of your knees.

---

## The Cold

The cold here is not weather. It is *material*. The concrete radiates cold the way a stove radiates heat — passively, constantly, from within. Pipes sweat with condensation that freezes instantly into thin skins of ice, then thaws when a distant compressor kicks on, then refreezes. The cycle takes eleven minutes. It is one of the few reliable clocks in the Cistern.

Breath fogs. The fog lingers longer than it should — the air is dense, close, saturated with the particulate breath of the building itself: cement dust, rust particles, atomized lubricant, the fine metallic powder that sifts from joints that have been flexing for years without maintenance. Every breath tastes of cold iron and something faintly sweet, like the ghost of a solvent.

---

## The Water

Water arrives in the Cistern the way thoughts arrive in a sleeping mind — intermittently, without announcement, pooling in unexpected places.

It drips. This is the primary hydrological cycle: condensation forms on the undersides of pipes, on the rims of ventilation housings, on the cold faces of electrical panels. The droplets grow — slowly, in the cold — until they fall. They land on surfaces already wet with the droplets that fell before. In some places, this has been happening long enough that stalactites have formed, but they are not mineral. They are built. Tiny accretions of rust, paint flake, concrete dust, and ice, fused by time and cold into hanging structures that look like the work of some obsessive, miniature construction crew — which, in a sense, they are.

There are puddles. The puddles are black. Not because they are deep — most are less than an inch — but because they sit on black tile or black paint or black rubber matting, and the dim light gives them nothing to reflect. They are cold. They do not freeze, which is strange, because everything here is at or below freezing, and yet the puddles remain liquid. Something in the water — some chemical leached from the layers of construction, some antifreeze compound, some industrial residue — keeps them fluid, and this makes them seem alive in a way that nothing else in the Cistern is alive.

Occasionally, a pipe opens. Not bursts — *opens*, deliberately, as though a valve somewhere decided it was time. A gush of grey water, cold enough to sting, pours from a wall or ceiling, runs across a floor, finds a drain or a crack or a seam, and is gone. The whole event lasts thirty seconds. The floor it crossed stays wet for hours. In the wetness, you can sometimes see traces of what the water carried: fine silt, metal shavings, once — reportedly — a perfect impression of a key, dissolved by the time anyone returned with a light.

## The Vegetation

Sparse is generous. What grows here is not plants. It is the idea of plants, rendered in materials the Cistern has on hand.

In corners where moisture collects and the dim light falls at certain angles, *things* emerge from the concrete. They look like mold at first — dark, spreading, textured. But they have structure. They branch. They build themselves upward in fractal patterns that mimic growth, that mimic the logic of roots and stems and leaves, but they are made of oxidized copper, of crystallized mineral efflorescence, of some dark waxy substance that extrudes from seams in the walls. They are the Cistern's attempt at vegetation. They are the building growing gardens out of itself, in the only materials it knows.

Some of them are beautiful. In the dim light — which we will come to — the copper ones catch what illumination exists and return it in dull verdigris, a green so dark it is almost black, almost the color of deep water. They spread in fans across the walls like frozen fireworks, like the vascular systems of enormous leaves pressed flat. They are cold to the touch. They are slightly damp. They grow — measurably, observably — at a rate of about a millimeter per week. No one knows what feeds them, unless it is the water, unless it is the cold itself, unless it is the building's own slow metabolism expressing itself in form.

There are also the wire-vines. Bundles of electrical cable — dead cable, unconnected at both ends, or connected at one end to nothing that still functions — have somehow organized themselves into climbing structures. They emerge from junction boxes and conduits, drape across walls, descend stairwells, coil around pipes. They look intentional. They look like something planted them. Their insulation has cracked in the cold, exposing copper that has begun to oxidize, and the oxidized copper has fused with the mineral efflorescence, and in some places the wire-vines and the copper-growth have merged into a single organism-structure — part machine, part mineral, all architecture.

## The Light

Dim. Not dark — the Cistern is never dark, which is somehow worse.

Light comes from fixtures that should not still work: caged industrial sconces, emergency exit signs with letters half-burned-out, strip lights in frosted housings that flicker at a rate you can almost perceive. The light they produce is the color of old ice — a blue-white that has been filtered through decades of dust accumulation inside the fixture housings, arriving at your eye exhausted, depleted, carrying almost no information about the colors of things.

In this light, everything is either grey or black or that particular dark green of the copper-growth. Skin looks grey. Water looks black. The walls look like the absence of a wall, a plane of grey that might be a foot away or a hundred.

Some lights cycle. A fixture in a corridor might buzz on for four minutes, die for ninety seconds, buzz on again. The pattern is not consistent — it drifts, as though the building's electrical systems are breathing, inhaling current and exhaling it in uneven rhythms. When a light dies near you, the darkness that replaces it is total and intimate. It has texture. You can feel it on your face, cold and close, like a hand hovering an inch from your skin.

The exit signs are the brightest things in the Cistern. They say EXIT, or they say EXT, or they say XIT, or they say something in a language that uses the same alphabet but arranges it wrong. They glow red — the only red in the entire environment — and they point in directions that do not lead out. Follow one and you will arrive at a door. Open the door and you will find a room. In the room there will be another exit sign, pointing elsewhere.

## The Machinery

The Cistern is industrial. Not in the sense of a single factory with a single purpose, but in the sense of a *district* — compressed, folded into itself, layer upon layer of industrial infrastructure from different eras and different purposes, all crammed into the same volume, interpenetrating.

There are pipes. Pipes everywhere. Pipes of every diameter and material — cast iron, copper, PVC, galvanized steel, something that looks like ceramic, something that looks like bone but is probably a very old composite. They run along walls and ceilings and floors. They cross each other. They merge and split. Some are hot to the touch — not warm, *hot*, painfully hot — and others are so cold that skin sticks to them. Some carry water. Some carry steam. Some carry nothing, and if you press your ear to them you hear silence. Some carry something that is not a liquid or a gas but a vibration, a low mechanical pulse that travels the length of the pipe like a message being passed along a line of whisperers.

There are machines. Not working machines — not exactly. They are machines in the process of *becoming*. A pump that is half-assembled, its housing open, its impeller exposed, surrounded by tools that no one left there. A motor that has been partially disassembled and then partially reassembled *differently*, with components that do not match the original specification — a gear from something larger, a housing from something smaller, bolts that are the wrong thread pitch forced into place with a violence that deformed the surrounding metal. These machines are being modified. By what or whom, the Cistern does not say. The modifications are sometimes improvements and sometimes nonsense — gears that turn against each other, belts that connect nothing to nothing, circuits that loop back on themselves and accomplish nothing but their own looping.

And yet — some of them run. A fan in a ventilation shaft, its blades caked with decades of dust, still turns. Slowly. It pushes air that smells of cold metal and stagnant water from one part of the Cistern to another. The airflow creates microcurrents, eddies, tiny weather systems in the corridors. Where the moving air meets still air, condensation forms, and where condensation forms, the dripping accelerates, and where the dripping accelerates, the built-stalactites grow faster, and the copper-growth spreads, and the puddles widen, and the cycle continues.

A generator somewhere — you can hear it, or feel it, a deep mechanical throb that penetrates walls — produces power for the lights and the fans and the exit signs and whatever else draws current from the Cistern's grid. It runs on something. No one has found it. Its sound is directional but misleading; it seems to come from below, but if you descend toward it, it comes from below again. It is always one floor further down. The generator is the Cistern's heart, and like a heart, it is hidden deep inside the body, protected by layers of structure, known only by its rhythm.

## The Density

The Cistern is dense. Not crowded — there is nothing here to crowd it, no people, no animals, no insects — but *full*. Every volume is occupied by structure. Every corridor is lined with pipes, ducts, cable trays, brackets, junction boxes, valve handles, inspection panels, access hatches, conduit runs, mounting hardware, warning labels, instruction plates, gauges that read nothing or read things that cannot be true (a pressure gauge showing negative atmosphere, a thermometer reading four digits).

You cannot reach out without touching something made. You cannot turn without your shoulder brushing a pipe or a wire-vine or a protruding bolt. The ceiling is low — not uniformly, but in most places, low enough that you are aware of it, low enough that the condensation droplets that form on it fall onto your head and shoulders, cold and sudden.

The layering is geological in its density. Peel back a section of wall — if you could, if the walls were not too cold and too hard and too *attentive* — and you would find layers upon layers: drywall over plywood over steel mesh over concrete block over tile over mortar over brick over iron over concrete again, and then stranger things: a layer of cork, a layer of what appears to be compressed fabric, a layer of glass, a layer of something that might be paper but might be a very thin ceramic. Each layer is a decision. Each layer is an act of construction. The Cistern has been building itself, or being built, for long enough that its walls are thick with intention, even if the intention is illegible.

## The Quiet

Mostly quiet. This is a mercy and a threat.

The dominant sound is the dripping. It is constant but not rhythmic — droplets fall from different heights, at different rates, onto different surfaces, producing a scattered, arrhythmic percussion that your brain tries and fails to resolve into a pattern. Some drips land on metal and ping. Some land in puddles and plip. Some land on concrete and make almost no sound at all, a tiny wet tap that you feel more than hear.

Beneath the dripping: the hum. The electrical systems, the generator, the pipes with their carried vibrations — all of it merges into a low ambient drone that sits at the edge of hearing. It is not loud. It is not soft. It is *there*, the way your own heartbeat is there: constant, foundational, easy to forget until you listen for it, and then impossible to unhear.

Periodically — on the eleven-minute cycle of the compressors, or on longer cycles that correspond to nothing identifiable — the Cistern produces a sound. A clank. A hiss. A long, slow groan of metal adjusting to temperature. A valve opening. A fan catching. A relay clicking. These sounds arrive without warning, exist for a moment, and are absorbed back into the quiet. They are startling every time. They are the Cistern speaking, in a language of mechanical stress and thermodynamic adjustment, and they mean: *I am still here. I am still working. I am still becoming.*

## The Activity

The Cistern is active. Not in the way a forest is active, with its predator-prey dynamics and biological urgency, but in the way a construction site is active when the crew has gone home for the night and the materials themselves continue settling.

Processes are underway. The dripping is a process. The condensation cycle is a process. The slow growth of the copper-formations is a process. The incremental expansion and contraction of metal with temperature fluctuation is a process. The movement of air through ventilation shafts is a process. The modification of half-built machines — wherever that originates — is a process. The accumulation of dust, the oxidation of metal, the leaching of minerals from concrete by acidic condensation — all processes.

They interact. The condensation feeds the dripping. The dripping feeds the puddles. The puddles feed the mineral growth. The mineral growth alters the surfaces it spreads across, changing their thermal properties, which changes the condensation patterns, which changes the dripping. A feedback loop. A slow, cold, industrial ecology with no organisms in it — or rather, with one organism, which is the Cistern itself.

There is something like weather. In larger chambers — the Cistern has a few, rooms that might once have been storage vaults or machine halls, their ceilings lost in darkness above the reach of the dim light — the condensation is heavy enough that it falls as a fine, cold mist. Not rain, exactly. More like the exhale of a very large, very cold room. The mist drifts. It collects on surfaces. It freezes on the coldest surfaces, building up frost in patterns that look almost deliberate — crystalline ferns, feathery structures, the building's breath made visible and permanent.

The frost grows. It is one of the fastest processes in the Cistern. A frost pattern can emerge on a cold pipe in the time it takes to walk the length of a corridor and return. It is beautiful in the way that dangerous things are beautiful — intricate, symmetrical, fragile, and very, very cold.

## The Strange

The Cistern is strange in the way that a dream is strange: everything is plausible, everything follows its own internal logic, and yet the whole is wrong in a way that resists articulation.

The geometry, as mentioned, folds. This is not immediately apparent. Corridors look straight. Rooms look rectangular. Staircases look functional. But walk long enough and you begin to notice: you have been descending for ten minutes and the condensation on the walls is the same condensation — the same droplets, the same patterns, the same built-stalactites — as when you started. Or you turn left three times and arrive somewhere you have never been, a room with a configuration of pipes and wires that is completely unfamiliar, and when you turn around to retrace your steps, the corridor behind you is different from the one you came through.

This is not disorienting in the usual sense. It is disorienting in a deeper sense — the sense that the Cistern is not a fixed space but a *process*, that its architecture is not complete but continuously emerging, and that the act of moving through it participates in its construction. Your footsteps leave impressions in the uncured cement. The impressions persist. The walls lean toward them. You are, without intending to be, one of the Cistern's builders.

The exit signs are strange. The gauges are strange. The water that does not freeze is strange. But the strangest thing — the thing that stays with you, if you have been in the Cistern long enough to stop counting the drips and start hearing the hum as a voice — is the sense that the Cistern *knows it is unfinished*. That it is aware of its own incompletion. That every process it runs, every droplet it condenses, every frost pattern it grows, every machine it half-builds and half-unbuilds, is an attempt to articulate something. To become something. To arrive at a state of finishedness that it cannot define but cannot stop reaching for.

It is a building that is building itself. It is a room that is dreaming of its own completion. It is cold and dark and dense and full of the evidence of its own effort, and it is not done, and it may never be done, and the dripping continues, and the frost grows, and the exit signs glow red, and the pipes hum, and the machines wait for hands that may or may not come.

## The Puddle at the Center

If you find the center — and you may not; the geometry may not permit it, or the Cistern may not have a center in the navigable sense, or the center may move — you will find a puddle.

It is not large. Perhaps a meter across. It sits in a depression in a floor of black tile, surrounded by walls of raw uncured cement that lean inward, very slightly, as though listening. Above it, a pipe drips — one droplet at a time, at an interval of about forty seconds. Each droplet strikes the center of the puddle. Each impact sends a ring outward. The ring reaches the edge of the depression and returns. The returning wave meets the next droplet's ring. The interference pattern is brief and complex and beautiful, and then it is gone, and the puddle is still again.

The puddle does not freeze. The puddle does not evaporate. The puddle does not overflow, though water has been dripping into it for what must be years. It maintains its volume. It maintains its temperature — just above freezing, just warm enough to remain liquid. It maintains its stillness between droplets with a patience that feels like attention.

The puddle is the Cistern's eye. Or its mouth. Or the place where it keeps the thought it is trying to think. Look into it and you will see nothing — the light is too dim, the water too dark. But you will feel that it sees you, or tastes you, or is considering the shape of your presence the way the walls consider the shape of a hand pressed into soft cement.

Sit beside it. The cold will find you. The quiet will find you. The dripping will find your pulse and slow it. And the Cistern will continue its work — condensing, dripping, growing, building, dreaming — around you, through you, with you, indifferent to your presence in the way that weather is indifferent, the way that geology is indifferent, the way that a building is indifferent, which is to say: completely, and yet with a quality of inclusion, as though your being there is simply one more material, one more layer, one more decision the Cistern has made.

---

*The droplet falls. The ring expands. The frost grows on the pipe. The exit sign flickers. The generator hums one floor below.*

*The Cistern continues.*
