Here is a place that holds those parameters, or is held by them. It is best to approach it slowly, to let its scale resolve. It is small. It is dense. It is very quiet.

***

There is no ground. Or, there is only ground, a single, continuous, hand-wrought surface. You are inside, or upon, a vessel of warm, pale terracotta, its curvature so gentle you might mistake it for flatness until you try to walk a straight line and find your path bending ineluctably inward, toward a center you cannot yet see. The vessel is the size of a great, shallow bowl, a small world, a room. Its walls slope up on all sides, curving out of sight, and the light—a normal, honey-colored, late-afternoon light—comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the terracotta itself is gently luminous. The air is warm and still, and smells of dust, dry pollen, and something else: a faint, clean, mineral scent, like the inside of a geode.

This vessel is not empty. It is full. It is packed with a lushness that has no root in soil, for there is no water here, not a molecule of H2O. The chemistry of this life is different, patient, and dry. The vegetation is a riot of forms, but they are not green. They are terracotta, ochre, rust-red, bone-white, and the grey of ash. They grow directly from the vessel’s floor, which is made of the same fired clay, but older, smoother, worn by the passage of countless slow cycles.

Look closer. The "plants" are not plants. They are constructs. They are built. A fern is not a fern, but a frond of paper-thin, hammered copper, its edges sharp and new, its surface beginning to bloom with a velvety, turquoise patina that is not corrosion but a slow, dry, crystalline metabolism. A flower is a spiral of woven reed, its petals stiff with a lacquer of pine resin and powdered ostrich shell, opening and closing not with the sun, but with the gradual, almost imperceptible warming and cooling of the terracotta floor over a cycle that lasts a generation. Moss is a felt of shredded bark and sun-bleached grass, glued into crevices with a paste of clay and tree sap. It is a garden made by hand, but the hands are long gone, or perhaps they are still here, moving too slowly to be perceived.

And the fauna? They are abundant. They are also built. Or, they are in the process of being built, or unbuilt. A tortoise, its shell a mosaic of polished river stones and shards of mirrored mica, creeps across the floor. Its legs are not bone and muscle, but jointed wood, connected by sinews of braided leather. It moves with the creak of a unoiled hinge, leaving a faint trail of fine, white powder—pulverized eggshell, the calcium-rich dust that is the lifeblood of this place. A dragonfly hovers, its wings four panes of scraped, translucent horn, its body a lacquered reed. It does not hunt. It gathers. It lands on a copper fern and, with a proboscis of sharpened bone, gently scrapes at the turquoise patina, collecting the dust. It is a pollinator, carrying the dry, mineral life from one construct to another.

The sound is a sparse tapestry. The dominant sound is the slow, deep groan of the terracotta itself, a sound so low it is felt in the teeth more than heard. It is the sound of the vessel settling, of its young, sharp edges still cooling from the firing. Then there are the small sounds: the *tick* of a stone-shell tortoise's foot, the whisper of a reed-flower's petals shifting, the faint, papery rustle of the horn-winged dragonfly. There is no birdsong, no running water, no wind. The air is too dense, too full of the scent of dry dust and resin.

The erosion here is not of wind and water. It is an erosion of intent. The sharp, fresh-cut edges of the copper leaves are slowly being dulled by the deposition of the fine white dust. The woven reed flowers are becoming saturated with it, their colors muting. The very substance of the built world is being slowly covered, softened, by the patient accumulation of its own life-process. The young, sharp features are being buried in a gentle, dry snowfall of calcium and powdered shell.

And here, the weirdness begins to bleed in at the edges. The light, which seems so normal, sometimes catches a surface—a mica shard on a tortoise shell, a pane of horn—and for a moment, it reflects not the vessel's interior, but something else. A flash of star-dusted blackness. A glimpse of a different, wetter geometry. The density of the place is not just physical. It is a density of being. The air is thick with the quiet purpose of its inhabitants. They are not just animals; they are processes. They are the slow, living mechanism of this world. The rules here are not laws of physics, but of craft. To move, you must be built. To live, you must gather dust. To die is to be slowly, patiently, disassembled and repurposed.

The center of the vessel, where all paths converge, is not a fountain or a tree. It is a single, low, circular table of polished slate. On it, a single object: a half-finished bird, its body a woven basket, one wing a perfect, articulated fan of eagle feathers, the other a mere framework of bent twigs. Around it, a small, patient congregation of the stone-shell tortoises and horn-winged dragonflies, their movements slow and deliberate. They are not eating. They are not mating. They are adding. A tortoise deposits a pinch of white dust from a pouch under its shell. A dragonfly carefully places a single, iridescent beetle wing into the framework. They are building. They are the gardeners, the fauna, the ecosystem, and the inheritors. The world is their work, and their work is never done. The light holds. The dust settles. The slow, quiet, dense, and beautiful construction continues, in a world without water, without soil, without end.
