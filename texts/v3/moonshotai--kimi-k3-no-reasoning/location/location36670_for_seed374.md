The place is warm and dim and it has always been this warm and this dim. The light arrives from a low-hanging haze that never thins, never thickens—a gray-gold murk, sourceless, as if the air itself were faintly luminous the way old paper is faintly luminous. There is no sun to track, no shadow that moves. Nothing moves, except the water, and the water only moves in the ways it has always moved: down, out, through.

The ground is young. This is the first thing. The whole region—call it a basin, though it has no rim, just a long shallow sag in the crust perhaps a day's walk across and many days' walk long—is paved in stone that has not yet learned to be old. The rock is dense and black and fine-grained, split into flat plates as if poured and cooled in layers, and the plates are still sharp. Unweathered. Their edges cut. Where two plates meet, the seam is a knife-line, and along the knife-lines water rises, because water is everywhere just beneath, and the ground sweats it up through the new fractures. The whole basin weeps. Rock faces weep. The undersides of ledges are beaded and dripping into pools that never grow, never shrink, never overflow their rims by more than the thickness of a fingernail. The hydrology of the place was solved long ago and has been holding its solution ever since: water comes up, water goes down, the level is the level.

Nothing grows. No moss, no film of algae, no weed in a crack. The water runs clear over black stone and leaves no green behind. Whatever chemistry the basin runs on does not include plants, or has not yet, or has decided against them. The stone stays bare and the pools stay glassy and nothing softens the hard geometry of the young rock. It gives the place a swept quality, like a courtyard.

And it is, more or less, a courtyard. Because the basin is built.

Not built with high craft. Built the way termites build, or the way corals build, or the way a thousand generations of hands without metal build: cell by cell, lump by lump, always adding, never revising. The makers—call them the wade-folk, though they are not folk exactly; we will come to them—have been laying stone on stone here for longer than the stone beneath has been old, which is to say forever, which is to say the city and the geology are the same age and the same substance. They quarry nothing. They build from the shed skins of their own dead, from shell-plates and ossified secretions and a kind of mineral spit that sets hard as the bedrock, so that the city is, materially, continuous with the ground: black on black, plate on plate, one accretion indistinguishable from another except that some of it is roof.

The construction is low. Nothing in the basin stands taller than two or three times a person's height, and most of it stands much lower—vaults and humps and long half-buried halls, like a city built by people who preferred to live mostly inside the ground and only wear it like a shell. The roofs are the streets. You walk across the city on the city, over domes slick with condensate, over flat plates that ring slightly underfoot because there is a room beneath, down stair-seams where the water runs in stepped rivulets from roof to roof to roof, the whole urban surface one vast terraced watershed. Every surface is wet. The dim air condenses on every cool stone, and the stone is always cool, and so the city drinks the sky and passes it down, level by level, into the pools between buildings, into the channels, into the weeping ground, into whatever drinks beneath.

It is dense. This is the second thing, and you feel it before you understand it. There is no empty ground in the basin. Every plate has something on it—a hut-hump, a cistern mouth, a rack of drying cords, a stack of shell-plates, a shallow basin where something is being soaked—or someone. And there are so many someones.

The wade-folk are broad and low and wet, and there are more of them than the ground should hold. They stand in the pools up to their middles. They lie along the warm seams in the rock. They crowd the roof-streets shoulder to shoulder, moving slowly or not at all, hundreds of them in any glance, thousands in any neighborhood, a population with no horizon to it. They are not human-shaped and it is not worth forcing them into a human shape; what matters is that they are many-limbed and smooth and dark as the stone, that they handle things with patient, unhurried grips, that they communicate constantly, and that communication is the third thing.

The basin is deafening.

It never stops and it has never stopped. The wade-folk call to each other across the roofs in a register that carries through wet air the way sound carries through water—low, booming, vowel-heavy, a slow moaning speech that stacks on itself, hundreds of voices going at once, call and answer and chorus and counter-call, until the whole basin is one sustained chord of conversation that swells and settles and swells but never ceases, not for a moment, not anywhere. Add to that the water: the tick and hiss of condensation, the chuckle of the stepped rivulets, the slap of pools disturbed by bodies, the deep glug of drainage mouths taking the overflow down. Add to that the work: the rhythmic scrape of shell-plate on stone, the wet thock of mineral spit being tamped into seams, the clatter of stacked plates. Add the bodies themselves—limbs on wet rock, the slide and suck of movement through shallow water, multiplied by thousands. The sound has texture. It has weather inside it. Standing in it, you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it the way you hear your own blood: a total, surrounding, information-saturated roar that the inhabitants navigate as easily as you navigate light.

They make their things by hand, in the open, on the roofs. Cord twisted from something fibrous and secreted—not plant fiber, there are no plants, but a protein rope they draw from their own bodies or harvest from the sediment. Tools of flaked shell and knapped black stone, worked to edges that would shame flint. Baskets of woven cord used to sieve the pools. Drying racks where sheets of some translucent stuff, skin or membrane or paper-thin mineral, are pegged out under the dim sky to cure. Nothing metal. Nothing fired. The technology is stone-age in its materials but urban in its scale: the channels are engineered, the cisterns are engineered, the stepped drainage that carries the weeping water down through the city without ever flooding a single vault is the work of uncounted generations of small corrections, each generation laying one plate, adjusting one runnel, and then lying down in the pools to join the chorus.

And here is the thing that takes longest to understand, because it contradicts everything a visitor expects: none of it is going anywhere.

The city is finished. It has always been finished. The young stone is not young because the place is new; it is young because the place replaces itself at exactly the rate it wears. A plate splits, a plate is laid. A vault sags, a vault is re-domed. The wade-folk die and their plates go into the walls and new wade-folk swell up out of the sediment or the deep pools or each other and take their places on the roofs, and the chorus continues at exactly the volume it has always had. There is no growth because the water cannot support more, and no decline because the water cannot support less. The population is the solution to an equation written in seepage and shell, and it was solved so long ago that the solving and the being are the same act.

Nothing changes. This is not stagnation; it is something stranger and harder to hold in the mind, a perfection. The same voices—not the same individuals, the same voices, the same parts in the chorus, the calls answered by the answers that have always answered them, the work-rhythms falling into the gap in the song where a work-rhythm has always fallen. A visitor walking the roof-streets would swear the basin was holding its breath. It is not. It is breathing at exactly one breath per forever.

There is a smell: warm stone, and the mineral flatness of standing water, and something faintly sweet from the curing sheets on the racks, and under it all a low organic note, the smell of many bodies in warm water, not foul, not clean, simply present, the way the sound is present, the way the wet is present.

At the basin's long edges—there are no walls, just a gradual thinning—the city cell-count drops, the roofs give way to bare weeping plates, the chorus thins to scattered voices, and finally there is only black rock and standing water and the sourceless dim, and the silence there is enormous, almost violent, after the roar. A wade-folk who wandered out that far would turn back. Not from fear. From the shape of the equation. There is nothing out there to balance against. The water falls off. The warmth holds but the water falls off, and without the water there is no chorus, and without the chorus there is—nothing anyone has needed a word for.

So the basin keeps its solved self: warm, wet, dim, low, crowded, young, loud, and eternal, the black plates weeping their unchanging water up through the unchanging city, the voices layering on voices in the heavy air, the whole thing humming like a struck bell that has decided, once and for all, never to stop ringing.
