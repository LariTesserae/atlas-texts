The first thing is the sound. It reaches you long before the place does—a dense, woven wall of noise tumbling down the high slopes, composed of a hundred distinct notes. There is the deep, sub-auditory thrum of water moving inside rock, the percussive clatter of shale-sheets sliding against one another, and the sharp, rhythmic striking of stone on stone. Underneath it all, a chorus of voices—human, but pitched to carry—singing in a language of pure tone, call-and-response patterns that match the cadence of hammer blows.

Then the mist thins, and you see the Ascending Pools.

It is a single, steep-sided valley hanging between two raw, black cliffs, no more than a thousand paces across. The air is perpetually cool and wet, heavy with the smell of ozone and bruised chlorophyll. Water is everywhere, but it is not still. It sheets down the rock faces in threads that catch the pale, high-altitude light. It condenses on your skin and on the broad, waxy leaves of the plants that crowd every surface. This is a lushness that feels almost aggressive. Mosses in impossible shades of emerald and electric blue smother every stone. Giant horsetails, their segmented stems thicker than your wrist, stand in dense, dripping thickets. Vines heavy with moisture cascade from ledges, their tips unfurling in slow, green explosions. Every plant seems to be drinking the air itself.

And the valley is being built. This is the shock of it. The lush chaos is not natural. The water is not allowed to fall where it will. A settlement is emerging, not constructed from the top down, but grown upward, cell by cell, against the cliff face. The people here are building with the two materials they have in abundance: water and stone.

They have carved a sequence of shallow, circular terraces into the slope, each one a perfect, brimming pool. The water overflows from one to the next in a continuous, sonorous spill. This is the source of the music. But the pools are not merely reservoirs. They are acoustic devices. The people, small figures in soaked, moss-dyed wool, move along the edges. They strike the water with shaped wooden paddles, not to splash, but to send specific vibrations through the liquid. The sound travels down the water-filled channels carved into the rock, into the very bones of the mountain.

At the base of the valley, where the vibration is concentrated, a new thing is happening. The rock itself is responding. The stone is young—geologically infantile. It is still settling from some recent, cataclysmic uplift. It is soft, almost plastic, riddled with microscopic fractures. The sustained, directed sound is coaxing it. Where the frequencies resonate, the rock weeps. It exudes a fine, mineral-rich paste that the builders guide with their hands into rising forms. They are not carving the mountain; they are singing it into shape, note by note, generation by generation. Pillars thicken like stalagmites. Arches grow from the walls to meet each other. It is architecture as a slow, geological process. The buildings are not separate from the cliff; they are the cliff, persuaded.

The place is nearly empty of people, and the few you see are singular, deliberate figures, moving with the slow certainty of those who think in centuries. There are no animals except for a single, white-feathered bird that sometimes glides between the pools, utterly silent, as if out of respect for the human-made din.

This is a workshop. A sacred, wet, verdant workshop where the boundary between craft and geology has dissolved. The tools are songs and patience. The material is time. The goal is not a finished city, but a process—a mountain learning to become a home, note by resonant note, in the cool, dripping, magnificent noise of its own creation.
