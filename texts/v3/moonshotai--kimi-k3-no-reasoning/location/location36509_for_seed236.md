**The Terraces of the New Ascension**

Here, the sky has forgotten how to weep. 

The plateau thrusts upward like a clenched fist through the roof of the world—granite and basalt so recently cooled that the edges still carry the sharpness of intent, as if the mountain were birthed yesterday and has not yet learned to apologize for its existence. There is no water. There has never been water. The very concept of liquidity feels alien here, where moisture is a rumor carried only in the wax of leaves and the blood of things that crawl.

Yet life riots.

The vegetation is a green dementia, a lushness that defies the arid void. It is not fed by rain but by the mountain’s own slow exhalations—mineral-rich vapors rising from deep vents, captured by leaves that have evolved into dense, fleshy plates the color of oxidized copper. These plants stack themselves in impossible density, a cathedral of flora where every surface is colonized. Vines thick as ship’s cables braid themselves into living walls; epiphytes the size of houses cling to vertical cliffs, their roots not seeking soil but drilling directly into the young stone, cracking it further in slow, patient embraces that take centuries to complete.

The built environment is not separate from this growth but *of* it. Here, architecture is a form of disciplined photosynthesis. The settlement—if it can be called that, this density of dwelling—consists of structures grown rather than built: hollowed-out bulbs of lignified cellulose, interconnected by bridges of braided root that have hardened into something stronger than bronze. These are not ruins, nor are they new; they exist in a perpetual state of *becoming*, maintained by a slow, communal dreaming. The walls weep a dry, resinous sap that hardens into amber filigree, sealing cracks against the wind. There are no tools, no metal, no fire—only the gradual persuasion of biology into geometry. Streets are channels worn smooth by the passage of soft bodies over millennia, lined with cushions of moss that glow faintly in the normal, unremarkable light of a sun that seems too lazy to burn brightly.

The fauna move through this like thoughts through a mind. They are abundant, silent, and strange—six-legged grazers with hides of living felt that browse on the mineral crusts; gliding membranes of translucent skin that drift between the high spires, catching the thermal updrafts with wings that make no sound. They have no need for calls or songs. Communication here is chemical, a drift of pheromones through the dense air, or perhaps something slower—a meeting of eyes, a touching of antennae that carries the weight of a novel. The density is such that one cannot walk without brushing against fur, leaf, or stone; every step is a negotiation with a neighbor.

The air itself is a substance, thick with the dust of spores and the exhalations of the living rock. It tastes of ozone and greenness. Despite the warmth—a heavy, enveloping heat that presses like a palm against the skin—the silence is absolute. Sound seems to die here, absorbed by the layered vegetation, muffled by the density of bodies. The only noise is the occasional, startling crack of stone giving way to root, a sound like a bone breaking, followed by a resettling that lasts for years.

Time moves like honey here. The erosion that shapes other worlds—the patient grinding of water, the violent theft of wind—has no purchase. Instead, the mountain is being slowly, deliberately *replaced* by the biological. The sharp features of the young peaks are being wrapped in living skin, softened by the accumulation of centuries, yet the underlying violence of the geology remains visible in cross-section: black glassy flows frozen mid-cascade, crystalline structures that catch the light and hold it, unmoving.

There is a weirdness here, subtle and pervasive. The light, though normal in spectrum, behaves oddly when filtered through the dense canopy—it pools in places where it should scatter, creating chambers of illumination that feel intentional, as if the forest were lighting its own altars. And sometimes, in the deepest thickets where the vegetation grows so dense that it becomes a solid green darkness, the air grows suddenly cold, and the stone beneath seems to remember being magma, radiating a warmth that has nothing to do with the sun.

This is a world within a world, a single mountain held in the palm of the sky, where life has become so dense that it has achieved the weight of geology, and where silence is not an absence but a presence—a held breath that has lasted ten thousand years and shows no sign of exhaling.
