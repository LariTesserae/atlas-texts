At the bottom of the world, where the ground sags into a slow, shallow bowl, the city has only been a city for a few centuries—and it shows.

The Word-Hollows, they call it: a settlement of no more than a mile across, cradled in a depression that never rises more than a hundred feet above the waterline of the surrounding flatlands. The rim of the hollow is unfinished—raw, unweathered shale and wet, crumbling clay, as though some great force had scraped this bowl from the earth only yesterday and left it cooling, unhealed. There are no rounded stones here, no gentle contours. Everything about the terrain is abrupt, nascent, still deciding what it means to become.

And yet, into this rawness, life has not merely crept—it has overbuilt.

The vegetation is total. Not merely a canopy, but a substrate, a matrix, an architecture. There is no soil visible anywhere within the Hollows; only roots, only stems, only the thick, layered flesh of the *Verdant Lattice*—a single, colony-scale organism that has come to dominate every surface. It is part ivy, part fungus, part something older that dreams in green. It grows in sheets and in buttresses, in ribbed arches and in sagging, honeycombed walls. It has swallowed buildings whole and kept them standing: what you walk beneath is not stone, but the hollowed-out impression of stone, the plant having replaced mortar and brick with lignin and living pulp. The streets are not paved—they are *grown*. You tread upon a mat of slick, resilient root-flesh that pulses faintly underfoot, cool and damp, exuding a fine mist that beads on every surface.

Water here is intermittent, sly, and atmospheric. It does not fall in rivers or lakes, but *condenses*. The Lattice breathes out moisture constantly; droplets form on the undersides of leaves the size of doors, on the undersides of eaves made of woven vines, on the undersides of your eyelashes. The air is perpetually cool—hovering just above chill, just below mist—and smells of wet bark, of ozone, of the faint metallic tang of unformed stone. When rain comes, it is brief and directional, falling in slanted silver threads that the Lattice catches and reroutes into narrow, pulsing channels along its own veins. These channels glow faintly blue in the dark, carrying not just water but nutrients, and if you press your ear to a thick enough stem, you can hear the slow, hydraulic *thump* of sap moving like blood through a sleeping giant.

Light is scarce. The canopy above is not so much a canopy as a lid—a dense, interwoven ceiling of overlapping fronds and translucent fungal membranes that filter what little daylight exists down to a twilight murk. There is no true dawn here, only a slow, gray brightening that never quite becomes day. At the heart of the Hollows, where the plant is thickest, darkness is nearly absolute, broken only by the bioluminescent veins of the Lattice and the occasional flicker of machinery.

For the city is industrial, though not in the way of smokestacks and soot. The factories are *grown* into the plant, half-organic, half-mechanical. Engines hum within hollowed-out trunks; pistons pump not steam but pressurized sap; conveyor belts are living tendrils that coil and uncoil to move goods through narrow, vine-walled corridors. The power grid is a hybrid—copper wires fused with conductive fungal hyphae, flickering bulbs grown around bioluminescent nodes. The machinery is quiet, subdued, as though the plant absorbs sound, but it is always *moving*: gears turning, fluids circulating, small valves opening and closing with wet, precise clicks. There are no crowds. The Hollows are a city of function without mass—buildings stand empty, hallways stretch unoccupied, streets curve through dense greenery without footsteps. The population is not absent, but diffuse, existing somewhere in the machinery, in the plant, in the space between.

Fauna, however, is everywhere—abundant, adaptive, and strange. In the near-dark, things with too many joints skitter along root-bridges. Pale, wingless birds with membranous sails glide between buttresses, feeding on spores. Amphibious predators with translucent skin lurk in condensation pools, their bodies flickering with internal light as they hunt. Insects the size of fists drone in slow, heavy loops, their wings vibrating at frequencies that make the air hum just below hearing. Life here does not compete for space—it *infuses* it, every niche filled, every shadow inhabited, yet always sparse in number, spread thin across the damp expanse.

And reality… bends.

Not catastrophically, but persistently. The deeper you go into the Hollows, the less the world obeys its own rules. Distances stretch and compress: a street that should take minutes to cross might take hours, or seconds. Shadows fall in directions that have no light source. The plant grows in patterns that repeat at scales too small and too large to be natural—fractal arches, self-similar spirals, structures that seem to remember shapes they have not yet taken. Sometimes, if you stand very still, you can feel the ground *not quite there*—a momentary hollowness beneath your feet, as though the world has not finished rendering itself. Objects duplicate in your peripheral vision. Sounds arrive before their causes. The boundary between the living Lattice and the machines embedded within it blurs, and occasionally, you will see a gear grow from a stem, or a leaf unfold into a circuit board.

This is not decay. This is *emergence*—a place still being written, where the laws are not yet fixed, where the city and the plant and the animals and the damp, cool dark are all negotiating what kind of reality they will allow to take root.

The Hollows are quiet, but not silent. The machinery hums. The plant breathes. The fauna stir. And beneath it all, there is the slow, wet sound of something growing—always growing—into the shape of a world that has not decided what it wants to be.
