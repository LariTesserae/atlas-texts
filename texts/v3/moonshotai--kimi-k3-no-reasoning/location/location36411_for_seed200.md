The world here is still deciding whether it wants to exist.

You stand at the edge of something that has no name yet because it has not finished becoming. The ground is less terrain than intention—black mud the consistency of held breath, stitched through with pale quartz veins that pulse faintly, as if the earth's first nervous system were being threaded through its body before your eyes. Water is everywhere and nowhere: not standing in pools, not flowing in channels, but *present*—seeping upward through the substrate with the slow insistence of a secret, condensing on every surface, hanging in the air so densely that breathing feels like drinking.

The heat is absolute and patient. It does not blaze; it presses. It has pressed for as long as anything has been here to press upon, and it will press long after you leave. Nothing in this place experiences temperature as a variable. Heat is the medium, the given, the rule.

Vegetation crowds every available surface in a state of arrested explosion. Ferns with fronds like green flames lean into one another, their tips touching, fusing, exchanging cells through membranes so thin they might be agreements rather than boundaries. Vines thick as pythons wrap the raw volcanic pillars that rise from the mud—not climbing them, not yet, because the pillars are still forming, still extruding upward from the earth's interior at a rate so slow that the vines have adapted to grow *with* them, synchronizing their expansion to the geology's improvisation. The plants are a deep, saturated green that absorbs what little light filters through the canopy, holding it, metabolizing dimness itself.

And the canopy—there is no single canopy. There are canopies layered upon canopies, seven or eight strata of leaf and frond and aerial root, each one filtering the light from above until what reaches the floor is a green-black half-glow, the color of deep water. You see by it the way you might see by memory: things resolve slowly, imperfectly, always with the suspicion that something else is standing just beside the thing you're looking at.

The fauna are everywhere. They are the point.

You cannot take a step without disturbing something. Beetles the size of your fist carve slow tunnels through the wet wood of fallen logs, their mandibles clicking in patterns that repeat every seventeen seconds without variation. Moths with wingspans like outstretched hands cling to every vertical surface, their scales shedding a phosphorescent dust that drifts in the heavy air and settles on your skin and does not brush off. Tree frogs cluster in the axils of leaves in densities that defy territorial logic—hundreds of them, each no bigger than a thumbnail, their throats pulsing in a synchronization so perfect it produces not a chorus but a single, sustained tone that has been sounding without pause for longer than the frogs themselves have been alive.

Something large moves in the mid-canopy. You never see it clearly—none of the large things here allow themselves to be seen clearly—but you feel the displacement of its passage, the way the vine-curtains swing and the smaller creatures adjust their rhythms to accommodate it. It might be a mammal. It might be something for which the category does not yet exist. The distinction seems unimportant. What matters is that it is *there*, has always been there, will always be there, moving through the same circuit of branches in the same unhurried pattern.

There are no rivers, but the water must go somewhere. It percolates through the root-mat in sheets so thin they are less liquid than suggestion, carrying nutrients in a cycle so closed and complete that nothing is ever lost and nothing is ever gained. The entire system exists in perfect equilibrium: every molecule of water accounted for, every joule of heat distributed evenly, every organism consuming and being consumed at rates that have never fluctuated. Evolution here is not a process but a state. The species that exist are the species that have always existed. Mutation occurs—surely it must—but each variation is absorbed into the whole so smoothly, so instantly, that no record of difference is ever preserved.

This is the strangest thing about this place: its rawness is not the rawness of youth. It is the rawness of something that will never mature because it has no need to. The volcanic pillars do not rise toward some completed mountain range. The mud does not await compression into stone. The quartz veins do not foreshadow ore deposits or crystal chambers. Everything is exactly as finished as it will ever be, and that unfinishedness is permanent. You are not witnessing a beginning. You are witnessing an eternity that has chosen to look like a beginning.

The sound is constant and structured. Beneath the frog-tone, beneath the beetle-clicks, beneath the white-noise hiss of ten thousand insects doing whatever insects do, there is a deeper layer: the slow, almost subsonic groan of the pillars growing, the wet subterranean shift of water seeking equilibrium, the soft detonation of spore-pods releasing their cargo into air so thick with life that the spores rarely travel more than a meter before being intercepted, consumed, incorporated.

Nothing echoes. The density absorbs all echoes. Sound moves outward from its source and is immediately caught, muffled, swallowed by the sheer proximity of other things.

You look up—what you can see of up, which is perhaps thirty meters before the canopy layers converge into green opacity—and you notice that the light does not change. There is no sun to track, no cloud to shift. The dimness is a constant, a feature, a rule of the world as fundamental as the heat. Photosynthesis here must work differently than you understand, must have found a way to extract energy from scarcity, or perhaps the plants feed on something else entirely: the warmth, the wetness, the slow mineral exhalations of the forming earth.

The air smells of chlorophyll and wet stone and the musk of a thousand species whose names have never been spoken because there has never been anyone to speak them.

You realize, slowly, that you have been standing still for a long time, and nothing has changed. The moths have not moved from their perches. The frog-tone has not wavered. The large thing in the canopy has completed its circuit and begun it again, precisely. The water continues to seep. The pillars continue to grow at a rate indistinguishable from stillness.

This place does not resist you. It does not welcome you. It simply *includes* you, the way it includes everything: completely, without adjustment, without preference. Your breathing has already synchronized with the frog-tone. Your body temperature has already equilibrated with the ambient heat. The phosphorescent dust on your skin has already begun, perhaps, to integrate with your cells.

You could stay here forever, and forever would not notice.
