The root-chamber lies some thirty meters beneath a basalt plain, accessible only through a volcanic vent that narrows to the width of a human shoulder before opening into this singular, self-contained world. The space is perhaps forty paces across at its widest—a rough sphere of stone with a domed ceiling that drips no water, has never dripped water, and exists in a state of mineral dryness so complete that the basalt walls have developed a powdery, desiccated patina like the skin of some ancient creature. The heat rises from below, a constant 35 degrees Celsius that emanates from the bedrock itself, creating an environment that feels less like a cave and more like the interior of a kiln, though one where things grow rather than fire.

The vegetation here does not drink. Instead, it transpires in reverse—pulling moisture not from soil but from the thin, dry atmosphere itself, extracting hydrogen from mineral hydrates and assembling water molecule by molecule through a process that makes the air feel sharp, metallic, and faintly electric. The plants are low, dense, and luminous in their obscurity. Their leaves are thick as leather, overlapping in rosettes of deep violet and bruised orange, colors so saturated they seem to vibrate in the dimness. These are the *kell*—succulent, waxy things that grow in spiral formations across the floor, their roots penetrating microscopic fissures in the basalt to extract trace salts. Between these anchor plants, climbing species have colonized the walls: *thresh-vines* with photosynthetic bark that appears black until touched by the light, at which point they flare with bioluminescent chlorophyll, revealing a deep, arterial red that pulses slowly, a heartbeat of photosynthesis.

The light enters through a fissure in the ceiling—a crack no wider than a finger that admits a single, persistent shaft of pale, dusty illumination. This beam does not move with the sun; the fissure is angled toward a fixed star, or perhaps the world above has ceased to rotate in any perceptible way, or perhaps time simply does not pass here in gradients. The light remains always at the same angle, always the same wan, pewter intensity, casting shadows that never shift, never lengthen, never fade. It is a frozen noon, or a frozen dusk. Where this light touches the wall, a garden of crystalline lichens has bloomed—silicate-based life that grows in fractal plates, transparent as glass, refracting the dim beam into faint rainbows that lie scattered on the floor like dropped coins.

The fauna have evolved to match this abundance of food in the absence of water. They are small, dense, and loud. The *skitters*—six-legged mammals the size of a thumb with transparent exoskeletons—move in constant, clicking herds across the vegetation, their mandibles striking sparks from the mineral-rich leaves. These sparks are the primary illumination beyond the ceiling-beam: tiny, orange flashes that pop and fade in the undergrowth, accompanied by a sizzling sound like fat on a fire. Above them, gliding on thermals from the warm floor, are the *drakes*—reptilian creatures with wingspans no longer than a hand, their scales evolved to absorb and store heat. They emit a continuous, resonant hum, a low-frequency drone created by air passing over specialized ridges in their throats, which serves both as echolocation and as a mating call that never ceases. The combination of clicking, sparking, and droning creates a cacophony that fills the chamber completely—a dense, layered sound that seems to have physical weight, pressing against the eardrums.

Scattered throughout this biological noise are the works of hands. They are old, though they show no erosion; the dry heat preserves perfectly. A cluster of three structures sits near the eastern wall, built from stacked basalt plates and bound with woven *thresh-vine* that has petrified into a woody, metallic rope. These are low, dome-shaped dwellings, each large enough for a single occupant to crouch within, their interiors lined with the same desiccated leaves that carpet the floor. Tools lie where they were last set down: scrapers made from the shed exoskeletons of *skitters*, bound to basalt handles with sinew; hollowed gourds (from some plant now extinct, or perhaps imported from a distant, wetter world) used to store the mineral salts harvested from the vegetation; and, most notably, a series of shallow depressions carved into the floor itself—mortars where the plants were ground to release their stored moisture, a drop at a time, collected and hoarded against a drought that has lasted geological ages.

The preservation is absolute. A footprint in the dust of the floor—three-toed, narrow—looks as though it was made yesterday. It was made ten thousand years ago. Nothing here decomposes; instead, things mummify, becoming part of the dry archive of the chamber. A *drake* that died mid-flight rests on a ledge, its wings still extended, its hum silenced but its body intact, covered in a fine layer of mineral dust that sparkles when the occasional spark from a *skitter* lands upon it.

The air itself is thick with floating spores—bioluminescent, though dimly so, drifting in the stagnant heat like slow, green snow. They catch in the throat. They taste of copper and ozone. To breathe here is to participate in the ecology; the lungs become coated with the same waxy secretion that covers the leaves, an adaptation that visitors develop within hours, a biological acclimation that renders the dry heat breathable but leaves the voice permanently altered, lower, resonant, humming with the same frequency as the *drakes*.

There is no wind. There is no weather. The temperature does not fluctuate. The light does not change. The chamber exists in a state of dynamic equilibrium so complete that it has achieved a kind of temporal stasis—not frozen, exactly, but cycling through identical states forever. The *skitters* click in the same patterns. The *drakes* hum the same sustained chord. The plants grow and are consumed and grow again in the same configurations, a closed loop of entropy managed so precisely that nothing is lost, nothing gained.

To stand in the center is to feel the pressure of this perfection—the loud, hot, dry, crowded emptiness of a world that has solved the equation of survival and has no need to change, no capacity to evolve further, no desire to do anything but continue, exactly so, in the dim and the noise and the heat, hidden from the sky, complete unto itself.
