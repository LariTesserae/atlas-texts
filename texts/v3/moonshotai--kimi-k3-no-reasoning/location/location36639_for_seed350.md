The first thing you notice is that the water is not below you. It is around you, above you, pressing against your skin from every direction at once. You are not drowning. You are breathing it. The water here is thick, warm as blood, and it moves through your lungs like a second kind of air. It tastes of iron and something sweet, like overripe fruit left too long in the sun. This is the world now. The flood came and never left, and the water learned to let you live inside it.

You are in a low basin, a shallow depression in the earth that stretches for miles in every direction. The ground is soft, young, still settling. It has not yet decided what it wants to be. Ridges rise and fall like the backs of sleeping animals, sharp-edged and fresh, as if the land was carved yesterday and has not yet been worn smooth by time. The sediment is loose, shifting under your feet with every step. You sink to your ankles, and the mud releases you with a wet, sucking sound that is immediately swallowed by the larger noise.

The noise is everywhere. It is not one sound but a thousand, layered and colliding. There is the deep, grinding groan of machinery, pistons and turbines turning somewhere below the water, their vibrations traveling through the liquid and into your bones. There is the hiss of steam escaping from vents in the earth, the clank of metal on metal, the rhythmic pounding of something vast and tireless. And over it all, there is the sound of the water itself, churning, roiling, never still. It slaps against the ruins, gurgles through broken pipes, crashes against itself in waves that have no wind to drive them. The water is alive with motion, and it is loud.

The light is dim, a murky amber that filters down from somewhere far above. It is not sunlight. It is the glow of a thousand submerged lamps, their bulbs still burning despite the flood, casting halos of sickly yellow through the murk. The light does not reach far. It is swallowed by the water, by the dense growth that fills every available space. You can see perhaps ten feet in any direction before the world dissolves into shadow and motion.

The vegetation is everywhere. It is not green. It is black, deep purple, the color of bruises and rot. It grows in thick, tangled mats that float on the surface of the water, in long tendrils that reach up from the mud below, in vast, pulsing blooms that cling to the sides of drowned buildings. The plants are not plants, not exactly. They move. They writhe. They open and close like mouths, and when you brush against them, they curl toward you, not with malice but with a kind of blind, hungry curiosity. They are warm to the touch, and they exude a thick, oily sap that clouds the water and smells of fermentation.

There are no animals. No fish, no insects, no birds. The water is too warm, too thick, too full of chemicals and noise. Nothing that once lived here could survive. But the plants have filled the void. They have grown monstrous, unchecked, and they have begun to take on the roles of the creatures that are gone. Some of them hunt. They lie in wait, their tendrils still, until something brushes against them, and then they snap shut, pulling their prey into their sticky, suffocating embrace. Others scavenge, breaking down the remains of the old world, dissolving metal and stone with acids that drip from their leaves. And still others simply grow, spreading outward in every direction, filling the water with their bulk until there is no room for anything else.

The ruins of the old world are scattered throughout the basin. They are not grand. They are small, partial, broken. A rusted gearbox half-buried in the mud. A section of pipe, its interior lined with algae. A single wall, its bricks crumbling, its windows empty. These are the remains of factories, of workshops, of the machines that once ruled this place. They are not ancient. They are recent. The flood came quickly, and the world was not given time to decay gracefully. Everything here is still sharp, still raw, still bleeding rust and oil into the water.

But the machines are not dead. They are still running. Somewhere below the water, the engines are still turning, the pistons still pumping, the gears still grinding. You can feel them in the soles of your feet, in the pit of your stomach. The whole basin vibrates with their motion, a low, constant hum that never stops. And sometimes, if you are still, you can hear something else beneath the noise. A pattern. A rhythm. The machines are not just running. They are working. They are building something, or unbuilding it, or both at once. You cannot tell.

The water is not still. It is never still. It moves in currents that have no source, in whirlpools that form and dissolve in moments, in waves that rise and fall without warning. The turbulence is constant, chaotic, and it carries with it debris from the old world. A sheet of corrugated metal spins past you, its edges sharp enough to cut. A tangle of wire drifts by, sparking faintly. A wooden crate, its contents long since dissolved, bumps against your leg and is gone. The water is a conveyor belt, a river, a storm, all at once. It does not rest.

And the world is changing. Constantly. You watch as a ridge of sediment rises from the mud, pushed upward by some unseen force, and then collapses, dissolving into a cloud of silt. You see a bloom of vegetation burst into flower, its petals opening wide, and then wither and rot in the space of a breath. The machines below the water are not just running. They are reshaping the land, tearing it down and building it up in an endless, frenzied cycle. The basin is alive, not with life as you know it, but with a kind of mechanical, vegetal, hydraulic frenzy. It is creating and destroying itself over and over, and it does not care if you are here to see it.

The air, if you can call it that, is thick with moisture and the smell of ozone. It is hard to breathe, not because there is no oxygen, but because there is too much of everything else. The water is full of particles, of spores, of microscopic fragments of metal and stone. They coat your skin, your hair, your eyes. They get into your mouth, your nose, your lungs. You are becoming part of the water, and it is becoming part of you.

You move forward, slowly, pushing through the dense growth, the swirling debris, the constant noise. You do not know where you are going. There is no destination here. There is only the basin, the water, the machines, the plants. The world has shrunk to this place, this moment, this endless, deafening, turbulent now.

And then you see it. A structure, larger than the others, rising from the mud ahead. It is a factory, or what is left of one. Its walls are intact, its roof partially collapsed, its windows glowing with that same sickly yellow light. It is surrounded by a thicket of vegetation, so dense that it forms a wall, a barrier, a fortress. The plants here are different. They are taller, thicker, their leaves broad and waxy, their stems as thick as your arm. They do not move as you approach. They are waiting.

You push through them, and they part for you, reluctantly, as if they are allowing you to pass. Inside, the factory is dry. The water stops at the threshold, held back by some unseen force. The air here is cooler, clearer, and the noise is muffled, distant. The floor is concrete, cracked and stained, and the walls are lined with machines. They are not rusted. They are clean, polished, their surfaces gleaming in the dim light. They are not running. They are waiting.

In the center of the room, there is a table. On the table, there is a map. It is not a map of the basin. It is a map of the world, the world as it was before the flood. The continents are outlined in black, the oceans in blue. But the map is changing. As you watch, the lines shift, the colors bleed, the shapes distort. The world is being redrawn, over and over, and the map is trying to keep up. It cannot. The changes are too fast, too chaotic. The map is failing.

You reach out to touch it, and your hand passes through. The map is not real. It is a projection, a hologram, a memory. It is the last echo of a world that no longer exists. And as you stand there, watching it flicker and fade, you realize that you are not in the factory. The factory is in you. The water, the machines, the plants, the noise, the light—they are all part of you now. You are the basin. You are the flood. You are the world, remaking itself in real time, and you are the only one left to witness it.

The noise returns, louder now, a crescendo of grinding metal and churning water. The walls of the factory begin to shake, the machines to hum, the map to dissolve into static. The water rushes in, filling the room, filling your lungs, filling your mind. You are no longer breathing. You are no longer thinking. You are simply being, a part of the endless, turbulent, deafening now.

And the world goes on, creating and destroying itself, over and over, forever.
