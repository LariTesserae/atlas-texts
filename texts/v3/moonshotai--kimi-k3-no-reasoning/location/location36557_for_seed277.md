The highlands are called the Hollow Plateau, though they are not hollow in any sense you could verify from the ground. The name came from the acoustics—sound behaves strangely up here, swallowed and redistributed. A shout dies at arm’s length. A footstep might arrive, delayed, from a direction you did not step.

It is warm and it is dark. The darkness is not night; there is no night cycle here that corresponds to anything you’d recognize. Light comes from low, diffuse sources—bioluminescent veins in the exposed basalt, the faint chemical glow of condensation pooling in eroded channels, the occasional pulse of something large moving through the sparse canopy. The sky, when you can see it, is a deep violet-black, starless, thick. Clouds hang low and heavy but rarely release. When rain comes, it comes as a fine, warm mist that never quite satisfies.

The elevation creates its own weather. Not the kind you’re used to—no mountain storms, no alpine clarity. Instead, the plateau generates slow, rolling pressure systems that move like thoughts. Fog forms in the morning, burns off by what would be noon, reforms at dusk. The fog is warm. It carries spores. The spores glow faintly, so the fog glows faintly, so the world exists in a state of dim self-illumination.

---

Vegetation is sparse but deliberate. Nothing here sprawls. Plants grow in isolated clusters, widely spaced, as if each had negotiated its own territory and agreed not to encroach. The dominant form is a kind of pillar-cactus, tall and segmented, with a skin like cured leather. They store water in their cores—water harvested from the fog through a process of condensate collection that feels engineered, though nothing built them. Their spines are long and hollow. When wind moves through a stand of them, it produces a tone so low you feel it in your sternum rather than hear it.

Between the cactus stands, the ground is mostly bare. Exposed rock, eroded into smooth undulations, like the surface of a brain. The rock is dark basalt, shot through with veins of something pale and phosphorescent. These veins carry moisture—they sweat. The condensation collects in natural basins, shallow pools that never freeze, never quite evaporate. The water is warm. It smells faintly of copper and something organic, like a distant memory of blood.

---

The fauna is abundant, but you will rarely see more than one creature at a time. They exist in the space between presences. The density parameter is not violated; it is simply that the animals here are solitary by nature, territorial, spaced across the vastness like punctuation.

The most common are the long-legged grazers—creatures that stand perhaps three meters at the shoulder, with bodies like elongated barrels and heads that seem too small. They move slowly, methodically, from cactus to cactus, stripping bark with prehensile lips. Their eyes are enormous, reflective, adapted to the perpetual dusk. They make almost no sound. When they die—and they do die, often, from predation or age or the simple accumulation of time—their bodies become resources. Other creatures arrive within hours. The decomposition is fast, warm, thorough.

The predators are harder to see. They are built for this darkness, this warmth. Some hunt by heat, some by vibration, some by means you have not yet categorized. They are not fast, not in the way you expect. They are patient. They wait in the eroded channels, in the shadow of cactus stands, in the pockets of mist. When they strike, it is quiet. The plateau absorbs the sound.

There are also the flyers—things that move through the low cloud layer, visible only when they descend. They are large, silent, with wingspans that blot out what little light exists. They do not land. They hunt by dropping, snatching, rising. Their prey is usually the small, warm things that live in the rock crevices—rodents, insects, the occasional young grazer separated from its mother.

---

The industrial presence is strange because there are no structures. No factories, no engines, no power lines. And yet the plateau hums with a low, constant mechanized rhythm. It comes from the ground.

The basalt veins—the phosphorescent ones—they are not mineral. They are conduits. They carry heat, moisture, electrical charge. They pulse. If you place your hand on exposed rock, you feel it: a slow, regular throb, like a heartbeat, but mechanical. The entire plateau is wired, though nothing built the wiring. It is a natural system that functions like infrastructure. The veins connect to deeper networks, geothermal nodes, places where the planet’s internal heat rises close enough to the surface to be useful.

The flora taps into it. The cactus pillars have root systems that interface with the veins, drawing power, converting it into bioluminescence, into chemical energy, into the faint glow that illuminates their spines. The fauna taps into it too—some creatures have evolved symbiotic relationships with the vein-network, plugging in, literally, through specialized organs that draw current. The long-legged grazers have them. So do some of the predators. You can see the connection points: small, bioluminescent nodes along their spines, pulsing in sync with the ground.

It is an ecosystem that runs on electricity, though no one electrified it. It is industrial in function, organic in form.

---

The erosion is old. The plateau has been here long enough to settle into its own shape. There are no sharp edges, no fresh breaks. The channels carved by ancient water are smooth, rounded, worn down to their essential forms. The rock has been exposed to wind and fog and time for so long that it has become soft-looking, though it is not soft. It is dense, heavy, permanent.

The scale is difficult to comprehend. The plateau stretches beyond any horizon you can reach. It has its own weather systems, its own ecology, its own physics. The air pressure is lower here, but not so low that you cannot breathe. The temperature is warm, constant, unchanging. The light is dim, constant, unchanging. There are no seasons, only cycles of fog and clarity, mist and stillness.

You could walk for days and see the same things: cactus stands, rock, pools, fog. And yet it would not feel repetitive. The scale creates its own variety. A cactus stand here is not the same as a cactus stand there. The spacing changes. The vein-patterns in the rock shift. The fauna you encounter will be different, though the same species. The fog will be thicker or thinner. The hum from the ground will be louder or softer. It is a landscape that reveals itself slowly, in increments, over distances that do not feel like distances because there are no landmarks, no destinations, only continuation.

---

The rules of this world are not written anywhere, but they are consistent:

1. Sound is precious. Do not waste it. The plateau takes sound and does not give it back.

2. Water is communal. The pools are shared. The condensation is shared. Nothing owns the moisture, though everything uses it.

3. The veins are sacred, though nothing worships them. They are simply essential. To damage a vein is to damage the network. The network will heal, but slowly. The area around the damage will dim. The creatures that depend on it will move away. The cactus will wither. The plateau will remember.

4. Darkness is not absence. It is a medium. It carries information—chemical signals, electrical pulses, the faint vibrations of things moving far away. To navigate the darkness is to learn to read it.

5. Time moves differently here. Not faster or slower, but differently. Processes that should take hours take days. Processes that should take days take hours. The decomposition of a grazer is fast. The growth of a cactus is slow. The fog forms and reforms on its own schedule. You will lose track of time. This is normal.

---

The quiet is the most striking thing. With so much life, so much activity, so many processes in motion, you would expect noise. Instead, there is a kind of mutual agreement toward silence. The grazers move quietly. The predators hunt quietly. The flyers pass overhead without a sound. Even the wind, when it moves through the cactus spines, produces only that subsonic tone you feel rather than hear.

The plateau hums, but it is a background hum, so constant it becomes silence. The fog glows, but it is a dim glow, so constant it becomes darkness. The creatures live and die and hunt and graze, but they do it all quietly, as if volume itself were a resource too scarce to squander.

You will find yourself whispering, though there is no one to hear you. You will find yourself moving slowly, though there is no reason to hurry. You will find yourself attuning to the rhythm of the veins, the pulse of the ground, the slow cycles of fog and clarity. You will become part of the ecosystem, whether you intend to or not.

The plateau does not welcome you. It does not reject you. It simply includes you, absorbs you into its processes, its quiet, its dark warmth. You are another presence in a sparse landscape, another creature in an abundant ecology, another pulse in a network that predates you and will outlast you.

And when you leave—if you leave—you will carry the silence with you. You will carry the darkness. You will carry the memory of warmth that never burned, of light that never illuminated, of life that never announced itself. The plateau will remain, humming, glowing faintly, continuing its slow, industrial, organic processes in the dark.
