They call it the Hollow. Not because nothing is there — it is one of the most densely inhabited places on Earth — but because the mountain, Haraq Tuun, has opened itself, and everything lives inside the open.

---

The approach to Haraq Tuun does not suggest an entrance. The mountain rises like every other in the chain — a hard, dark ridge against the twilight that is all this region ever gets. Whatever dims the sun here is old; the sky cycles between a deep indigo day and a pitch-black night, and no living thing in the Hollow seems to mind. Against the mountain's flank, nearly invisible in the darkness, yawns a crack the width of three men standing shoulder-to-shoulder. This is the Mouth, and passing through it means descending, sometimes vertically, sometimes scrambling down long scree slopes inside, for nearly a mile before the main gallery begins.

The Hollow is one chamber. That is important. It is not a cave system, not a branching network — it is a single, immense heart, roughly the size of a temple interior, perhaps four hundred meters across, six hundred high. A cathedral conceived by water, not by builders, and finished by the same water. The dome of the ceiling was measured, once, by someone who tried: they threw glow-fungus upward until it scattered the vault with brief constellation and revealed a basin-smooth crown studded with stalactites. But the stalactites are not the point. The point is the walls.

Every surface — every shelf, bulge, pocket, column, arch — is studded and hung and layered with living body. Fauna that learned the vertical. Fauna that clung through evolution, learned the texture of the wet rock. They cover the walls so thickly that from inward-facing points one cannot see stone at all. They form a crust, a tapestry, a quivering textile of living meat. It pulses with breath and sound and the constant chatter of thousands of cheeping mouths.

The water. The Hollow is fed by seep and seep only — a trickle of condensation and a long, slow drip from ancient moments, routed through fissures and then emerging at several points along the inner wall, ten meters above the floor. It lands on the encrusted fauna and glistens. The floor itself is not wet except in three natural basins, each no larger than a few strides across, where seep has pooled into standing lenses of water black as unlit silver. Beyond these, it is merely damp: enough to balance an ecology that thrives on marginal moisture, but never so wet that anything decays from saturation. The fauna need it, and have arranged themselves accordingly, clustering near the collection points, thinning slightly in the drier zones, then re-establishing again around the next source, all in thickly predictable patterns.

The result is an organized tapestry that has had eons to make itself. It formed not over geological deep-time but over the span of undisturbed biological succession; the mountain is old, the chamber, old; the life, old and layered — old the way a market square is old, or a settled town. The crust is thickest at the bases — the pachyderms among the fauna, burden-bearers the size of mastiffs, slow-grazers on what drifts down — and thins to less obtrusive darting things, no bigger than a finger, in the lofty vault. In between: middle-sized custodial species, spreading sheet-like bodies across the stone to maximize what wet slips past. Every pocket where water becomes available is occupied. Every seam where condensation gathers is colonized. Where rain inside the chamber — a rain of drops from stalactite tips above — compensates for seep that never arrives, those zones bloom more aggressively than any other.

The fauna have odd names. No one wrote them down; the creature sounds of the Hollow itself do the naming: chets, olo-drops, sket-runners, the velvet eoliths, the Grand Assemblage (which is the collective designation of the wall's tapestry), and the Ancients — great leathery beings slow as growing stone, recognizable only for their stillness among the relentlessly kinetic small-fry. But all are matched in being present. There is no ecology outside the Hollow that this life-shape so heavily betrays. No light except the fungal skymarks and bioluminescent pattern-traces across some of the common species — pale green mint-glow; cold sapphire; shifting violet — and even this is dim enough that a man inside the Hollow must wait for his eyes to stop searching, for the scale of the chamber to settle into a general, pulsing visibility.

The Hollow also possesses one commodity of great strangeness not derived from any creature: the Migration. Occasionally — not annually, not predictably, and not recorded except by generations — the lower half of the assemblage tears itself in an organized rush and shifts to a far wall. Nothing hitherto could explain this. That there is no gradient powerful enough to compel it across that distance is one test of the law. That the motive seems unanimous among species, without example of a single pregnant individual refusing, is another. When the Migration occurs, the fog of sound in the Hollow, always at the volume of a shouting theater, escalates to a shriek that draws people's noses to bleed, and the wall on which the Migration lands explodes with renewed vigor within a few days. The abandoned surface then opens to colonisation of fungi. The fungi are nearly the only vegetation to be found inside; they arrive in leathery fans ranging from thumbnail to forearm scale, in slow colors of maroon, bone, and ashy teal, and in older cycles they aggressively cover the abandoned zones the fauna once owned. A crust grapples with the crust that comes after; that is the slow game this chamber plays across hundreds of human generations.

Noteworthy, too, is the absence of human traces. Nothing at all was ever built. Not one inserted lamp-holder, no scratched sign, no path marked across the floor among the basins. People may have come here — the chets' call patterns suggest something once harassed them, centuries ago, and have retained alarm-notes passed down mother-to-offspring against two-foot entry — but nothing was left, no attempt at settlement. The Hollow does not yield to building because its floor is largely water-smoothed rock and the three pools simply fill the convenient hollows of that floor without needing channels. One does not build upon crusts of living bodies; one enters a crust and stands outside the ecology and observes. Some would-be occupiers came generations ago with chisels and roofing-plans. The accounts say they left after a Migration had separated them from their scaffolding-by-craft and after someone in the night had been slowly consumed by a Grand Ancient mistaking turfed flesh for the slow approach of lichen.

Nor was it ever mined. A seam of something glitters occasionally through the crust where light nearly fails — a mineral vein across three walls. It looks valuable and there is much argument in the histories about what it is. No one has ever cut a sample from it; no one has needed it enough. It sits behind the tapestry of the Assemblage, licked occasionally where seep admits. In this, as with all the Hollow's features, the chamber has decided upon permanence without development, and remains unconsented.

Erosion has made the Hollow a chamber of the kind it wants: smoothed, domestic-scale despite its great volume, settled into a bowl that holds water that behaves, walls that seem less raw than prepared. The most dramatic of the water-features is the Fall Spot, one point where all collected drips gather into a near-silent fountain; the stream arrives in a ribbon no broader than a plait of hair. It descends, hugging a wall that might be limestone, might be something stranger. It lands on a great root-like cluster of velvet eoliths, which receive it with deliberate patience, as though it were rare. A relatively fecund zone surrounds it, where the chets swing from their perches and flicker, excited by the irregular light of the impacts, and where the sket-runners line along in neat microhierarchy, pecking among tiny basins and not infringing the ritual hierarchy of the water-path.

Navigation of the Hollow is easy once the Migration patterns are understood. Deep zones are beneath dense crust, shallow ones on the old abandoned crust. The risk-to-life is presently low; the Ancients crush when rampaged into confusion, but only in the confusion of Migration shrieks, which toll altitudes of pain among all creatures nearby and force climbs over the beasts as they seek the walls. The floor itself shifts very slowly between slimy film and bless-dry slick. It is possible to slip, and certain species, normally commensal, do attempt predation in gloom.

To remain inside the Hollow long is to be changed by it. Long exposure does what patience can; adaptation might replace what was only observation. The body learns where the water walks. The ear sorts morphology in sound. The tongue adapts to fungal harvest, to small blind flesh-creatures, to never cutting metal tools against the veil, chisel, shaft, or anything hard. The Hollow outlasts cultures. It preexists Empire. It will remain when nothing like a light again crosses the far mountain chain, because the only thing its species need is seep, and seep, in Haraq Tuun, does not forget.
