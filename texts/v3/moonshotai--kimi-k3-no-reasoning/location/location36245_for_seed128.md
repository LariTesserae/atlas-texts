# The Choir Cistern

There is a room, and the room is the world.

It measures perhaps sixty metres across — no more — and it is shaped like a bowl turned on its side, or the inside of a cupped palm. Every surface was made. There is no stone here that was ever in the ground, no soil that was ever soil. The floor, the walls that curve up and over until they meet at a dark apex overhead, the pillars that rise from the shallow water to buttress the vaulting — all of it is constructed, and all of it is old, and all of it is singing.

---

**The sound first.** You hear it before you understand anything else about this place, because you cannot think past it. It is not noise. It is too organised for noise, too layered, too deliberate. But it is *enormous*. The walls are ribbed with resonator fins — thin blades of some ceramic-metal composite, spaced at intervals of varying width — and each fin carries its own note. There are hundreds of them. Thousands. They are driven by the water that moves constantly through the structure: dripping from the ceiling condensate, running in grooves down the walls, flowing through the shallow basin that constitutes the floor. The water vibrates the fins at their resonant frequencies, and the fins in turn shape the water's motion, and the whole system breathes out a chord so dense with partials that it feels like standing inside a struck bell that never stops ringing.

The chord changes. Slowly — over hours, days — it modulates, because the water flow changes, and the temperature shifts, and the fins themselves are not passive. They are grown, or grown-like, engineered at a scale where material and organism blur. They thicken and thin in response to stress. They repair themselves. They adjust their own geometry minutely, and in doing so, they retune.

The result is a landscape that you experience primarily through your chest cavity. The bass notes — and there are many, some so low they are more tremor than tone — press against your sternum, make your ribs buzz, turn your breathing shallow and rapid if you are not accustomed. The middle registers form a wash, a shimmering veil of overtones that makes speech nearly impossible at close range; you would have to shout, and even then your voice would be folded into the chord, absorbed, made part of it. The high frequencies are the strangest: thin, piercing filaments of sound that seem to come from specific points in the vaulting above, like needles of audio descending from invisible sources, threading through the warm wet air.

No one designed this sound in its current form. The original builders — whoever they were, whenever this was made — designed the system, but the system has been running long enough that it has settled into its own equilibrium, its own settled-form, and what it sings now is what *it* has chosen to sing. The chord is not random. It has structure, recurrence, motifs that cycle and return. But it is not a composition in any human sense. It is the sound a machine makes when it has been alive long enough to develop habits.

---

**The light is scarce.** The room is lit by bioluminescent films that coat certain surfaces — not all, not evenly, but in patches and streaks, as though something once painted them on with a broad wet brush and the coverage has eroded over time. The light they give is a deep amber-orange, warm-toned, the colour of a low flame seen through honey. It is bright enough to navigate by, barely. Where the film has worn away — and it has, in long streaks down the walls where water runs heaviest, in the centres of the floor where the water pools deepest — there is darkness, and the darkness is filled with sound.

Some of the pillars carry secondary light sources: nodes of brighter phosphorescence, small concentrated points that glow pale green-white, like bioluminescent fungi, though they are part of the structure. These seem to be newer — the structure's own additions, grown in the centuries or millennia since its original making, installed (if that is the right word for a thing that grows itself into place) to illuminate areas where the primary film failed.

The overall effect is of being inside a vast, dim instrument — a cathedral built inside a cello, or a reactor core redesigned as a music box. The darkness and the amber glow and the sound create an atmosphere that is simultaneously claustrophobic and vast, intimate and overwhelming. The room is small. The room contains multitudes.

---

**The water is everywhere and nowhere deep.** The floor is a basin — the room's lowest point — and it holds a sheet of water that averages perhaps ankle-deep, warm as blood, moving slowly in patterns dictated by the floor's subtle topography. The floor is not flat. It is textured: ridged, dimpled, channelled, so that the water does not simply sit but circulates, pushed by thermal convection (the room is warm, and the warmth comes partly from below, from whatever heat source the builders embedded in the foundation) and pulled by gravity through drainage channels that lead somewhere you cannot see.

The walls weep. Constantly. Moisture condenses on every surface — the air is saturated, humidity near one hundred percent — and the condensate runs down in thin films, gathering in the grooves and channels the builders cut (or grew) into the walls, directed with astonishing precision to specific points: a particular fin that needs more vibration, a particular basin that feeds a particular cluster of organisms. The water system is not incidental to the room. It *is* the room, as much as the walls and the light and the sound. The water is the working fluid, the power transmission medium, the coolant, the habitat, the voice.

In the deeper pools — where the water reaches calf-depth, perhaps knee-depth in the very centre — the bottom is visible through the amber-lit water, and it is alive with movement.

---

**The fauna.** This is where the room's density becomes apparent, where the pocket-scale intimacy of the space reveals itself not as emptiness but as crowding. The water teems.

The most visible creatures are what you might call, for lack of better taxonomy, *fin-mites*: small, translucent arthropods, perhaps two centimetres long, that cluster on the resonator fins in vast numbers. They are clearly symbiotic with the structure — they clean the fins, removing mineral deposits and biological fouling, keeping the surfaces smooth and the resonance pure. In return, they feed on the biofilm that grows in the warm wet environment, a biofilm that is itself partly engineered, partly evolved, a grey-green film of microorganisms that coats every submerged surface. The fin-mites move in coordinated waves, thousands of individuals acting as a loose collective, flowing across a fin like a single rippling organism. When they are disturbed — by a change in the chord, by a shift in water flow — they release a brief burst of pale blue bioluminescence, and a whole wall will seem to shiver with cold fire for a moment before settling back to amber.

In the deeper pools, there are larger things. Slow-moving, soft-bodied, roughly the size of a human hand, they drift through the warm water with an unhurried undulation that suggests either supreme confidence or complete lack of predators. Their bodies are translucent, and inside them you can see structures that are not organs — or not only organs. There are geometries in there: lattices, spirals, crystalline formations that catch the amber light and refract it into brief rainbows. These creatures are part biological, part fabricated, and the boundary between the two is not clear. They may have been designed. They may have been designed to evolve. They may be what the room looks like when it dreams.

They are not the only swimmers. Smaller, faster things dart between them — silver slivers of motion, too quick to focus on, travelling in schools that flash and turn in the amber water like a single organism. These may be fish, or fish-analogues, or something else entirely. They feed on the biofilm and the fin-mites and the microorganisms that cloud the water, and they are in turn fed upon by the larger drifters, and the whole food web is compact, efficient, enclosed. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The system cycles, and has cycled, for a very long time.

On the walls, above the waterline, in the zone where condensation is heaviest but submersion does not occur, there are sessile organisms — colonies of something that looks like a cross between a barnacle and a crystal, growing in fractal clusters that follow the grooves in the wall where water runs. They are warm to the touch (you would know if you touched them; the warmth of the room is everywhere, a humid heat that wraps around you like a wet blanket, that makes your skin prickle and your breath come slow). They are also resonant. If you tapped one — gently — it would ring, briefly, a clear high note like a struck glass, before the room's chord swallowed it.

---

**The air** is thick enough to taste. Warm, wet, carrying a smell that is difficult to categorise: not organic decay, not chemical sharpness, but something between — a warm metallic sweetness, like the smell of a circuit board running hot, combined with the green mustiness of a greenhouse. It is the smell of a place where biology and technology have been in contact long enough to exchange scents.

Breathing here requires adjustment. The air is oxygen-rich — the biofilm and the sessile colonies are photosynthetic or chemosynthetic or some hybrid of the two, and they produce oxygen as a byproduct, and the room is sealed enough that the concentration has risen above what a human would find comfortable. Too much oxygen. Too much warmth. Too much moisture. The effect is a kind of dreamy, half-drugged alertness: colours seem more saturated, sounds more vivid, time more elastic. The room does this to you. Whether it does so deliberately — whether there is anything here that could be called deliberate — is a question you would have to answer by spending time in it, and the room is very good at making you lose track of time.

---

**The structure's intelligence**, if that is the right word, is distributed. There is no central processor, no control room, no interface. The room is not a machine you can operate. It is a machine that operates *itself*, and has been doing so for long enough that its operations have become indistinguishable from natural processes. The fins retune themselves. The water routes itself. The light appears where it is needed. The fauna maintain the systems that maintain the fauna. The whole thing is a closed loop, a self-sustaining chord, an ecosystem that was built but has become something more than built.

And yet — and this is where the slight strangeness of the place asserts itself, the low-level weirdness that is not shocking but persistent, like a note slightly out of tune that you can't stop hearing — the room *responds*. Not obviously. Not dramatically. But if you stand in the shallow water long enough, the fin-mites will begin to cluster on the fins nearest to you, cleaning more vigorously, brightening the tone of those fins slightly, so that the chord around you becomes fractionally clearer, fractionally brighter, as though the room is *paying attention* to where you are. If you move, the brightening follows you, slowly, a lag of perhaps thirty seconds, as the mite colonies communicate your position through the network.

The larger drifters in the deep pools will also, eventually, drift toward you. Not threateningly. They are simply curious, in the slow, ambient way that warm-water organisms are curious. They will circle you at a distance of perhaps a metre, their internal geometries catching the amber light, and if you are very still they will come closer, and if you remain still for a very long time — an hour, two — they will brush against your legs with a touch that is not slimy but smooth, almost glassy, and warm, and in that touch you will feel, or imagine you feel, a faint vibration, as though they are humming along with the room's chord, each one carrying its own small version of the great sound.

---

**The origin of the room is unknowable from inside it.** It is clearly very old. The settled quality of every surface — the way the water channels have been smoothed by centuries of flow, the way the biofilm has developed its own stratigraphy, layers upon layers of growth and dormancy and regrowth, the way the fins have thickened at their bases and thinned at their tips from uncountable cycles of vibration — all of it speaks to deep time. The room has not been maintained. It has *maintained itself*, and the difference shows: there is no sense of repair, of patching, of human-scale intervention. The room is not cared for. It cares for itself, with the slow patience of a thing that does not die.

It is embedded within something larger — this much is clear from the fact of its enclosure, its smallness, its pocket-scale containment. It is a room *inside* a structure, a node in a network, a single cistern in what may be a system of cisterns. But whatever surrounds it is not visible from within. The walls are solid. The apex of the vault is dark and distant, lost in shadow and condensation. There are no doors. There may never have been doors. The room was not built to be entered. It was built to *be*, and entering it is either an accident or an anomaly — the room does not seem to mind, but it does not seem to have expected you, either.

---

**Time here moves differently**, not because of any physics-defying technology (though the technology on display certainly defies current physics in subtler ways) but because the room's rhythms are so unlike surface rhythms. There is no day and night. The light does not change. The chord modulates slowly, over periods that might be hours or might be days, and the modulation follows patterns that are too complex to perceive directly but too structured to be random. The fauna have their own cycles — the fin-mites have periods of activity and torpor, the drifters have feeding and resting phases, the sessile colonies pulse with a slow bioluminescent rhythm that is visible only if you watch a single colony for a long time — but these cycles are not synchronised with anything outside the room. They are synchronised with each other, and with the chord, and with the water, and the whole system keeps its own time, a time measured in resonance and flow and the slow accumulation of biofilm.

If you stayed long enough — and you could, in theory; the water is clean, the oxygen is plentiful, the warmth is survivable, and the fauna are not hostile — you would begin to feel the room's time replacing your own. Your heartbeat would slow to match the bass frequencies. Your breathing would fall into rhythm with the water circulation. You would begin to hear the chord not as a single overwhelming sound but as a texture, a fabric woven from thousands of individual threads, each one a fin, each fin a voice, each voice contributing to a whole that is greater than the sum of its parts in a way that is not metaphorical but literal, physical, acoustic.

You would begin to understand that the room is not singing *to* you or *for* you or *at* you. It is singing *because that is what it does*, and you are simply inside the song.

---

The water is warm against your ankles. The fin-mites flicker blue in the corner of your vision. The chord presses against your chest like a hand. The amber light dims and brightens in slow waves that you realise, after a long time, correspond to the feeding cycles of the sessile colonies — they absorb more light when they are active, and the room grows fractionally darker, and when they rest, the light returns.

Everything here is connected. Everything here was made. Everything here has been making itself for so long that the distinction between *made* and *grown* has collapsed, and what remains is a place that is both entirely artificial and entirely natural, a pocket of dense, loud, warm, wet, alive, constructed, self-sustaining *presence*, hidden inside something larger, singing its endless chord to no one, to itself, to you.

The room does not need you. But it notices you. And in its noticing, there is something that is not quite welcome and not quite indifference — a kind of acoustic hospitality, a willingness to adjust its song slightly, to make space for your frequency in the great chord, to let you resonate.
