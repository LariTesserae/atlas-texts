**The Immortal Engine**

High above the cloud-line, where the air should be thin and cruel, there exists instead a pressure that feels like swimming through warm milk. The plateau stretches beyond the curvature of the horizon—not so much a mountain as a world-crust thrust upward, a continental shelf torn from some deeper stratum and held suspended in a state of violent, birthing instability. This is the Immortal Engine, and it is still becoming.

The ground is sharp. Not metaphorically—literally. The erosion hasn't happened yet. The stone is glass-basalt, freshly crystallized, cooling from a melt that occurred minutes ago or will occur in minutes; time here moves in spasms. You walk on edges that could split obsidian, and sometimes you do split, because the stone hasn't decided whether it's solid or liquid or something else entirely. The heat comes up through your soles—2.5 on whatever scale the universe uses to measure fever—and it is the heat of industry, of metabolism, of a billion pistons firing just beneath the crust.

Because this is an engine. Not metaphorically.

Beneath the dense canopy—vegetation so thick it forms a tertiary atmosphere, leaves pressing against leaves until individual plants become a single, breathing colossus—the bedrock is machined. Great gears of native quartz, kilometers wide, mesh and turn with tectonic slowness. Rivers of warm water (1.0—intermittent, condensing, weeping) run through channels that are too perfectly hexagonal to be natural, too overgrown with screaming orchids to be safe. The humidity hangs in veils; it condenses on your skin in beads that glow faintly blue, because the water here remembers being lightning.

The light is dim—0.7, the quality of a room after the power has failed but before the eyes adjust. It comes from nowhere specific. The sky is a bruise-colored murk, heavy with vapor, yet you can see clearly for miles because the air itself is luminous, lit from within by chemical fires that burn without consuming. Shadows fall upward. Shadows fall in spirals. Shadows sometimes detach and hunt.

This is where the reality breakdown begins.

The fauna are abundant (2.2), but they are not consistent. A creature with the general intention of a jaguar stalks through the understory, but its spots keep sliding off its body and becoming independent, beetle-like things that skitter away to rejoin the herd. Birds with too many wings—seven, nine, a prime number that hurts to perceive—circle the thermal updrafts, their cries swallowed by the oppressive quiet (0.7). The silence is heavy, a pressure on the eardrums, broken only by the infrasonic thrum of the great gears below and the occasional, wet *snap* of a plant consuming something that was briefly real.

Density here means layers. The vegetation is 1.6—lush, yes, but dense in the way of a crowded elevator, a bazaar at noon, an anthill kicked open. Vines don't just climb; they braid into ropes thick as houses, then unbraid because they've changed their minds. Flowers bloom in reverse, closing into buds that burn like embers. The ecology is industrial despite the lack of built structures (0.4)—nature here has mechanized itself. Trees pump water through copper-rich xylem that functions like wire, carrying electrical current. Fungi form circuit boards. The "forests" are really factories, producing oxygen, yes, but also strange isotopes, hard vacuum in sealed seed-pods, gravity anomalies in the sap.

Nothing here was built by hands. The "tech" (1.9) is endemic, grown, *secreted*. The plateau itself is a single organism-machine hybrid, vast enough (2.8) to generate its own weather—thunderheads that rain upward, lightning that strikes from ground to sky and stays there, branching into permanent, frozen trees of electricity that serve as nesting sites for silicon-based raptors.

The dynamic is turbulent (2.7), verging on catastrophic. Creation and destruction are simultaneous. A hill of glass-basalt will suddenly flower into a canyon, not through erosion but through *decision*—the stone choosing to become void. Water pools in the palms of rock formations, then leaps upward as columns that writhe like living things before dispersing into mist that smells of ozone and crushed mint. Time stutters; you may see yesterday's rain falling upward into tomorrow's clouds.

The weirdness is not decorative here. It is structural. Physics operates on a 2.7 setting—roughly functional, but prone to baroque exceptions. Gravity varies by altitude in discrete steps rather than gradients. Mass communicates; you can feel the weight of the vast plateau pressing against your mind, a psychic pressure that matches the atmospheric density. Sounds (0.7) are swallowed by the thickness of the air, but when they do carry—a twig snapping, a stone falling—they arrive from impossible directions, often before the event that caused them.

The warmth is damp, intimate, invasive. It is the warmth of a body, of a machine running too hot, of compost heaps steaming in the dark. It encourages growth that is almost too rapid to witness: bamboo analogues that shoot up ten feet in an hour, only to be consumed by carnivorous moss that moves faster than reflex.

And yet, for all the industrial chaos, there are no artifacts. No ruins. No tools. If you find a perfect cube of metal, it is a seed. If you find a gear, it is a fossilized flower. The plateau builds itself through biological processes that have learned to mimic machinery, or machinery that has achieved photosynthesis—here, the distinction is academic, then impossible, then heretical.

To stand on the edge—where the young, sharp cliffs drop away into cloud banks that churn with their own internal lightning, independent of the sky above—is to feel the scale of the place in your marrow. It is vast enough to have its own gravity wells, its own orbital mechanics. Small moons of condensed vapor circle specific groves of ironwood trees. A waterfall near the edge doesn't fall; it decays into slow-motion droplets that hang in the air for days, creating a sphere of water-beads that function as a lens, focusing the dim light into beams hot enough to melt the fresh basalt, which then flows upward to reform the cliff face.

This is the Immortal Engine: a high, hot, dense, and desperate place, where evolution has hit the fast-forward button and physics is negotiating the terms of surrender. It is mostly quiet, always warm, wet in patches, and so alive that the distinction between predator, prey, machine, and weather has become a matter of temporary convenience rather than taxonomy. It has been here for three minutes. It has been here forever. It is still deciding whether to let you leave, or whether you were always part of the mechanism.
