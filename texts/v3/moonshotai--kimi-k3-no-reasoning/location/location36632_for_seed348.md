The cool, damp air hangs heavy in the hollow, saturated with a drone that never falters—a low-frequency hum vibrating through the soles of your boots and the roots of your teeth. You stand in a shallow, weathered basin perhaps a half-mile across, cupped like a palm. The sky is a uniform, pale gray, cast by the industrial overcast of a sun that has risen and fallen behind the smokestacks for three centuries, though the smoke no longer rises. The place is finished. It has reached its equilibrium and settled there, into a permanent, roaring stasis.

Around you stretches a city of iron and glass, but one long past any need for inhabitants. The architecture is dense yet sparse in population—vast, cathedral-like halls of corrugated steel connected by rusted catwalks, their footprints leaving broad, empty plazas of polished basalt between structures. Everything is scaled for a human presence that has not been required in generations: doors hang open on pneumatic hinges, conveyor belts idle with a soft, electric whine, and great piston-houses stand like monuments to themselves. The erosion here is not decay but *settling*—the way a stone wall relaxes into the earth. Rust blooms in slow, orange lichen-patterns across I-beams, and condensation beads eternally on the cold faces of pressure gauges that no longer measure anything but their own stasis. There is no green, no leaf, no blade of grass. The only growth is a white, calcified fungus that etches frost-like patterns across windowpanes, feeding on the constant dew.

The fauna are overwhelming. They are called the Gear-Hounds—though they have no masters to name them now—and they move through the infrastructure in packs of hundreds. Their bodies are constructs of brass, leather, and hydraulic muscle, their joints venting steam in the cool air. They are not simulations of life; they are a mechanized ecology, self-repairing, self-replicating, driven by geothermal taps deep in the bedrock. Their howls are not vocal but *mechanical*: the shriek of metal on metal, the percussive clatter of claws on steel decking, the bass thrum of their charging cycles. This is the source of the loudness—the constant, industrial chorus of their movement. They hunt the electrical leaks that spark from ancient capacitors, fighting in slow-motion, sparks cascading like water from their collisions. Their eyes are lensed glass, glowing with a soft, amber phosphorescence that provides the only variation in the flat, normal light of the day.

Water moves through the place in strict, artificial cycles. Rain comes not from weather but from the condensate towers—great, sweating columns that bleed moisture into the air, maintaining a perpetual dampness that beads on the Gear-Hounds’ brass hides and drips from the eaves of empty factories. The moisture feeds the fungal patterns and keeps the dust down, but never accumulates into mud or pools deep enough for reflection. It is a managed wetness, a lubricant for the world’s ongoing function.

You can walk the catwalks, but the Hounds ignore you. They are governed by rules older than their current forms: they maintain the city, polish its gears with their passage, and cull any component that threatens the equilibrium. If a belt frays, they dismantle the loom. If a pipe bursts, they seal it with their own welding-torch mouths. They are the immune system of a body that no longer lives but refuses to die.

The strangeness lies in the stillness beneath the noise. Despite the overwhelming presence of the mechanical fauna and the thunder of their activity, nothing *changes*. The same Hound you see at dawn will be in the same patrol route at dusk. The rain will fall at the same rate. The temperature will not shift. The city is a closed loop, a perpetual motion machine that has achieved perfect, terrible balance. It is loud with the sound of forever, and empty of everything but the echo of its own design.
