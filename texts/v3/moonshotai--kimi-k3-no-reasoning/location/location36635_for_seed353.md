There is no ground. There has never been ground.

What you are standing on—what the word *standing* can barely accommodate—is the outer skin of a root-mat that goes down four hundred meters and then meets another root-mat growing upward, and then another, and the whole strata-stack of them constitutes a planetary surface that was never rock. The engineers who maintain the under-vaults have a term for this, translated roughly as *the accumulated decision*, the choice made so long ago that no archive dates it, to let the built world become the grown world, and then to forget which came first.

You are at altitude—high on one of the continental humps where the mat bulges toward a darkness that should be sky but is instead a ceiling. The ceiling is ceramic, ancient, self-repairing at a pace slower than thought, and it hangs only two kilometers above you here, studded with dead light-panels that no longer pretend to be suns. The heat—wet, constant, a 2.9 that the climate machinery maintains as though someone left a thermostat set and died before adjusting it—comes from below, from the compost-reactors, from the metabolic pressure of a world that is entirely alive and entirely artificial and entirely packed with itself.

There is no water. The plants do not need it. They run on a conductive gel, a synthetic phloem that the original fabricators engineered to carry both nutrient and current, and the whole world hums with this slow electricity. The light is dim—amber bioluminescence from the fungal-shrubs, the occasional arc-flash when a root-system discharges excess voltage into the ceramic sky. Most illumination comes from the machinery embedded in the mat: old grow-lamps, their glass clouded, still cycling through spectrums that no longer serve any purpose, like a heart beating in a body that has forgotten why.

The vegetation is not *on* anything. It *is* everything. The trunks are structural, load-bearing, integrated with the vault-arches below. The leaves are wide as highways, ribbed with copper-alloy veins that were planted there, or grew there in imitation of planting—the distinction is not preserved. Some leaves have been trained into shapes that suggest function: platforms, conduits, heat-exchangers. Others have escaped their training and simply *grow*, thickening into walls, splitting into corridors, sealing themselves into chambers that may or may not contain machines, may or may not have been rooms once, may or may not be rooms now.

Everything is close. There is no vista. Even here, on the high hump, you cannot see more than fifty meters in any direction before another trunk, another buttress, another engineered thicket interrupts. The scale is open only in the sense that you know, intellectually, that this continues for thousands of kilometers, that the vaults and the mat and the ceramic sky wrap an entire planet. But your eyes cannot confirm it. The density is total. The space is full. Every cubic meter is occupied by something growing, something built, something that used to be one and is now the other.

The erosion here is not the erosion of wind or water—it is the erosion of *time on living infrastructure*. This world is old in the way a circuit board is old: not crumbling, but *settled*. Every surface has been grown over, repaired, regrown, repurposed, absorbed. The ceramic sky has healed its own cracks with mineral deposits extruded by root-tips. The machines embedded in the mat have been overgrown so many times that they are now organs, not devices. A turbine-intake, fossilized in bark, still spins slowly when the gel-pressure spikes. No one turns it off. Turning it off would require understanding what it does, and the manuals are stored in a language that the maintenance-caste no longer reads fluently.

There are no animals. None. The world does not permit them. The density is such that a rat, a bird, an insect would have no ecological room—it would be a foreign body, either crushed or encapsulated. The only motion that is not plant-growth or machine-cycle is the turbulent churn of the world *maintaining itself*, and this is constant, chaotic, layered. A branch thickens and cracks a ceramic conduit; the conduit leaks gel; a different root-system detects the leak and grows a seal; the seal displaces a strut; the strut's failure triggers a sensor; the sensor activates a drone; the drone, blind and ancient, harvests the wrong branch and carries it to a recycling-vat that was decommissioned eight thousand years ago. This is not malfunction. This is the weather.

The sound is mostly a low, vegetal groan—growth under pressure, the slow complaint of cellulose and alloy under tension. Sometimes a chime, when a sensor misreads its input. Sometimes a hiss, when gel-pressure equalizes. The darkness swallows most of it. The heat carries the rest poorly. You hear your own breathing, and beneath that, the world deciding, constantly, what shape to be next.

The air is thick—not with moisture, there is none, but with volatiles, terpenes, the exhalations of a trillion leaves that are also photovoltaic cells, that are also insulation, that are also the walls of rooms you cannot see into. Breathing it is safe. The atmosphere is manufactured, balanced, maintained by the same system that maintains everything else. It tastes faintly of ozone and sap.

What is strange here is not any single thing but the totality: that a world can be so completely made and so completely alive that the categories stop applying. That a built environment can erode, can age, can settle into a final form that is still changing. That darkness and heat and density and silence can combine into something that is not oppressive but *intimate*, as though you are inside a body that does not know you are there but would, if it noticed, incorporate you gently into its structure.

You walk—carefully, because the mat is uneven, because roots thicken while you sleep—toward a gap in the canopy where a shaft of slightly-brighter amber light suggests a clearing, or a chamber, or a place where the world has decided to become something else. You will not reach it quickly. The world is dense, and it is patient, and it has all the time there is.
