# The Waterlands

*The remnants of a world that forgot how to be one.*

---

You don't so much *enter* the Waterlands as *slip* into them, the way a stone might drop into mud—slowly, with no solid edge to mark your passage. One moment you're on normal ground; the next you're knee-deep in a landscape that no longer remembers the difference between solid and liquid, between above and below, between one thing and another.

The light comes at you from everywhere and nowhere—a dim, bruised luminescence that seems to seep from the water itself rather than from any sky. Above, if there is an above, hangs a ceiling of perpetual mist that glows with the faintest hint of what might once have been a sun. It's not so much *clouds* as *the idea of clouds*—a suggestion of opacity that shifts and pulses with the rhythm of the waters below.

The ground, such as it is, spreads out in vast shallow basins separated by ridges of compacted silt no wider than a footpath. These ridges—ancient, smooth as old bone—divide the world into cells, each one a slightly different shade of wet. Some hold water that reflects the strange light back at itself. Others are thick with mud that bubbles and sighs, releasing gases that have been trapped for millennia and now rise in slow, deliberate spheres that pop with soft, wet sounds.

And the *sound*—oh, the sound. It's not loud in the way thunder is loud, not sharp or sudden. It's a *sustained* roar, a constant white noise that seems to come from the water itself, from the way it moves and shifts and breaks against itself. It's the sound of a billion droplets hitting a billion other droplets, of waves collapsing into foam and reforming, of currents grinding against mudbanks. It's a sound that gets into your bones, that vibrates through your sternum until you can't tell if you're hearing it or *feeling* it.

The vegetation, where it exists at all, clings to the highest points of the ridges—stunted things that might once have been trees but have long since given up on the pretense of reaching upward. They grow sideways instead, twisted into shapes that suggest they've been blown by a wind that no longer blows, their leaves reduced to scales that glisten with a perpetual sheen of moisture. They're not so much *plants* as *memories of plants*, ghosts of vegetation that have forgotten how to photosynthesize and now subsist on the damp itself.

Here and there, half-submerged in the muck, you might find something that looks like a structure—a low wall of fitted stones, a broken column, the curved edge of what might once have been a dome. But these aren't ruins in any conventional sense. They're too *integrated* with the landscape, too thoroughly colonized by algae and sediment. They're less *built things* than *geological features that happen to be rectangular*—the world has absorbed them so completely that it's hard to say where architecture ends and erosion begins.

The water itself is the dominant presence here. It's not just *in* the landscape—it *is* the landscape. It rises and falls in great, slow tides that have nothing to do with any moon. It seeps up from below, filling depressions that were dry moments before. It condenses out of the air itself, forming droplets on every surface that merge and run together in rivulets that have no destination. The boundary between *water* and *not-water* has become so blurred that sometimes you'll step on what looks like solid ground only to find yourself sinking into a thick slurry that holds you like a hand.

The heat is a presence of its own—a warm, wet blanket that presses against your skin and makes the air feel thick enough to chew. It's not the dry heat of a desert but something more *biological*, as if the world itself has a fever and is sweating out its illness in the form of this perpetual damp.

And the *change*—the constant, chaotic change. The landscape doesn't just *shift*—it *boils*. New pools open up where there were none before, spilling over their banks to flood neighboring cells. Ridges that have stood for centuries suddenly subside, vanishing beneath the surface in a matter of hours. Channels cut new paths through the mud, carving out tributaries that didn't exist yesterday and may not exist tomorrow. It's as if the world is trying to *unmake* itself, to return to some primordial state where everything was potential and nothing was fixed.

The few creatures that live here are as strange as the landscape itself. They're not so much *animals* as *possibilities of animals*—translucent things that might be fish or might be amphibians or might be something else entirely, their forms shifting and blurring at the edges as if they're not quite committed to being any one thing. They move through the water with a deliberate slowness that suggests they're in no hurry to get anywhere, that the journey itself is the destination.

And the *scale* of it all—the sheer, overwhelming scale. The Waterlands don't just stretch to the horizon—they *are* the horizon, a vast, unbroken expanse that curves away from you in all directions until it meets the misty ceiling above. There's no *end* to it, no *edge*, just an infinite repetition of water and mud and the occasional ridge that might as well be a mountain for all the difference it makes.

The strangest thing of all is the way the world here seems to *breathe*. Not metaphorically—*literally*. The water rises and falls in a slow, rhythmic pulse that has nothing to do with tides or weather. It's as if the landscape itself is alive, as if the water is its blood and the mud its flesh and the strange light its nervous system. You can feel it in your feet, in your hands, in the way the air seems to press against you with a steady, insistent rhythm.

This is a world that has forgotten how to be solid, how to be separate, how to be *one thing* as opposed to *another*. It's a world in the process of *dissolving*, not into nothingness but into *everything*—into a state where all boundaries are permeable, all distinctions are provisional, and the only constant is change itself.

And you, standing in the middle of it, are just another *thing* that's beginning to blur at the edges, to lose your definition, to become part of the great, wet, roaring *whole*.
