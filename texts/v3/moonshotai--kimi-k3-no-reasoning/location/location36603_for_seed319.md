The Clagwall Basin sits on a high plateau where the air is perpetually thick with the smell of wet iron and ozone. Here, the elevation feels less like altitude and more like a vast, sustained intake of breath—a massive, porous ledge of bedrock that hangs over the lower plains. The landscape is defined by its heavy, warm dampness; the water does not flow in rivers so much as it weeps. It seeps from vertical strata of rust-colored sandstone, dripping from undercut shelves in long, silvery threads, and pools in hollows that never quite dry, creating a landscape that is perpetually sodden and soft.

The human presence here is not a town, but a monolithic, semi-ruined industrial complex known as the Weepworks. Built across the basin’s span, it consists of towering, fluted chimneys and vast, skeletal gantries of green-oxidized copper and black iron. These structures have been settled into the wet earth for centuries, and they lean at strange, gravity-defying angles where the soggy foundations have slowly surrendered. There is an unsettling order to the place—a geometry of pipes and pressure-vessels—but the density is sparse. The buildings are islands of industrial architecture, separated by wide expanses of cracked, grey clay and shallow, steaming pools. 

The sound is the defining law of the Clagwall Basin. It is a deafening, mechanical droning, a deep-frequency vibration that emanates from the Heart-Engine at the center of the complex. The Engine is a massive, cylindrical mass of pistons and boilers, half-buried in the plateau’s rock. It does not produce anything recognizable to an outsider; it merely churns the heavy, waterlogged air, pushing it through a network of vents that emit a rhythmic, bone-shaking roar. The noise is so pervasive that it renders the landscape strangely silent in terms of biological life—birds and insects are scarce, likely deafened or driven away by the constant, pressurized thrumming.

Vegetation is minimal, mostly restricted to hardy, pale lichen that grows in neon-yellow patches on the south-facing sides of the machinery, and occasional tufts of tough, rubbery grass that cling to the edges of the weeping rock faces. There is a strange, localized phenomenon where the steam from the factory vents meets the cool, damp air of the plateau: a fine, warm mist settles over everything, coating the iron and the stone in a permanent, slick film. 

Movement here is glacial. The rust does not flake; it thickens, layer by layer, over decades. The pools expand by millimeters, fed by the constant seepage. The Heart-Engine cycles through its functions with the patience of a tectonic plate, its internal fires stoked by an automated, long-forgotten process. To walk through the Clagwall is to navigate a world that feels permanently caught in a single, long, industrial exhalation—loud, heavy, and profoundly ancient, despite the presence of the machines. It is a place that does not change; it simply persists, vibrating at a frequency that makes your teeth ache, existing in a state of heavy, humid, metallic equilibrium.
