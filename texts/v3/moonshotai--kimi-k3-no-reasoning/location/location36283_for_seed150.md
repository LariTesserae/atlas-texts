The rain stopped an hour ago, but nothing here has stopped dripping.

You stand at the edge of what the maps—if this place had maps—would call a hanging terrace. A single shelf of ground, no larger than a village common, juts from a mountainside that has not yet decided whether it is cliff or slope. Below, mist. Above, mist. The terrace holds its own weather in a bowl of green, and the weather is wet.

Everything is alive. This is not hyperbole. The ground beneath your feet is not soil but a compression of moss, liverwort, and the fibrous roots of things growing above—layered, interlocked, springy as felt. Step and it exhales cold water. The walls are not stone but the woody, braided trunks of a single ancient rhododendron-analog, its branches having fused over decades into architecture: corridors, vaults, chambers. The ceiling is leaf. The floor is root. The boundaries of the terrace are a hedge of living holly-fern, shoulder-high, dense enough to lean against.

It is cold. Not killing cold, but the kind that lives in your collar. The air carries the mineral chill of recent snowmelt, and every surface condenses it. Water beads on the waxy undersides of leaves, gathers, falls. The sound is a slow, irregular patter, like someone turning pages in another room.

In the center of the terrace, where the root-floor dips into a basin, stands the water. A pool, no wider than a courtyard, fed by seepage from the mountain’s flank. It is black with tannin, still, and cold enough to ache. Around its edge, the vegetation achieves a density that approaches solidity: skunk-cabbage analogs with leaves the size of table-tops, their veins thick as fingers; horsetail reeds in tight stands, segmented and hollow, clicking faintly when they shift; and everywhere the moss, the deep moss, climbing the trunks, muffling the light, holding the wet.

The light is dim. The sun is somewhere above the mist, and what reaches here is filtered through three canopies: the fused rhododendron vault, a stratum of dripping vine-maples, and the persistent exhale of fog that moves through the terrace in slow, visible currents. Shadows are soft-edged, green-black. Colors are saturated but dark: bottle-green, bruise-purple, the deep rust of wet bark.

Fauna is present but scarce, and quiet. A single species of amphibian—pale, translucent-skinned, the size of a thumb—clings to the undersides of leaves, pulsing. You have seen three in an hour. A bird, unseen, makes a sound like water dropping into a bottle. That is all.

The terrace is young. The mountain is still shedding its skin, and this shelf of ground emerged perhaps fifty years ago, when a section of cliff face slumped and caught. The rhododendron colonized first, then the ferns, then the moss. Nothing here is old, but everything is crowded, competitive, growing over and under and through. The effect is not chaos but urgency—a landscape still assembling itself, every niche claimed, every surface occupied.

You notice the built quality only gradually. The fused trunks form arches. The fallen branches, woven by time and pressure into the root-floor, create paths. The basin of the pool is lined with flat stones, not placed but pressed into position by the slow insistence of roots. It resembles a settlement, a cloister, a single-room cathedral. No hand made it, but the hand is not the only maker.

The rules of this place are simple. Stay on the paths; the root-floor between them is thin, and below it is void. Do not drink the pool; the water is sterile but cold enough to stop a heart. Move slowly; the mist responds to disturbance, thickening around sudden motion.

It is active. The mist cycles, the water rises and falls with the mountain’s hidden pulse, the leaves drip in rhythm. Something is always growing, always dying, always becoming something else. The terrace is not static. It is a process, paused.

You leave the way you came, through a gap in the holly-fern hedge. Behind you, the dripping continues.
