**The Rust-Bowl of Graven-Hollow**

There is a valley, or rather a bowl—a shallow depression scooped from the dark, cool earth, roughly the size of a town that has forgotten its own name. Here, the ground is not ground but a seamless, sprawling crust of packed clay, fired in place by some ancient, forgotten heat, and laced with veins of hand-hammered copper that has oxidized to a bruised, iridescent green. Every step rings with a dull, metallic resonance, for the entire landscape is a single, continuous artifact: a vast, tilted disc of human construction, built from compacted river mud and scrap metal, then left to settle under its own weight.

The air is cool and perpetually damp, carrying the scent of wet iron, ozone, and something like old, cold bread. Condensation beads on every surface—on the rough, slag-like walls of the dwellings, on the edges of the copper seams, on the sparse, fungal growths that cling to the undersides of ledges. It drips constantly, a slow, rhythmic percussion that echoes through the hollow. Rain comes not in storms but in a fine, drifting mist, settling over the bowl like a breath, pooling in shallow, irregular depressions that have been worn into the clay by centuries of feet. These pools are not clear but clouded with fine, suspended particles of rust and pulverized stone, reflecting only the dim, sourceless light that seems to emanate from the damp air itself.

There is no sky here, not in the sense of open blue or clouded gray. The light is a low, uniform gloom, as if the sun has been replaced by a bank of cold, distant embers. Shadows are soft-edged and numerous, pooling in the angles between structures, clinging to the undersides of the crude, arching bridges that span the wider pools. The buildings—if they can be called that—are dense, clustered, and layered, built one upon the other in a chaotic, organic accretion. They are made of the same fired clay as the ground, reinforced with twisted, rusting metal rods and roofed with sheets of beaten copper, now green and pitted. Walls lean against walls, sharing structural support, creating narrow, lightless canyons between them where the air grows colder and the condensation thickens into a slow, steady rain.

No plants grow here. The only life is fungal: pale, shelf-like growths that fan out from damp crevices, glowing faintly with a bioluminescent blue-green light. They pulse slowly, almost imperceptibly, and their light is the only relief from the pervasive gloom. They are tended, in a crude way, by the inhabitants—small, wiry figures who move through the narrow passages with a quiet, purposeful haste. They wear garments of layered, waxed cloth, stiff with damp, and carry tools of simple metal: hammers, chisels, tongs. Their work is constant and noisy. The sound of hammering is a near-constant backdrop, a rhythmic, metallic clanging that reverberates off the clay walls and the copper roofs, mingling with the drip of water and the occasional, sharp crack of a cooling metal plate. The air hums with this noise, a dense, layered cacophony that seems to press against the skin.

The fauna is rare and strange. Small, six-legged creatures, like armored mice with coppery shells, scuttle through the drainage channels, feeding on the fungal spores. They are quick and silent, their movements a blur in the dim light. Occasionally, a larger shape—a shadow with too many joints—slips between the buildings, gone before it can be fully seen. The inhabitants do not speak of these.

The rules of this world are simple and unyielding. Water is precious, collected from the condensation and the shallow pools, stored in great, sealed clay jars. Metal is life; it is hammered, shaped, and reused endlessly. Nothing is wasted. The valley is alive with the sound of making, of shaping, of forcing the raw, constructed earth into new forms. It is a place of constant, low-level activity, a hive of human industry in a world without sun or soil. The damp seeps into everything, softening edges, encouraging the growth of the luminous fungi, and ensuring that the air always carries the weight of moisture, cool and heavy, like a held breath. The valley is a machine, and its people are the gears, turning slowly, endlessly, in the dark.
