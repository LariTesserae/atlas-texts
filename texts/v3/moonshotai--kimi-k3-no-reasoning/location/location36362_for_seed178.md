The valley doesn’t sleep; it convulses.

I stand on a ridge of black basalt so freshly split that the edges still carry the glassy sheen of a break that happened yesterday—or perhaps this morning. The rock weeps. Every pore exudes a cold vapor that condenses instantly into droplets the color of weak tea, running in rivulets down the fractured faces. This is the **Splitting**, a lowland basin where the crust has recently torn itself open, and the wound remains wet, steaming with a chill that burns the lungs.

The darkness here is not absence but a medium. It hangs in the air like wet felt, swallowing the weak bioluminescence that tries to fight it. I carry a light—a filament of coherent photons woven into my retinal implants—but even this beyond-modern tech struggles against the gloom. The beam travels perhaps twenty meters before it dies, not from obstruction, but from exhaustion; the atmosphere here eats photons, metabolizing them into a low, subsonic thrum that vibrates in the marrow of my teeth.

That sound. It never stops.

The valley floor—visible only in strobing flashes when the ion-storms discharge overhead—is a chaos of density. Thousands of **spring-trees** crowd together, their trunks not wood but columnar crystals of frozen atmospheric pressure, translucent and straining. They grow with such speed that the cracking of their expansion forms the bass note of the landscape’s cacophony: a continuous, splintering report like a million rifles firing in slow motion. Between them, the undergrowth is a solid mass of **shiver-moss**, ankle-deep and phosphorescent green, which compresses underfoot and releases spores that glow briefly before the dark consumes them.

Water moves everywhere. Not in rivers—there is no time for such organization—but in sheets that slide across the young basalt, finding every fissure, every crack. The erosion is so fresh that the water doesn’t so much flow as *attack*, carrying microscopic shards of rock that scour the surface clean in real-time. I watch a boulder the size of a house dissolve into pebbles over the course of an hour, the debris carried away by a sudden flash-flood that appears without warning from a crevice that didn’t exist ten minutes prior.

The fauna are desperate, numerous, and adapted to the din. **Siren-eels**—long, ribbon-like creatures with throats that open into resonating chambers—swim through the air itself, their bodies undulating against the dense, wet atmosphere. They emit screams that exceed 140 decibels, sounds meant to stun the **basalt-mites** that swarm in the moss. The eels hunt in packs of hundreds, their combined shrieking creating interference patterns that can shatter weaker crystal formations. When they feed, the valley floor erupts into a froth of green moss and black chitin.

Scattered throughout this frenzy are the **Resonance Spires**—remnants of a technology that operates on principles my implants can’t fully parse. They look like artifacts left by giants: slender towers of what appears to be spun carbon, rising three hundred meters from the valley floor, but they exist in a state of quantum superposition, flickering in and out of visibility. When they materialize, they sing—a pure, agonizing tone that cuts through the biological chaos like a scalpel. The sound drives the siren-eels into frenzies of mating or violence; it’s hard to tell the difference.

I find one of the scattered structures mentioned in the old surveys: a **listening post** built from the same impossible carbon weave, half-buried in the moss. It’s small—a single room with walls that breathe in and out with the atmospheric pressure. Inside, the noise drops to a manageable roar, and I can see the previous occupants’ marks: handprints pressed into the walls, preserved by the nanite coating that keeps the structure from dissolving into the valley’s metabolism. The tech here doesn’t just resist the environment; it *negotiates* with it, rewriting local physics to maintain a bubble of stability.

But the stability is temporary. The ground lurches—a fresh tear opening in the crust—and the listening post tilts fifteen degrees. Through the doorway, I see the valley reorganizing itself. A stand of spring-trees explodes into shards as the seismic wave passes, the fragments instantly colonized by a red fungus that wasn’t there yesterday. The fungus glows with a fierce, hot bioluminescence that actually penetrates the dark, revealing for a moment the true scale of this place: not a single valley, but a network of them, dozens of similar wounds in the earth, all connected by aquifers that roar like freight trains beneath the surface.

The temperature drops further. My breath crystallizes instantly, and the moisture in the air begins to precipitate as a strange, oily snow that hisses when it touches the warm basalt. The siren-eels dive into the crevices, their screams dopplering down into the earth. The Resonance Spires flicker faster, their song rising to a crescendo that makes my vision blur.

And then—the moment of creation. The valley’s turbulence reaches a peak, and the physics breaks. A new feature births itself before my eyes: a pillar of water rises from a fissure, defying gravity, twisting into a helix that freezes mid-air into a sculpture of ice and compressed atmosphere. It stands for three seconds, beautiful and impossible, before the Resonance Spires shatter it with a harmonic pulse. The debris falls as rain.

The noise is deafening now, a physical pressure that pushes against my eardrums. The spring-trees are growing at visible speed, their crystalline branches reaching for the spires, only to be shattered by the next seismic convulsion. The shiver-moss releases a cloud of spores so dense that the air becomes a green soup, and in that soup, a thousand new species might be born or die in the next hour.

I retreat to the listening post, sealing the door. Through the carbon walls, I can feel the valley’s heartbeat: chaotic, arrhythmic, accelerating. This is a place where the earth is still deciding what it wants to be, where every rainstorm is a creative act and every sound is a weapon. The technology I carry—the nanites in my blood, the quantum entanglers in my pack—feels primitive compared to the raw, violent engineering happening outside.

The darkness presses against the walls. The cold deepens. And the valley continues its endless, screaming work of becoming.
