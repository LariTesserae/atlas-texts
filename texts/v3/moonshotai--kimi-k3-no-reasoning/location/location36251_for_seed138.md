The city of Threnos lies in a shallow basin where the water table has never receded, a flat sprawl of low-rise structures built on pilings that have been sinking at the same rate for two centuries without ever quite reaching bedrock. The ground here is young in the sense that it is perpetually new—sediment washed down from distant uplands and spread thin over ancient foundations, a skin of silt that cracks and reforms with each season, though the buildings above remain unnervingly stable, as if anchored to something beneath the shifting surface. The air holds heat like a wet cloth, pressing against the skin with a density that makes breathing feel like an act of consumption, and the light that filters through the perpetual haze is the color of old brass, dim enough that shadows exist more as suggestions than as true darkness.

Water is everywhere and nowhere useful. It seeps up between the paving stones of the narrow streets, weeps from the mortar of brick walls, condenses on the undersides of corrugated iron roofs and falls in a slow, irregular rhythm that never quite becomes rain. The canals that divide the city into its irregular blocks are less waterways than slow-moving currents of silt-laden liquid, thick as paint, steaming faintly in the heat. They carry the chemical signatures of the factories that line their banks—low, sprawling complexes of concrete and rusted steel where machines have been running at the same steady tempo since before anyone living was born. The sound is constant: a bass thrum of engines that never cycle down, the clank of piston rods and the hiss of steam release valves, all blending into a single overwhelming hum that vibrates in the teeth and makes conversation require proximity and effort.

Vegetation is sparse but tenacious, consisting mainly of a single species of reed that has adapted to the mineral-rich waters. These grow in isolated clumps along the canal edges, their stalks bleached pale by the chemical runoff, rattling dryly against each other despite the humidity. No trees remain within the city limits; the heat and the perpetual damp make wood rot too quickly to be useful for construction, so the city is built of stone, brick, and metal, all sweating with condensation that never evaporates.

The fauna is abundant but confined to specific niches. Swarms of small, iridescent flies hover above the water, their wings catching the dim light in flashes of green and gold. Larger creatures—long-legged birds with beaks adapted for probing the silt, amphibious mammals with slick, hairless bodies—move through the shallows with unhurried confidence, their populations sustained by the chemical-rich detritus of the industrial process. They have learned to tolerate the noise, the heat, the perpetual dimness, and their cries, when they make them, are harsh and truncated, designed to cut through the mechanical drone.

The scale of Threnos is intimate despite its urban density. The streets are narrow, the buildings rarely exceeding three stories, and the boundaries between districts are marked by changes in the type of industry rather than by walls or gates. One moves from the tannery quarter to the dye-works to the metal-fabrication zone by crossing a single bridge, each area maintaining its own distinct smell and color of effluent while sharing the same oppressive atmosphere. The people who live here—pale-skinned from lack of direct sunlight, their clothing perpetually damp—move through their routines with the same steady rhythm as their machines. There is no sense of urgency, no visible change from year to year. The factories produce the same quantities, the canals carry the same volume, the heat remains constant. It is a place that exists in equilibrium, a closed system where energy is expended but nothing is transformed, where the water rises and falls by the same centimeters each day, where the flies hatch and die in cycles that have repeated themselves for generations. The young erosion of the ground beneath is the only sign of time's passage, a slow settling that the inhabitants counteract by adding new layers of brick and stone to their thresholds, building up as the earth sinks down, maintaining a surface that has been at the same level for as long as anyone can remember.
