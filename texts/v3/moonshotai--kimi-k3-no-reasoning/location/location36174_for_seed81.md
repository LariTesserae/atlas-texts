# The Settling of Vhir

The first thing to understand about Vhir is that it is a city built on a lake, but no one has ever seen the lake. It lies somewhere beneath — beneath the foundation-plates, beneath the sintered crust, beneath the old quarried bedrock that the first constructors cut into blocks and stacked into the earliest retaining walls. The lake is down there still, pressing upward, and the whole city sweats with it.

Vhir sits high, near the top of a broad volcanic plateau that tilts gently southeast for what might be a hundred kilometers. The plateau is old lava — not recent, not raw, but lava that has had time to become something other than catastrophe. It has settled into itself. The flows cooled into long, overlapping benches of dark basalt, each one slightly lower than the last, like the treads of a staircase built for something enormous and patient. The edges of these benches are rounded now, softened not by water-cutting but by thermal cycling: the daily expansion and contraction of stone under relentless heat. The plateau breathes, in its way — swelling in the long dim day, contracting in the barely cooler night — and over centuries this has worn its angles smooth.

The heat is constant and enveloping, the kind that does not blaze but saturates. It rises from the rock itself, from some deep thermal engine that keeps the plateau warm the way a body keeps its skin warm. The air is thick with it and thick with moisture, because the buried lake will not stay buried. It finds every fissure, every joint in the basalt, every gap in the construction. It rises as vapor through the pores of the stone, condenses on the undersides of bridges and platforms, weeps from the faces of walls. There is no rain — there has never been rain here, not in the way rain works elsewhere — but there is constant condensation, a slow dripping that never ceases, water appearing on every surface that is marginally cooler than the air around it.

The light is poor. This is not darkness — it is a persistent, sourceless dimness, as though the sun is always behind something, always about to emerge, never quite doing so. The sky is a low, luminous haze, the color of old tarnish, bright enough to see by but never bright enough to cast a clean shadow. Everything in Vhir exists in a state of near-shadowlessness. Edges blur. Distances are deceptive. The farther benches of the plateau do not recede into atmospheric perspective so much as simply fade, losing contrast until they merge with the haze and are gone.

---

The city occupies perhaps a dozen of the great basalt benches, spilling across them and down between them, connected by ramps and stairs and hanging walkways that follow the natural architecture of the cooled flows. It is not a dense city. It is, in fact, one of the emptiest places that could still be called a city. The buildings are there — hundreds of them, perhaps thousands — but they are spread across a vast area, separated by wide plazas of bare stone, by empty terraces, by the sheer scale of the plateau itself. You can stand in one district and see the next as a faint cluster of shapes in the haze, and between you and it there may be nothing at all: just the dark rock, wet and gleaming, and the slow curl of vapor rising from a fissure.

The construction is layered. This is immediately apparent. The oldest structures are cut directly from the basalt — not built on it but carved from it, rooms and corridors excavated into the solid flows, their walls the same dark stone as the plateau, unadorned, close-fitted, enduring. These are the deep foundations, the root-structures. Above them, later generations added platforms of dressed stone, then frames of rust-colored metal, then the most recent stratum: a scaffolding of pipes, conduits, and prefabricated panels that seems to have been assembled quickly, almost carelessly, as though whoever built it was in a hurry or had lost interest in permanence. These newest additions are the ones that show the most distress — panels warped by heat, joints weeping condensation, paint (if it was paint) blistered and peeling.

The industrial character of the city is unmistakable but strangely subdued. There are engines here — you can feel them more than hear them, a low tremor in the stone beneath your feet, a rhythm like a slow heartbeat. Great pump-houses sit at the edges of the benches, their intakes plunging down through drilled shafts into the buried lake, drawing water up and distributing it through the city's network of pipes. The pipes run everywhere — along walls, under floors, across plazas on low trestles, always wet on the outside, always warm. They are the city's circulatory system, and they sweat like skin.

There are factories, or what were factories: long, low buildings with ranks of tall openings along their sides, inside which sit rows of machines whose purpose is not immediately clear. Some are still running — you can tell by the vibration, the faint clink-and-hiss of moving parts, the warmth radiating from their housings. Others are silent and cold, their mechanisms frozen in positions that suggest they stopped mid-task, mid-motion, mid-something. No one has turned them off. They simply stopped, and no one has started them again, or perhaps there is no one left who knows how.

---

There is no vegetation. None. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a single thread of anything that could be called a plant. The plateau is as bare as the day it cooled, and the city has done nothing to change that. Whatever grows here — if anything grows here — is not recognizable as life in any familiar sense. There are crusts in the wettest places, along the weeping wall-faces and around the outflow points of the pumps: thin, iridescent films that shift color when you move, that seem to be mineral deposits but that also seem to thicken and spread over time in ways that mineral deposits should not. They are not alive in any way that can be easily determined. They are simply present, and slowly increasing, and no one scrapes them away.

The fauna — what little there is — is equally ambiguous. There are things that move. They are rare enough that you might spend hours in the city without seeing one, but they exist. Small, quick shapes that dart between the pipes when your attention is elsewhere. A slow, heavy presence that occasionally dislodges a stone somewhere in the empty plazas, sending a small clatter echoing across the rock. Once, on the underside of a bridge, something pale and many-jointed, clinging to the stone in the condensation, utterly still, so perfectly still that it might have been a formation of the mineral crust — until it shifted, slowly, one limb at a time, and repositioned itself with the unhurried patience of something that has no predators and no prey and no particular reason to be anywhere at any particular time.

These creatures — if they are creatures — do not interact with the city. They do not enter buildings. They do not avoid the machines or seek the warmth or show any interest in the pumps and pipes and platforms. They exist in the same space as the city the way lichen exists on a statue: present, indifferent, following rules that have nothing to do with the built world around them.

---

The emptiness of Vhir is its most pervasive quality, more than the heat, more than the wet, more than the strangeness. It is an emptiness that feels engineered, deliberate, as though the city was designed to be mostly empty — as though the spaces between things were the point. The plazas are too large. The corridors are too wide. The buildings have too many rooms and the rooms have too little in them. You walk through a hall that could hold five hundred people and it holds none, and the floor is wet, and the walls weep, and somewhere a machine is humming, and the hall extends ahead of you into the dim haze and there is nothing in it but the sound of your own footsteps and the distant, rhythmic tremor of the pumps.

The sound of the place is mostly this: footsteps, dripping, the pump-rhythm, the occasional clink or hiss from a machine. The wind, when it comes, is warm and wet and moves slowly, and it does not howl or whistle because there are few sharp edges for it to catch on. It simply pushes through the city like a slow tide, carrying the smell of hot wet stone and something else — a faint metallic quality, like the taste of a coin held on the tongue.

The emptiness extends to the people, or the absence of people. Whether anyone lives in Vhir is a question the landscape does not answer readily. The machines are maintained — some of them, anyway — or at least they have not all failed, which suggests ongoing attention. The pipes are patched in places, the patches newer than the pipes. But there are no signs of habitation in the usual sense: no doors that look recently opened, no paths of wear on the stone that suggest regular foot traffic, no accumulation of the small debris that living creatures leave behind. If someone is here, they are very few, and very quiet, and very good at not being found.

---

The weirdness of Vhir is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It accumulates slowly, the way the mineral crusts accumulate on the wet walls. It is in the way the condensation sometimes moves upward — droplets forming on the floor of a plaza and then rising, slowly, to cling to the underside of a bridge or the ceiling of a colonnade, as though gravity here is a suggestion rather than a law, a suggestion that water mostly follows but occasionally forgets. It is in the way the pump-rhythm sometimes changes, not gradually but all at once, as though all the pumps in the city have simultaneously decided to beat at a different tempo, and the stone beneath your feet shifts its vibration, and the change lasts for minutes or hours and then reverts, and there is no explanation.

It is in the way the haze sometimes parts — not lifts, not disperses, but parts, like a curtain drawn aside — revealing for a moment a stretch of plateau that extends farther than it should, benches of basalt continuing to a horizon that is too far away, under a sky that is briefly, startlingly, a different color than it was before. And then the haze closes again, and the distance snaps back to what it was, and you are left with the memory of having seen more than the place is supposed to contain.

It is in the way the machines that are still running seem to be running toward no end. They pump water and circulate it, and the water goes somewhere and comes back, and the cycle continues, but there is no product, no output, no discernible purpose. The factories produce nothing. The pumps move water that no one uses. The engines vibrate in their housings and drive mechanisms that accomplish nothing beyond their own continuation. The city is a machine that runs because it was built to run, and it has not stopped, and perhaps it will not stop, and the question of why is one that the landscape poses without expecting an answer.

---

At the southeastern edge of the city, where the benches descend and the plateau begins its long tilt toward whatever lies beyond the haze, there is a place where the buried lake comes closest to the surface. Here the rock is not merely wet but saturated, and the vapor rises in steady columns, and the heat is most intense, and the iridescent crust is thickest. The oldest structures cluster here — the carved rooms, the deep foundations — as though the first builders came for this, for the water and the heat and the closeness of the lake, and built their city around it the way you would build a house around a fire.

Standing here, at the edge, with the haze below you and the dark rock beneath and the columns of vapor rising and the pump-rhythm steady under your feet, you have the sense of a place that has settled — not decayed, not declined, but settled, the way sediment settles in still water, finding its level, coming to rest in a configuration that is stable and complete and unlikely to change. The city is not dying. It is not thriving. It is existing, in its own way, at its own pace, in its own state, and it has been doing so for a very long time, and it will continue to do so, and the vapor will rise and the water will weep from the walls and the machines will hum and the crusts will slowly, slowly thicken, and the dim light will neither brighten nor fade, and the emptiness will remain, vast and warm and wet and quiet, like a held breath that is never quite released.
