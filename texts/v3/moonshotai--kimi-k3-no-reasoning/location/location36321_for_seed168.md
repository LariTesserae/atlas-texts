The plateau of Corrigia sits at an elevation where the atmosphere thins into a precise, crystalline medium, transmitting a warm, lemon-colored light that never shifts in intensity or angle. Here, the air is bone-dry—not merely arid, but aggressively anhydrous, as if the world itself has been scoured of molecular hydrogen and oxygen. There is no water here, not even locked in mineral hydrates or frozen in shadowed craters; the chemistry is silicate and chloride, a realm of dry acids and subliming salts that have never known liquidity.

The ground is not yet ground. It is embryonic—a tectonic infant still soft in the middle, barely cooled from its accretion. Great slabs of raw quartzite and unweathered feldspar thrust upward at awkward angles, their edges knife-sharp, unblunted by wind or rain. The erosion here is negative; rather than wearing down, the landscape is actively accreting, growing new crust through a process of slow, electrostatic accretion that builds mineral whiskers into delicate, hollow lattices. These structures, kilometers tall and wide as cities, are the only architecture—unbuilt, grown rather than made, though they bear an uncanny resemblance to industrial scaffolding.

The vegetation is not plant life as understood elsewhere, but it is lush in its proliferation. It consists of vast, static colonies of litho-trophic crystal-ferns—organisms (if they can be called that) that metabolize geothermal heat and trace gases, extruding emerald and viridian filaments into the thin air. These filaments do not sway; they are rigid, glassy, arranged in fractal tiers that catch the warm light and shatter it into stationary rainbows. They carpet the valley floors between the rising rock slabs, dense and impenetrable, yet utterly silent. There are no leaves to rustle, no water to drip, no fauna to tread the crystal undergrowth into mulch. The vegetation simply exists, photosynthesizing the unwavering light, growing imperceptibly thicker over eons, locked in a equilibrium of perfect stasis.

Despite the absence of constructed artifacts—no roads, no smokestacks, no wires—the land is saturated with industrial process. The plateau itself is a factory floor. Natural combustion vents, fueled by the oxidation of metallic veins, maintain a constant, warm ambient temperature through invisible exothermic reactions in the bedrock. Mechanical rhythms emerge from the geology: the slow, magnetic shifting of iron-rich monoliths that align and realign like gears in a vast, unmoving engine; the piezoelectric discharge of quartz veins that power electrochemical gardens of crystalline growth. It is an industry without workers, without products, without purpose—an automated landscape that manufactures nothing but its own continuity, humming with electrical potentials that never discharge into weather or motion.

The scale is regional, a high-altitude basin the size of a small nation, yet it contains almost nothing per volume. Between the cathedral-like mineral lattices and the dense, silent thickets of crystal-fern, there are vast expanses of emptiness—pure, amber-tinted vacuum-thin air stretching between features. One can stand on a promontory of raw, uneroded basalt and see for hundreds of kilometers across a terrain that feels simultaneously crowded with vegetation and hollow, because nothing moves, nothing breathes, nothing decays. The density is spectral; the place is a ghost of a biome, a lush desert where abundance and void coexist.

Sound here is a rare, almost theoretical phenomenon. Without water to carry vibration, without animals to cry out, without wind strong enough to disturb the rigid crystal flora, the plateau rests in a silence so complete it has texture. Occasionally, the growing crystals will stress-fracture, producing a single, clear tone like a struck wine glass that hangs in the air for minutes, undamped by humidity. These notes are events, spaced years apart, and they do not echo—they simply stop.

Time has ceased to be a dimension of change here. The warm, normal light remains fixed at a perpetual late-afternoon angle, casting long, motionless shadows that never lengthen into dusk. The emerging geology, though technically “under construction,” proceeds at a rate indistinguishable from zero on any meaningful timescale; a mountain might rise a millimeter per millennium, maintaining the plateau in a state of eternal, suspended becoming. It is stable, frozen at the moment just before formation completes, a landscape caught in the act of its own birth and held there indefinitely.

To walk through Corrigia is to traverse a museum of industrial nature, where the engines are bedrock and the vegetation is glass, where the warmth is a chemical constant and the light is a fixed resource. It is a place of high, dry clarity, where the absence of water and fauna creates a vacuum that the lush, crystalline flora fills without softening, and where the vast, empty distances between the growing mineral towers emphasize the silence of a world that has achieved perfect, changeless equilibrium without ever deciding to be finished.
