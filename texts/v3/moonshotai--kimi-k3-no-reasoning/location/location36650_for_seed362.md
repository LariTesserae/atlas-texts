**The Circadian Engine**

The world does not have a floor. It has a *substrate*—a living interlocking mass of crystalline succulents, copper-leaved vines, and pneumatic root-systems that have grown over, through, and finally *become* the machinery that once contained them. There is no soil, no stone, no water. There is only the Green, and the Green is made of moving parts.

To walk here is to tread upon pressure-plates disguised as chloroplasts. The ground breathes. Not metaphorically: the entire continental mass—stretching beyond every horizon, curved gently under a low, bright sky—expands and contracts on a thirty-hour cycle, inhaling photons, exhaling oxygen-scent and the hum of well-maintained pistons. The vegetation is the architecture is the factory. Towering structures that resemble cathedrals are actually single, gargantuan bromeliads with steel ribs; their petals are solar arrays of translucent chitin that track the triple suns with mechanical precision, clicking like abacuses as they tilt.

The light is aggressive. It falls from a sky that never clouds, striking surfaces polished by windless abrasion into mirrors. Everything reflects. The light bounces between leaf-glass and brass bark until the air itself seems to shimmer with solid luminescence. Yet the temperature remains stubbornly cool—a refrigerated brightness, as if the world were a vast, lit larder. This is the doing of the Engine: heat is converted instantly to motion by photosynthetic turbines embedded in the trunks of kilometer-wide "trees," their canopies forming a second sky of interlocking gears and pollen-filters.

There is no water. Instead, a clear, viscous silicone oil circulates through transparent vines, lubricating the joints of the world. It drips sometimes from high leaves, thick and slow, pooling in depressions where the substrate has worn concave through centuries of friction. These pools do not evaporate; they are reclaimed by capillary roots that snake up from below, metallic and fibrous, to drink them back into the system. The chemistry is non-aqueous, based on liquid ammonia and synthetic hydrocarbons, keeping the Green fluid and functional in the cold.

The silence is absolute enough to hear your own blood. Sound here is dampened by the density—too many surfaces, too many layers of velvet moss that grows over conveyor belts, muffling their rotation. When noise does occur, it is structural: the deep *thunk* of a root-piston firing somewhere in the under-dark, the high whine of a distant nutrient pump, the rustle of carbon-fiber leaves adjusting their angle. No birds. No insects. The fauna is rare and strange—occasional "shepherds," six-legged machines of rusted chrome that tend the green, moving slowly through the undergrowth on magnetic feet, their eyes glowing soft blue. They are the only moving things that are not plant, not engine, but something between, and they are so few that seeing one is considered an omen.

The weirdness begins with the scale. It is too big, and it knows it. The horizon curves upward at the edges, suggesting a planet too small for its own gravity, or a space station folded into a Klein bottle. Distances do not add up. If you walk toward a distant spire—a flower the size of a city, blooming in mechanical slow-motion—you may find it receding faster than you approach, not because it moves, but because the geometry between you is stretching. The Engine is large enough to have weather, but the weather is internal: high-pressure zones of oxygen build up in the hollow trunks of dead megaflowers, then release in windless gusts that travel through sealed corridors, knocking down forests of ferns that are actually circuit boards, their fronds etched with gold pathways.

The erosion is ancient, settled. The world has been in its current state for so long that the processes have become ritualized. The Green grows over the machines, the machines prune the Green, and the balance holds. Rust does not corrode here; it is cultivated, forming specific patterns that serve as circuitry. Decay is a form of memory. When a leaf-gear wears down, it is not replaced but absorbed, its structure breaking down into a fine black dust that feeds the roots, which then push up a new, identical gear in the same spot, as if the world is dreaming of itself and refusing to wake.

The dynamic processes are relentless. The triple suns never set—they are fixed in a triangle at the zenith, perhaps artificial, perhaps caught in the world's gravitational throat—but the Engine creates its own cycles. Every thirty hours, the great inhalation occurs: the entire landscape tilts slightly, valleys becoming ridges, the dense undergrowth compressing with a sound like a sigh held too long. During the exhalation, spores are released—metallic, magnetic, glowing. They drift in the bright air, catching the light, until they are drawn to the induction fields of the growing towers, where they settle and sprout into new cogs, new leaves, new walls.

To be here is to realize that you are either a contaminant or a component. The Shepherd-machines will ignore you unless you damage the substrate, at which point they will approach with terrifying, silent speed, their tools unfolding like surgical instruments. The Green itself is indifferent. It will grow over your footprints within minutes, absorbing the impression, incorporating your mass into its silicon-lattice memory.

This is a world that has eaten its own wilderness and replaced it with something hungrier and more orderly. It is a garden the size of a universe, maintained by physics that occasionally hiccup—a door that opens into the same room, a shadow that falls toward the light, a flower that blooms in reverse, gathering its petals from the air and sealing them into a bud that whirs like a dynamo. The reality breakdown is subtle, structural. It is the realization that the "plants" are not pretending to be machines, and the machines are not pretending to be plants; they have forgotten the difference, and in forgetting, have become something else entirely.

You stand on a leaf that is also a bridge. It vibrates with the turning of the world. The cool light bathes everything in a glare that reveals no dust, no decay, only the endless, dense, bright working of the Circadian Engine, ticking softly, growing imperceptibly, dreaming of gears.
