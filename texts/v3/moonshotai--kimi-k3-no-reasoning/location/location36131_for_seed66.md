# The Crying City of Aerys-Van

---

They found it by accident, which is the only way anyone has ever found it. There are no maps because maps require borders and Aerys-Van has none that hold still long enough to be useful. The city occupies a plateau — or a series of fused plateaus, or possibly the flattened crown of a single ancient mountain so thoroughly built upon that geology is now speculation. The elevation is enough that clouds pass *through* the streets rather than overhead, threading between towers like pale riverfish, leaving damp fingerprints on every surface they touch.

---

## The Water

The first thing — always the first thing — is the water.

Not falling water. Not rain. Rain here is a memory of a thing, an event so rare it generates festivals. The water of Aerys-Van is *persistent condensation*, an atmosphere so laden with moisture at this altitude that every surface above body temperature weeps, and every surface below it blooms with slow-moving droplets that gather, shiver, and release in thin rivulets that find their way into the city's vascular system of gutters, channels, and ceramic-lined runnels. The stone is always dark with wetness. The metal is always streaked. There is a smell — warm, mineral, faintly metallic — that newcomers mistake for humidity until they realize it is not the air but the *ground itself* that is producing it, exhaling vapor from ten thousand pores in basalt so ancient it has forgotten its own name.

The water is warm. Not hot — warm like a hand that has been resting on your shoulder too long, warm like breath against your ear. The geothermal gradient beneath the plateau pushes heat upward through the rock, and the thin mountain atmosphere, pressed against this warmth, surrenders its moisture in a perpetual slow cycle: rise, condense, weep, gather, sink, warm, rise again. The city has been living inside this loop for so long that the loop has *become* the city. You cannot separate one from the other.

Where the water collects — and it collects everywhere, in shallow basins carved into street corners, in rooftop cisterns that have become rooftop *ecosystems*, in the deep cisterns beneath the lower tiers where the oldest tunnels drink from a seep-warm aquifer — it is thick with dissolved minerals. Calcium. Silica. Something darker that the water-smiths call *iron-blood* and use to stain their ceramics. The water leaves pale crusts on everything it touches over time, and these crusts are not cleaned away but *carved*, incorporated into the architecture as though the city and its own mineral accretions are collaborating on a design neither fully controls.

---

## The Absence of Green

There is nothing alive here in the way the word is usually used. No trees. No grasses. No moss on the stones, though the stones are perpetually wet enough to host it. This absence is so total and so ancient that the people of Aerys-Van have no native word for *plant*. They have loan-words, theoretical terms from the outside, dead syllables they roll in their mouths like foreign coins.

The ground is bare — bare and beautiful in a way that unsettles visitors, the way a face without eyebrows unsettles. Volcanic glass in dark sheets, obsidian flows frozen mid-cascade, their surfaces dulled by millennia of warm water and warm wind to the texture of worn leather. Basalt columns, hexagonal in cross-section, clustered like organ pipes where some ancient eruption cracked its own crust as it cooled. Veins of milky quartz running through darker stone like capillaries, catching the light when the sun breaks through the perpetual haze — and it does break through, often enough, casting hard warm shadows that make the whole city look like a relief map of itself.

Without plants, the water does not root. It runs where it runs, follows gravity and capillary action, carves thin channels into soft stone and polishes hard stone to a dark mirror. The runoff patterns are the city's gardens — tended, redirected, celebrated. There are *water-gardeners* whose entire profession is the careful management of flow across a single wall, coaxing mineral deposition into spirals and lattices over the course of decades. Their work is invisible to anyone who hasn't been told to look, and revelatory to anyone who has.

---

## The Built World

Aerys-Van is not large. It is *dense*, which is different. The city stacks itself — tier upon tier, structure upon structure, every horizontal surface claimed and built upon until the vertical becomes the only available direction. Towers of dark stone and black-fired brick rise from the plateau's crown like the quills of some enormous patient animal, and between them, bridging them, wrapping around them, are the *secondary structures*: walkways of hammered copper, green with patina; enclosed passages of glazed ceramic tile that sweat on the inside and rain on the outside; platforms of riveted iron, warm to the touch, thrumming faintly with the vibration of machinery housed in the levels below.

The architecture is not planned. It is *sedimentary*. Each generation built on the last — not on top of, but *into*, weaving new structures through old ones until the city resembles a cross-section of its own history, all layers visible simultaneously. A wall in the Ninth Tier might incorporate stones from the First Tier's original foundation, carried upward and repositioned. A drainpipe installed three centuries ago might pass through the bedroom of a family that has lived in the same apartment for eleven generations. The city does not demolish. It *absorbs*.

The machinery is everywhere and nowhere — hidden inside walls, beneath floors, coiled around support columns like ivy on a tree. Aerys-Van runs on steam and pressure and the patient exploitation of its own geothermal warmth. Great engines in the lower tiers — pistons the size of rooms, flywheels that turn so slowly they seem still until you notice the position of a spoke has changed since morning — pump water upward through ceramic mains, circulate air through the deep galleries, drive the bellows that keep the foundries breathing. There is no electricity here, not yet. There is pressure and heat and the vast slow patience of mechanical advantage: pulleys, cams, counterweights, escapements. The city ticks.

The sound of it —

---

## The Sound

— the sound is constant and layered and *structural*. It is not noise. It is the sound a body makes when it is alive and too large for you to stand far enough away from to hear as silence. The engines produce a bass tone so low it is felt in the sternum rather than heard — a standing wave that resonates through the plateau's basalt and makes the entire city vibrate at a frequency just below conscious perception. Over this, the hiss of steam escaping from ten thousand joints and valves, each one slightly different in pitch, creating a composite chord that shifts as the pressure cycles shift. Over this, the water: dripping, running, splashing, seeping, a thousand small percussive events that never synchronize but never fully fall into chaos either, like rain that has been going on so long it has become a texture rather than a weather event.

And over all of this, the fauna.

---

## The Fauna

They are the reason visitors leave or stay forever. There is no in-between.

They are called *kesset* by the inhabitants, which means something between "the ones who are here" and "the ones we share with," and they are not like anything else. Not birds. Not insects. Not reptiles or mammals or fish, though they share features with each. They are warm-bodied and soft-skinned, ranging in size from a fingernail to a horse, and they are *everywhere* — in the air, on the walls, in the water channels, inside the machinery, underfoot, overhead. The city does not have a population of them so much as it *is* a population of them, with human inhabitants making up a minority that everyone politely agrees not to quantify.

The smallest ones are translucent and vein-winged, drawn to warmth, clustering on warm pipes and warm stones in quivering drifts that scatter when touched and re-form within seconds. They make no sound individually but in their thousands produce a faint harmonic hum, like the overtone of a bell long after the strike.

The mid-sized ones are the visible majority — sleek-bodied, four-limbed, with broad flat heads and eyes that reflect the ambient warmth of the city in shades of copper and bronze. They climb everything. They are unhurried. They move through the city with the casual entitlement of creatures that have never been hunted, never been threatened, never needed to hurry. They sit on windowsills and watch the street with an attentiveness that is not quite intelligence and not quite vacancy. They drink from the condensation channels in long, slow sips. They are warm to the touch — all of them are warm, every size, every variant, warm as though they are running a gentle fever that never breaks, and the people of Aerys-Van have long since stopped finding this strange.

The largest ones live in the lower tiers, in the deep cisterns and the steam galleries, and are rarely seen. They are felt more than seen — a displacement of warm air, a shift in the vibration of the floor, a slow rhythmic exhalation that can be mistaken for the breathing of the engines if you don't know what you're listening for. The engine-tenders say the big ones are harmless. The engine-tenders say this the way people say things they have decided to believe.

The kesset do not eat, as far as anyone can determine. They absorb — warmth, moisture, ambient vibration. They are drawn to the machinery the way moths are drawn to light, clustering on flywheel housings and steam manifolds, their bodies pulsing faintly in time with the mechanical rhythm. Whether they are feeding on the heat or *responding* to it — whether the distinction even matters — is a question the city has been not-answering for centuries.

They reproduce slowly. Everything here is slow.

---

## The Slowness

This is the thing that defines Aerys-Van more than any other single quality: it is a city that has stopped changing and started *being*. The erosion that shaped the plateau completed its work long ago. The architecture reached its density equilibrium centuries back — there is no more room to build, and so nothing new is built, only maintained, adjusted, absorbed. The engines run at a pace dictated by the geothermal gradient, which does not vary. The water cycles at the pace of condensation and evaporation, which is the pace of the atmosphere, which is the pace of the altitude, which does not change.

Days here are long and featureless in the way that a long, slow chord is featureless — full, sustained, unresolving. The light is the light of altitude filtered through perpetual haze: bright but directionless, warm but shadowless, a diffuse amber glow that gives everything the quality of being seen through slightly tinted glass. Shadows exist but they are soft-edged, indecisive, as though the light cannot commit to a source.

The people — and there are people, a stable population that has neither grown nor shrunk in living memory — move at the pace of their city. Not lazily. Not slowly in the way of exhaustion or reluctance. They move with the deliberate economy of those who have nowhere to get to that won't still be there when they arrive. Conversations are long. Meals are long. The water-gardeners think in decades. The engine-tenders think in maintenance cycles measured in generations. A young person apprenticing to a craft will be told, without irony, that they should not expect to produce competent work for at least fifteen years, and this is said not as a warning but as a simple fact, like being told the altitude.

---

## The Weirdness of It

It is not a strange place in any way that announces itself. There are no impossible geometries, no violations of physics, no portals or floating stones or colors that don't exist. The strangeness is subtler — a wrongness of proportion, of expectation. A city this dense should feel crowded; it feels intimate. A place this wet should be alive with green; it is bare and beautiful. A world this full of creatures should be loud with their noise; they are nearly silent, and the noise comes from the machines instead. Everything is slightly *beside* what you expect, like a word on the tip of your tongue that turns out, when it arrives, to be a different word entirely.

The kesset watch you. All of them. Not with menace, not with curiosity — with something more passive and more total, as though they are not observing you but *including* you, drawing you into whatever ambient awareness they share. After a few days in Aerys-Van you begin to feel watched even when you are alone, and after a few more days you stop minding, and after a few more you begin to understand, in a way you cannot articulate, that the watching is not directed *at* you but simply *includes* you, the way warmth includes you, the way gravity includes you.

The water tastes like the inside of your own mouth.

The engines never stop.

---

## What the Place Is

Aerys-Van is what happens when a city stops aspiring and starts persisting. It is not growing. It is not dying. It is *maintaining*, and the maintenance has become so thorough, so patient, so deeply woven into every stone and rivet and channel and breath, that the city has achieved something close to what the water-gardeners achieve on their walls: a pattern that is always the same and never identical, a flow that goes nowhere and arrives everywhere.

The warm rain that is not rain falls on the dark stone that is not quite stone anymore — stone that has been touched by so many warm hands and warm bodies and warm machines over so many centuries that it has taken on the quality of skin, of membrane, of something that is not alive but has been adjacent to life for so long that the boundary has worn thin. The kesset cluster on the warm pipes and pulse. The engines breathe. The water weeps from the sky that is too thin to hold it and runs down the walls in thin bright lines and gathers in the basins and sinks into the warm dark earth and rises again.

And the city continues, vast and dense and slow, at an altitude where the clouds pass through the streets like visitors who have learned not to knock.
