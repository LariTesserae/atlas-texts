There is a room that does not know it is a room.

It sits at the top of a structure too tall for the air around it, a slab of industry punched into the cold, thin light of an altitude where clouds pass beneath the floor plates and thin into nonexistence. The room is not large, not by the standards of the vast works below — perhaps a hundred meters on a side, sealed under a ribbed ceiling of dark alloy, every surface manufactured, every seam riveted, every bolt visible and purposefully exposed. No stone. No soil. No accidental thing has ever entered here. Even the dust is engineered: a pale, dry mineral powder left by the slow abrasion of mechanisms against one another, ground finer than ash and settled into the corners in soft, perfect drifts that no wind has ever disturbed, because there is no wind.

The cold is absolute and architectural. It does not seep; it is built into the walls. Frost grows in rigid geometries along the structural members, feathering outward in perfect crystalline lattices that follow the edges of girders like moss on a cathedral, but mineral — ice frozen from some internal atmospheric moisture long since exhausted. That frost has not changed in years. It does not melt. It does not grow. It exists in a state of permanent, exquisite stillness, each hexagonal spike locked in place as though the temperature itself were load-bearing.

The light is ordinary. That is what makes it wrong. Even, industrial, fluorescent-equivalent illumination from recessed panels in the ceiling — the kind of light designed for human eyes doing human work, calibrated to something like daylight on a temperate afternoon at sea level. It falls evenly across the room, undramatic, unshadowed, and in doing so makes the emptiness louder. This is light for a workspace. The workspace is empty. The light does not care. It has been on — uniformly, without flicker or dimming — since the room was finished, and it will remain on, because the power source is not a circuit that can be interrupted but something deeper, some electrochemical heart that beats in the walls and will not stop. The light is part of the structure's equilibrium, as constant as the cold.

And the sound — the sound is enormous.

It does not come from inside the room. That is the first thing a visitor would notice, if a visitor could exist here without dying: the room itself is silent, but the structure beneath it is not. Below the floor plates, down through hundreds of meters of vertical industry, something is running — engines, turbines, some vast mechanized process of manufacture or conversion whose purpose is no longer legible. The vibration travels upward through the frame like sound through bone. It arrives in the room as a pressure, a subsonic roar that you feel in your teeth before you hear it, a deep and layered drone composed of frequencies too low to resolve into pitch and too loud to ignore. It is not rhythmic. It does not pulse. It is steady — one continuous, deafening, unchanging note that has been sustained for so long it has become a property of the space, the way gravity is a property of mass. The air shimmers with it. The frost on the girders vibrates but does not fall. The dust in the corners holds its shape against the pressure because the pressure never varies — it pushes always with the same force, in the same direction, forever.

There are plants.

This is the strangest thing in a room full of strange things: something grows here. Sparse, scattered, individual — spaced meters apart, rising from the floor plates as though seeded by accident, which is impossible, because nothing here is accidental. They are not green. Green requires water, requires biology as we understand it, and this place has neither. They are pale — bone-white, ash-grey, faintly translucent — and their structure is crystalline, branching, fractal, each specimen a small mineral tree no taller than a knee. They grow from the seams between floor plates, their roots threading into the joints where the industrial sealant has, over decades, cracked just enough to admit something — not water, not soil, but some thin volatile chemistry leaking upward from the works below. They metabolize vibration, perhaps, or trace gases, or the light itself. Their growth is so slow as to be undetectable. They have reached their current size and stopped. They are part of the equilibrium too — the room's sparse, cold garden of mineral botany, unchanging, each one cast in sharp shadow by the even light, each one exactly where it has always been.

The scale of the room creates a peculiar intimacy. Despite the altitude, despite the monstrous sound rising from below, the space feels contained — pocket-like, a single held breath at the top of a tower of effort. You could cross it in a minute. You could stand at its center and see all four walls. There is a door — one door, sealed, its mechanism frozen into immobility by the cold, its handle sheathed in frost that has never been gripped. Whether the door opens to a corridor, a shaft, an exterior ledge above the abyss of thinning air, is impossible to say. It does not open. It is part of the wall now.

Nothing moves. Nothing changes. The light is on. The sound roars up from below. The frost holds its shape. The pale plants stand in their scattered solitude. The dust lies where it settled. The cold is total. The room is complete.

It is a finished thing — not abandoned, not ruined, not waiting. Finished. A place that achieved its final state long ago and has been holding it ever since, suspended in the thin cold air at the top of a machine that does not sleep, does not know it is alone, and does not need to.
