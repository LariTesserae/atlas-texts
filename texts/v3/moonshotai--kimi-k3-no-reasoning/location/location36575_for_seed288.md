# The Hollow Cities of the Root-Vault

## First Impression

You descend through a crack in the world — a fissure no wider than a doorway, its edges worn smooth by what must be a hundred thousand years of patient wind. The descent is long. Stairs of packed clay spiral downward, and then the ceiling opens away above you, and you are standing at the lip of a cavern so vast that its far wall is a rumour, a suggestion of verticality lost in brown half-light.

The air hits you first. Cool. Dry as paper. It tastes of minerals — iron, chalk, something faintly bitter like old aspirin. Your lips begin to crack within the hour. There is no water here. There has not been water here for longer than the word *water* has meant anything to anything living in this place.

And yet it is alive. Densely, improbably, roaringly alive.

---

## The Terrain

The cavern floor — if you can call something this large a *floor*, as though it were a room — stretches for perhaps forty kilometres in its longest axis. It is not flat, not exactly, but it is *settled*. This is a landscape that has finished becoming. Every hill has slumped to its final angle of repose. Every ridge has been ground to a smooth whale-back curve. The valleys are shallow, wide, gentle — the kind of valleys that rivers make and then abandon, and then ten million years of stillness rounds off whatever edges remained.

The ground itself is a composite: ancient clay, compacted to something approaching ceramic hardness, overlaid with a thin skin of mineral dust — gypsum, halite, traces of something coppery that gives the whole surface a faint warm undertone, like the colour of weak tea. In places the dust has drifted into low dunes, but even these are old, stabilized, their surfaces armoured by a crust of crystalline salts that crunch underfoot like frozen snow.

Scattered across this terrain — sparse, deliberate, with vast breathing room between them — are the cities.

---

## The Cities

They are not built. Not in any way that involves tools, or plans, or hands. But they are *constructed* — organized, layered, infrastructural — and they are unmistakably the product of intelligence, even if that intelligence is distributed across ten thousand bodies and operates on the timescale of centuries.

They are made by the **vault-trees**.

A vault-tree begins as a root — a single, questing filament that pushes down through the cavern ceiling from whatever sunlit world exists above. It finds no water. It finds mineral dust and cool dry air and, crucially, it finds the **breath-fields**: vast colonies of chemotrophic bacteria that live in the clay, metabolizing sulphur compounds and exhaling, as waste, a slow trickle of organic molecules. The root taps this. It feeds. It grows.

And then it does something no tree should do. It *builds*.

The root thickens, lignifies, and begins to extrude structural tissue — not wood exactly, but a dense, calcified biopolymer, pale grey, hard as concrete. It shapes this material into walls. Chambers. Corridors. Vaulted ceilings that arc overhead in smooth paraboloid curves, each one a perfect load-bearing arch, grown rather than raised. The tree constructs rooms the way a body grows organs: because the blueprint is in it, because the shape serves a function, because over a million years of evolution the trees that built better rooms survived and the trees that built worse ones did not.

A mature vault-tree city covers perhaps half a square kilometre. It rises three, four, sometimes five storeys — though "storeys" implies human architecture, and this is not that. The chambers flow into one another organically, connected by passages that widen and narrow according to traffic patterns established over centuries. Some rooms are vast and echoing, their walls ribbed with structural buttresses. Others are small, cell-like, barely large enough for a single occupant. There are granary chambers where the tree stores concentrated nutrient paste in waxy alcoves. There are ventilation towers — hollow columns that rise to the cavern ceiling and draw cool air downward through passive convection. There are chambers whose function is not obvious, whose walls are smooth and bare, which seem to exist for no reason other than that the tree grew them.

Between the chambers, the tree's living tissue runs like plumbing — thick vascular bundles embedded in the walls, pulsing slowly, imperceptibly, moving nutrients from the breath-fields below to the growing tips above. The walls are warm to the touch. If you press your ear against them, you can hear a faint, slow *throb* — the tree's equivalent of a heartbeat, cycling once every few minutes.

There are perhaps two dozen of these cities scattered across the cavern floor. Between them: nothing. Open mineral plain, dry and silent and empty, stretching for kilometres. The emptiness is not absence. It is *spacing*. The trees need room. Their root systems extend far beyond their built footprint, and two trees too close together will compete for the same breath-field, and both will suffer. So they keep their distance, and the result is a landscape of isolated urban islands in a sea of rust-coloured dust.

---

## The Light

There is no sun here. The light comes from the cities themselves.

The vault-trees are bioluminescent — faintly, grudgingly, as though light were an afterthought, a waste product of some deeper metabolic process. Their walls glow with a dim amber phosphorescence, brightest along the vascular bundles, fading to near-darkness in the spaces between. The effect is like walking through a city lit by dying embers: everything is visible, but nothing is *illuminated*. Shadows are not sharp; they are gradients, slow transitions from barely-lit to barely-dark.

Outside the cities, on the open plain, the light fades to almost nothing. The cavern ceiling — a hundred metres overhead, sometimes more — is invisible. You know it is there because the air changes, because sound behaves differently, because occasionally a droplet of mineral-rich condensation falls from the darkness above and shatters on the hardpan with a sound like a small bell.

The overall effect is a landscape perpetually at dusk. Not the dramatic dusk of sunset, with its reds and golds, but the flat, exhausted dusk of a world that has forgotten what brightness is.

---

## The Sound

And here is the thing that undoes you.

It is *loud*.

Not loud the way a waterfall is loud, or a city street. Loud the way a *body* is loud, if you could hear it from inside. The cavern is a resonating chamber, and everything in it makes noise, and the noise does not dissipate — it *accumulates*, bouncing off the hard clay walls, reflecting from the ceiling, layering upon itself until the air itself seems to vibrate.

The vault-trees are the primary source. Their vascular systems, pumping nutrient fluid through channels that narrow and widen and branch, produce a deep, continuous **hum** — a bass note so low you feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Each tree hums at a slightly different frequency, and where the sound of two trees overlaps, the interference patterns create slow, pulsing beats — a *wah-wah-wah* that rises and falls over the course of minutes, like the breathing of something enormous.

But the trees are not alone. The fauna — and there is *so much* fauna — add their own layers to the cacophony.

---

## The Fauna

They are everywhere, and they are loud, and they are strange in the way that things are strange when they have evolved in isolation for a very long time — not alien, not fantastical, just *deeply themselves*, committed to forms and behaviours that make perfect sense here and would make no sense anywhere else.

The **vault-runners** are the most visible: quadrupedal, roughly the size of a large dog, with elongated limbs and broad, padded feet that let them move silently across the hardpan — when they choose to. They often do not choose to. They are social animals, living in packs of twenty or thirty within the vault-tree cities, and they communicate through a repertoire of clicks, hoots, and a distinctive *rattle-hiss* that they produce by vibrating a specialized membrane in their throats. A pack of vault-runners in full voice is deafening — a wall of sound that fills every chamber and corridor, that seems to come from everywhere at once, that serves no obvious purpose beyond the assertion: *we are here, we are here, we are here.*

They are not predators. Nothing here is, exactly — the food web is built on the vault-trees, and the vault-trees are built on the breath-fields, and the breath-fields are built on the slow chemistry of sulphur and stone. The vault-runners eat the tree's fruit — dense, waxy pods that grow in clusters along the chamber walls, rich in lipids and faintly sweet. They are messy eaters. The floors of the feeding chambers are littered with discarded husks, and the husks are colonized by smaller creatures — **husk-mites**, translucent arthropods the size of a thumbnail, which in turn are hunted by **crevice-wings**, bat-like creatures with membranous wings and faces like folded paper, which navigate the dark corridors by echolocation and add their own high-pitched *chitter-chitter-chitter* to the ambient roar.

On the open plain, between cities, the fauna is sparser but no less present. **Dust-crawlers** — armoured, segmented creatures the length of a man's arm — move slowly across the hardpan, grazing on the thin biofilm of bacteria that coats the mineral surface. They are ancient animals, slow-metabolizing, patient. Some of them may be centuries old. They make a sound as they move: a low, rhythmic *scrape-scrape-scrape* of chitin against clay, audible from surprising distances in the thin dry air.

And then there are the **bell-moths**. Enormous — wingspans of a metre or more — they drift through the cavern in loose, scattered swarms, their wings producing a soft, resonant *tonk* with each downbeat, like someone striking a clay pot. A swarm of bell-moths passing overhead is an event: the sound builds gradually, a chorus of soft percussive notes, each one slightly out of phase with the others, creating a shimmering, aleatoric music that fills the cavern and then, just as gradually, fades.

---

## The Ecology

The whole system runs on sulphur. The breath-fields — those vast bacterial colonies in the clay — oxidize sulphur compounds and produce, as a byproduct, a steady trickle of organic carbon. The vault-trees tap this. The fauna eat the trees. The smaller fauna eat the larger fauna's waste. The bacteria eat everything, eventually. It is a closed loop, nearly perfect, and it has been running for a very, very long time.

How long? The erosion tells you. The cavern itself is old — the smooth walls, the settled floor, the absence of any sharp edges or recent collapses. But the *ecosystem* is old too. The vault-trees are not pioneering species. They are the final, stable form of a succession that played out over millions of years. They are what this place looks like when it has finished experimenting and settled into its answer.

The dynamism here is slow. The trees grow — but slowly, adding a new chamber perhaps once a decade. The vault-runners breed, and their populations fluctuate, but the fluctuations are gentle, bounded, self-correcting. The dust-crawlers inch across the plain. The bell-moths drift. The breath-fields exhale. The cavern endures.

Nothing is in a hurry. Nothing needs to be.

---

## The Rules of This World

Water is a memory. The chemistry of life here is not aqueous — or rather, it is *minimally* aqueous, relying on trace moisture extracted from the air and from the metabolic processes of the breath-fields. The vault-trees are masters of water economy: their tissues are waxy, sealed, their vascular systems closed loops that lose almost nothing to evaporation. The fauna have similar adaptations — the vault-runners produce no urine, excreting nitrogen as a dry paste; the bell-moths have spiracles that open only during flight, when the airflow justifies the moisture loss.

There is no rain. There is no weather, in the surface sense. The cavern maintains its own microclimate — cool, dry, stable — and the only variation is the slow, seasonal pulse of the breath-fields, which are slightly more active during whatever passes for summer on the surface above, when the temperature gradient between the cavern and the outside world drives marginally stronger convection through the ventilation towers.

The cities are not permanent. Even here, in this most settled of landscapes, things end. A vault-tree lives for perhaps ten thousand years. When it dies — when its root system exhausts the local breath-field, or when some slow accumulation of metabolic damage finally overwhelms its repair mechanisms — the city does not collapse. It *desiccates*. The walls dry, shrink, crack. The bioluminescence fades. The chambers fill with dust. The fauna move on, migrating across the open plain to the next living city, a journey that may take weeks.

And then the dead city stands — a hollow, silent ruin, its architecture intact but its life gone — for another ten thousand years, until the slow chemistry of the cavern breaks it down and returns its minerals to the clay.

There are dead cities on the plain. You can see them from the living ones: darker shapes in the dimness, their walls unlit, their chambers empty. They are not mourned. They are not avoided. They are simply *there*, part of the landscape, the way mountains are part of a landscape — features, not tragedies.

---

## Standing in It

You are standing in a chamber near the top of a living vault-tree city. The walls glow amber around you. The hum of the tree's vascular system vibrates in your chest. Somewhere below, a pack of vault-runners is in full voice, their rattle-hiss echoing up through the corridors. A bell-moth drifts past the window — there is no glass, just an opening, a smooth-walled aperture shaped by the tree's growth — and its wings go *tonk... tonk... tonk* as it passes.

The air is cool and dry. Your lips are cracked. Your eyes have adjusted to the dimness, and you can see, through the window, the open plain stretching away — the rust-coloured hardpan, the distant glow of the next city, kilometres away, a smudge of amber in the brown dark.

The sound is enormous. The light is almost nothing. The life is everywhere.

This place has been here for a very long time. It will be here for a very long time after you leave. It does not need you. It does not notice you. It is complete.
