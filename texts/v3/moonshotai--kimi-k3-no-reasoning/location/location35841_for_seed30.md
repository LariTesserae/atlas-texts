The plateau holds its breath at the edge of becoming.

You stand on a skin of basalt so new it hasn't decided whether to be rock or glass. It rings faintly underfoot—hollow, waiting, as if the mountain beneath is still assembling itself atom by atom, and you are walking on the crust of a thought not yet fully thought. Every step sends minute vibrations through the ground, and somewhere below, something answers.

The air is warm and saturated, pressing against your skin with the weight of amniotic fluid. Clouds move through you here, not above you. They condense on the incomplete stone in droplets that do not fall but *rise*, wicking upward along vertical seams, defying gravity's patient instructions. The rock weeps in reverse. It is learning to hold water, practicing for an ocean that has not yet arrived.

Scale collapses here. What feels like a cathedral of stone is perhaps only a room—a chamber of new geology no larger than a village, bounded by walls that were magma last week. The edges are visible. You could walk to them in minutes if the ground did not shift subtly beneath your feet, rearranging itself, testing configurations. This is landscape as rough draft, as clay on a wheel still spinning.

What grows here does not grow in the usual sense. Sparse filaments—iridescent, no thicker than spider-silk—stretch between the unfinished stones, humming with a frequency that makes your teeth ache. They are not roots, not quite. They are the mountain's nervous system, still bootstrapping, teaching itself sensation. When you touch one, it learns the shape of your finger and remembers it. The vegetation, if it is vegetation, is distributed intelligence, waking up slowly across this high, wet place.

And there are structures. Small ones. A lattice of carbon-silicate no taller than your knee, arranged in spirals that suggest both mathematical inevitability and abandoned play. They were built by something that understood stress tensors and aesthetic delight in equal measure—or perhaps they *grew*, secreted by the same process that is secreting the mountain. They contain rooms the size of fists. Something lives in them.

The life here is dense despite the emptiness, abundant in the negative space. Creatures move through the dimness in pulses and flows—translucent things that might be the mountain's antibodies, or its dreams. They are the size of dogs, the size of birds, the size of insects, and they move with the coordinated purpose of a single organism testing its own reflexes. When they pass, they emit sounds that are not sound: pressure-waves, chemical cascades, the sharp tang of ozone and possibility. The loudness is informational, not acoustic. The air itself is shouting.

Light falls strangely. It is dim, yes, but not from absence—from *processing*. The plateau is converting photons into something else, something the new stone requires. Shadows here are active sites of transformation. Where light touches ground, you can see the surface brighten, learn, adapt. Where shadow pools, structures emerge: crystalline outcroppings that were not there an hour ago, growing with the quiet urgency of code compiling.

The whole place hums at the edge of stability. Rain falls upward. Stone softens and reforms. Creatures flicker between states. The built structures disassemble themselves and reassemble in more efficient configurations while you watch. Everything is in motion, everything is wet, everything is trying to become more real.

You understand, standing there, that you are present at an origin. This place will be a world someday—a mountain range, a watershed, an ecosystem. For now, it is a rumor of itself, a promise whispered in stone and vapor and the strange arithmetic of things not yet bound by physics as you know it.

It does not need you. But it notices you. And in noticing, it learns.
