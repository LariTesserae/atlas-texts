# The One Who Tends the Hollow

The place knows your name before you speak it.

This is the first thing you understand, though it takes you weeks to recognize it as understanding. It happens the fourth time you descend through the gap—your body has learned the path by then, knows which roots to grip, which handholds will hold your weight. You are climbing down through the root-woven throat of the entrance, and you feel it: a subtle shift in the phosphorescence around you, a dimming and brightening in a particular pattern. Three pulses, a pause, then two more. You have seen this pattern before, but only now do you understand it as *address*. The hollow is greeting you. Or rather, someone in the hollow is greeting you, using the light the way you might use a wave or a nod.

You stop climbing. You wait. The pattern repeats: three pulses, pause, two pulses. Slowly, carefully, you reach out and touch the root beside you. You trace your finger along its surface—three quick taps, a pause, two more taps. The light around you flares bright for just a moment, then settles back to its usual dim glow.

*Yes*, it seems to say. *Yes, that's right.*

You descend the rest of the way into the hollow, and you are not alone.

---

Her name was Vess, once, though she does not use it anymore. Names are for the world above, and she has not been part of that world for years now. But when you meet her—or rather, when she finally allows you to see her fully, after weeks of glimpses and suggestions—she tells you the name, and you can use it if you wish. She says it slowly, as if the word has grown strange in her mouth through disuse.

You see her first as movement in your peripheral vision: a pale shape slipping between root-curtains, there and gone. Then as a disturbance in the water: ripples that suggest passage but show no source. Then, gradually, as a presence that becomes more consistent. She is always somewhere in the hollow when you descend. You begin to recognize the signs of her proximity—a particular quality in the air, a faint scent like fresh-turned earth and something else, something sharper, almost mineral. The fauna reacts to her presence: the small clicking things grow quieter when she is near, the larger shapes in the water move with more purpose, less randomness.

The first time you see her clearly is by accident. You have been exploring a chamber you have not visited before, a small alcove where the roots have grown in a spiral pattern, creating a space barely large enough to crouch in. You are examining the carved symbols on the roots—old marks, older than the others you have found—when you turn and she is there, crouched at the alcove's entrance, watching you.

She is pale. This is your first coherent thought. Her skin is the color of cave fish, of things that live in darkness: almost translucent, with a faint blue-grey tint. Her eyes are large, the pupils enormous, reflecting the dim phosphorescence like pools of black water. Her hair is dark but shot through with pale strands—not grey, but actually *translucent*, as if some of her hair has begun to convert into something root-like, something that belongs to this place more than to her original form.

She wears clothing, but it is strange: woven from the hanging mosses, from the translucent floating plants, from materials you cannot identify. It looks grown rather than made, integrated with her body in ways that blur the distinction between clothing and skin, between her and the hollow.

She watches you for a long moment. You do not move. Something about her stillness suggests that movement would be wrong, that this is a test, a moment of evaluation. Then she raises one hand—her fingers are long, the nails dark and thick, almost like claws—and makes a gesture. Three taps in the air, a pause, two more taps. The greeting pattern.

You respond the same way, tapping the pattern on the root beside you.

She smiles. Her teeth are very white in the darkness.

---

She does not speak often, and when she does, her voice is rough, as if the mechanism of speech has grown stiff through disuse. But she understands you when you talk, and she has her own ways of communicating: through touch, through gesture, through the light-patterns that she can somehow influence or direct.

Over the course of many visits—she does not live here always, you realize, but she returns here, again and again, and she has made this place her responsibility—you begin to piece together her story.

She found the hollow the same way you did: by falling. This was seven years ago, maybe eight. She does not count time the way you do anymore. She was running from something in the world above—she does not say what, and you do not ask—and she fell through the gap, and she survived the fall, and she stayed.

The first year was the hardest. She almost died several times: from cold, from drinking water that was contaminated with something that made her feverish and delirious, from a wound inflicted by one of the larger creatures that hunt in the deep pools. But she learned. She learned which roots were safe to eat (some of them are, when prepared correctly, though she warns you that this knowledge took her months of trial and error to acquire, and you should not experiment on your own). She learned which pools held clean water. She learned the patterns of the fauna: when they hunted, when they slept, which ones were dangerous and which were merely strange.

And she learned the place itself.

---

She shows you things. On your visits, she guides you through the hollow, pointing out details you would have missed: a root that has grown in a perfect spiral, a pool where the water flows *uphill* for reasons she cannot explain, an alcove where the phosphorescence forms patterns that might be writing in a language no one remembers.

She shows you the work she has done. The water channels were not entirely natural, you learn. She has modified them, expanded them, created new ones. She has built the platforms—some of them, anyway. Others were here before she came, built by whoever tended this place before her. She has repaired the old ones, built new ones, created a network of dry spaces where she can sleep, store food, shelter from the dripping water.

She shows you the markers she has made: small arrangements of stones and bones that serve as maps, as reminders, as communication with whoever might come after her. She is documenting the hollow, she explains, in her rough voice. She is trying to understand it, to learn its rules, to become part of its system in a way that is sustainable, that does not deplete or damage.

*I am learning to tend it*, she tells you. The word "tend" carries weight. Not to own, not to control, not even to inhabit in the way you might inhabit a house. To *tend*. To care for. To maintain. To be part of.

---

She has changed in her years here. You see it in ways both subtle and obvious.

Her body has adapted. Her eyes have grown larger, more sensitive to the dim light. Her skin has become paler, more translucent. She moves differently than she must have once—quieter, more fluid, her balance perfect on the slick roots and in the shallow water. She can hold her breath for a very long time. She can see in darkness that is nearly absolute.

But the changes are not just physical. She thinks differently, she tells you. Time has become strange for her. The world above—the world of seasons and sun and wind—feels distant, unreal. Her memories of that life have begun to fade, to lose their sharpness. She remembers faces but not always names. She remembers events but not always their order.

*I am becoming part of this place*, she says, and you cannot tell if this is a statement of loss or of acceptance, or both.

She shows you her hands. The skin on her palms and fingers has grown thick, almost bark-like. When she presses her hand against certain roots, you can see a faint exchange happening: a pulsing of light, a subtle movement in the root itself. She is *connected*, in some way you do not fully understand. The hollow has accepted her, or she has accepted it, or both.

---

The fauna knows her. This becomes clear as you spend more time with her.

The small creatures—the clicking things, the translucent swimmers, the eyeless worms—do not flee from her the way they flee from you. Some of them approach her, crawl on her hands and arms. She feeds some of them: small offerings of root-tissue, of the pale floating plants. In return, they seem to... help her, somehow. They bring her things: small bones, interesting stones, once a piece of worked flint that must have come from the world above, carried down through the gap somehow.

The larger creatures treat her with respect. You see this clearly one day when you are with her near one of the deep pools. Something large moves beneath the surface—you can feel its presence, sense its size. You tense, preparing to move back, but she touches your arm. *Wait*, she mouths. She crouches at the pool's edge and extends her hand into the water.

The surface ripples. Something rises: a head, pale and eyeless, with jaws that part to reveal rows of needle-teeth. It is a fish, or something like a fish, but it is as long as your arm, and its body is translucent enough that you can see the shadows of its internal organs. It rises until its head is level with her hand, and she touches it gently, running her fingers along the smooth surface of its skull.

The creature—she calls them *deep-swimmers*, and this one she has named *Patience* for its habit of waiting motionless in the pools—allows her touch. It stays for a moment, then sinks back into the water and disappears.

*We have an agreement*, she tells you later. *I do not hunt them. They do not hunt me. We share the water.*

---

She is not entirely alone. There are others who come to the hollow, though rarely. She speaks of them obliquely: a woman who came down three winters ago and stayed for a season, learning from her before climbing back out. A man who fell through the gap and was injured, whom she nursed back to health before he left. A child—this story she tells reluctantly, and only once—who came down and did not want to leave, who stayed for almost a year before she finally convinced them to return to the surface, to the life they still had a chance to live.

*This place is not for everyone*, she says. *It takes something from you. It changes you. You have to be willing to let go of what you were.*

She has made her choice. She has let go. But she recognizes that not everyone should make that same choice, and she acts as a kind of guardian, a gatekeeper. Those who fall through the gap and are injured, she helps. Those who fall through and are lost, she guides. Those who fall through and want to stay, she evaluates carefully, watching to see if they understand what staying means.

---

The strangeness of the place does not disturb her the way it disturbs you. When the roots move, she adjusts. When the light pulses in patterns, she reads them—or tries to. When the water rises and falls in ways that defy weather and logic, she accepts it.

*This place is alive*, she tells you. *Not just the plants and animals. The place itself. The whole system. It thinks, in its way. It responds. It remembers.*

She has learned to communicate with it, in limited ways. The light-patterns are one method. Touch is another: she can press her hands against certain roots and *feel* something, a presence, an awareness. Sometimes she receives impressions, images, feelings. Not language, exactly, but meaning.

*It knows we're here*, she says. *It knows I tend it. It accepts that. Maybe it even appreciates it, in its way.*

You ask her what it wants, this place, this system, this awareness.

She is quiet for a long time. Then: *To continue. To grow. To become more itself. The same thing everything wants.*

---

She shows you the deepest parts of the hollow, the places where she rarely goes. There is a chamber where the water is warm—actually warm, not just less cold—and where the phosphorescence is bright enough to read by. There is a corridor where the roots have grown so densely that you must crawl, and where the air is thick with spores that make you dizzy and fill your vision with strange patterns. There is an alcove where the carved symbols are so old they have been almost entirely absorbed into the root-tissue, visible only as faint variations in texture.

*These were here before me*, she says. *This place has been tended for a long time. I am not the first.*

She does not know who came before. The markers they left are too old, too weathered. The carved symbols are in a language she cannot read. But she feels their presence sometimes, their influence. She is part of a lineage, she believes, a succession of tenders stretching back further than she can trace.

*Maybe it goes all the way back*, she says. *Back to the beginning. Maybe this place has always had someone to tend it.*

---

You visit her many times. You learn from her: which roots to eat, which water to drink, how to move quietly, how to read the light-patterns. But you do not stay. You have a life above, connections you are not ready to release, and she understands this. She does not try to convince you to stay, though you sense that she would welcome it if you chose to.

On your last visit—you know it is your last, though you do not say this aloud—she gives you a gift. It is a small object: a piece of root, carved and polished, shaped into a form that is almost but not quite recognizable. It might be a seed, or an egg, or a heart. It is warm to the touch, and it pulses very faintly with its own phosphorescence.

*To remember*, she says. *When you are above, and this place feels far away. To remember that it is here, and that it continues.*

You want to ask her if she is happy, if she regrets her choice, if she misses the world above. But you do not ask, because you can see the answer in her face, in her movements, in the way she has become part of this place. Happy is not the right word, perhaps. But she is *complete*. She has found her place, her purpose, her work.

She tends the hollow. The hollow accepts her tending. And in that exchange, in that relationship, something essential is maintained: a small pocket of life in the darkness, complete and contained, continuing.

---

You climb back out through the gap, into the grey world above. The light hurts your eyes. The air feels thin, dry, empty of the dense moisture you have grown accustomed to. You stand at the edge of the gap and look down, but you can see nothing: just darkness, just the suggestion of depth.

You hold the carved root in your hand. It is still warm. It still pulses faintly.

You think of her, down there in the darkness, moving through her root-woven world. You think of the deep-swimmers, the clicking things, the translucent plants. You think of the water dripping, the roots growing, the whole slow dance of life continuing in the dark.

And you know that she is there, tending, maintaining, being part of something larger than herself. You know that the hollow is in good hands.

You turn and walk away, back into the world of light and wind and seasons. But you carry the memory of the hollow with you. You carry the memory of her.

And sometimes, at night, when the world above is quiet, you hold the carved root and you think you can feel it: a faint pulse, a distant rhythm, the slow heartbeat of the place she tends.

The place that continues.

The place that becomes.

The place that remembers.

The place that is, in its own way, *aware*.

And she is part of it now, woven into its fabric like one of its own roots, like one of its own creatures, like one of its own slow, patient processes of growth and change.

She is the tender of the hollow.

And the hollow, in turn, tends her.
