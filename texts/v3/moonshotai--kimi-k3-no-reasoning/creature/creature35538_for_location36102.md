The cavern does not have residents. It has **inhabitants**, in the most literal and biological sense of the word: it is inhabited, the way a body is inhabited by its own processes. The people of the Sump Districts are not the city's occupants. They are its cells. They are the labor of its metabolism.

To understand who lives here, you must first abandon the idea of the individual. The Sump Districts do not produce individuals. They produce **roles**. They produce **functions**. The cavern's overwhelming, layered hum is a language, and the people are the words. They are the sound of a hammer on metal, the whisper of a pressure gauge, the chuff of a pump. Their lives are measured in the same units as the machines they tend: furnace cycles, pump cycles, the slow, patient drip of water from a specific stalactite onto a specific metal plate.

They are a population of specialists, each so deeply embedded in their task that the line between person and process blurs into irrelevance. The city does not have a population. It has a **workforce**. It has a **crew**. It has a **maintenance schedule**. The people are the parts of the machine that are warm and soft and bleed.

### The Foundry-Called

In the lowest, hottest levels, where the air shimmers and the brick sweats black moisture, live the Foundry-Called. They are the oldest inhabitants, not in age, but in proximity to the city's origin. They tend the cupola furnaces, the city's fiery, iron-gutted hearts. Their skin is a permanent, deep bronze from the heat and the coal dust, their hair singed to a brittle curl. They move with a slow, gravitational certainty, their bodies accustomed to the weight of the heat. They communicate in a language of nods and glances, their voices too valuable to waste on the humid air. They are the city's alchemists, its high priests of transformation. They do not cast metal; they coax it, they persuade it from one state to another. Their hands are calloused into something closer to hoof than flesh, capable of judging the temperature of molten iron by the color of its glow, a skill that is not learned but absorbed through the pores over a lifetime.

They are not born. They are **called**. A child who shows an unnatural stillness, a preternatural focus, an immunity to the heat's dizzying press, is taken from the stacked housing modules and brought to the foundries. They are apprenticed not to a master, but to the furnace itself. Their life becomes a cycle of charge, melt, pour, cool. Their identity is subsumed into the process. They have names, but they are rarely used. They are known by their furnace, by their shift, by the specific, irreplaceable role they play in the city's fiery metabolism.

### The Machine-Listened

Above the foundries, in the middle terraces, are the Machine-Listened. They are the machinists, the millwrights, the keepers of the lathes and the line shafts. Where the Foundry-Called are still and heavy, the Machine-Listened are all taut, focused energy. They are the surgeons of the Sump Districts, their workspaces pools of intense, articulated light in the amber gloom. They wear tight-fitting leather aprons and goggles that magnify their eyes into the wide, unblinking orbs of nocturnal creatures. Their world is one of tolerances measured in thousandths of an inch, of the high, singing whine of a cutting tool biting into steel.

They do not hear the city's hum as a single sound. They hear it as a symphony of individual instruments, each with its own pitch, its own rhythm, its own state of health. They can diagnose a worn bearing by the subtle flattening of a whine, a misaligned shaft by the faint, rhythmic shudder it imparts to the floor plates. They are the city's auditory nerve, its sense of touch. Their hands are stained with oil and metal filings, their fingernails permanently black-rimmed. They are quieter than the Foundry-Called, their communication reduced to a series of hand signals, of chalk marks on slate, of the shared, unspoken understanding of a blueprint. They are the city's intellect, its problem-solvers, the ones who translate raw power into precise, useful work.

### The Water-Wives and Sump-Husbands

And then there are the ones who tend the city's circulatory system. They are the Water-Wives and Sump-Husbands, the keepers of the pumps, the gutters, the thousand trickling channels that make up the district's web of water. They are the most numerous, and the least visible. They are the city's kidneys, its lymphatic system, its slow, patient, indispensable pulse. They move through the city's arteries with a quiet, unceasing diligence, clearing blockages, patching leaks, ensuring the slow, black water keeps moving.

Their tools are not hammers and wrenches, but long, hooked poles, buckets of thick, black sealant, and bundles of absorbent rags. Their clothes are perpetually damp, their boots caked in the fine, grey silt of the sumps. They are a practical, pragmatic people, their humor as dry as their clothes are wet. They are the city's realists, the ones who understand that the city's greatest enemy is also its most essential medium. They do not fight the water. They negotiate with it. They are the diplomats of the Sump Districts, the ones who maintain the fragile, necessary truce between the built and the born.

### The Unseen Hand

Who built this? The question hangs in the humid air, unanswered. The people of the Sump Districts do not ask it. It is a question for philosophers, for men with dry clothes and soft hands. The city's logic is too perfect, its systems too integrated for its apparent youth. This is the work of a single, guiding intelligence, a Planner with a capital P. The Planner is not a person. The Planner is a set of blueprints, a book of standards, a ghost in the machine. The Planner is the reason the voltage is consistent, the pipe threads match, the drainage works.

The people of the Sump Districts are the Planner's hands. They are the ones who translate the perfect, abstract logic of the blueprint into the messy, physical reality of brick and iron and sweat. They are the ones who make the city work, despite the heat, despite the damp, despite the slow, patient, corrosive drip of the water. They are the ones who have built a civilization in a cave, not out of choice, but out of necessity. They are the ones who have made a home in the belly of a machine.

### The Children of the Drip

The children are the most startling. They are not born in the residential modules. They are born in the alcoves, in the recessed corners where the sump overflow has created a permanently wet, warm microclimate. They are born among the pale, translucent ferns, the only green thing in the entire district. They are small, quiet, and unnaturally still. They do not cry. Their eyes are wide and dark, adapted to the perpetual twilight. They learn to walk on the slick, uneven surfaces of the cavern floor, their balance as perfect as a cat's.

They are the city's future, its next generation of cells. They are the ones who will be called to the foundries, listened to by the machines, taught the ways of the water. They are the ones who will carry the city upward, ever upward, toward the ceiling, toward the source of the drip, toward the void. They are the ones who will build the next layer, and the next, and the next, until the cavern is full, until the city is a solid, seamless block of brick and iron and human endeavor.

They are the Sump Districts. They are the heat, the water, the light, the sound. They are the city's slow, patient, inexorable becoming. They are the ones who are born into it, who are assigned to it, who find themselves in it after a series of decisions they did not fully understand at the time. They are the ones who stay. They are the ones who become. They are the ones who are, in the end, the city itself.
