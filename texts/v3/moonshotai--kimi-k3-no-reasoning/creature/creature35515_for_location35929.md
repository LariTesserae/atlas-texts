# The People of the Weeping Stone

They call themselves nothing that could be translated into sound. Their name for themselves is a gesture: both palms pressed flat against wet ground, held there for a long moment, then lifted slowly. It means something like *we are of the seep* — we are what rises from below, what gathers in the hollow places, what stays.

If you saw one from a distance — which you would not, because they would know you were coming long before you arrived, the vibrations of your footsteps traveling through the saturated peat like signals through a wire — you might take it for a rock. A low, rounded stone, grey-green, moss-patched, sitting at the edge of a pool. This is partly camouflage and partly simply what they look like. Evolution here has not favored distinctness. It has favored blending, patience, the ability to be still for long periods in cold water without losing core heat, and so they are shaped like the landscape they inhabit: low, broad, smooth, mottled, damp.

---

## The Body

An adult stands perhaps a meter and a half tall when fully upright, but they are rarely fully upright. Their natural posture is a forward lean, weight distributed between their legs and their long, thick arms, knuckles resting on the ground or on the tops of their thighs. They are not knuckle-walkers — they walk bipedally, and efficiently, covering ground with a slow, rolling gait that conserves energy — but they default to a crouch, a hunch, a settledness that brings them closer to the ground, closer to the water, closer to the vibrations and the wet that are their primary sources of information.

Their skin is their most immediately striking feature. It is thick, slightly loose, and perpetually moist — not slimy, but wet in the way that a stone pulled from a stream is wet, a dampness that seems intrinsic rather than applied. The color varies from individual to individual and across the body: the base tone is a grey-green that matches the plateau's dominant stone, but it is broken by darker patches (nearly black in some individuals), lighter patches (a pale, lichenous grey), and, in older individuals, a faint rust-colored mottling along the shoulders and the backs of the hands that mirrors the rust-tips of the sedges. Whether this is coincidence or the result of some long symbiosis between their skin microbiome and the sedge chemistry is a question they do not ask, because they do not ask questions in that way. It simply is.

The skin serves multiple functions. It is a sensory organ, rich with mechanoreceptors that detect vibration, pressure, temperature gradients, and the subtle chemical signatures dissolved in the water and the air. It is a respiratory supplement — they breathe primarily through a pair of low-slung nostrils set flat against their broad faces, but they can absorb a meaningful fraction of their oxygen through their skin when submerged, and they are, as a consequence, comfortable in the water in a way that purely air-breathing creatures are not. And it is a social organ: the texture of an individual's skin — its smoothness or roughness, its warmth or coolness, the specific pattern of its moisture — communicates information about health, emotional state, reproductive status, and recent activity that is as rich and nuanced as any spoken language.

Their hands are large relative to their bodies, broad-palmed, with thick, blunt fingers that end not in claws or nails but in dense, slightly flattened pads — something between a fingertip and a hoof, hardened by constant contact with stone and peat and the abrasive fibers of the sedges. These hands are their primary tools. They cut peat with the edge of a palm. They fit stone with a patience that borders on the geological, turning a rock over and over until they find the face that will sit flush against its neighbor. They weave sedge with a dexterity that seems impossible given the apparent clumsiness of the fingers, but the clumsiness is an illusion: the fingers are strong rather than delicate, and their work is strong work, work that requires force and endurance more than precision.

Their faces are broad and flat, with wide-set eyes that are dark — almost black — and large, adapted to the diffuse, shadowless light of the plateau. They do not squint. They do not blink often. Their gaze has a quality of absorption, as if they are taking in the world through their eyes in the same way they take it in through their skin: passively, completely, without the aggressive focus that predators bring to looking. They are not predators. Nothing on the plateau is, really. The food web is too thin for predation to be a viable strategy. They are gatherers, grazers, processors of what the land provides.

Their mouths are wide, thin-lipped, set in a default expression that a human might read as solemn but is simply neutral. They have teeth — flat, grinding teeth suited to the tough sedge stems and the bitter berries and the occasional small crustacean scooped from a pool and eaten whole, crunching between the molars like a mouthful of wet gravel. Their jaws are powerful. Their chewing is slow and rhythmic, and a group of them eating together produces a sound — a soft, wet, grinding murmur — that is one of the few regularly audible sounds in the settlement.

The thrumming organ sits in the upper chest: a hollow, bony chamber that they can resonate by controlling the flow of air across a pair of internal membranes. The sound it produces is felt more than heard — a vibration in the range of 30 to 80 hertz, low enough to travel through the ground and the water with minimal attenuation. This is their long-distance communication. A creature standing at one end of a settlement cluster can send a thrum through the peat that is felt by every other creature in the cluster, and the specific pattern of the thrum — its frequency, its duration, its rhythm — carries meaning. It is not a language in the human sense. It is more like a set of shared signals: *water rising*, *stranger approaching*, *come here*, *I am here*, *all is well*. The last is the most common. Most of what they say to each other, across the humming distances of the plateau, is simply *all is well*.

## The Young

They are born in the pools. Not in the open pools — in the birthing pools, which are specific structures within the settlement clusters: shallow, warm (as warm as anything gets here), lined with a thick mat of sedge fiber and living moss, their water enriched by a slow seep from a thermal spring that exists in only a few locations on the plateau. The thermal springs are sacred, if that word can be applied to a people who have no concept of the sacred as separate from the practical. The birthing pools are sacred the way a hearth is sacred: because life happens there, because it is where things begin.

The young are small — no longer than a forearm — and they are born with their eyes open and their skin already wet-adapted, already mottled, already capable of absorbing oxygen through its surface. They spend their first weeks almost entirely submerged, clinging to their mother's submerged body with hands and feet that are, at this stage, slightly adhesive — a trait they lose as they mature and their skin thickens. The mother stays in the birthing pool with them, sometimes for days at a time, leaving only to eat. Other adults visit, pressing their palms to the water's surface, feeling the vibrations of the young one's movements, learning its particular signature. Every individual has a unique vibrational signature — a specific frequency at which their body resonates when they thrum — and the community learns a newborn's signature within days of its birth. This signature is their name, their identity, their place in the social fabric. It does not change over their lifetime.

Childhood is short by human standards — perhaps five or six years — and it is spent largely in the water and in the passages, learning. They learn the tactile map of the settlement: the passages, the pools, the walls, the channels. They learn to cut peat, to fit stone, to weave sedge. They learn the thrumming patterns and the tactile vocabulary — the hundreds of distinct touches that constitute their primary language. They learn which pools have fish and which do not, which shrubs bear the bitter berries and when, which channels are likely to flood during the condensation season and which are safe.

They learn all of this without instruction, in the human sense. There are no lessons. There is simply presence: the young ones are with the adults, constantly, and they absorb what the adults do the way their skin absorbs moisture — passively, completely, without the need for formal transmission. A child watches an adult weave a sedge wall for a season, and then, one day, the child weaves. Not perfectly, at first. But adequately. And the adult presses a palm to the child's shoulder — *good* — and the child continues.

## The Old

They age slowly, and they age visibly. The rust-mottling spreads. The skin loosens further, developing deep folds around the joints and along the spine. The eyes, always dark, develop a faint cloudiness — not blindness, but a softening of focus that mirrors the softening of everything else. They move more slowly. They spend more time in the pools, submerged to the neck, their bodies buoyed and supported by the dark water.

The old ones are the keepers of the tactile map. Every individual carries the map in their body, but the old ones carry it most deeply, most completely, with the weight of decades of accumulated sensation. They know not just where things are but where things *were* — where a pool used to be before it silted in, where a channel ran before the water changed its course, where a wall stood before it died back and was regrown. They are the memory of the settlement, and they are consulted — not formally, not through any structured process, but simply by presence. A creature considering where to cut peat this season will sit beside an old one in a pool, pressing palm to palm, and the old one will produce a pattern of touches that conveys: *here, not there; the peat is deeper here; the water will rise there*. This is not advice. It is knowledge, transmitted through the skin.

When they die, they die in the pools. This is not a choice or a ritual; it is simply what happens. An old one will submerge one day and not surface. The body is left where it lies. The water and the microorganisms and the slow chemistry of the peat will do what they do, and within a season the body will be unrecognizable, and within a decade it will be peat itself, and within a century it will be part of the ground that the next generation presses their palms against, learning the map.

There is no mourning. There is acknowledgment: the community thrums, once, in a pattern that means *this one's signature is gone from the ground*. And then the water rises and falls, and the sedges grow, and the wind blows, and the absence becomes part of the map.

## The Work

They work constantly, but their work does not look like work to an outside observer. It looks like sitting, standing, walking slowly, touching things. It looks like nothing. But it is the constant, ongoing maintenance of a settlement that is alive and therefore always in the process of dying, always in the process of growing, always in need of attention.

The sedge walls require daily tending. Not every wall, every day — the settlement is too large for that — but every wall, regularly. A creature walks a passage, trailing a palm along the woven sedge, feeling for weak spots: a section where the living sedge has died back, a place where the weave has loosened, a patch where moss or lichen has colonized and begun to break down the fibers. They repair as they go, tucking in new stems, pressing living shoots into gaps, redirecting a seep channel to water a section that is drying out. The work is meditative, unhurried, and it is done in silence or in the low company of thrums and touches.

The peat-cutting is seasonal. When the water is at its lowest — the dry season, the relative dry — they move out into the open meadow, to the peat banks that they have been cutting for generations. The cutting is done with the edge of the hand, a downward strike that shears through the dense, wet fibrous mat. The blocks are roughly rectangular, roughly uniform, and they are carried back to the settlement on broad, flat backs — a creature can carry three or four blocks at a time, stacked between their shoulders, held in place by the slight adhesive quality of their wet skin. The cut banks are left to recover. The sedges recolonize within a few seasons. The peat itself regenerates more slowly — a millimeter a year — but the plateau is vast, and the banks are numerous, and the cutting is done with a restraint that has been shaped by centuries of practice: take what is needed, leave what is not, let the land close over the wound.

The stone-fitting is the most visible work, the work that most resembles construction in the human sense. The plateau's boulders are their quarry — not the large, half-sunken ones, which are left alone (they are landmarks, they are part of the map, they are not to be disturbed), but the smaller stones that litter the meadow: head-sized, torso-sized, flat-sided and rounded and angular and every shape that erosion produces. They select stones with an eye that is not an eye but a hand: they run their palms over the surfaces, feeling for the faces that will sit flush, the angles that will lock. They carry the stones to the building site — a new wall, a repair, an extension of an existing structure — and they fit them, one by one, in the dry-stone tradition that requires no mortar, no adhesive, nothing but the weight and the shape and the patience.

A well-fitted stone wall will stand for decades without maintenance. But it will not stand forever. The freeze-thaw cycle works on it, the water seeps into its gaps, the moss colonizes its surfaces and pushes the stones apart with the slow, irresistible force of growth. And so the stone walls, like the sedge walls, are tended: inspected, adjusted, repaired, rebuilt. Nothing here is finished. Everything is ongoing.

The water management is the most complex work, and the most invisible. The settlement's channels — hundreds of them, threading between the structures, connecting the pools, directing the seep — are a hydraulic system of remarkable sophistication, all the more remarkable for having been built without tools, without measurements, without any of the apparatus that human engineering requires. It was built by feel, by generations of creatures pressing their palms to the ground and sensing where the water wants to go, and then helping it get there. A channel is dug with hands, lined with flat stones, its gradient adjusted by the placement of a single pebble here, a single block of peat there. The water is directed to pools that need filling, away from walls that need to stay dry, through the settlement in patterns that serve both practical purposes (drainage, irrigation of the living walls, maintenance of the birthing pools) and social ones (the pools are gathering places, the channels are thoroughfares, the seep-walls are sites of tactile communication — a creature can press its palm to a wet wall and feel the vibrations of the entire settlement transmitted through the water-saturated stone).

## The Social Fabric

They live in clusters — the dense knots of structure and passage and pool that dot the plateau — but the clusters are not villages in the human sense. They have no governance, no hierarchy, no leadership. Decisions are made by consensus, and consensus is reached not through discussion but through a kind of distributed tactile sensing that is difficult to describe in human terms.

When a decision needs to be made — when the water is rising and the community must decide whether to reinforce the walls or abandon a low-lying section of the cluster, when the peat banks are thinning and new ones must be located, when a child is born with a condition that will require extra care — the community gathers. Not in a meeting, not in a designated space, but simply: they come together, drawn by the thrumming, and they touch. They form a dense knot of bodies, palms pressed to palms, shoulders to shoulders, backs to backs, and they stay that way for a long time — hours, sometimes — while the tactile information flows through the network of their touching bodies.

What emerges from this process is not a decision in the human sense. It is a convergence. The community, through the slow exchange of touch and vibration, arrives at a shared understanding of what must be done, and then they do it. There is no dissent, because there is no debate. There is no persuasion, because there is no rhetoric. There is only the slow, patient accumulation of shared sensation, and the equally slow, patient arrival at a state of agreement that is less like a vote and more like the way water finds its level: not because any individual molecule decides to be level, but because the system as a whole tends toward equilibrium.

Their relationships are tactile. Pair bonds exist — creatures who spend more time together than with others, who share a sleeping platform, who raise young together — but the bonds are not exclusive, and they are not permanent. They form and dissolve with the same slow fluidity as everything else on the plateau. A pair bond might last a season, a decade, a lifetime. The community does not track them or regulate them. What matters is not the bond but the network: the web of tactile connections that links every individual to every other individual in the cluster, directly or through intermediaries.

They touch constantly. This cannot be overstated. In the narrow passages, they brush against each other as they pass, a brief palm-to-shoulder contact that is both acknowledgment and information exchange. In the pools, they sit in groups, their bodies in contact, their hands linked or resting on each other's backs. During sleep — they sleep in clusters, on the raised platforms that are woven from sedge and lined with moss — they pile together, a tangle of limbs and torsos, their breathing synchronized, their skin exchanging moisture and warmth and the subtle chemical signals that maintain the social bond.

To a human observer, this constant physical contact might seem overwhelming, even suffocating. To them, isolation is the suffocation. A creature separated from the network — cut off from the tactile flow, unable to feel the vibrations of the community through the ground — experiences something akin to what a human experiences in solitary confinement: a slow, disorienting unraveling of the self. They do not have a word for loneliness. They have a touch for it: a palm pressed flat against bare stone, held there, feeling nothing. It is the only touch in their vocabulary that means something bad.

## The Food

They eat what the plateau provides, and the plateau provides little. Their diet is sparse, monotonous, and sufficient — a word that describes everything about their existence.

The sedges are the staple. Not the blades, which are too tough and fibrous even for their powerful jaws, but the rhizomes: the thick, starchy underground stems that the sedges use to store energy. These are dug from the peat with the hands, washed in a pool, and chewed slowly. They taste like nothing — like wet starch, like the inside of a raw potato, like the plateau itself. They are nutritious enough to sustain life, and they are abundant, and that is enough.

The berries come from the low shrubs that crouch in the lee of the boulders. They are small — the size of a pea — and dark, almost black, and bitter in a way that puckers the mouth and tightens the throat. They are eaten in season, gathered by hand, stripped from the branches in a single sweeping motion that strips leaves and all. The leaves are chewed and swallowed along with the berries; the whole mass is digested slowly, and the bitterness, over time, becomes something the palate expects and even desires. A creature who has not eaten berries in a long time will seek them out with a purposefulness that is unusual in a species whose default mode is patient waiting.

The crustaceans are a supplement. The small, translucent things that flicker through the dark pools — they are scooped up in cupped hands, a quick, darting motion that is the closest thing to speed that these creatures ever display. They are eaten alive, whole, crunching between the flat molars. The pale, eyeless fish are eaten too, when they can be caught, which is not often — the fish are slow but the creatures are slower, and the catching is more luck than skill. A creature standing motionless in a pool, submerged to the waist, hands cupped just below the surface, waiting for a fish to swim into them: this is hunting, on the plateau. It can take hours. It often yields nothing. The creature does not appear to mind.

Water is not drunk. It is absorbed — through the skin, through the lining of the mouth, through the simple fact of being constantly wet. They do not experience thirst. They experience dryness, occasionally, when the condensation season fails or when they have spent too long on the dry upper ridges of the plateau, and the remedy is simply to return to the pools, to submerge, to let the water do what it does.

## The Senses

Their world is not primarily visual. This is perhaps the hardest thing for a human to understand about them, because humans are so thoroughly, so helplessly visual. We build our understanding of the world from light. They build theirs from touch.

Their vision is adequate — they can see well enough to navigate, to recognize individuals, to detect the movement of a bird against the grey sky. But their visual acuity is modest, their color perception limited (they see in shades of grey and green and brown, with a sensitivity to ultraviolet that allows them to detect patterns on stone and lichen that are invisible to human eyes), and their visual attention is diffuse. They do not focus on things the way humans do. They take in the whole visual field at once, passively, without the spotlight of attention that humans direct at specific objects. A creature looking at a pool is not looking at the pool; it is aware of the pool, and of the meadow beyond it, and of the sky above it, and of the walls beside it, all at once, all with equal weight.

Their tactile sense, by contrast, is extraordinary. The mechanoreceptors in their skin are dense enough that they can detect a single raindrop (or condensation-drop) landing on a stone a body-length away. They can feel the vibration of another creature's footsteps through a hundred meters of saturated peat. They can distinguish, by touch alone, between stone types, between living and dead sedge, between water that is flowing and water that is still. Their palms are their most sensitive organs — the skin there is thinner, more densely innervated, and the specific pattern of ridges and whorls on each individual's palms is unique, a tactile fingerprint that they can recognize in each other and that they use, in close contact, as a form of identification more reliable than sight.

Their hearing is secondary to touch but still acute, tuned to the low frequencies that carry through the ground and the water. They hear the wind as a felt pressure more than a sound. They hear each other's thrums as full-body experiences, the vibration entering through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands and the skin of their backs simultaneously. They hear the bird — the large, dark, croaking thing that rides the updrafts — and they recognize its call as a marker of the season: it comes when the condensation is heaviest, when the pools are fullest, and its voice, harsh and solitary, is one of the few sounds on the plateau that could be called loud.

Their sense of smell is moderate, adapted to the limited chemical palette of the plateau: wet stone, peat, sedge, the faint mineral tang of the seep water, the specific organic signatures of individuals (each creature has a distinct scent, determined by their skin microbiome, their diet, their age). They use smell for identification at close range and for assessing the health of the living walls — a wall that is dying has a different smell than a wall that is thriving, a sourness that precedes visible decay.

They have a sense that humans do not: a sensitivity to the water table itself, to the level and movement of the water beneath their feet. This is not a single organ but a distributed capability, arising from the combination of their tactile sensitivity, their skin's ability to detect moisture gradients, and some deeper, less understood sensitivity to the electrical conductivity of the saturated ground. They can feel the water table the way a human feels the temperature of the air: constantly, passively, without effort. They know when it is rising and when it is falling, and they know it before the pools show any visible change. This is the sense that drives their seasonal adjustments, their decisions about where to build and where to cut peat and where to move the sleeping platforms. It is the sense that connects them most directly to the plateau, most intimately to the slow dynamics of the water that is the first thing here, always the first thing.

## The Rhythm of Days

They do not sleep on a strict cycle. The light here is diffuse and the days are long or short depending on the season, and their sleep follows the light only loosely. They sleep when they are tired, which is often — their metabolism is slow, their energy expenditure low, and sleep comes easily and frequently. A typical day involves several periods of sleep, each lasting a few hours, interspersed with periods of activity that are similarly unhurried.

They wake in the pools or on the platforms, and the first thing they do is touch: reach out to the nearest body, the nearest wall, the nearest patch of wet ground, and press a palm against it. This is not greeting. It is orientation. The touch tells them where they are, who is near, what has changed during their sleep. The tactile map updates itself with each contact, and the waking touch is the first entry in the day's record.

The morning (such as it is — the light does not change dramatically, but there is a subtle brightening, a slight warming of the grey) is for the water. They move to the pools, submerge, sit. The water does what it does: cleanses, hydrates, oxygenates, connects. They sit in the pools and they feel the seep rate and the water table and the vibrations of the community waking around them, and this is enough. This is the morning ritual, if ritual is the right word for something so practical, so unselfconscious.

The middle of the day is for work: the tending, the cutting, the fitting, the weaving. This work is distributed across the cluster, each creature doing what needs doing in the area where they find themselves, without assignment or coordination beyond the tactile network's quiet guidance. There is no urgency. There is no deadline. The walls need tending; they tend them. The peat needs cutting; they cut it. The channels need clearing; they clear them. The work is its own rhythm, its own satisfaction.

The evening (a subtle darkening, a slight cooling) is for gathering. They come together in the larger pools, the communal pools, the ones that sit at the centers of the clusters like hearts. They sit in the water and they touch and they thrum, softly, and the thrumming synchronizes: dozens of chest cavities resonating at the same frequency, the same rhythm, a low, deep hum that fills the pool and the ground beneath it and the air above it with a vibration that is almost too low to hear and too deep to ignore. This is the closest thing they have to music, to ceremony, to art. It is also simply what they do when they are together and the day is ending and the water is warm (relatively) and the community is intact.

They eat throughout the day, grazing, chewing slowly, their jaws working in the steady rhythm that is the background sound of the settlement. They do not have meals. They have the constant, low-level intake of a species that lives in a low-energy environment and has adapted by needing less and taking what it needs continuously.

## The Inner Life

This is the hardest part to describe, because it is the part that is most different from human experience, and the tools of human language are poorly suited to it.

They do not think in words. They think in touches, in vibrations, in the felt sense of the water and the ground and the bodies around them. Their inner life is not a monologue but a texture: the constant, quiet awareness of the world as it presses against their skin, as it resonates through their chest cavity, as it seeps into their awareness the way water seeps into peat.

They have memories, but their memories are not narrative. They are spatial and tactile: the memory of a specific pool is the memory of its temperature, its depth, the specific quality of its bottom (silt or stone or peat), the pattern of vibrations it transmitted on a specific day when something significant happened (a birth, a death, a flood). These memories are stored not in a sequential timeline but in a network of associations: this pool connects to that channel connects to that wall connects to that creature's signature connects to that season. The map is the memory, and the memory is the map.

They have emotions, but their emotions are not the sharp, differentiated things that humans experience. They are broad, slow, pervasive: a general sense of rightness when the water is at the correct level and the walls are sound and the community is together; a general sense of unease when the seep rate changes unexpectedly or a familiar vibration is absent from the network; a general sense of warmth (not thermal warmth — emotional warmth, the warmth of connection) when they are in physical contact with others. These emotions do not spike or crash. They rise and fall with the slow rhythms of the plateau, and they are, for the most part, mild. Contentment is their default state, and contentment, for them, is not the absence of negative emotion but the presence of a positive one: the deep, quiet satisfaction of being exactly where you are, doing exactly what you are doing, in a world that is exactly as it should be.

They do not have religion. They do not have philosophy. They do not have art, unless the thrumming counts as art, unless the woven walls count as art, unless the fitted stones count as art — and perhaps they do, perhaps the distinction between art and craft and maintenance is a human distinction that has no meaning here. They do not have stories. They do not have history, beyond the tactile memory of the old ones and the physical record of the settlement itself (this wall is older than that wall; this channel was dug before that pool silted in; this boulder has always been here).

They do not wonder about the stars. They see them, on the clear nights, sharp and numerous in the thin air, and they register them as part of the visual field, part of the world, part of what is. They do not ask what they are. They do not ask why they are. The asking of *why* is a human compulsion, born of a visual, narrative, temporal way of being that these creatures do not share. They are not curious. They are attentive. The difference is subtle but fundamental: curiosity seeks what is not there; attention receives what is.

## The Stranger

Occasionally — very occasionally, perhaps once in a generation — a creature from one cluster encounters a creature from another cluster. The plateau is vast, and the clusters are separated by open meadow and pool-fields that can take days to cross, and most creatures never leave their cluster's immediate territory. But some do, driven by the slow expansion of the settlement or the search for new peat banks or the simple, rare impulse to move.

When two strangers meet on the open meadow, the encounter is cautious, tactile, and brief. They approach each other slowly, stopping frequently to press palms to the ground, reading each other's vibrations. When they are close enough, they extend their hands — palms up, the universal gesture of openness — and they touch. The touch is long and thorough: palm to palm, then palm to forearm, then palm to shoulder, then palm to chest, where the thrumming organ sits. Each touch conveys information: health, identity, cluster of origin, recent experience. The exchange takes perhaps an hour, and when it is complete, each creature knows the other in a way that is more intimate than most human relationships ever achieve.

Then they part. Or, occasionally, one follows the other back to their cluster, and is absorbed into the community with the same slow, tactile process: touching, being touched, learning the map, being learned by the map. Integration takes a season. After that, the stranger is no longer a stranger, and the community is slightly larger, slightly different, slightly more itself.

## The Long View

They will not last forever. The plateau is eroding, and in the deep future — a future so deep that even the old ones cannot feel its approach — the water will win. The pools will drain. The peat will dry. The sedges will die. The creatures will adapt or migrate or die, and the plateau will become a valley, and the valley will become something else, and the slow granular surrender of stone to time will continue, as it always has, as it always will.

But this is not a thought they think. It is not a knowledge they carry. They carry the water level and the season and the map and the network, and these things are enough. They are sufficient. They are, in the deepest sense, complete.

They are the people of the weeping stone, and they are weeping too, in their way — not with grief but with the same slow, patient, persistent seep that brings the water up through the rock and into the pools and into their skin and into the fabric of their lives. They are what the plateau produces when it is left alone for long enough. They are what the water builds when it is given time.

They press their palms to the wet ground, and the ground presses back, and this is all the conversation they need.
