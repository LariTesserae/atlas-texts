The pale thing beneath the bridge was the first one you noticed, but not the first one there.

It has been clinging to that same stone for what might be years, might be decades. Its limbs—seven of them, or nine, the count shifts when you try to hold it steady in your attention—are jointed like a crustacean's but move like something testing whether the joints still work, slowly, one at a time, as though each articulation requires individual consideration. Its body is the color of the mineral crusts, that same iridescent not-quite-white, and when it is still it could be mistaken for a deposit, an accretion, a formation of the wet stone. When it moves it does not hurry. It has never needed to hurry. It has no memory of needing to hurry.

This is one of them. One of the things that live here.

---

They are not numerous. This is the first thing to understand. You could walk through Vhir for days and see none, or see one, or see the evidence of one—the displaced stone in the plaza, the sudden absence of a crust where a crust had been thickening for months—and know only that something had passed through, or was present, or had been present recently enough that its presence still lingered in the configuration of the space.

They are not a population. They are not a species in any way that implies breeding, or dying, or the passage of generations. They are simply the ones that are here. They have always been here, or they have been here for as long as the city has, or longer—the distinction is difficult to make and perhaps does not matter. They do not seem to age. They do not seem to change. They are constants, like the heat, like the wet, like the pump-rhythm in the stone.

The one beneath the bridge is the largest you have seen. Most are smaller—much smaller, small enough to fit in a cupped hand, small enough to disappear between the pipes without disturbing the condensation that coats them. These smaller ones are quicker, though quick is a relative term in Vhir. They move in darts and pauses, a sudden repositioning from one shadowless place to another, always at the edge of vision, always when attention is elsewhere. You never see the movement itself, only the before and after. They were here. Now they are there. The interval between is not something they allow you to witness.

What they are is difficult to say. They are not machines, though they have a quality that recalls the machines—an economy of motion, a patience, an indifference to purpose. They are not animals in any familiar sense, though they are clearly alive, or clearly something that the word alive only partially covers. They do not eat. They do not breathe in any way that can be detected. They do not interact with each other, or with the city, or with anything except the rock and the water and the slow accumulation of the crusts.

---

The crusts are where they are most often found, if found is the right word. The iridescent films that thicken on the wettest surfaces, along the weeping walls, around the pump outflows—these are not their food, exactly, and not their habitat, exactly, but there is a relationship. Where the crusts are thickest, the creatures are most likely to be present. Where a crust has been disturbed—scraped, broken, cleared—the creatures will sometimes appear in the days that follow, motionless, positioned at the edge of the disturbance, as though observing the absence.

Whether they create the crusts or merely prefer them is not known. Whether the crusts are a byproduct of their presence or a precondition for it is not known. The two things go together, that is all. The creatures and the crusts. The crusts and the creatures. In the oldest parts of the city, down in the carved chambers near the southeastern edge where the buried lake presses closest to the surface, the crusts are thick enough to obscure the shape of the stone beneath them, and the creatures are there in numbers that could almost be called crowds—dozens of them, perhaps more, clinging to the walls and ceilings in the wet heat, so still and so perfectly camouflaged that you might stand among them for an hour before realizing that the room is full.

They are not hiding. They do not know how to hide, because they have never needed to hide. They are simply still, and their stillness is so complete, so absolute, that the eye slides over them without catching. They are the color of the crusts. They are the texture of the crusts. They are, in some way that is difficult to articulate, continuous with the crusts, as though they are not separate organisms but expressions of the same process, the same slow accumulation that coats the walls and thickens over time and has no discernible purpose beyond its own continuation.

---

The larger ones are rarer. The one beneath the bridge is perhaps the size of a man's torso, though its shape is not easily compared to anything that has a torso. It is radial in some ways, bilateral in others, symmetrical in a pattern that seems to shift depending on the angle from which you observe it. Its limbs are not legs and not arms but something in between, appendages that it uses to cling to the stone and to reposition itself when repositioning is required, which is not often.

It has been on that bridge for a long time. Long enough that the crust has grown up around the points where its limbs meet the stone, anchoring it, incorporating it into the architecture of the surface. It could leave if it wanted to—you have seen it move, seen it shift its position with that slow, deliberate, limb-by-limb patience—but it does not seem to want to. It is content there, or something that functions like contentment. It is in its place. It is where it is.

The larger ones are always near water, or near the places where water is closest to the surface. The southeastern edge, where the vapor columns rise and the heat is most intense, has several of them. They cluster around the oldest structures, the carved chambers, the deep foundations, positioned at the edges of doorways and in the corners of rooms, sometimes on the walls, sometimes on the ceilings, sometimes on the floor in the shallow film of condensation that never quite dries. They do not move among the chambers. They do not explore. Each one seems to have its location, its particular configuration of stone and moisture and crust, and it remains there, year after year, in a stillness that is not sleep and not waiting but simply being.

---

What they perceive is unknown. They have no visible eyes, no obvious sensory organs, no features that suggest how they take in the world. But they respond to certain things. They respond to disturbance in the crusts—if you break a section of the iridescent film, the nearest creature will shift, slowly, in the hours that follow, repositioning itself to face the breach, as though attending to it. They respond to the pump-rhythm, or to changes in the pump-rhythm—when the tempo shifts, as it sometimes does, all at once and without warning, the creatures become briefly more active, their limbs articulating in sequences that might be communication or might be something else entirely, a physiological adjustment to the new vibration in the stone.

They do not respond to human presence. This is perhaps the strangest thing about them. They do not flee. They do not hide. They do not approach. They do not alter their behavior in any way that suggests awareness of being observed. You can stand directly in front of one of the larger ones, close enough to touch it, and it will not react. It will not freeze more completely—it is already as frozen as it is possible to be. It will not move toward you or away. It will simply continue to be what it is, in the place where it is, and your presence will be as irrelevant to it as the presence of the stone, the water, the dim light, the slow passage of time.

This indifference is not hostile. It is not fearful. It is not even, exactly, indifference, because indifference implies a choice to ignore, and these creatures do not seem capable of choosing anything in the way that choosing is usually understood. They are not ignoring you. They are not aware of you in a way that would make ignoring possible. They are simply existing, according to their own principles, in their own mode, and you are not part of that mode, and the fact of your existence does not register in whatever passes for their experience.

---

The smallest ones—the darting shapes between the pipes—are more difficult to observe. They are fast, as fast as anything in Vhir moves, and they are wary, though wary in a way that seems instinctive rather than learned. They do not like to be looked at directly. They prefer the periphery, the corner of the eye, the moment when attention has shifted elsewhere. You will see them most often as a flicker, a repositioning, a small pale shape that was in one place and is now in another, without having crossed the intervening space in any way you could witness.

They are more numerous than the larger ones, or they seem to be. It is hard to count things that do not hold still, that do not allow themselves to be counted. But there are many of them, or at least several, scattered through the city in the places where the pipes are densest and the condensation is most constant. They cling to the undersides of the pipes, to the joints and flanges, to the brackets that hold the pipes against the walls. They are always near water. They are always near warmth. They are always, in some way that is difficult to define, near the city's circulatory system, as though they are drawn to the flow, to the movement of the water through the pipes, to the vibration of the pumps that drives it.

Whether they feed on something in the water—some mineral, some microorganism, some quality of the heat itself—is not known. They do not seem to eat in any observable way. They have no mouths, no openings, no features that suggest consumption. But they are alive, or they are something that functions like alive, and aliveness requires sustenance of some kind, and the only sustenance available in Vhir is the water and the heat and the mineral crusts, and the creatures are always near these things, and so the assumption is that they derive something from them, in some way that is not visible, at some pace that is not measurable.

---

The crusts themselves may be alive. This is a possibility that is difficult to rule out. They grow, or they accumulate, or they increase in a way that suggests growth. They spread. They thicken. They change color slowly over time, shifting through iridescent patterns that have no obvious cause. They respond to damage by healing, filling in breaches over days or weeks, restoring themselves to their previous configuration. Whether this is biology or chemistry or something in between is not clear. The boundary between living and non-living is blurry in Vhir, as blurry as the edges of things in the dim light, as indistinct as the horizon in the haze.

The creatures and the crusts are part of the same system. This much seems certain. They are connected, interdependent, expressions of the same underlying process. The city pumps water from the buried lake, and the water spreads through the pipes and condenses on the walls, and the crusts grow where the water is, and the creatures gather where the crusts are, and the whole arrangement continues, cycle after cycle, without purpose or product or discernible end. The creatures are not separate from this. They are not inhabitants of the city in the way that people are inhabitants of cities. They are part of the city's functioning, or part of its non-functioning, part of the slow, steady, purposeless continuation that defines the place.

---

There are no dead creatures. This is worth noting. You have never found a body, a husk, a shell, a carcass. You have never found evidence of one dying, of one ceasing to be. They do not seem to die, or if they die they do so in a way that leaves no trace, or they are replaced, or they are not individual organisms at all but temporary configurations of some larger process, formations that arise and dissolve the way the crusts arise and dissolve, the way the condensation forms and drips and forms again.

The one beneath the bridge has been there for as long as you have been aware of it. It may have been there for decades, for centuries, for longer than the bridge itself. It shows no signs of age, no wear, no deterioration. Its limbs articulate as smoothly now as they did when you first observed it. Its color is unchanged. Its position is nearly unchanged—slightly shifted, perhaps, over the months, repositioned by millimeters, but essentially the same. It is a constant. It is a fixture. It is as permanent as anything in Vhir, which is to say it is very permanent indeed.

---

Whether there are people in Vhir remains an open question. But the creatures are here. This is not in doubt. They are the city's inhabitants, its population, its complement of living things. They are few, and they are strange, and they are indifferent to observation, but they are present, and they have always been present, and they will continue to be present, clinging to the wet stone, positioned among the crusts, moving slowly or not moving at all, existing in their own mode, according to their own principles, in the dim light and the wet heat and the slow, rhythmic tremor of the pumps.

They are what lives in Vhir. They are what has always lived in Vhir. The city was built for them, or around them, or in ignorance of them, and they have persisted through all the layers of construction, through the carved chambers and the dressed stone and the rusted metal and the prefabricated panels, unchanged, unchanging, as constant as the heat and the water and the dim, sourceless light.

They are the pulse beneath the pump-rhythm. They are the stillness in the empty plazas. They are the slow, patient, iridescent life of a city that has no other life, that needs no other life, that continues and continues and continues, warm and wet and dim and quiet, in the haze, on the benches, above the buried lake, forever.
