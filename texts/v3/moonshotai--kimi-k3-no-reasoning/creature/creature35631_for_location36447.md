They are small things, mostly. They have to be.

**The chasm fills itself with plants, and the plants are one thing, and the one thing has no room in its body for anything that does not photosynthesize.** What lives here lives in the margins — in the thin spaces between leaf and light, in the brief warm pockets where the organism's metabolism bleeds heat into the bitter air, in the microsecond gaps between one surge and the next. They are stowaways in a green cathedral that was not built for them and does not know they are there.

## The Cup-Drinkers

The pale salamander is not a fluke. It is a species — the chasm's most common vertebrate, which is to say you might find one every few days if you know where to look.

They live in the Gatherer pools.

Each one is the length of a forearm, translucent-skinned, with a lateral line so overdeveloped it has become a kind of second spine — a pale ridge running from jaw to tail-tip that shivers constantly, reading the water. They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. The light in a Gatherer pool is so total, so enveloping, that vision would be redundant — they *are* inside the light, saturated by it, and they navigate by its pressure and its warmth the way a fish navigates by current.

They are not salamanders, not exactly. They are something the chasm has made — or permitted — a vertebrate body plan stretched thin and flattened, ribs visible through skin like the veins of a leaf. Their hearts beat slowly. Their blood, if you could see it, is faintly green — not from chlorophyll but from some dissolved pigment they absorb from the water, a byproduct of the chasm's own chemistry. They are cold-blooded, but the Gatherer pools never freeze, and so they persist: motionless for hours, drifting in the leaf-cup's center, their lateral lines trembling, reading the chasm's hum the way you might listen to a distant conversation through a wall.

They eat — what? Almost nothing. The pools are too pure, too clean, too devoid of organic debris. They subsist on the microscopic — single-celled organisms that drift in from the moss-floor, carried by condensation and upward rain. They filter-feed in slow motion, their gill-fronds pulsing once every few seconds, extracting from the water a nutrition so thin it barely qualifies as food. They grow slowly. They live long. A cup-drinker might spend its entire life in a single Gatherer pool, never leaving, never needing to, the pool refilling with each thin rain, the light constant, the temperature stable.

When a surge buries their pool — and surges do, eventually, bury everything — they do not flee. They cannot. The pool collapses, the leaf is crushed, the water drains into the moss, and the cup-drinker is left on the sodden vegetal floor, gasping, translucent skin drying in the cold air. Most die within minutes. The chasm absorbs them. Some — a few — are close enough to another Gatherer to make the crossing: a desperate, wriggling crawl across open moss, their lateral lines screaming with the unfamiliar sensation of open air, their bodies leaving a thin, wet trail that the moss begins absorbing even as they move. If they reach another pool, they slip in, and the water closes over them, and the light envelops them, and they are home.

You find one once in the act of crossing — a pale, writhing shape on the moss, no bigger than your hand, moving with a desperation that is almost mammalian. You kneel. You watch. You do not help it. The chasm has rules, and you are not sure you are allowed to interfere. It reaches the Gatherer's edge and hauls itself over the lip and drops into the water with a sound like a small stone dropped in a still pond. It vanishes into the green-gold light. The pool ripples once and is still.

You write in your journal: *The cup-drinkers are the chasm's conscience. They are what the chasm would be if it could feel: small, cold, dependent, alive.*

## The Paper Moths

The moth you saw on the upper tiers is not alone. There are more of them here, in the deep tiers, where the vine-curtains hang thickest and the light is most concentrated. They drift between the vines like ash on a windless day, their wings — white, thin, almost translucent — catching the golden light and scattering it into brief, pale rainbows.

They are not moths. They are not insects. They are something the chasm has produced — a fruiting body, perhaps, or a spore-dispersal mechanism — that happens to look like an animal. Their wings are not chitin but a thin, papery membrane of dried vegetal matter, shed from the vine-tips in seasonal cycles you cannot predict. They have no mouthparts. No digestive tract. No legs. They are wings and a central body-mass — a small, dense kernel of concentrated nutrients, packed with the chasm's own biochemistry — and they drift, and drift, and drift, until they find a suitable site on the moss-floor, and they land, and the kernel splits, and new growth erupts.

They are seeds. Flying seeds. The chasm's way of colonizing new territory without waiting for the slow creep of moss-runners and root-threads.

But they behave like animals. They steer. They avoid obstacles. They cluster in the warm updrafts near the tier-edges, where the rising metabolic heat of the chasm creates thin, invisible currents of air. They cluster in groups — dozens, sometimes hundreds — their white wings overlapping like scales, like a flock of birds at rest. And when they fly, they fly with intent: slow, deliberate, each wingbeat a deliberate stroke, as if they are thinking about flying rather than simply doing it.

You watch a cluster of them once for an hour. They are resting on a vine-curtain, wings folded, their pale bodies almost invisible against the green. A surge begins on a distant tier — a wave of growth, miles away, visible only as a brightening of the light and a deep, resonant groan. The paper moths stir. Their wings open. They lift, one by one, and drift toward the surge — toward the fresh growth, the new territory, the uncolonized moss. They are drawn to it the way moths are drawn to flame, except the flame is green and the moths are seeds and the whole thing is a slow, patient, vegetable act of reproduction that has been going on for longer than you can comprehend.

One lands on your shoulder. It is weightless. Its wings brush your cheek, dry and thin as tissue paper. You hold still. It stays for a moment — a second, perhaps two — and then lifts, and drifts away, and is gone.

You write in your journal: *The paper moths are the chasm's ambition. They are what the chasm would be if it could want: light, drifting, purposeful, alive.*

## The Root-Tenders

Deeper. Past the eightieth tier. Past the point where the chasm narrows into a throat, where the vines hang so thick they form a solid green wall, where the hum is so loud it vibrates in your bones.

Here, in the warm, compressed heart of the chasm, there are things that move.

You do not see them clearly. You see their effects: channels in the moss-floor that were not there yesterday, straight and deep, as if cut by a blade. Vine-curtains parted and re-draped, their segments rearranged into new configurations. Gatherer leaves tilted at angles that serve no obvious photosynthetic purpose — tilted toward each other, their cupped pools aligned so that the water in one drains into the next, forming a chain, a cascade, a deliberate system of water management in a place where water is the most precious thing.

Something is gardening the garden.

You catch a glimpse once — once, and never again. A shape, low to the ground, moving between the vine-curtains with a fluid, deliberate motion. It is the size of a large dog, or a small deer, but the proportions are wrong: too many limbs, or limbs in the wrong places, a body that seems to flatten and elongate as it moves, conforming to the spaces between the vines like water conforming to a vessel. Its skin — if it has skin — is dark green, almost black, textured like the moss it moves through. It does not glow. It is the only thing in the chasm that does not glow.

It sees you. You are certain of this. It pauses — a hesitation so brief you might have imagined it — and then it is gone, slipping between the vines, gone, and the vines sway behind it and are still.

You do not follow. You cannot follow. The vine-curtain is too thick, the space too narrow, and the thing — whatever it is — has moved through it as easily as you move through air.

You find its marks later. A channel in the moss-floor, freshly cut, the edges clean and sharp. Inside the channel, arranged with a precision that cannot be accidental, are small piles of debris: fragments of dead vine-tissue, pieces of shed paper-moth wings, the translucent shed skin of a cup-drinker. Arranged. Sorted. Organized. Not by the chasm — the chasm absorbs its waste, does not sort it. By something else. Something that lives here, in the deep tiers, and tends the chasm the way a gardener tends a plot: pruning, clearing, organizing, maintaining.

You do not know what it is. You do not know if it is one thing or many. You do not know if it is native to the chasm or if it came from somewhere else — from the surface, perhaps, or from some deeper, older place that the chasm has grown over but not entirely consumed.

You write in your journal: *The root-tenders are the chasm's mystery. They are what the chasm would be if it could choose: deliberate, hidden, purposeful, alive.*

## The People Who Stayed

The symbols. The circle-and-line. The tally marks. The wall on the seventh tier, the fire-pit, the woven basket, the drained Gatherer leaf.

Someone came here before you. Someone descended, tier by tier, marking their way, and they did not come back.

You find their last camp on what you estimate to be the ninety-second tier — if your counting is right, if the tiers have not surged and reformed since you last counted. It is not much. A cleared space in the moss, larger than the wall-enclosure on the seventh tier, large enough for two or three people to lie down. A fire-pit, long cold, the charcoal fragments so old they crumble at a touch. A pile of stones — not a wall this time, but a cairn, deliberate, stacked, the kind of marker that says *I was here* or *I stopped here* or *this is as far as I go*.

And beside the cairn, carved into the moss-floor itself — a process that must have taken days, given the density of the compacted vegetal matter — is a message.

It is not in any language you recognize. The characters are angular, precise, deeply incised, filled with green moss that has grown into the cuts but not yet obscured them. They are not decorative. They are not symbolic. They are *writing* — a sentence, perhaps, or a paragraph, carved by someone who knew they would not be going back and wanted to leave something behind.

You copy it into your journal, character by character, your hand shaking with cold and exhaustion. You do not know what it says. You may never know. But the act of copying it feels important — feels like the most important thing you have done since entering the chasm — because it is proof that someone else was here, someone else descended, someone else reached this depth and stopped and chose to leave a mark.

You sit beside the cairn for a long time. The light pulses around you, gold and green, warm and cold. The hum vibrates in your chest. The moss beneath you is soft and faintly warm. You are very tired. You are very far from the surface. You are very small.

You think about the people who stayed. Who built a wall on the seventh tier and a fire-pit and a cairn on the ninety-second. Who carved symbols into the moss and tally marks into the vines and a message into the floor. Who descended and descended and descended and then stopped.

You think about why they stopped.

You think about whether you will stop.

You add your own tally mark to the vine beside the cairn — the twenty-fifth mark, your mark, pressed into the tough green skin with the tip of your knife. The vine pulses beneath your hand. The light brightens slightly, as if in acknowledgment. Or curiosity. Or nothing.

You shoulder your pack. You descend.

## The Thing at the Bottom

You do not reach the bottom. You are certain of this now, with a certainty that settles into your bones like the cold. The chasm does not end. It narrows, it compresses, it becomes a throat and then a vein and then a capillary, but it does not end. The tiers continue. The light continues. The green continues.

But on the hundredth tier — or the hundred-and-tenth, or the hundred-and-twentieth; you have stopped counting — you find something that is not green.

It is a chamber. A hollow in the moss-floor, roughly circular, perhaps thirty feet across, its walls smooth and dark and *not moss*. The moss stops at the chamber's edge as if cut by a knife, a clean line between the living green and the bare, dark stone beneath. The stone is grey — true grey, the grey of the surface plateau, the grey of a world without light, without growth, without the chasm's relentless vegetal abundance.

Inside the chamber, the light is different. Dim. Amber. The bioluminescence of the chasm does not penetrate here — the moss-walls do not glow, the air does not shimmer. The light comes from a single source: a pool of water at the chamber's center, small and still and dark, and at the bottom of the water — visible through the clear, cold depths — is a light.

Not the chasm's light. Not gold, not green. White. Pure white, steady, unwavering, like a star. It does not pulse. It does not fluctuate. It does not respond to your presence, your movement, your voice. It simply *is* — a fixed point of light in a place of endless change, a constant in a landscape of becoming.

You kneel at the water's edge. The stone is cold beneath your knees — truly cold, not the chasm's metabolic warmth but the honest, dead cold of rock. The water is still. The light at the bottom is still. The chamber is still.

You reach into the water. It is freezing — colder than the Gatherer pools, colder than the thin rain, cold enough to make your hand ache. You reach deeper. Your fingers touch the light.

It is not a stone. It is not a crystal. It is not a living thing. It is — you do not know what it is. It is smooth and hard and warm — warm, impossibly warm, in the cold water, in the cold chamber, in the cold chasm. It fits in your palm. It is the size of a bird's egg. It glows with a light that does not flicker, does not dim, does not change.

You pull it from the water. The chamber does not react. The chasm does not react. The light in your hand is steady and white and warm, and it is the only thing in this entire vast, green, growing, indifferent world that does not change.

You hold it. You look at it. You do not understand it.

You put it in your pocket.

You stand. You leave the chamber. The moss resumes at the edge, green and glowing, as if the chamber does not exist, as if the stone and the water and the white light are a secret the chasm is keeping from itself.

You descend.

The light in your pocket is warm against your thigh. The chasm pulses around you, green and gold, growing and changing, endlessly becoming.

You are very small.

The chasm is very large.

And somewhere in the green, in the light, in the endless, patient, furious growth, there is a white light that does not change, and it is the only thing you have found in this place that feels like an answer.

You do not know the question.

You descend.
