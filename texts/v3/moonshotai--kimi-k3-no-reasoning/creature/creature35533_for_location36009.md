# The Voices of the Fall

## The Resonant

They were not always humanoid. Or perhaps they were, and the Plummet made them something else. The distinction has been lost to them, worn smooth like the scalloped walls.

The Resonant—as they call themselves in the third tone of their whistled speech, the one that means *those who answer when the chasm speaks*—are tall, elongated people, their limbs stretched by a lifetime of climbing and reaching across voids. Their average height is two meters, but they carry almost no bulk; they are built like rope, all tensile strength and flexibility, with musculature that lies flat and long against the bone. Their fingers are extraordinary: six digits on each hand, the outermost pair opposable, each fingertip broad and flat and textured with ridges that allow them to grip wet root-fiber and slick basalt with equal confidence. They have no fingernails. The ridges serve better.

Their skin is the color of limestone in deep water—a pale, mottled grey-green, neither attractive nor ugly but perfectly calibrated to disappear against the chasm walls. It is thick, almost waxy, and it does not wrinkle in the perpetual moisture. Water beads on it and slides off, as if they are oiled. In truth, they secrete a lipid compound from subcutaneous glands that makes them nearly waterproof. They never seem wet because they *cannot* be wet, not in the way surface-dwellers understand it.

Their faces are broad and flat, with wide-set eyes that are disproportionately large, the irises a translucent amber shot through with threads of bioluminescent blue. These threads glow faintly in darkness—an adaptation, some say, to living among the glowing thalloids, though the Resonant themselves claim the light was always there, that they carry a fragment of the Plummet's own luminescence in their bodies. Their ears are small, almost vestigial on the outside, but the internal structures are vastly complex: the cochlea elongated, coiled tighter than any surface human's, capable of parsing the overwhelming soundscape of the Plummet into discrete layers of meaning. They can hear a moss-skater's footfall at fifty meters through a curtain of falling water. They can distinguish the pitch of a filter-whale's song from the resonant hum of a bridge under tension. Their hearing is so acute that silence—for the rare few who have been taken to the surface—causes physical pain, a ringing absence they describe as *being deafened by nothing*.

They have no hair on their heads. Instead, a crest of fine, hollow filaments runs from forehead to the base of the skull, each filament perhaps fifteen centimeters long, pale and translucent. These are not decorative. They are resonance organs, sensitive to vibration in the air, and they fan or flatten in response to sound. A Resonant in conversation does not nod; their crest ripples. A Resonant in danger flattens their crest tight against the skull, reducing their profile and damping their sensitivity. In moments of deep emotion—grief, ecstasy, religious fervor—the crest fans wide and trembles, and other Resonant nearby can feel the vibration in their own crests, a sympathetic echo that binds them together.

Their voices are their most remarkable feature. They possess a double larynx—a primary vocal structure for producing sound in the normal range, and a secondary syrinx-like organ, positioned lower in the throat, that can generate and modulate ultrasonic frequencies. This allows them to speak two languages simultaneously: the **Low Tongue**, a melodic, whistled language that operates in the audible range and carries through the chasm's roar, and the **High Speech**, which exists above human hearing and functions almost like sonar—a way of mapping space, locating objects, communicating in whispers across voids that would swallow audible sound. A Resonant having a conversation is, at any moment, producing two streams of meaning, one braided over the other. They think in stereo.

They dress in woven thalloid fiber—tight-fitting, layered garments that flex with their movements and shed water. The weaving is intricate, geometric, each pattern encoding information: the wearer's settlement, their guild, their lineage, their standing in the harmonic hierarchy. To read a Resonant's clothing is to read their biography. The colors are muted—greys, pale blues, bone-white, deep greens—dyed with pigments extracted from the fungi and lichens that grow on the chasm walls. But woven into the fabric, almost invisible unless the light catches them, are threads of bioluminescent fiber, living strands of thalloid tissue that continue to glow after being woven. In the dimmer reaches of the Plummet, a Resonant's garment becomes a constellation of soft light, tracing the contours of their body in shifting patterns.

They wear no metal. There is no metal in the Plummet. But they adorn themselves with **sound-stones**: small, hollow concretions of mineral deposited by the dripping water, each one naturally resonant, each one producing a unique tone when tapped or blown across. A Resonant might carry a dozen of these on cords around their neck, their waist, their wrists. When they move, the stones click and hum against each other, producing a personal chord—a sound signature as unique as a voice. Children learn to recognize their parents' chords before they learn to recognize faces. Lovers exchange stones, blending their chords. When a Resonant dies, their stones are distributed among their kin, and their chord is slowly unraveled, scattered, until it exists only as fragments in the music of the living.

## The Harmonic Hierarchy

Resonant society is structured by sound. Not metaphorically—literally, rigidly, inescapably.

At the apex of each settlement stands the **First Voice**, the individual whose personal chord most closely matches the resonant frequency of the settlement's particular location in the chasm. This is not an inherited position, nor an elected one. It is discovered. When a First Voice dies—or, more commonly, when their voice begins to change with age, their chord drifting away from the settlement's frequency—a search begins. Every member of the community sounds their chord, one after another, in the great resonating chamber at the settlement's heart, until one is found whose voice makes the walls sing, makes the water-veils shiver, makes the entire living structure of the settlement hum in sympathetic response. That person becomes the First Voice, regardless of age, regardless of prior station. There have been First Voices of eight years old. There have been First Voices who were, before their discovery, the lowest of the fiber-spinners.

The First Voice does not lead in the way a king or a mayor leads. They *tune*. Their role is to maintain the harmonic balance of the settlement—to sound the notes that keep the living bridges supple, the thalloid masses healthy, the water flowing in the aqueducts rather than pooling or spilling. They conduct the great work-songs that coordinate harvests and construction. They stand at the edge of the settlement during the great wind-shifts and sing against the roar, shaping it, damping the dangerous frequencies, amplifying the beneficial ones. A skilled First Voice can prevent a cascade. A careless one can cause one. The position is revered and feared in equal measure.

Beneath the First Voice are the **Chord-Keepers**, a caste of musicians-priests who maintain the settlement's relationship with the Plummet's soundscape. They tend the great instruments—the hollowed-root horns that span entire terraces, the drums of stretched filter-whale membrane that can be felt in the chest from a kilometer away, the water-chimes that hang in the falling streams and produce melodies as the currents shift. The Chord-Keepers are the keepers of the **Sound-Map**: a vast, ever-changing mental model of the chasm's acoustic geography, memorized and passed down through generations. They know which frequencies will calm the filter-whales, which will attract glider-apes, which will cause a thalloid mass to contract defensively. They are the librarians of vibration, and they are treated with a reverence that borders on worship.

The **Root-Tenders** form the largest caste: farmers, cultivators, weavers, builders. They are the hands that persuade the thalloids into bridges and chambers, that harvest the fungi, that spin the fibers and knot the ropes. They work in crews called **strands**, each strand bound together by a shared work-song that coordinates their movements. Watching a strand of Root-Tenders build a bridge is like watching a single organism at work—each person moving in response to the song, reaching, cutting, weaving, knotting, in a choreography that has been refined over centuries.

The **Deep Singers** are the hunters and explorers, the ones who venture into the darker, quieter reaches of the chasm where the settlements thin out and the water grows cold and still. They hunt the pale eels and the larger filter-whales, harvest minerals from the deep walls, and map the unexplored regions below. They are solitary by nature, or perhaps by necessity—the deep reaches do not tolerate noise, and a Deep Singer who cannot control their own resonance does not survive. They are leaner than the other castes, their skin paler, their eyes larger. Their crests are often scarred or partially torn, damaged by the close, pressing silence of the deep. They speak a dialect of the Low Tongue that is barely above a whisper, punctuated by long silences that carry as much meaning as the sounds.

## What They Eat, and How

The Resonant are omnivorous, though their diet is constrained by the Plummet's peculiar ecology.

The staple of their diet is **veil-bread**: a dense, slightly sour dough made from the ground pulp of young thalloid tissue, mixed with powdered mineral from the chasm walls. The pulp is harvested only from thalloid masses that have been identified as dying—a determination made by the Chord-Keepers, who listen for the subtle shift in a mass's resonant frequency that indicates senescence. The pulp is fermented in sealed gourds lined with bioluminescent fungi, which imparts both flavor and a faint, pleasant glow to the finished bread. A fresh loaf of veil-bread, broken open in darkness, emits a soft blue light from its interior, fading over the course of an hour as it is exposed to air. Resonant children are told that eating veil-bread puts the Plummet's light inside them, and many believe this literally well into adulthood.

**Glider-ape meat** is the primary protein—lean, dark, slightly sweet, with a texture like very firm fish. The apes are farmed on the larger thalloid masses, kept in semi-wild troops and culled selectively. Their membranes are dried and used as drumheads and waterproof sheeting; their bones are hollowed for flutes and needles; their tendons are twisted into cordage of extraordinary strength. Nothing is wasted. The Resonant have a word in the High Speech that has no direct translation—it means something like *the obligation of the consumed*, the idea that when you take a life, you incur a debt that must be repaid by using every part of what you have taken with skill and reverence.

**Filter-whale** is a rarer food, reserved for feast days and rituals. A single whale can feed an entire settlement for a week. The hunt is conducted by Deep Singers, who locate the whales by their songs and drive them toward the settlements using carefully modulated sonic pulses. The kill is made by the First Voice, who must sound a specific harmonic that momentarily stuns the whale—a note so precise that if it is even slightly off, the whale panics and thrashes, potentially destroying bridges and aqueducts in its death throes. The meat is rich, oily, almost black, with a flavor that the Resonant describe as *the taste of depth*. The whale's baleen—the vast, flexible filtering plates in its mouth—is the most valuable part: it is cut into strips and used as springs, clamps, and the vibrating reeds in their musical instruments.

They drink the falling water, of course, but they prefer **root-wine**: a fermented beverage made from the sap of the thalloid root-system, tapped at specific nodes where the Chord-Keepers have determined the flow is strongest. Root-wine is mildly alcoholic and mildly hallucinogenic—it contains trace compounds from the thalloid's bioluminescent chemistry, and drinking it causes the drinker to perceive sounds as colors, a synesthesia that lasts several hours. The Resonant consider this a sacred experience, a way of *seeing* the Sound-Map rather than merely hearing it. Root-wine is consumed communally, during the great harmonic festivals, and the visions it produces are discussed and interpreted by the Chord-Keepers as omens and guidance.

## Birth, Death, and the Space Between

A Resonant child is born into sound. The birthing chamber—a small, thickly woven room lined with the softest thalloid fibers—is designed to muffle the chasm's roar to a gentle hum. But the mother, in the final stages of labor, is encouraged to vocalize, to produce a sustained, low tone that the midwives match and harmonize with. The child emerges into a chord, and the first sound it hears is the combined voices of its mother and the midwives, singing a note that has been chosen by the Chord-Keepers as auspicious. This note becomes the child's **birth-tone**, the foundation of their personal chord, and it is whispered into their ear before anything else touches them.

Children are raised communally within each strand. They learn to climb before they learn to walk—the first skill taught to a Resonant infant is the grip, the ability to hold on to a rope or a root with all six fingers, and they practice it for hours each day, hanging from their parents' harnesses, swinging from fiber loops suspended in the living quarters. By the age of three, a Resonant child can traverse a rope bridge alone. By five, they can identify the calls of every common animal in the chasm. By seven, they have begun to develop their personal chord, a process that takes years and is considered the true passage into personhood. A Resonant without a chord is not yet fully a person—they are a *half-sound*, a potential not yet realized.

Death in the Plummet is a matter of frequency. When a Resonant dies, their body is taken to the **Listening Chamber**, a small, perfectly round room at the settlement's lowest point, where the chasm's roar is loudest and most concentrated. The body is laid on a bier of woven root, and the Chord-Keepers gather around it and sing—not a lament, but a *matching*. They attempt to reproduce the deceased's personal chord as precisely as possible, and as they sing, the body is lowered on ropes into the void below the settlement. It falls. It falls for a long time—hours, perhaps days—tumbling slowly through the layers of vegetation and water, descending through the chasm's ecology like a note descending through a scale. The Resonant believe that as the body falls, it passes through every frequency of the Plummet's sound, and that somewhere in that long descent, the body's own residual vibration—the echo of the person's chord, still faintly present in their tissues—finds its match in the chasm's song and is absorbed, integrated, made part of the great voice that speaks through water and stone.

They do not grieve loudly. Grief is a private frequency, sounded in the High Speech, above the range of casual hearing. But if you stand near a grieving Resonant, you can feel it—a subsonic tremor in the air, a distortion in the ambient sound, like a string slightly out of tune. The entire settlement can feel when one of their own is in mourning, and they adjust: they soften their voices, they damp their instruments, they create a pocket of relative quiet around the grieving person, a space in the roar where sorrow can exist without being overwhelmed.

## What They Believe

The Resonant do not have gods, exactly. What they have is the **Chord-That-Is-Not-Sounded**: the idea that the Plummet—all of it, the water, the stone, the vegetation, the animals, the people—is a single instrument, and that the music it produces is one note in a chord too vast to be heard. The chord exists beyond the Plummet, beyond the sky, beyond the range of any ear. It cannot be sounded, because sounding it would require every voice in existence simultaneously, and it cannot be heard, because there is no listener large enough.

But it *is*. The Resonant are certain of this the way surface-dwellers are certain of gravity. They point to the sympathetic resonance that connects the thalloid masses—the way damage to one produces a response in all—as evidence. They point to the mirror-pools that reflect the future. They point to the moments—rare, unrepeatable, devastating in their beauty—when the entire chasm falls into harmony: when the roar of the water and the songs of the whales and the whistling of the wind and the voices of the people align, for a few seconds, into a single, perfect, unbearable chord. These moments are called **the Nearness**, and they are the closest the Resonant come to religious ecstasy. People weep. People fall to their knees. People hold each other and press their crests together and vibrate in sympathy. The Nearness lasts seconds, never more than a minute. When it passes, the Chord-Keepers record it in the Sound-Map, noting the date, the frequencies involved, the weather, the position of the floating gardens. They have been recording the Nearness for generations, searching for a pattern, a recurrence, a way to predict when the chasm will next align.

They have never found one. The Nearness comes when it comes, and the Resonant have concluded that it is not meant to be predicted—that the beauty of the Chord-That-Is-Not-Sounded lies precisely in its unknowability, its refusal to be reduced to schedule or formula. This acceptance of the unresolvable is central to their character. They are a people who live inside a sound they cannot control, and they have made peace with that. More than peace—they have made it the foundation of their identity.

## The Other Things That Live Here

### The Glider-Apes

They are not apes. They are not even mammals, though they share a superficial resemblance—fur, four limbs, forward-facing eyes. They are something older, something that evolved in the Plummet's unique conditions and has no analogue on the surface.

A glider-ape stands about a meter tall when upright, though they are rarely upright—they prefer to hang, suspended by their powerful hind limbs from roots and bridge-undersides, their long forelimbs folded against their chests. Their fur is dense and water-repellent, grey-black with a faint iridescent sheen that catches the bioluminescent light. Between their forelimbs and hindlimbs stretches a membrane of skin—tough, flexible, translucent—that unfolds when they leap, turning them into living sails. They are extraordinary gliders, capable of riding the Plummet's updrafts for kilometers, adjusting their trajectory with minute shifts of their membranes and the long, flat rudder of their tail.

Their faces are their most unsettling feature: broad, flat, almost humanoid, with large, dark eyes that reflect the chasm's grey light. Their mouths are wide and thin-lipped, and when they call—and they call constantly—the sound is produced not in the throat but in a pair of resonating chambers in their cheeks, which inflate like pale balloons as they vocalize. The calls are sharp, staccato, cracking sounds that carry extraordinary distances through the water-saturated air. A troop of glider-apes in full chorus sounds like ice breaking on a frozen lake—a rapid, cascading series of snaps and booms that echoes off the chasm walls and folds back on itself.

They are intelligent—not sapient, but close enough to make the Resonant uncomfortable. They use tools: stones to crack the shells of the armored mollusks that cling to the chasm walls, sticks to pry fungi from crevices. They have a social structure—troops of twenty to forty individuals, organized around a matriarch whose calls are deeper and more complex than the others'. They mourn their dead, carrying the bodies of fallen troop members to the edge of their territory and dropping them into the void, a behavior that mirrors the Resonant's own funeral practices so closely that both species find the other unsettling.

The domesticated glider-apes—the farm stock—are smaller, docile, their cheek-chambers atrophied from generations of selective breeding. They are kept for their meat, their membranes, their bones. But the wild troops are tolerated, even encouraged, because they serve as an early warning system: glider-apes are exquisitely sensitive to changes in the chasm's acoustics, and a sudden silence from a troop—an abrupt cessation of their calls—is a reliable indicator of an approaching cascade or wind-shift. The Resonant listen to the apes the way sailors listen to birds.

### The Filter-Whales

They are vast, and they are gentle, and they are old.

A mature filter-whale is fifteen meters from mouth to tail, roughly cylindrical, tapering at both ends. They are not whales in any taxonomic sense—they are more closely related to the thalloid masses than to any animal, a fact that the Chord-Keepers have long suspected and the Deep Singers have confirmed by observing that filter-whales, when injured, do not bleed but instead release a milky, luminescent sap identical to that of the thalloids. They are, in essence, mobile extensions of the chasm's root system—vast, slow, sentient organs of the Plummet itself.

They drift. That is their primary mode of existence. They ride the updrafts and the lateral currents, their enormous mouths—cavernous, baleen-lined, wide enough to swallow a Resonant hut—perpetually open, sieving the suspended water for plankton and microscopic thalloid spores. They have no visible eyes, but they navigate with precision, adjusting their course with subtle undulations of their massive bodies and the occasional pulse of the paired fins that run along their flanks. Their skin is smooth, pale, the color of deep-water ice, and it is warm to the touch—several degrees above the ambient temperature, heated by the slow fermentation of the organic matter in their guts.

Their songs are the Plummet's bass line. A filter-whale's vocalization is a sustained, multi-harmonic drone that can last for hours, produced by the vibration of specialized membranes in their throat. The sound is felt more than heard—a deep, chest-filling pressure that makes the water around the whale shimmer and the nearby thalloid masses pulse in response. The Resonant believe that the whales' songs serve a structural purpose: that the vibrations keep the chasm's root system healthy, stimulating growth and repair in the same way that sound stimulates bone density. Whether this is true or not, the correlation is observable—thalloid masses near frequently-traveled whale routes are noticeably denser and healthier than those in silent regions.

Whales live a long time. The Chord-Keepers recognize individual whales by their songs—each whale has a unique harmonic signature, as distinctive as a Resonant's personal chord—and they have records of specific individuals spanning centuries. There is one whale, known as **The Long Note**, whose song has been documented in the Sound-Map for over four hundred years. It is the largest whale ever observed—nearly twenty-five meters—and its song is the deepest, a drone so low that it is felt as a physical vibration in the stone of the chasm walls. The Long Note is considered sacred. No Deep Singer would dream of hunting it. When it passes near a settlement—which it does on an irregular cycle of roughly thirty years—the entire community turns out to listen, pressing their hands against the walls and the bridges, feeling the song in their bones. Children born during a passage of The Long Note are considered blessed, their birth-tones automatically harmonized with the whale's frequency.

### The Pale Eels

They are the reason the Deep Singers carry obsidian blades.

A pale eel is two meters of translucent, muscular hunger, its body nearly invisible in the dim water, revealed only by the feathery red gills that fan out behind its head like a collar of blood-colored lace. They hunt in packs of five to twelve, communicating through rapid, high-pitched clicks that are above the range of Resonant hearing but below the range of their High Speech—a frequency gap that makes them maddeningly difficult to track. They are fast, agile, and coordinated, capable of surrounding a prey animal and stripping it to the bone in minutes.

Their teeth are their most distinctive feature: not fangs, but a circular arrangement of needle-like denticles that line the interior of their mouth, pointing inward. Once an eel bites, it does not let go—it cannot let go, because the teeth angle backward, and any attempt to withdraw only drives them deeper. The eel must be killed or its jaw must be broken to release the bite. Deep Singers who have survived eel attacks carry the scars: circular, punctate patterns that look like the imprint of a gear, usually on the forearm or the calf.

But the eels are not merely predators. They are **keepers of the deep frequencies**. The Chord-Keepers have observed that pale eel packs congregate in areas where the chasm's natural resonance is changing—where a wall is beginning to crack, where a root-mass is dying, where the water flow is shifting. The eels are drawn to these acoustic anomalies the way sharks are drawn to blood. A concentration of pale eels in a region is therefore a warning sign, and the Resonant have learned to read it: *where the red gills gather, the chasm is changing*. Deep Singers who encounter large eel packs do not fight—they retreat, and they report the location to the Chord-Keepers, who adjust the Sound-Map accordingly.

The eels' red gills are prized by the Resonant as a source of pigment—a deep, vivid crimson that is the only true red available in the Plummet's muted palette. The gills must be harvested from a freshly killed eel and processed immediately, ground into a paste and mixed with the lipid secretions of the Resonant's own skin to create a dye that bonds permanently with thalloid fiber. Red-dyed garments are worn only by the Chord-Keepers and the First Voice—a mark of station, a visible sign of those who stand closest to the chasm's voice.

### The Moss-Skaters

They are small enough to be overlooked, and they are everywhere.

A moss-skater is a six-legged creature the size of a human thumb, its body flattened and streamlined, its legs long and slender, each foot tipped with a hydrophobic pad that allows it to walk on the surface tension of water. In the Plummet, where water exists not in pools but in suspended droplets and flowing veils, this means the moss-skaters are effectively *walking on the rain*. They skitter across the surface of falling droplets, riding them downward like tiny surfers, leaping from one droplet to the next with precision that seems impossible given the speed of the fall.

Their bodies are covered in a fine, dense fur that traps a layer of air, giving them a silvery, almost metallic appearance. They are herbivorous, feeding on the microscopic thalloid spores that saturate the water, and they are in turn preyed upon by glider-apes, small birds, and larger moss-skaters—cannibalism is common among them, particularly in the leaner regions of the chasm where spore density is low.

What makes them remarkable is the sound they produce. Each footfall on a water droplet creates a minute vibration—a ripple—that resonates at a frequency just below the threshold of Resonant hearing. A single moss-skater is imperceptible. But the Plummet contains *millions* of them, perhaps billions, and their combined footfalls create a continuous subsonic hum that permeates the entire chasm. The Resonant are not consciously aware of this hum, but their bodies respond to it: it is the background radiation of their acoustic world, the silence beneath the silence. When the moss-skaters are disturbed—by a wind-shift, by a cascade, by the passage of a filter-whale—the hum changes frequency, and the Resonant feel it as a vague unease, a sense that something is wrong even when they cannot identify what.

The Deep Singers have learned to pay attention to this feeling. They call it the **Under-Silence**, and they consider it the most reliable indicator of the chasm's mood. A calm Under-Silence means stability. A rising Under-Silence means change. A sudden absence of the Under-Silence—when the moss-skaters stop moving, all at once, across the entire chasm—means catastrophe. This has happened three times in recorded history. Each time, it preceded a cascade of such magnitude that entire settlements were destroyed, their bridges snapped, their aqueducts torn from the walls, their inhabitants cast into the void.

The moss-skaters resumed their footfalls within hours. The settlements took generations to rebuild.

## The Ones Who Fall

There is one more inhabitant of the Plummet, though the Resonant speak of them rarely and with great discomfort.

Sometimes—not often, but often enough that it has a name—a Resonant loses their chord. Not through death, but through trauma: a near-fall, a cascade witnessed too closely, a Nearness experienced at the wrong frequency. The result is a person whose personal chord has *fractured*—whose sound-stones no longer resonate together, whose birth-tone has shifted, whose presence in the settlement's harmonic structure creates dissonance rather than consonance.

These individuals are called **the Fallen**, and they are treated with a careful, sorrowful exclusion. They are not banished—the Resonant are not cruel—but they cannot remain in the settlement, because their broken chord interferes with the harmonic balance that the First Voice works to maintain. Their presence causes the bridges to stiffen, the thalloids to contract, the water to pool in the aqueducts. They are a wrong note in the settlement's song, and the song cannot accommodate them.

The Fallen descend. They gather their sound-stones—now discordant, clashing—and they climb down, past the settlement's lowest tier, past the last bridge, into the dimmer, quieter reaches of the chasm. They do not die, or not immediately. They find the unclaimed spaces—the ledges and crevices where no thalloid grows, where the water falls but nothing lives—and they make their solitary lives there, in the margins of the Plummet's ecology.

Some of them survive for years. The Deep Singers occasionally encounter them: gaunt, wild-eyed figures huddled in hollows in the basalt, their crests flattened and unresponsive, their sound-stones silent. They do not speak. They do not sing. They watch the Deep Singers pass with eyes that have forgotten how to reflect light.

The Resonant believe that the Fallen are not lost forever. They believe that the chasm, in its own time, will heal them—that the constant immersion in the Plummet's song will eventually re-tune their broken chord, restore the harmony that was shattered. And sometimes—rarely, so rarely that each instance is recorded in the Sound-Map as a Nearness of its own—a Fallen returns. They climb back up to the settlement, thin and scarred and silent, and they stand in the resonating chamber, and they sound their chord, and it is *whole*. Different from before—deeper, stranger, carrying frequencies that were not there before the fracture—but whole.

These returned ones are called **the Re-Tuned**, and they occupy a unique position in Resonant society: neither honored nor pitied, but *listened to*. Their chords contain notes that no other Resonant can produce, notes that resonate with the deepest and most remote parts of the chasm. They become advisors to the Chord-Keepers, guides for the Deep Singers, living bridges between the settlement and the void.

They rarely speak of what they experienced in the silence. But when they do—around the root-wine fires, in the hours when the roar is softest—they describe a sound. Not the Plummet's roar, not the whales' songs, not the wind. A sound beneath all of those, a sound so deep and so slow that it takes centuries to complete a single cycle. A sound that is not a note but a *sentence*. A sentence being spoken by something so large that the Plummet is only one of its mouths.

*I am falling*, the Plummet says. But beneath that, below that, in a register that only the broken can hear, something else says:

*I have not yet landed.*
