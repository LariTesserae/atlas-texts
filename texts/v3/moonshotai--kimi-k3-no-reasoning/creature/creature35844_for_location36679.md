**The Stranger at the Eastern Pillar**

There is one place in Vess where the residents will stop you from going, not with force but with a kind of gentle, physical redirection—a hand on your elbow, a body quietly interposed, a suggestion that the tea is ready back at the kettle, that the walkway here is slippery, that you are tired. It is the eastern perimeter, where the rock face meets the deepest accumulation of the borough's discarded time, near a pillar of rusted cast iron that was once part of a roof support and is now a shrine, though no one calls it that.

This is where the Forgotten lives.

He—or it, or they, for the pronouns used in Vess are slippery and context-dependent—is present in the way the light is present: pervasive, undeniable, and impossible to pinpoint. You might see him first as a figure standing at the pillar's base, wearing a coat of oilcloth that has gone gray with decades of damp. Or you might see him as a pile of those same coats, left in a heap, that slowly resolves into a sitting man. Or you might not see him at all, but instead feel a sudden, intense certainty that you have left something behind you, just out of sight, something vital, and when you turn to check, the space where he should be is empty except for the drip of condensation.

The Forgotten is the memory of Vess, but not the living memory of the residents, not the stories told over soup or the records moldering in the archive. He is the memory of the *absence*—the three thousand who are gone, the stairs that were built and then sealed, the names that were spoken into the wet air and not captured by the rock. He is the weight of every departure. When a resident dies—and they die rarely, but they do die, their breathing slowing to match the pumps until it stops altogether—the Forgotten becomes slightly more solid, slightly more present. When a child is born, which happens perhaps once a decade, he thins, becomes translucent, almost blows away like the vapor above the sump.

He speaks, but only in the language of subtraction. If you ask him a question—*Who built the generator? Why do the bulbs never fail? What is the wrong reflection in the sump?*—he answers by removing something from your mind. A date. A face. The reason you came down the four hundred and eleven steps. You walk away from the eastern pillar knowing less than you knew before, but feeling lighter, unburdened, as if you have set down a pack you didn't realize you were carrying.

The residents bring him things. Not offerings, exactly—more like items for safekeeping in a vault that is also a person. They bring objects that have become too heavy with meaning: a photograph of a surface-dweller who stopped writing letters; a key to a room that was demolished thirty years ago; the recipe for a bread that required sunlight to rise. They leave these at the base of the pillar, and the Forgotten absorbs them into his coat, or his pockets, or the space around his body, and the items cease to trouble the living.

He is not a ghost. Ghosts are remnants of specific individuals. The Forgotten is the composite sediment of all the specific individuals who have been erased by time. He has no face because he has too many, layered over one another until the features blur into a universal, ancestral smoothness. When the drip-rats approach him, they do not purr; they fall silent, pressing their bodies flat against the stone in a posture of profound, rodential respect, or perhaps fear, or perhaps simply recognition of a predator that eats not flesh but *significance*.

Sometimes, at the turn of the season—though seasons in Vess are marked only by the sump's slow breathing—the Forgotten will be seen to walk. He moves along the flooded walkways of the sump-side buildings, stepping not on the wood but on the water itself, which holds him because he is, fundamentally, no heavier than a thought that has been let go. He walks to the edge of the sump and stands there, looking not at the wrong reflection in the still water, but at the space above it, where the ceiling of Vess arches invisibly in the amber gloom. He looks up as if he can see through the rock to the surface, to the rumor of the moor or forest that once stood there.

If you encounter him—and you will, if you stay long enough, because he is as much a part of the infrastructure as the pumps—you will be tempted to speak to him, to ask him to take away your own burdens, your own memories of the sky. This is permitted. But know that he does not distinguish between pain and joy, between trauma and love. He takes only what is *gone*, and if you offer him something you still hold, he will look at you with those eyes that are not eyes but the absence of eyes, and you will realize that to be forgotten by him is not to be relieved, but to be dissolved into the slow, wet, patient dark, to become another layer of sediment in a place that is already too full of history to hold.

So the residents leave him his pillar, and his coats, and his slow walk on the water. They let him keep the names of the three thousand, and the secrets of the generator, and the memory of what the light looked like before it was amber. They let him be the one who remembers so that they can be the ones who forget, and in forgetting, continue to live in the slow, eternal present of the borough, where the pumps beat, and the sheen grows, and the water rises and falls, and nothing is urgent because nothing is ever truly lost—it is only handed to the Forgotten, who holds it in the deep pockets of his coat, in the deep rock, in the deep time, forever.
