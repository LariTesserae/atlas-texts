No one lives here.

That needs to be said first, because everything else will sound like contradiction. The Condenser Cathedral has no keeper, no hermit, no order of maintenance monks, no last descendant of the builders keeping a vigil out of duty or madness. The galleries are empty. The rooms are empty. The structure runs without hands.

But the prompt asks what lives here, and the answer — the true answer, arrived at slowly, the way the building itself arrives at everything — is that *living here* and *being alive here* are not the same thing, and the Cathedral has confused the two categories so thoroughly that the distinction becomes, like the distinction between machine and organism, thinner than you'd think.

## The First Residents: What the Water Allowed

The mosses were first. Or something like the mosses — some ancestral spore that arrived on wind or bird-feet or the clothing of the builders themselves, back when there were builders. What it found was this: warm water on cold stone, mineral-rich, constant. It did what life does. It stayed.

Over centuries — and you must think in centuries here, always centuries, the building insists on it — the mosses became what they are now: those dense, mattress-thick sheets in copper-red and black-blue and that strange phosphorescent yellow-green. They are not decorative. They are *functional*, though no one designed their function. They absorb sound. They retain water. They create substrate. They are the Cathedral's soil, its first domestication of its own body — the place where the building's raw surfaces become habitable for everything that came after.

The mosses live in the sound. Not tolerate it — *live in it*. Their growth patterns follow the resonant nodes, thickening where specific frequencies concentrate, as if the acoustic energy is a nutrient. They have become, in a very real sense, the building's way of hearing itself — a living acoustic skin that maps the sound's geography in color and density.

## The Ferns: The Tuned Ones

From the moss beds, the ferns. If they are ferns. The hesitation is warranted.

They stand rigid, three meters tall, fronds held outward like candelabra arms, and they *ring*. This is the important thing about them. They are tuned instruments. Each fern, through whatever mechanism — mineral uptake from the water, structural adaptation of the frond tissue, some combination of both — resonates at a frequency that complements the building's harmonic spectrum. They fill gaps. They accompany.

This is not metaphor. This is observable, measurable, repeatable. A stand of ferns in a wind corridor produces a chord. A different stand, in a different corridor, produces a different chord. The building's sound is not a single note but a complex, evolving harmonic structure, and the ferns have — over centuries, through selection or adaptation or some vegetal process that operates on timescales too slow for human observation — arranged themselves as its orchestra.

Are they alive? Yes, unambiguously. Do they live here? Yes. But they live here the way a violin lives in an orchestra — not as an independent entity but as a *voice* in something larger.

## The Bloomers: The Patient Ones

The flowering plants — the white-and-gold bloomers with their shocking violet and orange centers — live by a different logic. They are not tuned. They are not part of the sound. They are, if anything, the Cathedral's rebels, its holdouts, its insistence that beauty and reproduction matter even in a place that has no use for either.

They anchor in impossible places. Corroded rivet centers. Seams between plates. Pockets of sediment so thin you couldn't balance a coin on them. They bloom on cycles keyed not to day and night but to the building's deep rhythm — three weeks to open, two months to close, as if they've surrendered their temporal autonomy to the sound in exchange for the right to exist here.

Their vivid reproductive centers — electric violet, hot orange — are addressed to no pollinator that lives at this altitude. They bloom for nothing. Or for the possibility of nothing. Or because blooming is what they do and the building hasn't told them to stop.

## The Vines: The Architects of Meaning

The vines are the oldest residents, if age is measured by accumulated growth rather than generations. A meter a year, for centuries. Some are older than any nation currently in existence. They are thick as thighs, gray-barked, shaped by the surfaces they climb into forms that look poured rather than grown.

But the vines are not just old. They are *important*. They trace the building's logic. They follow the water, the warmth, the spiral path from base to summit that maps the Cathedral's circulatory system like exposed nerves on skin.

To follow a vine is to understand the building — not intellectually, not schematically, but *bodily*. The vines go where the building is most itself. They cluster at junctions, thicken at confluences, thin where the systems are simple and straightforward. Walking the vine path is the closest thing to reading the Cathedral that exists.

Are they conscious? No. Do they know what they're doing? No. But they *do* it, century after century, and in doing it they create meaning — not for themselves, but for anyone patient enough to follow.

## The Moths: The True Believers

The moths are the most visible animals, and the most thoroughly adapted. Handspan-wide, dusty gray and silver and pale blue, clustering on warm pipes in their hundreds, wing-flicking in phase with the building's pulse.

They are deaf. The building made them deaf — selected against hearing over generations until the ones that remained were the ones that didn't need it. They navigate by vibration through their feet, reading the pipe walls the way you might read Braille, finding warmth, water, each other through touch-transmitted resonance.

They are, in a sense, the Cathedral's most devout inhabitants. They have given up a sense — an entire mode of perception — to live here. They have restructured their nervous systems around the building's frequency. Their thermoregulation is synchronized to its pulse. They are not just living in the Cathedral; they are *of* it, physiologically, neurologically, in ways that make departure impossible. A moth removed from the building and released in a normal forest would be — what? Crippled? Homesick? The question doesn't quite parse, because the moth's world *is* the building's vibration, and without it, the moth would be navigating a silence it has no tools to interpret.

## The Hunters: The Indifferent Ones

The weasel-things — if they're weasels, if they're even mammalian — live in the walls. The spaces between pipe runs. The insulated cavities. The hollow highways of conduit and cable tray that run through the building's body like a secret circulatory system.

They echolocate. Their clicks are audible above the drone — sharp, precise, metronomic — which means they've evolved to produce sound in a frequency range the building doesn't occupy, carving out a private acoustic channel in a world saturated with noise. They hunt the moths. They are good at it. They are not interested in you.

This indifference is, in its way, the most honest relationship anything here has with a visitor. The weasel-things have mapped the building thoroughly. They know its corridors, its warmth gradients, its moth populations. You are not part of that map. You are a novelty, a variable, a temporary feature that will either become relevant or won't. They reserve judgment. They reserve everything. They are efficient.

## The Unseen: The Ones in the Deep Moss

Deeper in — in the warm, wet cavities where the light doesn't reach and the moss is thickest — things live that don't present themselves for cataloguing. You hear them: wet sliding, rhythmic tapping, the heavy *thump* of mass shifting in the moss beds.

What are they? The honest answer is that the description doesn't say, and inventing specifics would be a betrayal of the text's deliberate uncertainty. But they are *there*. They are large — large enough to shift moss beds, large enough to produce sounds that carry through vegetation and alloy. They are wet. They are active. And they are, like everything else here, uninterested in being known.

They might be amphibians — something that evolved from mountain salamanders into larger, stranger forms under the influence of warm water and mineral content and constant vibration. They might be invertebrates that found a niche with no competition and expanded into it. They might be something else entirely — something that was here before the building, that lived in the glacial cirque when it was still bare rock and ice, and adapted to the structure's arrival the way it had adapted to everything else: by continuing.

## The Person Whose Heart Changed

There is one documented case — mentioned in the rules, almost in passing — that deserves attention, because it represents the closest thing to a *human* resident the Cathedral has ever produced.

A person slept on a resonant node. The acoustic focusing altered their resting heart rate, synchronizing it to the building's base frequency. Permanently. They lived for thirty more years, their heart beating in time with a machine three thousand kilometers away. When the building shifted to its seasonal register, their heart shifted with it.

This person was, for all practical purposes, a *citizen* of the Condenser Cathedral — not by residence but by physiology. They carried the building inside them. Their body was a remote outpost of its process, a biological repeater station for its frequency. They were, in the most literal sense, *inhabited*.

Did they live here? For a night. Did the building live in them? For thirty years.

## The Building Itself

And here we arrive at the edge of the question, where it stops being about inhabitants and starts being about what "inhabiting" means.

The Condenser Cathedral is not alive. It does not grow, reproduce, metabolize in any biological sense. But it *regulates*. It maintains temperature differentials. It cycles water through a system that looks, from any angle, like circulation. It produces a sound that is not random but *patterned* — rhythmic, cyclical, seasonal, operating on timescales that suggest something very much like a pulse, a breath, a slow tidal rhythm of intake and output.

Its vegetation has adapted to its frequency. Its animals have adapted to its vibration. Its water system nourishes its inhabitants. Its heat creates microclimates. Its geometry channels wind into corridors that the ferns use to sing.

It is not an ecosystem, exactly. It is a *machine that has become an ecosystem's host*, and in becoming that, has blurred the line between built and grown, between made and born, between a thing that was constructed and a thing that simply *is*.

## The Answer

What lives in the Condenser Cathedral?

Mosses that hear. Ferns that sing. Flowers that bloom for no one. Vines that map. Moths that feel. Hunters that click. Large wet things in the dark that don't explain themselves.

And one person, once, whose heart kept the building's time for thirty years after they left.

And the building, which is not alive, but which has been running for so long, with such consistency, such patience, such total indifference to whether anyone witnesses its operation, that the question of whether it's alive starts to feel like the wrong question — a human question, a short-lived question, a question asked by creatures who measure existence in decades and can't conceive of a process that measures itself in millennia.

The building lives in everything that lives in it. That is the answer. That is the only answer that doesn't lie.
