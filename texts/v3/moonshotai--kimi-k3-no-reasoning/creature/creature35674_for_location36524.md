In Graven-Hollow, the distinction between the maker and the made has long since eroded, washed away by the condensation that beads on every surface. The inhabitants are not merely residents of the great bowl; they are its secretions, grown from the damp clay and the hammered copper, as much a part of the mechanism as the firing of the earth or the oxidation of the metal.

They are called the **Graven**, though they do not call themselves this, having little use for names in a place where sound is primarily functional. They are small of stature, wiry and compressed, as if the low ceiling of the world has pressed them into more efficient shapes. Their skin carries the gray-green pallor of the oxidized copper, marked by patches of vitiligo that resemble verdigris, and their fingers are stained permanently black by the metals they handle. They move with a peculiar, shuffling haste, their bodies bent slightly forward, eyes large and dark from a lifetime in the gloom, adapted to catch the faint blue-green luminescence of the fungi rather than the nonexistent sun.

Their biology has adapted to the wet, metallic air. Their lungs are specialized filters, capable of extracting oxygen from the dense, particulate-laden atmosphere, but this comes at a cost: their breath rattles with a dry, metallic rasp, and their voices, when they speak, are high-pitched and resonant, like coins dropped into deep wells. They rarely speak above a whisper, however, for sound carries strangely in the bowl, and the constant hammering provides all the communication they need.

The Graven are organized into loose guilds defined by their relationship to the material. There are the **Clay-Shapers**, who maintain the structural integrity of the dwellings, slapping wet, salvaged mud onto the walls and smoothing it with hands that have become indistinguishable from trowels. There are the **Copper-Seamers**, who tend the great veins of metal that run through the ground, chiseling away the oxidized crust to reveal the bright, reactive metal beneath, which they hammer into new sheets for roofing or tools. And there are the **Water-Seekers**, the most revered and most isolated, who tend the great sealed jars and the collection systems of gutters and funnels that harvest the condensation. These last are often marked by their hair, which grows white and brittle from constant exposure to the collected rust-particles, giving them the appearance of being dusted with powdered bone.

Their society is governed by the rhythm of the hammer. The near-constant clanging is not noise but language, a complex system of rhythmic patterns that convey information about structural weaknesses, the discovery of new metal veins, or the presence of the larger, shadowy creatures that slip between the buildings. The Graven do not fear these shadows, exactly, but they acknowledge them in their work, striking certain patterns on the anvils to indicate that the way is clear, or that the path should be avoided.

They have no children in the conventional sense. When a Graven dies—usually from the gradual accumulation of metal in their lungs, which eventually silences the rasping breath—their body is not buried but rendered. The soft tissues are returned to the clay pits to feed the fungal growths, while the bones, heavy with accumulated metals, are carefully cleaned and added to the structural supports of the buildings. The dead become the literal framework of the hollow, their femurs reinforcing archways, their vertebrae stacked into pillars. Thus, the population remains stable, a closed loop of matter and energy, with new Graven emerging from the communal crèches where infants are raised on a diet of fungal protein and rust-filtered water.

The crèches are located in the deepest, driest parts of the bowl, where the condensation is carefully controlled. The infants are born with soft, cartilaginous bones that harden rapidly in the metallic environment, and by the age of five, they are already apprenticed to the guilds, their small hands perfect for reaching into narrow crevices to retrieve dropped tools or to scrape away the earliest stages of oxidation.

Despite the harshness of their existence, the Graven are not without culture. They possess a form of music, created by striking the copper roofs with padded mallets, producing deep, resonant tones that vibrate through the ground and into the bones of the listeners. They gather in the wider plazas during the periods when the mist thickens into a true rain, standing with their faces upturned, mouths open to catch the falling water, while the drummers play complex, interlocking rhythms that mimic the sound of the condensation dripping into the pools. It is a form of worship, perhaps, or simply a celebration of the fact that they have not yet been reclaimed by the machine they maintain.

And then there are the **Anomalies**, those Graven who, for reasons unknown, begin to change. They develop an affinity for the larger, shadowy creatures, and rather than avoiding the darkness between buildings, they seek it out. Their eyes grow even larger, their skin even paler, until they become translucent, revealing the dark, metal-rich blood moving beneath. These individuals eventually leave the society of the hammer and the clay, disappearing into the narrowest canyons, where they are said to tend the fungi in ways the guilds cannot understand, or perhaps to communicate with the shadows that flit through the gloom. The Graven do not speak of them, but they leave offerings of bright, unoxidized copper at the mouths of these passages, a tribute to those who have become something other than gears in the machine, something closer to the rust that binds it all together.
