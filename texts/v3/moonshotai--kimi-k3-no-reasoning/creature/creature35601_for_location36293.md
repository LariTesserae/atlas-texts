# The Inhabitant

---

## I. The Frog That Is Not One Frog

There is a frog on a hummock of sphagnum three feet from the water's edge — if "edge" is a word that means anything here, which it is not. The frog is the size of the first joint of a man's thumb. Its skin is the color of wet peat, which is to say it is the color of the hummock, which is to say it is not visible. You know it is there because the description told you so. You would not otherwise know.

This frog has not moved in eleven days.

This is not an approximation. Eleven days ago, a midge larva — one of the translucent, red-blooded *Chironomus* larvae that thread through the moss in densities of several thousand per square meter — wriggled within range of the frog's tongue, and the frog ate it. That was the last event in the frog's life. Before that, there was a gap of six days. Before that, nineteen. The frog's metabolism runs so low, so cool, so perfectly calibrated to the ambient temperature of four degrees Celsius, that a single midge larva — a thing that weighs less than a snowflake — can sustain it for the better part of a month. The frog is not starving. The frog is not waiting. The frog is *operating*, and its operating looks like nothing at all.

Its heart beats. This is the only continuous motion. A two-chambered amphibian heart, small as a peppercorn, pushing blood that is barely warmer than the water it sits in. The beat is slow — one contraction every few seconds — and between beats the frog is, by any measure available to human senses, identical to the peat it sits on. It does not breathe visibly. Cutaneous respiration through its perpetually damp skin handles most of its gas exchange. Its lungs — small, simple sacs — inflate perhaps once an hour, a slight swelling of the throat that you would need to be watching for days to notice.

Its eyes are open. They are always open. The frog has no eyelids in the mammalian sense — only a transparent nictitating membrane that slides across the eye periodically to moisten it. This happens every few minutes and is the frog's most frequent voluntary movement. In the amber-green gloom of the hollow, the membrane catches what little light there is and produces a brief, dim flash — a signal, if anything were watching, that the lump on the hummock is alive. Nothing is watching. Or rather, everything is watching, in the sense that everything here is alive and sensate, but nothing is attending, because attention implies a shift in state, and nothing here shifts.

The frog's visual world is built for motion detection. Its retina is dominated by ganglion cells tuned to small, dark, moving objects — the fly-detector system described by Lettvin and his colleagues in their famous 1959 paper, *What the Frog's Eye Tells the Frog's Brain*. The frog does not see the hollow. It does not see the moss or the stone walls or the canopy or the darkness. It sees *movement*, and in the hollow, movement is rare. A midge larva wriggling in the moss. A copepod drifting past. A droplet falling. The frog's world is a blank field punctuated by tiny events, and between events there is — from the frog's perspective — nothing. Not darkness. Not stillness. *Nothing*. The frog's brain does not process the static world. It is not built to. The hollow, as a place, does not exist for the frog. Only the events exist.

This means the frog's subjective experience of time is radically different from yours. You stand in the hollow and feel the weight of its stillness, the oppressive density of its unchanging completeness. The frog feels nothing of the kind. The frog exists in a series of instants, each one defined by the presence or absence of prey movement, and the gaps between instants are not experienced at all. The eleven days of stillness since the last midge larva are not eleven days to the frog. They are *zero*. The frog ate, and then — immediately, seamlessly — the next event will arrive, and the frog will eat again. The frog's life is a sequence of meals separated by no time at all.

There are — and this number is as precise as field biology can make it — approximately four hundred frogs in the hollow. This is an extraordinary density for a body of water this small, and it is possible only because of the crushing productivity of the peat-water ecosystem. The copepods and midge larvae and seed shrimp that fill the water are so numerous, so continuously reproducing, that they support a standing biomass of predators that would be impossible in any system with seasonal variation. The frogs do not compete. Competition requires scarcity, and there is no scarcity. Each frog occupies its hummock or its patch of water or its crease in the moss, and the food comes to it, and it eats, and it does not move, and the frog on the next hummock does the same, and they have been doing this — this exact thing, in this exact configuration — for so long that the population has achieved a kind of demographic crystallization. No frog encroaches on another's space. No frog needs to. The space between frogs is not contested territory. It is simply the distance between two points of absolute stillness.

The frogs are all the same species — a small, dark member of the family Ranidae, unremarkable in every way except for the extremity of its adaptation to this environment. Its coloration is not camouflage in the active sense. It is *convergence*. The frog has, over thousands of generations, become the color of its substrate because there has never been selective pressure to be anything else. No predator in the hollow hunts by sight. The salamanders hunt by chemical detection and lateral-line sensitivity to water displacement. The birds — the wrens, the dippers — are insectivores and do not take frogs. The frogs are not hiding. They are simply *the same color as everything else*, because everything else is the same color as everything else, because the light is too dim and too green-shifted for color to carry information. In the hollow, color is not a signal. It is a side effect of chemistry — the melanin in the frog's skin, the chlorophyll in the moss, the humic acids in the peat water — and all of these chemistries converge on the same narrow band of near-black.

The frog does not know it is the same color as the hummock. The frog does not know anything, in the propositional sense. But its body *is* a kind of knowledge — the accumulated, embodied knowledge of every generation that came before it, refined by selection into a form so perfectly fitted to this place that the distinction between the frog and the hollow begins to blur. The frog's skin is wet because the hollow is wet. The frog's temperature is four degrees because the hollow is four degrees. The frog's stillness mirrors the hollow's stillness. The frog's color is the hollow's color. The frog is not *in* the hollow. The frog is a small, temporary concentration of the hollow itself, a knot in the fabric, a place where the peat and the water and the cold and the darkness have organized themselves into a form that eats and breathes and occasionally — very occasionally — blinks.

## II. The Salamander in the Water

Beneath the surface — if "surface" is a word that means anything here, which it barely does — there is a salamander.

It is thirty centimeters long, which makes it one of the largest animals in the hollow. It lies half-buried in the peat at the bottom of the deepest part of the basin, which is not deep — perhaps sixty centimeters of water above the peat mat — but which is, by the hollow's standards, an abyss. The salamander's body is dark brown-black, smooth-skinned, thick as a man's wrist. Its four legs are small relative to its body, splayed outward in the lazy posture of an animal that has not needed to walk in months. Its tail is long and laterally compressed, a rudder that has not steered anything recently. Its head is broad and flat, with small, lidless eyes set wide apart. The eyes are functional but vestigial-adjacent. The salamander hunts primarily through its lateral line system — a series of mechanoreceptive neuromasts running along its flanks that detect water displacement with extraordinary sensitivity — and through chemoreception, tasting the water for the amino acid signatures of prey.

The salamander has been in this position, or a position very near it, for approximately one year.

This is not an exaggeration. The salamander's metabolic rate at four degrees Celsius is so low that its energy requirements can be met by the ambient prey density within a radius of less than a meter. Copepods drift into range. Midge larvae wriggle through the peat within reach of a slow, deliberate lunge. Occasionally — once every few weeks — a small frog, less cautious than its fellows or simply unlucky, shifts position on the peat mat above, and the salamander's lateral line detects the displacement, and the salamander rises from the peat with a speed that is shocking in an animal that has appeared motionless, and it takes the frog, and it returns to the peat, and it is still again.

The frog, in this scenario, is not a victim in any dramatic sense. It is a transfer of biomass from one part of the hollow to another. The frog was peat that had organized itself into a frog. The salamander eats the frog and the frog becomes salamander, which is another way of being peat. The hollow does not distinguish. The loop is closed.

The salamander's physiology is a study in reduction. Its heart rate is approximately six beats per minute. Its respiratory exchange occurs primarily through its skin — it has no gills as an adult, having absorbed them during metamorphosis, but its cutaneous respiration is so efficient in the cold, oxygen-saturated water that its lungs are almost supplementary. Its digestive system processes food slowly, extracting every available calorie over the course of weeks. A single frog provides the salamander with enough energy for a month of stillness. A copepod — the salamander takes these incidentally, almost accidentally, as they drift against its skin — provides perhaps an hour's worth of maintenance metabolism. The arithmetic of the salamander's life is simple: inputs are small, outputs are nearly zero, and the balance is maintained with a precision that would be the envy of any accountant.

The salamander's sensory world is richer than the frog's. The lateral line system provides a continuous, detailed map of the water column around it. It can detect the swimming motions of a copepod at a distance of several centimeters. It can feel the footfall of a frog on the peat mat above as a faint, slow pressure wave. It can taste the water — not in the way a human tastes, but in a way that provides continuous chemical information about the composition of its environment. The water tastes of humic acid, of dissolved organic carbon, of the faint metabolic byproducts of ten thousand crustaceans and a hundred thousand larvae. The water tastes, to the salamander, like the hollow itself — a complex, multilayered chemical signature that is as much a map of the ecosystem as any visual image could be.

The salamander has been alive for a long time. Small salamanders in cold, stable environments can live for decades, and this one shows no signs of senescence. Its cells divide slowly. Its telomeres — the protective caps on its chromosomes — shorten at a rate proportional to its metabolic rate, which is to say, almost imperceptibly. The salamander is not immortal, but it is *durable* in a way that warm-blooded animals cannot be. Its life is measured not in years but in the slow accumulation of the peat beneath it, which deepens by the width of a hair each year. The salamander has been lying in this spot long enough that the peat has risen around it, and it has adjusted its position by millimeters, and the peat has risen again, and it has adjusted again, and this process — so slow it cannot be observed, only inferred — has been going on for longer than the canopy above has been in its current configuration.

The salamander is not waiting. Waiting implies anticipation, and anticipation implies a future state different from the present, and for the salamander there is no future state different from the present. There is only the water, and the taste of the water, and the occasional pressure wave that means food, and the slow, slow deepening of the peat. The salamander's existence is not a story. It is a *condition* — a state of being that persists without narrative, without arc, without the need for anything to happen next.

## III. The Dipper at the Wall

On the wet rock face, where water exudes from the granite in a perpetual film, a dipper stands and bobs.

The dipper — *Cinclus cinclus*, the white-throated dipper, the water ouzel — is a bird built for moving water. It is the only passerine that swims underwater as a matter of course, using its wings to propel itself along streambeds, picking caddisfly larvae and small crustaceans from the gravel. Its adaptations for this life are extensive: dense, waterproof plumage; a nictitating membrane that functions as a diving goggle; blood with an unusually high hemoglobin concentration for oxygen storage; solid bones (unlike the hollow bones of most birds) to reduce buoyancy; and a habit of bobbing — dipping its body up and down on flexed legs — that may serve to signal territory, to aid in spotting prey through water surface distortion, or simply because the bird is incapable of standing still.

This dipper is bobbing now. It bobs constantly. In the stillness of the hollow, the dipper's bobbing is the most kinetic thing happening — a small, dark bird on a dark rock, pumping its body up and down with metronomic regularity, its white eyelids flashing each time it blinks, which is often. The white eyelid flash is a signal, or was a signal, in the evolutionary history of the species — a way to communicate with other dippers across the noise of rushing water. Here, in the silence of the hollow, there is no noise to compete with, and there are no other dippers close enough to see the flash. The behavior persists anyway. The dipper bobs and blinks because bobbing and blinking are what dippers do, and the hollow has not been here long enough — or perhaps the dipper has not been here long enough — for evolution to strip away behaviors that are no longer necessary.

The dipper is anomalous in the hollow. Everything else here is still. The frogs are still. The salamanders are still. The beetles cling and do not move. The moss grows so slowly that its growth cannot be distinguished from its stillness. But the dipper *moves*. It moves constantly, restlessly, as though it carries within its body a memory of the fast water it was built for — the tumbling streams, the white water, the churn and splash of a mountain torrent. The hollow has none of this. The water here does not move. It *sits*. And the dipper, built for motion, stands on the wet rock and bobs and blinks and does not know what to do with the stillness.

It feeds. There are caddisfly larvae in the seepage films on the rock face — small, pale larvae in cases of glued-together moss fragments, clinging to the stone where the water runs. The dipper picks them off with quick, precise movements of its bill. It does not need to dive. The prey is on the surface, exposed, available. The dipper takes them one by one, methodically, working its way along the rock face with short hops and constant bobbing. It is efficient. It is well-fed. It is, by any ecological measure, successful.

But it is also, in a way that is difficult to articulate without resorting to anthropomorphism, *wrong* for this place. The hollow rewards stillness. Everything in it has evolved toward stillness — toward the reduction of movement, the minimization of metabolic expenditure, the achievement of a state so close to inanimate that the boundary between living and non-living becomes theoretical. The dipper has not evolved toward stillness. The dipper is a bird of rapids and waterfalls, of turbulent water and constant motion. Its body is a machine built for a world that the hollow does not provide. And so it bobs, and blinks, and flicks from rock to rock with quick, nervous movements, and its sharp *zit-zit* calls ring out in the silence and are absorbed by the moss and the wet stone and the thick, humid air, and the silence closes over them like water over a stone.

The dipper nests behind the rock face, in a crevice kept perpetually wet by the seepage. Its nest is a dome of moss — the same sphagnum that covers every surface — woven into a ball with a side entrance, lined with grass and rootlets. The nest is damp. Everything in the hollow is damp. The dipper's eggs are white, unmarked, and the female incubates them with the same restless energy she brings to everything — sitting, shifting, leaving to feed, returning, shifting again. The chicks hatch blind and naked and are fed on caddisfly larvae and small crustaceans, and they grow, and they fledge, and they emerge into a world where everything is wet and dark and still, and they bob and blink and call, because they are dippers, and that is what dippers do.

The dipper is the hollow's anomaly — the one thing that has not settled, the one thing that still behaves as though the world is in motion. It is not unhappy. Unhappiness requires a conception of alternatives, and the dipper has none. It is simply *mismatched* — a creature of fast water living in still water, a creature of noise living in silence, a creature of light living in darkness. It persists because the hollow is productive enough to support it, and because no competitor has arisen to challenge it for the niche of "small bird that picks larvae off wet rocks." But it is the hollow's one discordant note — a flicker of restlessness in a system that has otherwise achieved perfect, absolute rest.

## IV. The Wrens

If the dipper is anomalous, the wrens are *absurd*.

There are dozens of them. Perhaps forty. Perhaps sixty. In a space that could be crossed in thirty steps, there are more wrens than in an entire hectare of lowland forest. They flick through the understory — the dwarf birch and stunted rowan that lean over the hollow like a closed hand — with a speed and agitation that makes the dipper look calm. They are everywhere: in the branches, on the moss, on the rock walls, in the crevices, under the overhangs, in the gaps between roots. Their sharp *tik-tik* calls are the loudest sounds in the hollow, and they are constant — not continuous, but frequent enough that the silence between drips is regularly punctuated by wren-noise.

The wrens are here because the hollow is, paradoxically, an extraordinarily productive environment for small insectivorous birds. The midge larvae and cranefly larvae that thread through the moss are present in densities that border on the obscene. Every crease in the moss mat, every fold of lichen on the rock, every film of moisture on a leaf surface contains prey. The wrens do not need to search. They need only to *look*, and the food is there. The hollow provides an all-you-can-eat buffet of soft-bodied invertebrates in a space so small that a wren can patrol its entire territory in under a minute.

This productivity has consequences. Wren territories in normal habitat are large — a pair might defend several hundred square meters of hedgerow or woodland edge. In the hollow, territories have compressed to the point of collapse. Each wren holds a territory of perhaps a few square meters — a single bush, a single stretch of rock wall, a single hummock of moss. The territories are contiguous, packed edge to edge with no buffer zones, and the boundaries are maintained by constant vocal display. The *tik-tik* calls are not song — not the elaborate, cascading trill that wrens are famous for — but contact calls, territorial markers, the acoustic equivalent of a dog pissing on a fence post. *I am here. This is mine. Stay on your side.*

The wrens are stressed. This is a claim that can be made with some biological precision. Chronically elevated corticosterone levels — the avian stress hormone — are associated with high-density populations, intense territorial competition, and reduced per-capita resources. The wrens of the hollow, by every physiological measure available, are living in a state of sustained territorial pressure. They are not starving — the food is abundant — but they are *crowded*, and crowding in wrens produces aggression, and aggression in a space this small produces a constant, low-level background of hostility that never resolves into open conflict because there is no room for conflict. The wrens cannot fight. They can only call, and flick, and posture, and maintain their boundaries with the furious, unrelenting energy of animals that have been compressed into a space too small for their behavioral repertoire.

They are, in this sense, the opposite of the frogs. The frogs have resolved the problem of density through stillness — through the reduction of need to the point where proximity is irrelevant. The wrens have not resolved the problem. They have *intensified* it, by maintaining a behavioral system — territorial defense, mate attraction, nest building, chick rearing — that requires space, in a place that does not have space. The result is a population of birds that are perpetually on edge, perpetually calling, perpetually flicking from perch to perch with the nervous energy of animals that cannot settle.

And yet they persist. They breed. They raise chicks — tiny, gaping, yellow-mouthed chicks in moss-lined nests tucked into crevices in the rock wall — and the chicks fledge and join the crowd, and the crowd grows denser, and the calling grows more frequent, and the boundaries grow tighter, and the wrens do not leave because there is nowhere to leave to. The hollow is the best habitat for miles — the most productive, the most sheltered, the most reliably provisioned. The wrens are trapped by their own success, compressed by their own abundance, living in a paradise that has become a pressure cooker.

They are the hollow's noise. Its restlessness. Its one remaining note of discord, played at high volume and without pause. Everything else in the hollow has achieved equilibrium. The wrens are still *trying*.

## V. The Small Things

Beneath the notice of the frogs and the salamanders and the birds — beneath the notice of anything with a spine — there is the water itself, and the water is *full*.

A single cupful of peat water from the hollow contains, conservatively, several hundred copepods. These are crustaceans of the order Copepoda, most likely members of the genera *Cyclops* or *Acanthocyclops*, each one between half a millimeter and two millimeters in length. They are translucent, with a single dark eyespot (the eponymous "cyclops" eye) and a teardrop-shaped body propelled by rapid vibrations of their antennae. Under magnification, their movement is frantic — a constant, jerky swimming that carries them through the water column in short, erratic bursts. To the naked eye, they are invisible. To the salamander's lateral line, they are a faint, continuous tremor in the water — a background hum of life so dense it is closer to a texture than to individual signals.

The copepods eat bacteria and detritus and smaller copepods. They are eaten, in turn, by everything larger than themselves. They are the base of the hollow's food web — the link between the microbial world and the world of things big enough to see. Their population density is staggering: in the absence of fish (there are no fish in the hollow — the water is too shallow, too acidic, too isolated), the copepods have achieved numbers that a stream or pond ecologist would find difficult to believe. They are not counted in individuals per liter. They are counted in individuals per *milliliter*.

Alongside the copepods: water fleas (*Daphnia* and related genera, though in this acidic environment more likely *Chydorus* or *Alona*, small chydorid cladocerans that creep along surfaces rather than swimming freely). Seed shrimp (ostracods — tiny bivalved crustaceans that look like animated sesame seeds, scuttling through the moss on jointed legs). Amphipods, if the pH permits — small, laterally compressed shrimp-like animals that browse on detritus. The water is not a medium. It is a *suspension* — a thick, dark, living fluid that is more animal than water by dry weight in some samples.

The midge larvae — *Chironomus* and related genera — are the architects of the moss mat. They build tubes of silk and detritus in the sphagnum, threading through the living tissue of the moss in numbers that defy estimation. Their blood contains hemoglobin — unusual for insects — which allows them to survive in the low-oxygen environment of the peat water, and which gives them a red color that is visible when they wriggle. They are the primary food of the frogs, the salamanders, the wrens, the dippers. They are the fuel that powers the entire system. Without the midge larvae, the hollow would collapse.

The beetles — small, black, patient — cling to the undersides of every leaf. They are mostly members of the family Hydrophilidae (water scavenger beetles) or Dytiscidae (diving beetles), though in the hollow's acidic, nutrient-poor water, the species composition is likely skewed toward the smaller, hardier genera. They are predators and scavengers, feeding on midge larvae, copepods, and each other. They do not fly. There is no reason to fly. The hollow provides everything they need, and the world outside the hollow — the bare granite, the thin soil, the cold wind — is inhospitable to the point of lethality. The beetles are as trapped as the wrens, though they do not experience it as trapping. They experience it as *everything*.

The flatworms — planarians and their relatives — glide across the wet rock faces on carpets of cilia, ingesting bacteria and small invertebrates through a pharynx that can be extended from the middle of their body. They are hermaphroditic. They reproduce by fission when conditions are good — tearing themselves in half, each half regenerating into a complete animal — and sexually when conditions are stable, which in the hollow is always. Their populations are limited only by the availability of bacterial biofilm on the rock surfaces, and the biofilm is unlimited. The rock never dries. The bacteria never stop growing. The flatworms never stop eating.

The snails — small, dark-shelled pulmonates — creep across the moss on muscular feet, scraping algal films from the surfaces of the sphagnum with their radulae. They are hermaphroditic. They are hermaphroditic in the way that everything small and isolated is hermaphroditic, because finding a mate in a world this dark and this dense is easier when every individual you encounter is a potential mate. Their shells are thin — calcium is scarce in the acidic water — and their bodies are soft and dark and slow.

The caddisfly larvae build their cases from moss fragments, gluing together tiny pieces of sphagnum with silk secreted from glands in their heads. The cases are portable shelters, carried on the larva's back like a snail's shell, and they are — like everything in the hollow — the color of the substrate. A caddisfly larva on a moss hummock is invisible. It looks like a small, irregular lump of moss. It *is* a small, irregular lump of moss, with an animal inside. The larvae graze on algae and detritus, and they are eaten by the dippers, which pick them off the rock faces with quick, precise movements of the bill.

All of these animals — the copepods, the water fleas, the seed shrimp, the midge larvae, the beetles, the flatworms, the snails, the caddisfly larvae — are present in densities that would be remarkable in any habitat and are *unprecedented* in a body of water this small. They are the hollow's true inhabitants — not the frogs, not the salamanders, not the birds, which are merely the most visible expressions of a productivity that begins at the microbial level and extends upward through every trophic level with no gaps, no losses, no waste.

The water is full. The moss is full. The rock is full. Every surface, every crevice, every film of moisture is occupied. There is no empty space in the hollow except the space you occupy, and the hollow is working on that — the moss is already growing around your boots, the copepods are already investigating the disturbance your presence has created in the water, the midges are already laying eggs in the film of moisture your breath has condensed on the rock. Given time — and the hollow has nothing but time — even your space will be filled.

## VI. The Moss Itself

The sphagnum is not one organism. It is a *nation*.

Each hummock — each cushion of green-black, corpse-pale, bottle-green moss — is a colony of genetically identical individuals, clones propagated by fragmentation over timescales that make the frogs look ephemeral. A single hummock of *Sphagnum fuscum* or *Sphagnum capillifolium* (the species most likely in this cold, acidic, high-altitude environment) can persist for centuries, growing upward at a rate of perhaps one millimeter per year while dying at the base, so that the living surface is always the same height while the dead tissue beneath compresses into peat.

The moss is the hollow's foundation — literally. The peat beneath the water is *dead sphagnum*, compressed and anaerobic and preserved by the very acidity that the living moss creates. Sphagnum acidifies its own environment through cation exchange — it releases hydrogen ions and absorbs calcium and magnesium, making the water more acidic and less hospitable to competing plants. This is not a side effect. It is a *strategy*. The moss makes the hollow unlivable for other plants, and in doing so, it makes the hollow its own. The dwarf birch and rowan that lean over the hollow grow in the peat only at the margins, where the sphagnum's grip is weakest. In the center, there is nothing but moss and water and the animals that live in both.

The moss is alive in a way that challenges the concept of individuality. Each plant — each tiny, branching stem with its clusters of leaves — is connected to its neighbors by physical contact, by shared water films, by the continuous capillary network that draws water up from the peat and distributes it across the surface of the hummock. The moss does not have roots. It absorbs water directly through its leaves, and it holds water in specialized hyaline cells — large, empty, dead cells that make up most of the leaf's volume and can hold twenty times the moss's dry weight in water. These cells are the reason the hollow is wet. The moss is a sponge, and the hollow is the water the sponge holds.

The moss does not sleep. It does not rest. It photosynthesizes continuously, whenever light is available, and in the hollow, light is available in a dim, exhausted trickle for perhaps twelve hours a day in summer and not at all in winter. During the dark months, the moss persists on stored carbohydrates and the slow, anaerobic metabolism of its deeper tissues. It does not die back. It does not go dormant in any visible sense. It simply *slows*, reducing its metabolic rate to match the available energy, and waits — not in the active sense, but in the way that all organisms wait when there is nothing to do but persist — for the light to return.

The sundews nestle in the creases between moss hummocks. *Drosera rotundifolia*, the round-leaved sundew — small rosettes of red-green leaves, each one covered in sticky, glandular tentacles that glisten in the dim light like dew. The dew is mucilage — a polysaccharide glue that traps small insects. When a midge or a small beetle lands on a tentacle, it sticks. The tentacle curls inward, slowly, over the course of minutes, bringing the prey into contact with more tentacles, which also curl. The leaf produces digestive enzymes — proteases, phosphatases — that dissolve the prey's soft tissues, and the nutrients are absorbed directly through the leaf surface. This is carnivory, and it exists because the peat water is too acidic and too nutrient-poor for most plants to extract enough nitrogen and phosphorus from the substrate. The sundew supplements its diet with animal protein. It is, in a sense, the hollow's smallest predator — a plant that has turned to hunting because the soil cannot feed it.

The sundews are abundant. In the creases between moss hummocks, in the thin films of water that collect on the surface of the peat mat, they form scattered constellations of red-green rosettes, each one no larger than a coin. They catch midges and small flies and the occasional careless copepod that blunders onto a tentacle. They digest their prey over days. They are not fast. Nothing in the hollow is fast.

The cranberry vines thread through the moss like dark veins. *Vaccinium oxycoccos*, the small cranberry — a wiry, creeping vine with small, dark, evergreen leaves and thin stems that root at every node. The vines bind the moss mat together, creating a structural network that gives the surface its coherence. Without the cranberries, the moss mat would be a loose aggregate of hummocks, easily disturbed. With them, it is a single continuous fabric, tough enough to walk on (though your foot sinks), resilient enough to recover from the rare disturbances (a falling branch, a salamander's lunge, a visitor's boot).

The cranberries flower in early summer — small, pink, reflexed flowers that look like tiny shooting stars — and produce red berries in autumn. The berries are eaten by the birds. The wrens take some. The dippers ignore them. The berries that are not eaten fall into the moss and rot, and the nutrients return to the peat, and the loop closes again.

## VII. The Beetle on the Underside of the Leaf

There is a beetle on the underside of a birch leaf, three feet above the water, in the lower branches of a dwarf birch that leans over the hollow's northern edge.

The beetle is four millimeters long, black, oval, convex. Its elytra — the hardened forewings that form the beetle's shell — are smooth and slightly reflective, catching the dim amber-green light and returning it as a faint, oily sheen. Its legs are short and strong, equipped with tarsal claws that grip the leaf's epidermis with mechanical precision. Its antennae are clubbed, short, held close to the head. Its mouthparts are chewing — mandibles designed for crushing soft-bodied prey.

The beetle has been on this leaf for six hours.

It is not resting. It is *waiting* — actively, attentively, with the full engagement of its sensory systems. Its antennae sample the air for chemical signals: the kairomones of midge larvae, the pheromones of other beetles, the faint organic signature of the moss below. Its compound eyes — small, faceted, covering most of the head — monitor the leaf's surface for movement. Its legs are in contact with the leaf, and through them it detects vibrations: the footsteps of other insects, the impact of falling droplets, the slow, rhythmic sway of the branch in the hollow's nearly nonexistent air currents.

The beetle is a predator. It eats midge larvae, small caterpillars, aphids, other beetles smaller than itself. It hunts by waiting — by selecting a leaf with high prey traffic and remaining motionless until prey comes within range. This is the same strategy employed by the frogs, the salamanders, the sundews. In the hollow, waiting is the dominant mode of predation. Movement is expensive, and prey is abundant enough that it comes to you. The beetle is not lazy. It is *optimal*.

When prey arrives — a midge larva, wriggling across the leaf's surface on its way to somewhere else — the beetle moves with a speed that is startling in the context of the hollow's general stillness. The attack takes less than a second: a lunge, a bite, the crunch of mandibles through chitin. The midge larva is consumed in minutes. The beetle cleans its mouthparts with its forelegs — a precise, almost delicate grooming behavior — and resumes its position, and resumes its stillness, and waits again.

The beetle has been alive for approximately eight months. It hatched from an egg laid in a crevice in the bark of the birch, spent its larval stage in the wet moss at the base of the tree, pupated in a cell of compacted moss fibers, and emerged as an adult in late spring. It will live through the winter — hibernating in a crevice in the bark, its metabolism reduced to near-zero, its body chemistry adjusted to prevent ice crystal formation in its cells — and emerge again in spring, and mate, and die. Its life is a single cycle: hatch, feed, pupate, emerge, feed, hibernate, emerge, mate, die. The cycle is short. The cycle is complete. The cycle has been repeated, in this hollow, by this species, for thousands of generations, in a darkness that has not changed, in a cold that has not shifted, in a stillness that has not been broken.

The beetle does not know this. The beetle does not know anything. But its body is an archive — a repository of genetic information refined by selection into a form so precisely suited to this place that the beetle cannot survive anywhere else. It is not a generalist. It is a specialist, exquisitely adapted to the specific conditions of the hollow: the temperature, the humidity, the prey community, the light levels, the chemistry of the water and the moss and the stone. Remove the beetle from the hollow and it dies. It is not a beetle that lives in the hollow. It is a beetle that *is* the hollow, expressed in chitin and muscle and nerve, a small black node in the network of life that fills every available space.

## VIII. The Flatworm on the Stone

On the wet granite face, three feet above the water, in a film of moisture that is perpetually renewed by the stone's slow exudation, a flatworm glides.

It is twelve millimeters long, two millimeters wide, less than half a millimeter thick. It is the color of the wet stone — dark grey, almost black, with a faint iridescence where the dim light catches the cilia covering its ventral surface. It moves by ciliary gliding — the coordinated beating of thousands of microscopic hair-like structures that propel it across the rock on a thin layer of mucus. The movement is smooth, continuous, unhurried. The flatworm does not stop. It does not pause. It glides, always, in a slow, meandering path across the rock face, following the gradient of the biofilm — the thin layer of bacteria and algae and dissolved organic matter that coats every wet surface in the hollow.

The flatworm is a planarian — a member of the class Turbellaria, though the taxonomy of these animals is perpetually contested. It has no circulatory system, no respiratory system, no anus. Gas exchange and nutrient distribution occur by diffusion across its thin, flat body. It ingests food through a muscular pharynx that can be everted from a pore on its ventral surface, and it excretes waste through the same opening. Its nervous system is a pair of cerebral ganglia — a primitive brain — connected to two longitudinal nerve cords. It has eyespots — simple photoreceptors that can detect light and dark but cannot form images. It uses these to orient away from light, toward darkness, which in the hollow means toward the deeper crevices in the rock, the undersides of overhangs, the places where the biofilm is thickest and the moisture most reliable.

The flatworm eats bacteria. It grazes on the biofilm like a tiny, living lawnmower, ingesting the bacterial cells and digesting them in its branched intestine. It is, in the hierarchy of the hollow's food web, close to the bottom — a primary consumer, feeding on the microbial base of the ecosystem. But it is not at the bottom. The bacteria are at the bottom, and the bacteria feed on the dissolved organic carbon in the water, and the dissolved organic carbon comes from the peat, and the peat comes from the moss, and the moss comes from the light. The flatworm is four steps removed from the sun, and in the hollow's dim, filtered light, the sun is a rumor rather than a presence.

The flatworm is hermaphroditic, as mentioned. It reproduces both sexually — exchanging sperm with another flatworm through a mutual insemination process that is, by invertebrate standards, surprisingly elaborate — and asexually, by transverse fission. In the fission process, the flatworm attaches its posterior end to the substrate and pulls forward with its anterior end until it tears in half. Each half regenerates the missing parts. The anterior half grows a new tail. The posterior half grows a new head, complete with eyespots and cerebral ganglia. This process takes days, and the result is two genetically identical flatworms, each fully functional, each ready to glide and graze and eventually fission again.

The implications of this reproductive strategy are strange and philosophically unsettling. A flatworm that reproduces by fission has no single point of origin. It is, in a meaningful sense, the same individual as its parent — and its parent's parent, and its parent's parent's parent, back through an unbroken chain of fission events that extends for as long as the population has existed. The flatworm gliding across the rock face is not a new organism. It is a *continuation* — a fragment of an older organism that split, and split again, and split again, each fragment carrying the full complement of genetic information and regenerative capacity. The flatworm is, in this sense, immortal — not in the sense that any individual cell or tissue persists indefinitely, but in the sense that the *lineage* persists, unbroken, through a process of continuous self-division and renewal.

The flatworm does not know it is immortal. The flatworm does not know it is a flatworm. It glides, and grazes, and fissions, and glides, and the rock face is its world, and the biofilm is its food, and the moisture is its medium, and the darkness is its shelter, and it is — like everything else in the hollow — a perfect, closed loop of function and persistence, a knot in the fabric of the place, a small, flat, gliding expression of the hollow's fundamental principle: *fill every space, waste nothing, change nothing, persist*.

## IX. The Snail

There is a snail on a hummock of sphagnum, moving.

The word "moving" should be understood in context. The snail's rate of movement is approximately one millimeter per minute — a pace so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness over any timeframe shorter than an hour. But it *is* moving, and this distinguishes it from the frogs, which are not moving, and the salamanders, which are not moving, and the beetles, which are not moving. The snail is, by the hollow's standards, *active*.

It is a small snail — eight millimeters in shell diameter, with a thin, dark, translucent shell through which the body is faintly visible. The shell is a coil of perhaps four whorls, fragile, easily crushed. The body within is dark grey, moist, muscular. The snail's foot — the broad, flat organ of locomotion — is in contact with the moss surface, and it propels the snail forward through a series of muscular contractions that pass along the foot in waves, each wave pushing against the substrate with the assistance of a thin layer of mucus. The mucus is secreted continuously, and the snail leaves a trail of it behind — a faint, glistening track on the moss that dries (slowly, in this humidity, almost imperceptibly) and marks the snail's passage.

The snail is grazing. Its radula — a ribbon of chitinous teeth, arranged in rows, working like a file — scrapes across the surface of the moss, removing the thin layer of algae and bacteria that coats the sphagnum leaves. The radula is housed in the snail's mouth, on the ventral surface of its head, and it works continuously, back and forth, rasping away the microbial film with a persistence that is, in its own way, impressive. The snail does not stop eating. It eats as it moves, and it moves as it eats, and the two activities are so tightly coupled that they are effectively the same activity.

The snail's sensory world is chemical and tactile. It has two pairs of tentacles on its head — the upper pair longer, with eyespots at the tips, the lower pair shorter and primarily chemosensory. The eyespots are simple — they detect light and dark, not images — and in the hollow's perpetual twilight, they provide little information. The chemosensory tentacles are more important. They sample the air and the substrate for chemical signals: the presence of food (algal films, bacterial biofilms), the presence of other snails (potential mates), the presence of danger (the chemical signature of a predator, though in the hollow there are few predators that specialize on snails). The snail's nervous system is a ring of ganglia around the esophagus — a simple brain, by any standard, but sufficient for the snail's needs. It does not need to see. It needs to taste, and touch, and move, and eat, and it does all of these things with a slow, patient, unhurried competence that is perfectly matched to the hollow's pace.

The snail is hermaphroditic. It can mate with any other snail of its species, and in the hollow's dense population, encounters are frequent. Mating is a mutual process — both snails exchange sperm, both are fertilized, both lay eggs. The eggs are small, gelatinous, deposited in a crevice in the moss, and they hatch into miniature snails that begin grazing immediately. The population is stable — not growing, not shrinking, maintained at a density determined by the availability of algal food and the space available on the moss hummocks. The snails, like everything else in the hollow, have achieved equilibrium.

The snail's life is measured in slime trails. Each trail is a record of where the snail has been — a thin, drying line of mucus on the moss surface that persists for hours or days before being absorbed by the sphagnum or washed away by a droplet. The trails crisscross the hummocks like the lines on a map, each one a snail's journey from one feeding site to another. The trails do not intersect with intention. The snails do not follow each other, do not avoid each other, do not communicate through their mucus in any meaningful way. The trails are simply *traces* — the incidental record of a life lived at one millimeter per minute, in the dark, in the wet, in the cold, in a place where there is no destination and no urgency and no reason to be anywhere other than where you are.

## X. The Water Itself

The water is not alive. But it is not *not* alive.

The distinction matters. The peat water of the hollow — dark as tea, acidic, cold, still — is a medium, but it is a medium so densely populated with living organisms that its bulk properties are altered by their presence. It is thicker than pure water — not measurably so, not in a way that would register on a viscometer, but in a way that you can *feel* when you move your hand through it. The water has body. It has *presence*. It is not empty space. It is full space — full of copepods and bacteria and dissolved organic matter and the metabolic byproducts of ten thousand small lives, and this fullness gives the water a quality that pure water does not have. It is not a liquid. It is a *habitat*.

The water's chemistry is extreme. The pH is low — perhaps 4.0, perhaps lower. This is the work of the sphagnum, which acidifies its environment as a competitive strategy. The low pH excludes most fish (there are none), most amphibians with complex life cycles (the frogs and salamanders here are acid-tolerant specialists), most aquatic insects with calcium carbonate shells (the snails have thin, fragile shells for this reason). The water is also rich in dissolved organic carbon — humic acids, fulvic acids, the breakdown products of centuries of accumulated peat. These compounds give the water its dark color, its tea-like appearance, and they absorb what little light penetrates the canopy, so that the water column below the surface is functionally lightless. A copepod swimming ten centimeters below the surface is in darkness.

The water is cold — four degrees Celsius, always. This is the temperature at which water is densest, and the hollow's water maintains this temperature through a combination of insulation (the peat mat, the moss cover, the canopy), thermal mass (the granite walls, which buffer against temperature swings), and the absence of any heat source (no direct sunlight, no geothermal input, no warm inflow). The water is, thermodynamically, a stable system — a body of cold, dark, acidic liquid that has achieved equilibrium with its environment and will maintain that equilibrium indefinitely.

The oxygen content of the water is variable. Near the surface, where the water is in contact with the air, oxygen is abundant — the water is fully saturated at this temperature, which means it holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water would. In the deeper layers, near the peat, oxygen is scarce — the decomposition of organic matter consumes oxygen faster than it can diffuse downward. The midge larvae, with their hemoglobin-rich blood, are adapted to this low-oxygen environment. The copepods, with their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, can extract sufficient oxygen from even the deeper water. The salamander, lying on the peat, supplements its cutaneous respiration with occasional trips to the surface — a slow, deliberate rise through the water column, a gulp of air, a slow descent. These trips are rare. The salamander's oxygen requirements are so low that a single breath can sustain it for hours.

The water is still. This cannot be overstated. There is no current, no flow, no circulation beyond the slow, density-driven mixing caused by the exudation of water from the rock walls. A droplet falls from a leaf and strikes the surface — *plik* — and the ripples spread outward in concentric circles and are damped almost immediately by the water's viscosity and the density of the peat mat beneath. Within seconds, the surface is flat again. The water does not remember the disturbance. It returns to stillness the way a held breath returns to silence — completely, instantly, without residue.

The water is the hollow's body. Everything else — the moss, the stone, the canopy, the animals — is an appendage, an organ, a surface feature. The water is the *thing itself*: the cold, dark, still, living fluid that fills the bowl in the rock and has filled it for longer than anything living remembers. The water is the reason the hollow exists. Without the water, the bowl is just a depression in granite — a feature, a curiosity, a place where rain collects and evaporates. With the water, the bowl is a *world* — a closed, self-sustaining, self-regulating ecosystem that has achieved a completeness so total that the word "ecosystem" feels inadequate. It is not a system. It is a *being* — a single, continuous, wet, dark, cold, living thing, and the water is its blood.

## XI. The Dipper's Chick

In the nest behind the rock face — a dome of wet moss tucked into a crevice where the granite exudes its perpetual film of moisture — there is a chick.

It is eight days old. Its eyes are open. Its body is covered in dark grey down, through which the pins of its first true feathers are beginning to emerge. Its bill is disproportionately large, yellow at the gape, and it opens — wide, wider, impossibly wide — whenever the nest vibrates with the footfall of a returning parent.

The chick is cold. Not hypothermic — the parent birds brood it regularly, and the nest's moss dome provides insulation — but *cold* in the way that everything in the hollow is cold. The ambient temperature is four degrees. The chick's body temperature is thirty-eight degrees. The gradient between the chick and its environment is thirty-four degrees, and maintaining this gradient requires a constant, ferocious expenditure of metabolic energy. The chick eats — caddisfly larvae, midge larvae, small crustaceans, delivered by both parents in a continuous relay — and converts the food into heat and growth. Its growth rate is extraordinary. It has doubled its body weight in the past three days. It will double it again in the next three.

The chick's world is the nest. It has never seen the hollow. It has never seen the water, the moss, the frogs, the salamanders, the beetles, the snails, the flatworms, the copepods. Its sensory experience is limited to the interior of the moss dome: the smell of wet moss, the taste of regurgitated caddisfly larvae, the sound of its parents' calls, the vibration of their footfalls on the rock, the warmth of their bodies during brooding. The nest is dark — darker than the hollow, if that is possible — and the chick's eyes, though open, receive almost no visual information. It navigates by sound and vibration and the chemical signature of its parents' presence.

The chick is one of four. Its siblings are identical — same age, same size, same downy grey bodies, same gaping yellow mouths. They compete for food, but the competition is mild — the parents deliver food in sufficient quantity that all four chicks are fed, and the dominance hierarchy that develops in some bird species (where older or larger chicks monopolize the food and younger chicks starve) has not emerged here. The hollow provides. The chicks eat. They grow.

In six more days, the chick will fledge. It will emerge from the nest into a world that is dark and wet and cold and still, and it will bob — because it is a dipper, and dippers bob — and it will blink its white eyelids, and it will begin the restless, kinetic, anomalous life that dippers live in a place that rewards stillness. It will find a rock, and stand on it, and bob, and the frogs will not notice, and the salamanders will not notice, and the wrens will notice and call and flick and return to their own frenetic business, and the hollow will absorb the chick into its fabric — another knot, another node, another small, warm, beating concentration of life in a place that is already full.

The chick does not know any of this. The chick knows only the nest, the food, the warmth, the dark. The rest — the hollow, the world, the life that waits — is coming, and the chick will meet it with the same unreflective, instinctive competence that every animal in the hollow brings to its existence. It will not marvel. It will not question. It will bob, and blink, and call, and feed, and the hollow will hold it, as it holds everything, in its cold, dark, saturated embrace.

## XII. The Beetle's Winter

The beetle on the underside of the leaf — the four-millimeter black beetle, patient and still — will not survive the winter as an active animal. It will survive as a *suspended* one.

As the days shorten — not that the hollow's perpetual gloom changes much, but the angle of the light shifts, and the temperature, which never rises above four degrees, begins its slow, almost imperceptible descent toward one degree, then zero, then just below — the beetle responds to cues that are older than the hollow itself. Photoperiod. Temperature. The chemical composition of the leaf it stands on, which changes as the birch prepares to shed its leaves.

The beetle leaves the leaf. It walks — a short journey, perhaps a meter — to the bark of the birch, and it finds a crevice, and it enters, and it stops. The stopping is not sleep. It is not hibernation in the mammalian sense. It is *diapause* — a state of metabolic suspension so profound that the beetle is, for practical purposes, dead. Its heart stops. Its breathing (through spiracles, the small openings in its exoskeleton) ceases. Its cells produce cryoprotectants — glycerol, sorbitol, antifreeze proteins — that prevent ice crystal formation in its tissues. Its body temperature drops to match the ambient temperature, which may fall below freezing in the crevice, and the beetle's internal fluids supercool — remaining liquid below their normal freezing point, protected by the cryoprotectants from the damage that ice crystals would cause.

The beetle spends the winter in this state. It does not dream. It does not experience time. It is, functionally, a small, black, lifeless object in a crevice in a tree in a dark, cold, wet hollow on a mountain. The winter passes. The light returns — dim, amber-green, exhausted, but *present* — and the temperature rises — slowly, from below freezing to one degree, then two, then three, then four — and the beetle's cells respond to the warming, and the cryoprotectants are metabolized, and the heart restarts, and the spiracles open, and the beetle walks out of the crevice and returns to the leaf and resumes its stillness and its waiting and its patient, predatory existence.

The beetle has done this before. It will do it again, if it survives. The cycle — active summer, suspended winter, active summer — is as much a part of the hollow's rhythm as the dripping water, the falling leaves, the slow deepening of the peat. The beetle does not choose the cycle. The cycle chooses the beetle, and the beetle obeys, and the obedience is perfect, because the beetle has no capacity for disobedience. It is a machine — a beautiful, intricate, exquisitely calibrated machine — and the hollow is its operator, and the winter is the operator's hand on the switch, and the spring is the hand lifting, and the beetle walks out of the crevice and onto the leaf and waits for the midge larva that will come, because the midge larva always comes, because the hollow always provides.

## XIII. The Peat

The peat is the hollow's memory.

It is six feet deep. It has been accumulating for — the number is difficult to establish with precision, but the rate of accumulation (approximately one millimeter per year, compressed to perhaps a quarter of that by the weight of the overlying layers) suggests an age measured in *thousands of years*. The peat at the bottom of the basin was alive — was green, growing sphagnum — at a time when the mountain's climate was different, when the treeline was higher or lower, when the species composition of the hollow's community was not what it is now.

The peat preserves. In the anaerobic, acidic environment of the waterlogged basin, decomposition is arrested. Organic matter that falls into the water — dead frogs, dead salamanders, dead beetles, dead moss, dead leaves — does not rot. It *sinks*, and it is buried by the slow accumulation of new material, and it is preserved in a state of suspended decay, its tissues intact, its cellular structure visible under microscopy, its DNA potentially recoverable. The peat is an archive — a stratified record of every organism that has lived and died in the hollow, layer upon layer, each layer a snapshot of a moment in the hollow's history.

The peat is not inert. Even in its anaerobic depths, microbial life persists — methanogenic archaea, sulfate-reducing bacteria, the slow, patient decomposers that work without oxygen, breaking down organic matter over timescales that make the salamander's metabolism look frenetic. These microbes produce methane — a slow, continuous effervescence that rises through the peat and the water column in bubbles so small and so infrequent that they are rarely observed. The methane is the peat's breath — the exhalation of a buried, ancient, still-living substrate that has been decomposing, very slowly, for millennia.

The peat is the foundation of everything. The moss grows on it. The water sits in it. The salamanders lie on it. The frogs sit on the moss that grows on it. The wrens nest in the crevices above it. The dippers pick larvae from the rocks that emerge from it. The beetles cling to the leaves of the trees that root in it. The peat is the hollow's ground, its bed, its floor, its memory, its future. Everything that dies in the hollow becomes peat. Everything that lives in the hollow lives on peat. The loop is closed. The loop has always been closed. The peat is the proof.

## XIV. The Whole Thing

You stand in the hollow. The water is at your ankles. The moss cushions your feet. A frog watches you and does not move. A droplet falls — *plik* — and the silence resumes.

You are aware of being surrounded. Not threateningly — *thoroughly*. The life here has no room for you and no hostility toward you. It has only a perfect indifference born of perfect fullness. You are the only empty space in the hollow. Everything else is taken.

The frog on the hummock has not moved in eleven days. The salamander in the water has not moved in a year. The flatworm on the rock face glides at a pace that cannot be observed. The snail on the moss moves at one millimeter per minute. The beetle on the leaf waits. The copepods in the water swim in their frantic, jerky bursts, too small to see. The midge larvae thread through the moss in their thousands. The peat deepens by the width of a hair.

The dipper bobs. The wrens call. The droplets fall. The water seeps. The moss grows. The beetles cling. The salamanders wait. The frogs sit. The flatworms glide. The snails graze. The copepods swim. The midge larvae wriggle. The peat deepens.

Nothing changes. Nothing has changed. Nothing will change.

And yet — everything is alive. Every cubic inch of water, every fold of moss, every film of moisture on rock is *occupied*. The hollow is not still because it is dead. It is still because it is *full* — because every niche is filled, every surface claimed, every resource utilized, every organism in its place, doing its thing, at its pace, in its time. The stillness is not an absence. It is a *presence* — the presence of life so dense, so complete, so perfectly equilibrated that it has achieved the appearance of stasis while being, in every cell and every droplet and every grain of peat, furiously, continuously, unrelentingly alive.

The frog blinks. The nictitating membrane slides across its eye, catching the dim light, and for an instant — a fraction of a second — the frog is visible: a small, dark, wet, living thing on a dark, wet, living surface, in a dark, wet, living place. Then the membrane retracts, and the frog is still again, and the stillness resumes, and the hollow holds.

You are the only empty space. The hollow is working on that. Given time — and the hollow has nothing but time — even your space will be filled. The moss will grow around your boots. The copepods will investigate your disturbance. The midges will lay eggs in the moisture your breath has left on the rock. The beetles will find the crevice your shoulder made in the moss. The salamander will taste your chemical signature in the water and file it away in whatever passes for memory in a brain the size of a pea.

You will leave. The hollow will not notice. The frog will not watch you go. The droplets will fall — one, then another, then a long pause, then three in quick succession — and the silence will close over your absence the way water closes over a stone: completely, instantly, without residue.

The hollow persists. The water seeps. The moss grows. The peat deepens. The frogs sit. The salamanders wait. The beetles cling. The wrens call. The dipper bobs. The flatworms glide. The snails graze. The copepods swim. The midge larvae wriggle. The sundews glisten. The cranberries thread. The lichens crust. The stone exudes.

Nothing changes.

Nothing has changed.

Nothing will.

And the water — dark, cold, still, full — holds everything, and is everything, and remembers everything, in its slow, deep, peat-dark way, and the hollow is complete, and the hollow is full, and the hollow is alive.
