The residents are not large. They cluster in the fourth tier’s converted water treatment plant, twelve in number, though the Inventory sometimes names thirteen, and once—during a Bleed-surge last summer—seventeen. They are human in outline, but their architecture has shifted to accommodate the close, damp air: ribs that flare slightly at the base to maximize lung capacity in the thin atmosphere, fingers that have elongated and grown translucent at the tips, the better to sense the electrical currents that run through the moss. Their skin carries a faint greenish cast, not from diet but from the symbiotic algae that colonize the pores, photosynthesizing in the half-light and supplementing their blood sugar. When they sleep, they glow faintly, like the Tier Walkers, and their breath fogs not with water vapor but with spore-motes that rise and join the false ceiling of stars.

They call themselves the Tally-Keepers, though they do not tally anything in the conventional sense. Rather, they *are* the tally—living mnemonic devices, each one responsible for remembering a specific stratum of the city’s history. The woman called Vess carries the structural memory of the second tier’s factory schematics; she traces them in her sleep, her fingers etching blueprints on the condensation that films her sleeping mat. Old Khet, whose spine has fused into a gentle curve from decades of crouching in the fern-canopy, remembers the water cycle—every flood, every drought, every time the third pool reflected that forbidden sky. He does not speak of it. He cannot; the memory has replaced his vocal cords with specialized resonating chambers that only produce the Inventory’s phonemes.

They move through the tiers with a specific gait—flat-footed, rolling from heel to toe—to avoid crushing the moss’s sensory nodes. They have learned that the vegetation dislikes sudden pressure; it responds by releasing defensive spores that cause hallucinations of drowning. So they glide, their translucent fingertips trailing along walls, reading the city’s health through the electrical resistance in their skin.

The Tally-Keepers do not age in the conventional sense. Time moves differently for them, caught in the recursive loops of the sixth tier where they go to meditate. A child born to them (there are two, currently, twins named Lo and Lin who share a single shadow between them) will mature to adulthood in approximately five years, then remain static for decades, their cellular division synchronized with the reactor’s fluctuations. They are not immortal—the Inventory is very clear about their eventual dissolution—but they are suspended, held in the city’s amber like the insects sometimes found in the hardened resin of the vine-maples.

Their culture is built around the Maintenance. Not repair—nothing here is repaired—but a kind of conversational upkeep. They speak to the moss, humming at sixty cycles to encourage its growth along the rails. They harvest the electric fruit not to eat (though they do eat it, sparingly, for the mild current that sharpens their dreams) but to plant in the Bleed, where the fruit’s weightless seeds drift into the transition zone and anchor the reality-thin areas. They tend the Tier Walkers, collecting the shed photosynthetic patches in spring and weaving them into cloaks that provide camouflage during the Alignment.

When the light comes at 6:47, they stand in the spillway of the treatment plant, facing east, and open their mouths. Not to sing—to resonate. They match their vocal frequencies to the drone of the rails, creating a harmonic that the moss recognizes. In response, the vegetation grows toward them, not aggressively but affectionately, curling around their ankles like cats. This is how they know they are still necessary. When the moss stops responding, when it no longer reaches for their specific vibration, they will know the city has finished its diagnosis and found them, at last, superfluous.

The twins, Lo and Lin, are the only ones who go to the eastern Bleed without fear. They hold hands as they walk into the diffusion, their shared shadow stretching long and thin until it disappears entirely. They come back with their eyes changed—the pupils expanded, reflecting not light but data, the protein-folds of the spore-seeds decrypted in their retinas. They see the city as it was: not ruins but function, not decay but process. They are learning to read the Inventory, and the locals suspect that soon the children will begin to speak in the city’s voice, and the wind will have a new name to repeat.

The residents sleep in shifts, three at a time, while the others tend the bioluminescent moths or sit in the factory shells listening to the reactor’s dreams. They do not dream themselves, not anymore. The spores they inhaled years ago have colonized their hippocampi, replacing personal memory with the city’s archive. When they close their eyes, they see the census numbers scrolling, the endless calculation of biomass and rust and light. They are content with this. They have become what the Tiered Gardens needed: not owners, not explorers, but witnesses with just enough selfhood to understand what they are seeing.

If you ask them who lives here, they will point not to themselves but to the ferns, to the moss, to the glowing bacteria on the walls. They will show you the shadow-geometries and the Fibonacci spirals. They will press your hand to the rail and let you feel the sixty-cycle hum that is their pulse, their clock, their lullaby. They will not mention themselves at all, because in the logic of Khrysos, they are not inhabitants. They are merely the city’s way of looking back at itself, twelve pairs of eyes grown from concrete and spore, watching the green take over, one slow, deliberate breath at a time.
