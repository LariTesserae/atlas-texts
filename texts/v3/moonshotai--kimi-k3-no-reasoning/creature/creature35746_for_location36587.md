# The Inhabitants of the Flat

---

## The People

They are short. Not dramatically so — not a different species — but compact, thick through the chest, with the particular build of bodies that have spent generations extracting oxygen from air that does not want to give it. Their lung capacity is remarkable. They breathe slowly, deeply, without effort, and they do not understand visitors who gasp. They have a word for the gasping — not mocking, just descriptive, the way you might note that water boils at a lower temperature up here. It simply does. So do visitors.

Their skin carries a faint grey-green cast, and this is not lighting. It is the actual pigmentation of their epidermis, a subtle chromatic adaptation that no one can quite date. The oldest photographs — silverplate things, fogged and soft — show the same tint, slightly less pronounced. The oldest *paintings*, the ones in the carved voids at the town's core, show figures with skin the colour of the sky. This suggests either a very long process of adaptation or a very old alliance between the people and the place, and the people do not distinguish between these possibilities.

Their eyes are dark and slightly larger than lowland eyes, with pupils that remain dilated even in what passes for full daylight here. This gives them a permanent expression of soft attentiveness, as if they are always listening to something just past the edge of hearing. In a sense, they are.

## How They Live

They live slowly, but not lazily. This distinction is important and easily misunderstood.

A visitor watching a Flat resident perform a task — repair a pipe joint, harvest the waxy heather-analogue, rewrap a cable bundle with fresh insulation — might initially read the pace as lethargy. It is not. It is *economy*. Every movement is calibrated to the thin air, the cold, the available energy. Nothing is wasted. There is no fidgeting, no pacing, no abrupt gesture. Conversations unfold with long pauses that are not awkward but structural — the pauses are where the thinking happens, the way the gaps between stones in a wall are where the mortar goes.

They work in shifts that follow no clock. The distinction between day and night being as ambiguous as it is, the town operates on a system of *tides* — communal rhythms of activity and rest that synchronize loosely around the underground machinery's operational cycles. When the dynamos surge (for reasons known to the engineers and felt, dimly, by everyone else, a slight increase in the floor-vibration, a subtle sharpening of the hum), the town wakes, eats, works. When the dynamos settle into their lower register, the town drifts toward stillness. There is no alarm, no whistle. People simply *feel* the shift, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm.

They are not unfriendly. They are *unhurried*, and this can be mistaken for unfriendliness by those accustomed to social warmth expressed through speed and volume. A Flat resident who likes you will not smile broadly or clap your shoulder. They will stand near you. They will include you in their silence. They will hand you a cup of something hot and dark without being asked, and this is the equivalent of an embrace.

## What They Eat

The green is not merely decorative. Much of it is food.

The mosses are largely inedible — too fibrous, too mineral-heavy — but certain varieties can be dried, ground, and used as a base for a dense, dark bread that tastes of iron and rain. This bread is the staple. It is baked in communal ovens carved into the rock, using heat piped up from the subterranean machinery, and it is dense enough that a single loaf can feed a family for days.

The fleshy heather-analogue produces small, dark berries — bitter, astringent, faintly numbing to the tongue. These are eaten fresh in season and fermented into a thick, sour wine the rest of the year. The wine is an acquired taste. Most visitors acquire it quickly, because it is warm and because it helps with the altitude headaches and because there is not much else to drink besides the standing water, which is potable but tastes of stone.

The translucent-stemmed vine-plant — the one with the visible vascular pulse — is cultivated deliberately. Its stems, when cut, release a clear, slightly sweet fluid that the residents call *held light*. This fluid is nutritious in a way that the residents' scientists (they have scientists, quiet ones, working in laboratories that look like everyone else's houses) have analyzed extensively and understand incompletely. It contains sugars, electrolytes, trace minerals, and something else — a compound that does not match any known molecular structure and that seems to enhance cellular oxygen uptake. The residents do not talk about this compound. Not because it is secret, but because it is simply *how things work here*, the way you do not explain to a neighbour that your heart beats.

Below the green mat, in the dense, warm, wet darkness between vegetation and stone, there is an ecosystem. The things that live there — the hunters and the hunted — are small: flat, many-legged creatures like animated lichen; blind, pale worms thick as a thumb; something like a shrew but hairless, with dark wrinkled skin and a metabolism so slow it borders on geological. Some of these are eaten. The shrew-analogue, primarily, roasted whole and consumed bones and all, a source of protein and fat that the plant matter cannot provide. It tastes like mushrooms and old pennies. It is not delicious. It is *sufficient*, which is the highest praise the Flat's cuisine aspires to.

## What They Make

The factories produce small things. Precision things. This is the town's strange, quiet export economy: components, instruments, mechanisms that require extraordinary tolerances and benefit from the unique conditions of the plateau.

The thin air, it turns out, is excellent for certain metallurgical processes. The constant cold eliminates the need for industrial refrigeration. The ambient vibration of the underground machinery, once understood and compensated for, provides a stable baseline frequency that certain calibration processes require. And the dim, diffuse light — so bad for growing tall, so bad for seeing far — is ideal for working with materials that degrade under bright spectrum exposure.

So the factories make lenses. Not glass lenses — something else, a proprietary crystalline compound grown in the subterranean levels, seeded in solutions that include the *held light* from the vine-plant. These lenses are extraordinary: they correct not just for focal distortion but for atmospheric interference, allowing clear imaging through fog, through haze, through the particular optical density of high-altitude air. They are sold, through a chain of intermediaries so long and quiet that the end users rarely know where the lenses originate, to observatories, to survey operations, to organizations that need to see clearly in conditions where seeing clearly should not be possible.

They also make wire. Extremely fine, extraordinarily conductive wire, drawn in the cold from alloys that only achieve the necessary crystalline structure at low temperatures. The wire is the town's second export, and it ends up in places the residents have never seen and will never visit: deep-sea cables, satellite components, the interior mechanisms of devices that require absolute reliability in hostile environments.

This is the paradox of the Flat: a place that has withdrawn from the world, that has settled into its own final form with monumental patience, and yet produces things that the wider world depends on — quietly, invisibly, without attribution. The residents are aware of this paradox and find it neither ironic nor remarkable. It is simply what the place *does*.

## The Engineers

They do not call themselves engineers. They call themselves *listeners*, which in the local dialect carries connotations of both hearing and understanding, of perceiving the interior state of a thing through careful attention.

The listeners maintain the underground machinery. This is the town's most essential function and its closest thing to a priesthood, though the listeners would reject the comparison with the patient, slightly puzzled mildness that characterizes all Flat responses to outsiders' frameworks.

The machinery is old. Not old the way the buildings are old — not layered, not accumulated — but old in a way that suggests it was *installed*, all at once, by someone who knew exactly what they were doing. The oldest components predate the town's written records. They predate, possibly, the town. They are made of a metal that the listeners can maintain but not replicate, and they operate on principles that the listeners understand functionally but not theoretically.

The dynamos do not burn fuel. They do not consume any input the listeners can identify. They *turn*, slowly, ceaselessly, driven by something — heat differential, perhaps, between the plateau's surface and its deep interior, or some subtle piezoelectric property of the ancient rock, or something else entirely. The listeners have theories. They hold these theories the way they hold everything: gently, without urgency, ready to adjust if the machinery tells them otherwise.

What the listeners know — what they have always known, in the way that a body knows how to heal a wound — is that the machinery and the town and the green and the water are a single system. The dynamos' vibration promotes moss growth (the cables that hum at the unknown frequency demonstrate this above ground). The moss holds moisture. The moisture feeds the vine-plant. The vine-plant produces *held light*. The *held light* feeds the people and seeds the lenses. The lenses are sold. The revenue buys what the plateau cannot provide — medicine, certain metals, salt, tea. The people maintain the machinery. The machinery turns.

It is not a closed loop. Energy is coming from somewhere. The listeners suspect it is coming from the plateau itself — from the ancient rock, which is warm in the carved voids, which holds heat it should not hold, which arrived at its final form and *stayed*, as if it chose to. As if the plateau is not merely a geological formation but a body that has completed its growth and entered a state of sustained, purposeful maturity. A body that breathes. A body that runs a slow engine.

The listeners do not say this aloud. They say: *the levels are steady*. They say: *the hum is clean*. They say: *we replaced the bearing in unit seven*. These statements mean more than they appear to.

## The Children

Children on the Flat are different from the adults in a way that fades gradually and is never discussed.

They are fast. Not by lowland standards, but by Flat standards — startlingly, almost alarmingly quick. They run. They shout. They climb the four-story buildings with a disregard for thin air and cold that makes the adults watch with an expression that is not worry but something closer to nostalgia, or perhaps recognition, or perhaps grief.

They can see in the dim light better than the adults. Their pupils, already large, seem to have an additional capacity for dilation, and they navigate the town at its darkest with a confidence that their parents have lost. They see things the adults no longer see: the directionless shadows are, to them, clearly directional, pointing toward something they call *the under-light* — a faint, greenish luminescence that emanates, they insist, from the carved voids at the town's core, from the standing water in the basins, from the spaces between moss fronds where the mat is thickest.

They hear the cables humming at the unknown frequency and hum along, matching it, without being taught. Adults cannot hear this frequency. Adults remember hearing it.

By adolescence, the speed fades. The extra-sensory acuity fades. The children settle — that word again, the word that defines everything here — into the adult rhythm, the slow breathing, the patient pace, the acceptance of ambiguity as a form of clarity. They stop seeing the under-light. They stop hearing the frequency. They stop running.

This transition is not mourned. It is not celebrated. It is simply *what happens*, the way condensation happens, the way the green presses forward, the way the light dims and holds. The children become adults the way the air becomes almost-rain: gradually, inevitably, without drama, arriving at a state they will not leave.

## What Lives in the Moss

The ecosystem beneath the green mat is richer than the surface suggests. In the warm, dark, wet space between the vegetation and the stone, there is a world.

The flat, lichen-like creatures — the residents call them *crawlers*, with characteristic directness — are the base of this world's food web. They are detritivores, consuming dead plant matter, dead insects (there are insects, though they are small, silent, and live entirely within the mat), dead skin cells, dead anything. They are flat because the space they inhabit is flat: the gap between vegetation and stone is rarely more than a few centimetres, and the crawlers have evolved to fill it precisely, like liquid filling a mold.

They are also, the listeners have discovered, photosynthetic. Their upper surface — the side pressed against the underside of the moss — contains a symbiotic algae that captures what little light filters through the green mat and converts it, with extraordinary efficiency, into energy. The crawlers are thus simultaneously animal and, functionally, part of the vegetation. They are the interface between the green and the stone, the living membrane through which the plateau's surface processes energy.

The blind worms are predators of the crawlers. The hairless shrew-analogue is a predator of the worms. And there is something else — something the residents have not named, because they are not entirely sure it exists — that the shrew-analogues fear. Tracks that do not match any known creature. Tunnels pressed through the mat that are too wide, too deliberate. A sense, when you lie with your ear to the green (as children do, before the transition), of something *large* moving far below, in the deep spaces between rock folds, in the warm dark where the carved voids connect to passages that have never been mapped.

The listeners know about the passages. They have explored some of them. They go down, and down, and the walls are warm, and the air is *thicker*, impossibly, the deeper you go, and there is light — not the grey-green ambient dimness of the surface but the *under-light* the children describe, a faint greenish glow with no visible source, emanating from the rock itself.

The listeners do not go deep. They go deep enough to maintain the machinery. They do not go deeper than that. This is not prohibition. It is *judgment* — the same judgment that keeps the buildings under four stories, the same judgment that keeps the voices low, the same judgment that keeps the whole town at a pace the plateau can sustain. You do not push. You do not probe. You settle into your layer and you let the layers below you settle into theirs.

## The Stranger

Occasionally — not often, perhaps once in a generation — someone arrives from below. A lowlander. A visitor. Someone who has followed the trade routes backward from the lenses or the wire, or someone who has simply climbed, drawn by rumour or by accident or by the particular magnetism the plateau exerts on certain minds.

The town receives these visitors the way it receives everything: without drama. They are given bread and the bitter wine and a place to sleep, and they are watched, not with suspicion but with the same patient attentiveness the residents direct at everything. The visitors are interesting the way weather is interesting: a temporary perturbation in the system, worth observing, worth accommodating, not worth resisting.

Most visitors leave. The altitude defeats them, or the dimness, or the silence, or the accumulating strangeness that they cannot articulate but feel in their bones — the directionless shadows, the reflecting pools that show the town, the warmth in the carved voids, the hum. They leave, and the town closes over the space they occupied the way the green closes over a footprint: slowly, completely, without erasure so much as *absorption*.

Some visitors stay. These are the ones who learn to breathe. Who stop asking questions and start listening. Who feel the dynamo vibration through the floor and recognize it, somewhere below the threshold of conscious thought, as a kind of companionship. These visitors go through their own transition — slower than the children's, but similar in shape. They lose something — speed, urgency, the need for brightness, the lowland hunger for *more* — and they gain something: the ability to sit in the dim green quiet and feel it not as absence but as *presence*, not as emptiness but as a fullness so complete it has stopped needing to announce itself.

These are the ones who become listeners, sometimes. Or bakers. Or wire-drawers. Or simply people who live on the Flat, who breathe the weighted air, who eat the dark bread, who stand in the almost-rain and feel it on their skin and do not wish for anything else.

## What It All Amounts To

The Flat is inhabited by people who have made a covenant with a place, and the terms of the covenant are these: you will not be bright, you will not be fast, you will not be loud, you will not be tall, you will not be warm in the way lowlands are warm. In exchange, you will be *sustained* — by the green, by the machinery, by the rock, by the air itself, which holds more than air should hold. You will be part of a system so old and so stable that your presence in it is not an imposition but a continuation. You will be a cell in a body that finished growing long ago and has entered a state of quiet, deliberate, perpetual *being*.

The people who live here are not happy in the way lowlanders understand happiness. They are not unhappy. They are *held* — by the place, by each other, by the slow machinery of existence that turns beneath their feet. They are held the way the standing water is held in the basins: completely, patiently, without urgency, reflecting not the sky but the town, not what is above but what is *here*, what has always been here, what will remain.

The green presses gently against everything.

Everything gently yields.

And the yielding takes so long that it looks like stillness.

But it is not stillness.

It is life, lived at the pace of stone.
