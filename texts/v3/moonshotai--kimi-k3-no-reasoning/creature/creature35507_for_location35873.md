### Those Who Live Within the Sound

They are not many. Perhaps eight hundred, perhaps a thousand — a scattered, thinning population sustained by a bowl of vibrating earth and relentless light. They call themselves **the Shen**, but the word is not a name so much as a frequency, a way of saying *we are the ones who resonate*. They do not remember arriving. They do not believe in arrival. They believe they were grown, the way the reeds are grown, the way the sound is grown: from vibration into form, from form into life.

They are adapted to the Hollow the way fish are adapted to water. Their hearing is not damaged — it is **tuned**, calibrated to a world where sound is not an accident but the ground of being. They hear the health of a wall in its hum, the approach of a storm in the shift of harmonics, the presence of a friend in the rhythm of footsteps on tile. They do not speak so much as **modulate**. Their language is a weave of voice and touch, of syllable and scrape, of hummed undertones and knuckles rapped against reed. To converse with them is to be enveloped in polyphony, to feel meaning arriving from multiple directions at once, like light through leaves.

Their bodies are lean, sun-darkened, dusted always with pale clay. They wear garments of woven reed-fiber — fine, dense, faintly rustling — and they move with a deliberate, resonant economy, as if aware that every step sends a signal into the earth. They are not ascetics; they are **attentive**. They live in structures that are instruments first and shelters second, and they maintain those instruments with the care of gardeners tending a crop. They prune the encroaching reeds. They replaster cracked walls. They replace rotting stalks in doorframes and rooftops. They tune their homes the way musicians tune strings, adjusting pitch by adding or removing reed-bundles, by shifting clay masses, by altering the angle of a window to catch the wind differently.

Their days are shaped by the sound’s rhythm. At dawn, when the drone is deepest and sparsest, they rise and move quietly, touching walls, checking for changes in resonance, listening for the subtle fractures that signal decay. By midday, when the bright hour turns the air to shimmering glass, they retreat indoors, into thick-walled interiors where the sound is muffled and the light enters only in thin, blade-like shafts. They eat sparingly — dried reed-seeds, cultivated in small, carefully isolated plots where vibration is controlled; lichen pastes harvested from shaded walls; the occasional tone-hopper, roasted whole, its carapace cracked open like a seed. They drink nothing. There is no water. They derive moisture from the reeds themselves, chewing the tender inner pith of young stalks, and from a thick, mineral-rich clay slurry that they filter through woven fiber and consume in small, ceremonial sips. It is not hydration as outsiders would understand it. It is communion with the earth.

They have no metal, no wheels, no writing in any conventional sense. Their records are kept in **tuning-marks**: notches carved into reed-posts, patterns of knots in fiber cords, arrangements of stones in courtyards — each one encoding a moment when the sound shifted, when a building was retuned, when a child was born, when the Great Resonator pulsed unusually deep. To read these marks is to feel them, to run fingers over grooves and sense the vibration they still hold. History, for the Shen, is not remembered. It is **resonated**.

Their social structure is loose, almost cellular. There are no chiefs, no councils, no laws written in clay. Authority arises from **attunement** — the degree to which an individual can hear, interpret, and respond to the city’s shifting frequencies. A master-builder is not elected; she is recognized, gradually, by the way her buildings hold their tone through seasons of heat and silence. A mediator is not appointed; he is sought out because his voice, when hummed against a wall, calms the frequencies that cause arguments to escalate into violence. Decisions emerge through a process the Shen call **sounding** — a gathering in which individuals speak not in turn but in layers, their voices and taps and hums interweaving until a pattern emerges, a chord that feels right, a resonance that carries the weight of consensus.

They are not without conflict. Reed-groves encroach; buildings decay; the Great Resonator’s pulse fluctuates; silence comes, unbidden, and terror follows. There are disagreements over where to prune, how to distribute the labor of maintenance, when to abandon a structure overtaken by root-harp. There are those who grow weary of the sound, who climb to the rim and stand at Keth, staring out at the silent tableland, wondering what it would be to live without the drone. Some do not return. The Shen do not speak of them. To leave is to step into not-sound, into meaninglessness. It is a kind of death.

Yet they are not prisoners. They are **participants**. They understand themselves as the Hollow’s nervous system, its tuning-forks, its voice. They believe the bowl is alive — not a god, but a **presence**, vast and slow and mostly dormant, and that the city of Shen is an organ within that presence, grown or permitted or dreamed into being. They do not worship it. They **maintain** it. They keep the sound going because the sound keeps them going, and the distinction between the two has long since dissolved.

They fear only one thing: the silence. When the sound thins, when the frequencies drop away one by one, when the Great Resonator’s pulse falters and stops, they huddle in their homes, wrapped in fiber blankets, and they do not speak. Speech in the silence is obscene, a small, human noise in a void that has swallowed the voice of the world. They wait. They listen. And when the Resonator pulses again — a single, deep, subsonic throb — they emerge, touching walls, feeling the vibration return, and they resume their lives, because resumption is what the sound demands.

They do not celebrate. They do not weep. They **resonate**.
