The Great Sloth moves first in your awareness—not because you see it, but because the world tilts. A slow, tectonic shifting of the green plane beneath your feet, accompanied by a groan that seems to rise from the wood itself rather than from any throat. Then you see it: a landscape that breathes. Its back is a continent of matted fur the color of old copper, colonized by entire ecosystems of moss and flowering epiphytes that bloom in the humidity of its body heat. It has no neck to speak of, only a head that blends into shoulders broad as a plaza, and limbs that do not grasp but *rest*, splayed out in flattened platforms of bone and sinew that distribute its mass across the interlocked branches like snowshoes on powder. It feeds by osmosis, pressing its face against the canopy and allowing the leaves to deposit their nutrients directly onto its tongue, which is rough as a file and long enough to strip a branch clean in a single languid stroke. When it sleeps—which is often—it becomes terrain, and smaller creatures nest in the warm hollows of its flanks, trusting in its obliviousness.

But you must look closer to see the true engineers of this world. The Lattice-Keepers are primates, though calling them that suggests a familiarity they do not invite. They are small, no larger than a housecat, with fur that has oxidized to the color of old pennies and hands that have elongated into tools for weaving. They do not merely live in the canopy; they *maintain* it. You will find them at the unions of the great branches, their slender fingers working vines into the gaps, reinforcing the architecture that holds their world together. They move with a specific, deliberate gait—three points of contact always, testing the flexibility of the substrate before committing their weight—and when they pause, they tilt their heads to listen to the wood, reading the stress-fractures in the bark the way a sailor reads clouds. Their villages are not built but *grown*, spherical chambers woven from living branches that continue to photosynthesize, creating rooms that glow faintly green in the darkness of the understory. They are the memory of this place, and they do not forget the old paths.

Beneath them, in the shadowed galleries where the light falls in solid bars, drift the Glasswing Swarms. These are not insects, though they fill the ecological niche of insects—delicate, translucent creatures with wings like sheets of mica that beat so rapidly they appear as blurs of prismatic color. They feed on the mist itself, filtering the condensed water vapor through membranes in their thoraxes, extracting the dissolved nutrients and exhaling a fine, pearlescent dew that coats the leaves in silver. When they swarm—and they swarm in the millions—they create weather, their collective wingbeats generating small cyclones of mist that spiral upward through the canopy like ghosts of tornadoes. They are the only creatures here that seem to exist without aggression, moving through the dense air in silence, their transparency making them visible only when the light strikes them at dawn and turns them into living stained glass.

In the deep hollows, where the sinkholes drop into the cold darkness of the Understory, the Root-Scaled Wyrms make their domain. These are not reptiles, though they have the serpentine grace of reptiles, but enormous amphibians with skin that has keratinized into overlapping plates that resemble the bark of the Keth trees themselves. They are blind, navigating by the vibration of footsteps above, and they hunt by patience, lying coiled in the peat-like substrate at the bottom of the sinkholes for weeks at a time until something falls through. Their mouths are circular, ringed with needle-teeth that point backward, and they do not chew but absorb, enveloping their prey in a muscular sac that secretes enzymes to dissolve bone and chitin alike. They are the counterweight to the canopy’s abundance, the gravity that ensures nothing wasted remains above for long. When the Great Sloths die—and they die rarely, perhaps once in a century—the Wyrms sense the cessation of movement and rise from the depths, their pale, eyeless heads emerging from the sinkholes to claim the massive bodies, dragging them down into the smoldering dark where the slow fires burn.

And then there are the beings who watch you from the edges of the clearings, who leave the baskets you find preserved in dry hollows and the fire-circles lifted into the air. The Keth are not gone. They have become something else, or perhaps they have always been this: tall, elongated figures with skin like weathered leather and hair that grows in symbiotic strands with the epiphytes, their scalps hosting small gardens of lichen and moss that change color with their moods. They wear no clothes, for the canopy provides all the covering they need—cloaks of living vines that wrap around their shoulders and take root in their flesh, feeding on their blood in exchange for camouflage and protection from the thorns of the defensive plants. They move differently than the Lattice-Keepers, differently than you—stepping not on the branches but on the *air* between them, or so it seems, for their feet are adapted to grip the invisible threads of fungus that span the gaps, the mycelial networks that knit the canopy together in a web of communication. They speak in frequencies that mimic the subsonic hum of the growing wood, their voices felt rather than heard, and they are the only creatures here who can walk beneath the Great Sloths without disturbing them, who can pass through the Glasswing Swarms without casting a shadow. They are tending the fire-circle you found, though it is centuries old; they are weaving the basket you discovered, though it was lost generations ago. They live in the duration of the trees, and they are waiting for something—perhaps for the canopy to finally close completely, perhaps for the sky to fall, perhaps simply for you to learn how to step lightly enough that the leaves do not close against your touch.
