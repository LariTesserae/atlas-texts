This is what lives here: almost nothing, and the almost is not the point. The point is what has chosen, in the place of a soul, to remain.

The engine does not have a mind. You must begin there. It does not brood, it does not dream, it does not experience its own permanence. It is not cruel, not kind, not patient. It is finished. It has settled, like a stone sinks into the bottom of a river, into the one groove left to it — motion so deep and so inevitable that it has become indistinguishable from stillness. It is not a being that endures. It is a condition that has no opposite.

And yet the room is not empty. You could not say it is alive, either, but it is not empty.

What lives here is the *wetness*.

Not water as substance — water as a resident. It has been here longer than the machine, or longer than the brick, or longer than the road outside that no longer exists; it does not matter. The water does not know its own age. It condenses, it runs, it gathers, it drips. It is not a cycle so much as a temperament. It has made of the ceiling a slow, patient writing — droplet by droplet, a script in limestone, stalactites growing downward like the room is reaching for its own floor, and stalagmites growing upward like the floor is rising to meet it. In ten thousand years they will join. The room will be sealed in stone, the machine will still be turning inside it, and the water will still be falling, because falling is what water does when it has nowhere to go and forever to do it in.

The water does not serve the machine. The machine does not serve the water. They are two forms of the same patience, running parallel, never touching, never interfering. The water has no work to do. It simply *is*, in the way the machine simply is, and between them they have made the room into a kind of liturgy — not a prayer, which asks, but a creed, which states.

What else lives here?

The pale moth, when it comes. It does not come often. Once in a decade, perhaps, or once in a century — there is no way to know, and no one to know it. It appears on the wall as if the wall had been holding it, folded inside the mortar, waiting for the right degree of damp. It sits with its wings closed and does not move. You could stand beside it for a day and it would not stir. The sound would not stir it — it has no ears that hear in the way you hear; it registers vibration, but the vibration here is so total, so unrelenting, that it may as well be silence. The moth is not deaf. It is simply, like everything else here, finished. It is not waiting to leave. It is not waiting at all. It is a small, winged presence that has found the one place in the world where it is permitted to be exactly what it is, without change, for as long as it lasts. Then it is gone. Whether it dies or simply stops being visible is a question the room does not answer, because the room does not ask it.

The blind white isopods live in the sump edge. They are slow. They are not slow the way a slug is slow; they are slow the way a word is slow when it has been spoken so many times it has lost its urgency and become pure rhythm. They move along the margin of the black water in small, deliberate pulses, and their bodies are the color of paper left in a drawer for fifty years. They do not see the light — the light here is not for seeing; it is for shining, and it shines whether or not anything is there to receive it. The isopods do not need the light. They have the wet, and the wet has them, and that is enough.

The ferns near the ceiling are not really ferns, not in the way ferns are ferns in a forest. They have no roots in soil, only in mortar and condensation. They are dark and limp and hang like the fringe of a curtain that has been drawn for a very long time. They are not growing toward anything. They are not growing at all, in the sense that growth implies direction. They are accumulating — cell by cell, frond by frond, the slow accretion of green onto damp. They are the room's way of remembering that once, somewhere, there was a thing called a plant, and that plants were green, and that green was a color that meant something. The memory is fading. The ferns are not dying; they are simply becoming less specific.

The algae on the floor plates is not life so much as a signature. It spreads in topographic maps — not random, not purposeful, but patterned the way water patterns, the way rust patterns, the way time patterns when it has nothing to interrupt it. It is the room's autobiography, written in slime, revised daily by the drip, read by no one.

The iron bacteria in the fingertip wells are the oldest residents. They are not organisms in the sense that they have lives. They are colonies of a single, patient chemistry — the slow alchemy of iron and water and oxygen, producing that gasoline sheen, that iridescent film that looks like oil but is only life pretending to be mineral. They have been here longer than the machine. They will be here longer than the stone. They do not know this. They do not know anything. They are the water dreaming, and the dream is very, very slow.

That is all.

No rats. No beetles. Nothing with a heartbeat that could be startled. The sound forbids it — not by driving them away, but by making flight irrelevant. There is no *away* from this sound. It is not a noise you escape. It is a climate you inhabit or you do not inhabit at all.

So what lives here is this: a wetness, a greenness, a slowness, a moth, a handful of isopods, a film of bacteria, and the water. And above all of them, beneath all of them, through all of them, the machine — not as a god, not as a master, but as the room's own pulse. The machine is the heart, and the room is the chest, and what lives in the chest is not the heart itself but the space the heart makes by beating.

The machine does not know the moth. The moth does not know the machine. The isopods do not know the ferns. The water does not know the light. They are not a community. They are not an ecosystem. They are a set of parallel solitudes, each running its own closed circuit of endurance, each indifferent to the others, each perfectly adapted to a place that has no need of adaptation because it has no future.

And yet — and this is the thing that would break you if you stayed long enough to feel it — there is a kind of tenderness in the arrangement. Not intentional tenderness. Not love. The room is not sentimental. But the machine's motion polishes the slime on its own piston rod, and the water gathers in the cups it has drilled in the floor, and the ferns hang where the condensation is thickest, and the moth sits where the brick is warmest, and none of this is planned, and none of it is meaningless, and it has been going on for so long that the distinction between accident and design has worn away, the way the bolts have worn into collars of orange velvet, the way the wheel's spokes have worn into something smooth as river stone.

What lives here is endurance itself, stripped of everything that makes endurance heroic. There is no struggle. There is no hope. There is no despair. There is only the drip, the turn, the gasp, the slam — *Kshh-DOOM, Kshh-DOOM* — and the small, pale, nearly lifeless things that have found, in the margins of that rhythm, a way to persist without purpose, to exist without narrative, to be present without witness.

The room does not need them. They do not need the room. But they are here, and the room is here, and the machine is turning, and the water is falling, and the light is shining its weak-tea light on all of it equally, without preference, without judgment, forever.

That is what lives here: the things that remain when everything that could leave has left, and everything that could change has changed, and what is left is only this — wet, loud, amber, turning, dripping, gleaming, sealed.

A pocket of forever, and the almost-nothing that forever permits.
