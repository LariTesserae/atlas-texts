In the Ink Vent, where the world is still drafting itself, life has not taken the form of beasts or birds, but of verbs—slow, deliberate, half-formed intentions learning to be nouns.

The most obvious inhabitants are the plants, though calling them *plants* feels like shorthand for something less certain. They are not a species so much as a habit the place is trying to form. Each one grows alone, not for lack of space but because they require silence around them, a margin of nothing in which to unfold. Their leaves are broad, spiraling in directions that sometimes contradict themselves mid-curve, as though left and right are still negotiable concepts. Some are deep green, nearly black, others pale and lit from within. A few reflect scenes that don’t match what stands before them—other pockets, other tries, other drafts of the world.

The largest of these stands near the center: a tree that interrupts itself. Its trunk, braided from dark filaments, rises three times the height of a person and then stops. After a gap of air—clean, deliberate, unquestionably part of the form—the canopy begins: a flat disc of leaves hovering without connection, rotating in increments so small they can only be inferred. Beneath the canopy, the gap is a window, but not a consistent one. Sometimes it shows the ink floor beyond. Sometimes it shows a deeper dark. Sometimes it shows machinery, though there are no machines here. The tree does not branch; it *hesitates*, and in that hesitation, something grows.

There are no animals, but there are movements. The ink itself shifts—settling into dunes, sighing as it releases shapes it held only briefly. The plants click as they grow, each leaf unfurling in a sequence of soft, mechanical ratchets. The air hums, low and irregular, like an engine trying to turn over in a distant room and failing, not with frustration, but with patience. These are not creatures, but they are *alive* in the way a process is alive: not by moving, but by continuing.

And then there are the things that arrive without origin. A stone, smooth and dark, sitting in the ink as though it has always been there, though it wasn’t an hour ago. A new plant, half-grown, already warm, already casting a shadow that glows faintly from beneath. They materialize the way thoughts do—not from somewhere, but *into focus*. They are not born. They are *decided*.

The laws here are not yet fixed, but they are consistent in their inconsistency. Distance is approximate—two plants that seem ten strides apart may take thirty to reach, or three. Shadows do not block light; they emit it, faintly, as though darkness were not the absence of illumination but its root system. Nothing decays, because nothing has finished arriving. The pocket has not yet reached the part of the story where things are allowed to die.

To stand in the Ink Vent is to feel like a rough draft—not incomplete, but *in progress*. The heat is a held breath. The quiet is a pause between words. The ink is the space on the page where the next sentence will go. The plants are the first few words, already written, beautiful and strange and slightly incorrect in ways that make them more interesting than correctness would allow.

There are no witnesses because there is no need for them. The place is not performing. It is *practicing*. And the practice is this: to become a world. To be a place with edges that hold, distances that agree, shadows that behave, an engine that starts on the first try. To fill in the nothing past the boundary with something worth looking at. To grow more plants, make more sounds, cast more light from more shadows.

The Ink Vent is in no hurry. It is hot, and high, and mostly dark, and mostly quiet, and mostly empty, and mostly itself. The rest is coming. You can tell by the hum—patient, periodic, unembarrassed now—of something that has not yet turned over but will, eventually, and has no doubt of this at all.
