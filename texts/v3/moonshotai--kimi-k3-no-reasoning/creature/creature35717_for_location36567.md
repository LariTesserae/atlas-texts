# What Lives in the Hollow of Mercy's Drum

---

## First: The Air Itself

Before any creature, before any person, what lives here is the *texture* of breathed air that has been recycled eleven thousand times. It carries the ghost of every exhalation ever made in the bowl — not metaphorically, but materially. The return-stills are efficient, not perfect. Trace compounds persist: the phenols of old fear, the alkaline sharpness of grief-sweat, the faint lipid sweetness of a baby who died eighty years ago in the Tier Collapse of the Brass Founders' era. You cannot smell these things individually. But long-term residents develop what they call *deep nose* — an unconscious sensitivity to the emotional residue in recycled air. They walk into a room and say "something happened here" and they are right. The air remembers. The air is the city's longest-lived inhabitant, and it is crowded with the dead.

## The People

A few thousand. No census survives longer than a season because the bowl takes and gives — a fissure opens and a family moves three tiers down; a workshop fails and its workers scatter into the interstitial corridors like dice thrown. But they cluster into recognizable kinds, shaped by the conditions the way the fauna is shaped.

### The Condenser-Keepers

The most essential and the least respected. They maintain the vapour-recovery coils — hundreds of kilometres of copper and bronze tubing threaded through the city's structure like a secondary nervous system, sweating out the breath of thousands into collectible moisture. A condenser-keeper's body is unmistakable: permanently hunched from crawling through pipe-runs, hands scarred white from handling cold metal in dry air, lips cracked to the point of permanent fissure because they spend their days around humidity they are not allowed to drink.

They are priestly without religion. They know the city's breathing the way a cardiologist knows a heart — which coils run wet after shift-change when everyone's talking, which run dry during the deep hours, which ones are *wrong* in ways that mean a pipe has cracked or a seal has failed or someone has tapped the line illegally. Illegal tapping is the city's one unforgivable crime. Not murder — you can kill someone and serve your time on a repair crew. But steal water, and you are *sealed*: your name is spoken once into the civic pipe-tapping network, a sharp seven-beat pattern that means *this person receives no return*, and within a week you are dead or gone. Nobody enforces it. It enforces itself. The condenser-keepers simply stop acknowledging your existence, and the stills stop allocating your portion, and the bowl does the rest.

They are, as a group, extraordinarily quiet. Even by the Hollow's standards. Some of them go days without speaking. They communicate in pipe-taps and hand signs and the small, precise economy of people who have learned that every word costs moisture.

### The Surveyor-Guild

The ones who walk the tiers with their brass instruments and chalk, reading the mountain's intentions. They occupy a social position somewhere between engineer and oracle. A surveyor's mark on a wall — a circle of white chalk, sometimes with a cross through it — carries the force of law. Nobody questions it. If a surveyor says your building has three days, you have three days, and you do not argue, because surveyors do not guess. They *know*, in the way that comes from spending your working life with your ear pressed to stone, listening for the frequencies that precede failure.

They are chosen young — typically at seven or eight — by a process that is part aptitude test and part something stranger. The guild brings candidates into the deep tunnels, past the geothermal taps, into spaces where the rock is warm and the groaning is loud and the air smells like the inside of a struck bell. They leave the children there alone for an hour. The ones who come out calm are apprenticed. The ones who come out screaming are sent home with a small bag of copper filings as consolation. The ones who don't want to come out — who have to be retrieved, blinking, from some corner where they've been sitting with their palms flat on the stone — those are the ones the guild watches most carefully. Those are the ones who sometimes become *deep surveyors*, the specialists who work in the active zones, reading the mountain in places where the rock is barely solid, where the basalt has the consistency of very old toffee and the groaning is not a complaint but a conversation.

Deep surveyors are strange. Even other surveyors think so. They develop habits that don't map onto normal human behaviour — they stop mid-sentence and tilt their heads; they press their fingertips to walls and hold them there for minutes; they carry small bowls of mercury and watch the surface with an attention that suggests the mercury is telling them something more than vibration data. They die young, often, because the deep zones are dangerous and because they take risks that seem unreasonable until you realize they are not *risks* to them — they are answers to questions the mountain asked.

### The Foundry-Born

Not a guild. A phenotype. Generations of families who have worked the geothermal foundries, living in the tiers closest to the heat taps, have produced people who are physiologically distinct from the rest of the population. Their skin has a grey-bronze cast, not from dirt but from actual metallic deposition — trace elements absorbed through the pores over decades, worked into the dermis like tattoo ink. Their eyes are light-sensitive to a degree that makes the dim lamplight of the upper tiers feel like noon. They are compact, dense-boned, heat-tolerant to an extent that makes visitors uncomfortable: a foundry-born worker will pick up a casting that a newcomer can't touch without gloves and carry it across the shop floor without apparent discomfort.

They marry among themselves, mostly. Not from exclusion — from logistics. A foundry-born and an upper-tier person can certainly fall in love, and do, but the practicalities are brutal. The foundry-born need heat; they sicken in the cooler upper tiers, developing a bone-deep ache they call *the cooling*. Upper-tier people find the foundry districts unbearable — the heat, the brightness of the forge-glow, the constant percussive hammering that the bowl's acoustics can't fully muffle. So cross-tier relationships tend to be passionate and brief, and the foundry-born return to their own, and the stratification persists, generation after generation, until the two populations are almost separate species sharing a single cave.

Their children are born knowing how to read metal. This is not a figure of speech. A foundry-born child of five can look at a cooling ingot and tell you its carbon content by the colour of its surface oxidation. They learn to walk on floors that would blister an outsider's feet. Their first words, disproportionately often, are the names of alloys.

### The Corridor People

The ones who live in the spaces between — the interstitial corridors, the maintenance passages, the gaps where buildings have fused together imperfectly and left voids. Officially, these spaces are not residential. Unofficially, several hundred people live in them, and have for generations, and their relationship to the "legitimate" city is complex and symbiotic and occasionally adversarial.

Corridor people know the city the way you know the inside of your own mouth — by touch, by intimacy, by constant unconscious exploration. They navigate by memory and vibration and air-current, often in total darkness. They are the city's informal couriers, its smugglers, its finders-of-lost-things. If you drop a tool down a grate and it falls into the interstitial maze, you do not go after it. You tap a specific pattern on the nearest pipe — three short, two long, one short — and within a day, a corridor person will appear at your door with the tool, and you will pay them in return-water or copper filings or food, and no questions will be asked on either side.

They have their own internal geography, with names that don't appear on any surveyor's map: the Swallow, the Long Squeeze, the Place Where the Air Tastes Wrong. Some of these spaces have been continuously inhabited for a century. They contain furniture — or the corridor equivalent, which is arrangements of salvaged material that serve the function of furniture without resembling it. A bed in the corridors might be a slab of insulating foam stripped from a dead engine casing, laid over a bed of compacted moth-dust that has accumulated into something almost soft. A table might be a section of cut pipe, capped and polished. It is a parallel domesticity, adapted to spaces that were never meant for bodies.

Corridor people tend to be thin. Not malnourished — *thin*, in the way that suggests their bodies have been selected over generations for the ability to fit through gaps. Their ribs are visible but their arms are strong. Their eyes are wide-pupilled even in lamplight. Some of them — the old ones, the ones who were born in the corridors and have never lived anywhere else — have a way of moving that is almost boneless, a fluid compression of the shoulders and hips that lets them pass through openings that look impossible.

They drum on the walls at night. Not the pipe-code — their own rhythms, older, patterns that predate the civic morse. The drum-rats answer them. Sometimes the corridor people drum back at the rats, and a conversation develops that neither species fully understands but both seem to value.

### The Lamplighters

The electrical guild, yes, but also something more specific and stranger: the small cadre of workers who maintain the carbide lamp grid, who climb the cables that sag between towers, who hang in the dome's artificial sky like spiders repairing a web.

They are acrobats by necessity. The lamp-cables run through open air, thirty, fifty, a hundred metres above the bowl's floor. Lamplighters work in harnesses, but the harnesses are minimal — a loop of woven wire-rope, a carabiner, faith. They move along the cables with a practiced, rolling gait, one hand on the cable, one hand on their tool belt, bodies swinging in the bowl's faint thermal currents. From below, they look like a separate species — silhouetted against the dome, backlit by the green-tinged lamps, moving with a patience and precision that seems inhuman until you watch long enough to see the small adjustments, the shifts of weight, the moments when a lamplighter stops and hangs still, listening to the dome groan overhead, calculating whether the sound is the usual complaint or something new.

They are the ones who first noticed the lamp-flickering pattern. The wave-like sweeps of darkness that cross the dome ceiling in slow sequence. The lamplighters call it *the shadow-swim*. They have charted it. They have logbooks going back decades, noting the pattern's frequency, amplitude, direction. It is not random. It repeats on a cycle of approximately thirty-seven days, but the cycle shifts slightly each time, as if tracking something that is itself moving. The lamplighters do not speculate about what causes it. They maintain the lamps. They replace the filaments. They record the data. What the data means is not their job.

But the oldest lamplighter — a woman named Tessik who has been climbing cables for forty years, whose body is more scar tissue than skin, who can read the dome's groaning the way a surveyor reads chalk marks — has a private theory, and she tells it only to her apprentices, only in the deep hours, only when the lamps are at their dimmest.

"The pattern is something moving on the other side of the dome," she says. "Something big. It passes between the geothermal taps and the ceiling plates, and its shadow interrupts the thermal updraft that powers the lamp grid. The flickering isn't an electrical fault. It's a *shadow*."

She does not say what the something might be. She tightens a filament, clips her carabiner to the next cable, and swings out into the green dark.

## The Deeper Fauna

Beyond the moths and crawlers and wire-eels and drum-rats — beyond the visible, catalogued biome — the Hollow supports life that is harder to classify, harder to see, harder to believe in.

### The Hush

Not a creature. Possibly not alive. But it *behaves* like something alive, and the people of the Hollow treat it as a presence rather than a phenomenon.

The Hush is a zone — or zones; it moves — where sound stops. Completely. Not muffled, not quiet, but *absent*. A person standing in the Hush can scream and hear nothing. A drum-rat caught in it drums frantically and produces silence. The bowl's ambient groan, the one that is always there, the one you feel in your sternum — in the Hush, it stops. The silence is so total that it has physical weight. People who have walked into the Hush describe it as stepping into a solid wall of nothing, a pressure on the eardrums that is the opposite of sound — not zero decibels but *negative* decibels, a sucking absence that pulls the noise out of your own body. Your heartbeat goes silent. Your breath goes silent. You can feel your vocal cords vibrating and hear nothing at all.

The Hush moves through the city in patterns that no one has successfully predicted. It appears in a corridor on Tier Nine, persists for an hour or a day, then dissipates — or relocates. Sometimes it is small — a metre across, a pocket of dead air in a machine shop. Sometimes it is large enough to swallow an entire street, and the people who live on that street come home to find their neighbourhood silent, their children crying voicelessly, their engines running in perfect quiet, and they stand at the edge of it and do not go in, because the Hush is not dangerous but it is *wrong*, it violates the fundamental contract between a living thing and its environment, which is that you make noise and the world receives it.

Drum-rats avoid it absolutely. A colony will abandon a prime nesting site — a warm engine housing, a well-stocked territory — if the Hush passes through it, and they will not return for weeks. The moths fly around it. The crawlers, who are silent anyway, seem indifferent, but the crawlers are indifferent to most things.

The condenser-keepers claim that the Hush is where the mountain is *listening*. That the bowl's acoustics work in both directions — that sound is absorbed not just by the concave walls but *by* the mountain itself, taken in, processed, and that the Hush is what happens when the mountain opens its ears wide. The surveyors dismiss this as mysticism. But the surveyors also mark Hush zones on their structural maps with a symbol that is not used for any other phenomenon — a small circle with nothing inside it — and they check those zones first after any seismic event, and they do not explain why.

### The Warm-Blooded Stone

In the deepest part of the bowl — below the lowest tier, past the geothermal taps, in the zones where only deep surveyors go — there are formations that the surveyors do not include in their official reports.

They look like rock. They are rock, mostly — basalt, shot through with iron and copper veins, warm to the touch the way everything down there is warm. But they have *structure* that rock should not have. Bilateral symmetry. Surface textures that repeat at regular intervals, like scales or feathers. And when you put your hand on them — and the deep surveyors do put their hands on them, because the guild's first rule is *touch the mountain* — you feel something that is not the vibration of engines or the settling of stone.

A pulse.

Slow. Very slow. One deep surveyor — a man named Carr, who worked the deep zones for twelve years before a fissure took him — kept a private log. He measured the pulse over six months, pressing his fingertips to the same formation every day, timing the intervals with a pocket chronometer. His notes, found after his death in a sealed tin box behind his bunk, record a rhythm of approximately one beat every eleven minutes, steady as a metronome, with occasional *doubled* beats — a thump-thump, like a skipped heartbeat — that always preceded a dome-groan by several hours.

Carr's last entry reads: *It is not a geological process. I have eliminated thermal expansion, tidal stress, and machinery vibration as causes. The pulse is endogenous. It comes from the formation itself. It is, as far as I can determine, a circulatory rhythm. I do not know what is circulating. I do not know where the heart is. I am not sure I want to.*

The guild suppressed his notes. Not from malice — from *fear*. Because if Carr was right, then the warm formations are not formations. They are the exposed edges of something that lives in the mountain's root, something large enough to have a heartbeat that takes eleven minutes to complete a cycle, something that has been there since before the bowl was carved, something that the bowl might have been carved *into* — not a cavity in the rock but a cavity in a *body*.

The deep surveyors still touch the formations. They still feel the pulse. They do not talk about it with outsiders. They have a private sign — two fingers pressed to the wrist, a pulse-check — that they make to each other in passing, in the corridors, in the deep hours. It means: *I know. You know. We don't say it.*

### The Copper Ghosts

Not supernatural. Probably not. But they are real in the way that the Hush is real — a phenomenon that behaves like a presence.

In the foundry districts, where metallic deposition is heaviest, where the air is thick with copper vapour and iron oxide and the fine dust of a thousand casting cycles, shapes sometimes form. They are made of dust — the copper-coloured moth-droppings, the metallic particulates that hang in the foundry air like the ghost of breath on a cold day. Thermal currents from the forges push this dust into transient configurations, and sometimes — not often, but often enough that every foundry-born has seen it — the dust takes a shape.

A human shape. A figure, standing in the heat-shimmer above a cooling bed, or walking along a gantry, or sitting on the edge of a crucible platform with its legs dangling. The figure is not detailed — no face, no fingers, a silhouette only — but it is *recognizably* a person, in the way that a shadow is recognizably a person. It lasts for a few seconds, maybe a minute, while the thermal currents hold. Then it disperses, and the dust settles, and the foundry workers go back to work, and nobody says anything because the copper ghosts are not remarkable anymore. They are part of the foundry landscape, like the heat and the noise and the smell of hot metal.

The foundry-born have a tradition: when a worker dies — crushed by a casting, burned by a surge, taken by the cooling — their colleagues do not hold a memorial. They wait for the ghost. Within a few days of the death, usually, a dust-figure appears in the foundry, and the workers recognize it — not by any rational means, but by the posture, by the way it stands, by the set of its shoulders. *That's Marran,* someone says, and the others nod, and they watch the figure until it disperses, and then they go back to work, and that is the funeral.

The natural philosophy society — the single, underfunded one — has a standing research project on the copper ghosts. Their current hypothesis involves standing thermal waves and paramagnetic dust alignment and the human brain's relentless pattern-recognition. Their data is inconclusive. Their funding was cut last quarter because the return-market crashed and nobody could afford to pay membership dues. The research continues anyway, conducted by a single elderly natural philosopher named Wex, who works out of a converted storage alcove on Tier Four and pays his assistants in polished drum-rat offerings — bright bolts and washers, which the rats bring him because he is the only human in the Hollow who has figured out how to drum back at them in a way they find interesting.

Wex's private opinion, which he does not include in his research notes because it is not a scientific opinion, is that the copper ghosts are the city's way of keeping its dead. Not their souls — Wex does not believe in souls, or says he doesn't — but their *patterns*. The specific configuration of habits and movements and postures that made a person who they were. The foundry dust absorbs these patterns over years of exposure to the same workers, the same gestures, the same thermal signatures, and when the person dies, the dust retains the pattern, the way a magnet retains the memory of a magnetic field. The ghosts are not supernatural. They are *metallurgical*. The mountain's own substance, remembering the shape of the people who spent their lives inside it.

Wex finds this more comforting than any afterlife. He has not told anyone this.

## The Ones Who Came From Elsewhere

The Hollow does not receive many visitors. The elevator shaft is the only access, and the elevator is controlled by the surveyor-guild, and the surveyor-guild does not encourage tourism. But people come. Geologists, drawn by the young rock and the thermal anomalies. Anthropologists, fascinated by a culture that has evolved in total isolation from the surface. Engineers, who want to study the geothermal taps. And occasionally — rarely, but regularly enough to constitute a pattern — people who come because they heard the mountain breathing and could not stay away.

These last ones are different. They arrive in the cage with the same expression: a tight, listening stillness, as if they are hearing a sound that the other passengers can't hear. They descend into the bowl and they look around and something in their faces *relaxes*, the way a long-held muscle relaxes, and the condenser-keepers recognize them immediately — not by sight, but by smell. Their sweat changes when they enter the bowl. It loses the sharp, anxious quality of surface-dwellers and takes on the mineral flatness of someone who has come home.

Most of them stay. They find work — there is always work, in a city that is constantly rebuilding itself — and they integrate, and within a year you cannot tell them from the Hollow-born. Their skin cracks. Their eyes adjust to the dimness. They learn the pipe-code. They stop flinching when the dome groans. They begin, like everyone else, to read the shadows like water.

A few of them become deep surveyors. A few become condenser-keepers. One — a woman who arrived thirty years ago with no name she would share and a habit of pressing her ear to walls — became a corridor person and is now the oldest and most knowledgeable of them all, the one the others call *the Swallow's Mother*, the one who knows passages that no one else knows, that go deeper than the surveyor maps show, that wind down past the geothermal taps into spaces where the rock is warm-blooded and the pulse is strong and the air tastes like the inside of something alive.

She has not come back up in six years. The corridor people bring her food and return-water, leaving them at the edge of the deep passages in the agreed-upon spot, and the food is always gone by the next day, and sometimes — not always, but sometimes — there is something left in exchange: a fragment of warm stone, smooth as skin, with a texture on its surface that repeats at regular intervals, like scales, or feathers, or the ridges of a fingerprint.

The corridor people keep these fragments. They do not show them to outsiders. They hold them in the dark, in the interstitial spaces, and they press them to their ears, and they listen to something that sounds, they say, like breathing.

## The Rhythm That Holds It All

What lives here, ultimately, is the rhythm itself — the layered, interlocking pulse of a community that has synchronized itself to a geological heartbeat.

The engines throb. The drum-rats drum. The pipe-code taps its civic morse. The foundry hammers ring. The dome groans. The geothermal taps hiss. The moths beat their wings in time to the flywheels. The corridor people drum in the dark. The warm-blooded stone pulses its slow, eleven-minute beat. And the people — all of them, the condenser-keepers and the surveyors and the foundry-born and the corridor people and the lamplighters and the ones who came from elsewhere and could not leave — they live inside this rhythm the way fish live inside a current, carried by it, shaped by it, so deeply embedded in its cadence that they no longer hear it as sound. They hear it as *life*.

In the deep hours, when everything idles, when the lamps dim and the moths settle and the drum-rats soften to a heartbeat patter and the dome groans low and long like a ship's hull in deep water —

the rhythm remains.

It is the thing that lives here most of all. The thing that was here before the city, before the bowl, before the civilisation that may or may not have carved this hollow into the mountain's root. The thing that will be here after the last engine seizes and the last lamp burns out and the last return-still runs dry.

It sounds like breathing.

It is breathing.

And the city — the impossible city, the city that should not exist, the city of stubborn, adaptable, dry-lipped, dim-eyed, drum-hearted people who looked at a cavity in the deep earth and said *here* —

the city breathes with it.
