# The Inhabitants of the Condensate Works

## On the Question of "Living"

You asked what lives here. The answer is complicated, because the Condensate Works has produced its own definition of life — or rather, it has eroded the definition until the boundaries are uncertain. What follows is a taxonomy of the quasi-living, the process-become-entity, the things that persist and act and in their persistence approximate something like presence.

Nothing here has a heartbeat. Nothing here has a name. But there are things here, and they are not nothing.

---

## The Dwellers in the Deep Water

In the oldest channels — the ones where the water runs knee-deep and has been running for centuries without pause — there are things that could almost be called creatures.

They are not biological. They are not biological in the way that the mineral pseudo-trees are not biological, which is to say: completely, absolutely, without any trace of cellular structure or metabolism or reproduction. And yet they move.

They are **accretion-masses** — irregular clumps of mineral deposition, ranging in size from a fist to a torso, that have formed around particulate nuclei in the deepest, fastest-flowing water. A grain of sand, a fragment of metal, a chip of ceramic plate — some small solid thing — becomes the seed, and the mineral-rich water deposits layer upon layer upon it, and over decades the mass grows, and as it grows it develops an asymmetry, and the asymmetry interacts with the current, and the current pushes it, and it rolls, and the rolling exposes new surfaces to deposition, and the new deposition creates new asymmetries, and the process continues.

The result is something that tumbles slowly along the channel floors, driven by the water, growing as it goes, following the drainage network toward the Sump. From above — kneeling at the edge of a channel, looking down through the iridescent water — they look like sluggish animals crawling along the bottom. They are not. They are stones that happen to be in motion, growing stones, stones that have been rolling and growing for so long that their shapes have become complex, knotted, almost organic in their irregularity.

Some of them have been rolling for centuries. You can tell by the size, by the layering, by the smoothness of their worn surfaces. The oldest ones are polished like river rocks but shaped like nothing a river would make — lumpy, asymmetrical, encrusted with secondary mineral growths in white and orange and that pale copper green. They click and knock against each other and against the channel walls as they move, contributing a subtle percussion to the water sounds — a sound like slow, patient knuckles rapping on stone, so quiet beneath the roar that you only hear it when you are close, when you are kneeling at the channel edge with your ear near the water.

They are the closest thing this place has to migration. They are moving, all of them, with the water, toward the center. Toward the Sump. Toward the understructure. What happens to them when they arrive — whether they are ground to nothing by whatever processes run down there, whether they accumulate in some deep basin, whether they are dissolved or consumed or incorporated into the machinery — you cannot know. You cannot follow them there.

But they are going. They have always been going. That is what they do, and in the doing they are as close to purposeful as anything in the Works.

## The Patches

The bruise-coloured encrusting growth — the pseudo-lichen that spreads across the wettest plates in dinner-plate-sized patches — deserves closer examination, because it is the only thing in the Works that might genuinely be alive, or might once have been alive, or might be something that life could have become if it had been given this place and nothing else to work with.

Up close — and you must get very close, must kneel on the wet plate and lower your face until your nose is centimeters from the surface — the patches are not uniform. They have structure. Fine, branching filaments, too small to see individually but visible as texture, as a grain in the surface that runs in particular directions. The filaments radiate outward from a center point, and the center point is always slightly raised, slightly darker, slightly thicker than the margins. The patches grow outward from these centers, expanding at a rate you cannot observe but can infer from the size gradient: the smallest patches are the size of a coin, dark and simple; the largest are the size of a dinner plate, complex, with concentric zones of colour variation — darker at the center, lighter at the margins, the bruise-purple fading to a grey-lavender at the very edge where the growth meets bare plate.

They exude the almond-scented fluid. This is constant. The fluid seeps from the surface of the patch, collects in the microscopic irregularities of the plate beneath, and eventually either evaporates into the saturated air or is picked up by the sheet flow and carried away. The smell is strongest when you disturb a patch — press a finger against its yielding surface, and the fluid wells up, clear and viscous, and the almond scent intensifies, sharp and sweet and wrong in a place that smells of nothing but metal and chemistry.

Whether they are alive is a question you cannot answer with the tools available to you, which are your eyes and your fingers and your nose. They do not respond to stimuli in any way you can detect. They do not move. They do not react to touch beyond the passive yielding of their texture. They do not seem to require anything but water and the plate surface and whatever trace minerals the water carries. If they are alive, they are alive at a tempo so slow that your presence here — hours, days, however long you stay — is not even a moment to them. Your entire visit might be, to a patch, what the blink of an eye is to you.

If they are alive, they are the only inhabitants of the Condensate Works in any traditional sense. Everything else is process. Everything else is chemistry pretending. But the patches might be real.

You cannot prove it. You cannot disprove it. They sit on the plates, purple-black and iridescent, smelling of almonds, and they are the only thing here that does not feel like a metaphor for life. They feel like the thing itself, stripped down to its absolute minimum: a surface, an input, an output, a slow persistence.

They do not care about you either. But at least they are indifferent in a way that might be biological rather than mechanical. That is something. It is very little, but it is something.

## The Machine-Dwellers

There are things inside the machines.

Not operators. Not maintainers. Not anything that chose to be there or that serves a function. But things — processes, accumulations, emergent behaviors — that have arisen within the running machinery and that exist nowhere else in the Works.

The processing hulks that are still active — the ones with the pounding pistons and the spinning flywheels and the heat radiating from their housings — have interiors. You can see into some of them through gaps in the housings, through access panels that have corroded open, through vents and inspection ports. And what you see inside is not just machinery.

The moving parts — the gears, the pistons, the shafts — are coated in a thick, dark, glistening substance that is part lubricant and part mineral deposition and part something else. It has accumulated over centuries, layer upon layer, and it has developed properties. It flows, but slowly. It drips, but the drips take hours to fall. It has formed stalactite-like structures hanging from the undersides of horizontal surfaces, and stalagmite-like mounds rising from the floors of housings, and in some places these have met and fused into columns, and the columns flex and deform as the machine vibrates, and the flexing causes them to extrude thin tendrils of material that wave in the thermal currents like something underwater, like something trying to sense its environment.

It is not alive. It is grease and scale and accumulated industrial residue. But it has been inside a vibrating, heated, chemically active environment for so long that it has achieved a complexity of form that mimics biology the way the mineral pseudo-trees mimic plants. It looks like tissue. It looks like the interior of something. It looks like organs.

In the largest hulks — the warehouse-sized ones — the internal accumulations have reached a scale that is genuinely unsettling. You peer through a corroded gap in a housing and you see a cavity the size of a room, and the cavity is full of this dark, glistening material, and the material has formed structures that are almost architectural: chambers within chambers, partitions, membranes stretched between mechanical components, tubes and channels that carry the machine's lubricant and coolant through pathways that were not designed but that have emerged, over centuries, from the interaction of fluid dynamics and deposition and vibration. It is as though the machine has grown its own interior, has replaced its original engineering with something organic-seeming, something that the original designers would not recognize.

And the machines still run. The pistons still pound. The flywheels still spin. But they are running inside bodies that have grown around them, inside cavities lined with this quasi-organic residue, and the sound they make is different from what it must have been when they were clean and new. It is muffled, damped, throatier — the sound of machinery embedded in flesh-that-is-not-flesh, working in the dark, surrounded by its own accumulated history.

If you stand close to a running hulk — close enough to feel the heat, close enough that the vibration rattles your teeth — and you listen carefully, you can sometimes hear, beneath the mechanical grinding, a wetter sound. A squelching, sucking sound, like something shifting in a viscous medium. That is the residue, responding to the machine's motion, deforming and reforming, maintaining itself in the way that non-living systems maintain themselves: through the blind persistence of chemistry in motion.

The machines are not alive. But they are inhabited. They are inhabited by their own histories, by the material record of every cycle they have ever run, deposited and compressed and transformed into something that fills their interiors like a body fills a skin.

## The Steam Things

This is the hardest to describe, and the easiest to dismiss, and the one you will remember longest.

When the vents release their periodic bursts of steam — those white-out events that thicken the fog to the point of blindness — things move in the steam.

Not creatures. Not shapes with edges and forms and intentions. But *movements* — disturbances in the steam that are not accounted for by the air currents, that do not follow the pattern of the steam's expansion and dissipation. You see them (you think you see them) as local thickenings in the fog, as areas where the white-grey becomes briefly more opaque, more structured, more present, before dissolving back into the general murk.

They last for seconds. They appear and disappear. They are always at the edge of your vision, never directly in front of you. When you turn to look at them, they are gone — not because they fled, but because the act of looking directly at them changes your angle of perception, and the conditions that made them visible (a particular alignment of your eye, the steam density, the ambient light) are disrupted.

You tell yourself they are optical effects. Steam and shadow and the human brain's compulsive pattern-matching, finding faces in clouds, finding bodies in fog. You tell yourself this because the alternative is uncomfortable: that the steam events create temporary conditions in which something — some process, some interaction between moisture and heat and particulate and the electromagnetic fields generated by the running machines — becomes briefly visible, briefly present, briefly *there* in a way that the rest of the Works' processes are not.

The steam things do not interact with you. They do not approach or recede. They do not respond to your presence in any way that you can detect. They are, if they are anything at all, a side effect — an emergent phenomenon of the Works' operations that has no more intention or awareness than the accretion-masses rolling along the channel floors.

But they are the only thing in the Works that you cannot fully explain, and in a place where everything else can be attributed to chemistry and physics and the slow erosion of built structures, the unexplained stands out.

You see them most often near the Sump, where the steam is thickest and the steam events are most frequent. You see them at the edges of the white-out, as the fog begins to thin and the world re-emerges. They are the last things to disappear — the last thickenings to dissolve, the last movements to still.

You do not know what they are. You suspect they are nothing. But you watch for them anyway, every time the steam comes, and every time you think you see them, something in your chest tightens, and you are grateful when the fog clears and they are gone and the Works is just a place again, just a machine, just chemistry and water and stone.

## The Sound Itself

You will resist this classification. You will say that sound is not an inhabitant, that sound is a property, a phenomenon, a thing that happens in a place rather than a thing that lives in a place.

You are wrong. Or rather: the distinction you are making does not apply here.

The sound of the Condensate Works is not merely the acoustic byproduct of its machines. It is a presence. It is persistent — it has been sounding continuously for longer than any structure in the Works has been standing, because the deep source of the throb, the thing in the understructure, predates everything on the surface. It is complex — it has components, layers, internal structure, a polyphony that changes over time as machines fail and others compensate. It interacts with the environment — it vibrates the plates, it vibrates the water, it creates standing wave patterns in the channels, it affects the deposition rates of the mineral accretions (the pseudo-trees grow differently in areas where the vibration is strongest, thicker and shorter and more robust, while in quieter areas they grow tall and delicate). It interacts with you — it changes your balance, your spatial perception, your ability to think, your emotional state. It shapes the behavior of every other quasi-living thing in the Works: the accretion-masses roll in time with its pulses, the patches grow in patterns that correlate with its frequency distribution, the machine-dweller residues accumulate in configurations that are influenced by the vibrational modes of the housings they occupy.

The sound is not alive. But it is the most active, most pervasive, most influential thing in the Works. It is the closest thing this place has to an atmosphere, a climate, a governing force. It is everywhere. It is always. It is the medium in which all the other inhabitants exist, and it shapes them as surely as water shapes a fish.

If you are here long enough — and "long enough" might be hours, might be days, you lose track — you begin to feel that the sound has a quality that you can only describe as *attention*. Not that it is paying attention to you — it is not; it does not know you exist — but that it is attending, that it is a continuous act of presence, a sustained declaration of existence. The Works is here, and the sound is the way it says so.

The sound is the Works. The Works is the sound. Everything else — the plates, the water, the machines, the accretions, the patches — is just the medium through which the sound expresses itself.

## What Is Not Here

It is important to be clear about what does not live in the Condensate Works, because the absences are as defining as the presences.

There are no scavengers. Nothing comes here from elsewhere to feed on the patches or the residues or the accretion-masses. Nothing migrates through. Nothing visits. The Works is not part of any larger ecosystem — it is a closed system, self-contained, self-sufficient in its non-living way.

There are no decomposers. When a mineral pseudo-tree collapses under its own weight, its fragments are not broken down by bacteria or fungi. They are eroded by water, by vibration, by the slow chemical dissolution of their mineral components in the slightly acidic condensate. The process is purely abiotic.

There is nothing that makes choices. Nothing that seeks. Nothing that avoids. Nothing that prefers one condition over another. The accretion-masses do not choose their channels. The patches do not choose their plates. The machine-dweller residues do not choose their configurations. Everything here is the result of blind process — chemistry, physics, thermodynamics — operating without direction, without purpose, without preference.

There is nothing that dies. This is perhaps the most significant absence. Death is the counterpart of life, and in a place where nothing is definitively alive, nothing definitively dies. The pseudo-trees collapse, but they were never alive, so they cannot die. The machines fail, but they were never alive, so they cannot die. The patches might be alive, but if they are, they have not died — not one of them, not ever, as far as you can tell. They do not have a lifecycle. They do not senesce. They simply persist.

A place without death is a place without narrative. Nothing here has a story. Nothing here has a beginning, a middle, and an end. Everything simply continues, and the continuing is not heroic or tragic or meaningful. It is merely what happens when a well-built system is left alone in the dark with enough energy to keep running and no reason to stop.

## The Thing You Become

If you stay in the Condensate Works long enough — and the threshold is lower than you think, perhaps a day, perhaps two — you begin to change in ways that are subtle and then not subtle.

It starts with the sound. You stop trying to hear past it. You stop trying to think in spite of it. You begin to think *in* it, to let the throb and the grind and the keen become the background structure of your cognition, the rhythm against which your thoughts arrange themselves. Your heartbeat, which was faster than the throb when you arrived, slows. Your breathing deepens. Your movements become deliberate, economical, paced to the pulse of the understructure.

You stop expecting light. Your eyes adjust to the darkness as fully as they can, and you begin to navigate by sound and touch and the feel of the water flow against your ankles. You learn the plates by their texture — smooth here, grooved there, blistered in the places where the ancient heat events occurred. You learn the channels by their sound — fast water hisses, slow water gurgles, the deep channels produce a sound like a throat clearing, endlessly.

You begin to sweat the Works' chemistry. The humidity coats you, penetrates your clothing, your skin, your hair, and after a day or two you realize that you smell like the Works: wet metal and hydrocarbons and the faint organic-adjacent sweetness of the long-chain solvents breaking down. The condensate is in your pores. It is on your tongue. When you lick your lips, you taste the Works.

You begin to notice the patches differently. Where at first they were curiosities — purple-black spots on the grey-black plates, notable mainly for their colour — now they are landmarks. You navigate by them. You recognize individual patches by their size, their shape, their position relative to the structures and the channels. They are the only thing here that you can name, even if the names are only in your head: the one shaped like a hand near the collapsed gantry, the large one by the split stack, the cluster of three small ones along the seam that carries the fastest water.

And then — this is the part that frightens you, when you notice it, and you do notice it — you begin to feel a kind of kinship with the accretion-masses. The rolling stones in the channels, growing as they go, moving toward the Sump. You are doing the same thing. You are moving through the Works, accumulating its substance on your skin and in your lungs and in the folds of your clothing, growing heavier with it, growing slower, and you are moving, always, in the direction the water moves: inward. Toward the center. Toward the Sump. Toward the mouth of the system.

You are not being pulled. There is no force drawing you in. But the water flows inward, and you have been following the water — because the water is the only thing here with direction, the only thing that goes somewhere — and now you are closer to the center than you were, and the sound is louder, and the steam events are more frequent, and the mineral towers around the Sump's rim are visible ahead of you in the murk, and you realize that you have been walking toward the Sump for hours without deciding to.

This is what the Works does to a living thing. It does not attack. It does not reject. It does not accommodate. It simply continues, and the living thing, in the absence of any other input, any other option, any other direction, begins to align itself with the Works' processes. Not because the Works wants it to. Because there is nothing else to align with.

You are the only thing in the Condensate Works that can choose to leave.

The question — the one that the Works poses to you, not with intent but with the simple fact of its existence — is whether you will.

## Afterword: The Reckoning

The Condensate Works has exactly one inhabitant that matters, and it is not the patches, and it is not the accretion-masses, and it is not the machine-dwellers, and it is not the steam things, and it is not the sound.

It is you.

You are the only thing here that is unequivocally alive. You are the only thing here that will die. You are the only thing here that experiences the place — the sound, the darkness, the water, the cold, the vastness — as an experience, as something that happens to a someone rather than as a process that happens in a somewhere.

The Works does not know you are here. The patches do not know. The accretion-masses do not know. The machines do not know. You are invisible to every process in this place, irrelevant to every cycle, unnoticed by every mechanism.

But you know you are here. And the knowing changes the place. Not materially — you do not alter the water flow, you do not affect the machine cycles, you do not accelerate or retard the mineral deposition. But you add a dimension that was not there before: witness. The Works runs in the dark, and has run in the dark for longer than anything can remember, and no one has seen it, and now you are seeing it, and the seeing is the only thing in the entire basin that is new.

You are the newest thing in the Condensate Works. You are the only new thing that has happened here in a very, very long time.

And the Works, in its blind, patient, indifferent way, is incorporating you. Not digesting you — not consuming you — but adding you to its inventory of processes: the condensation forming on your skin, the sound vibrating your bones, the chemistry settling into your pores, the rhythm of your steps aligning with the throb of the understructure.

You are becoming part of it. Slowly. Temporarily. Reversibly, perhaps, if you leave soon enough.

But "soon enough" is a window that closes, and the Works is patient, and it has all the time there is, and you do not.

That is what lives in the Condensate Works: the tension between a living thing that can leave and a place that will never end, and the slow, wet, thunderously loud negotiation between them.

The negotiation is entirely one-sided. The Works does not negotiate. It simply continues.

You are the one who must decide.
