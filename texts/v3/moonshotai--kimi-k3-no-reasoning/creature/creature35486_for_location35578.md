The **Lichen-Weavers** are small people, rarely taller than a child of the outside world, though their bones are dense and their movements heavy with the weight of many garments. They have the broad, flat faces and epicanthic folds of those adapted to glare, their eyes reduced to pale slits that never fully open against the quartz-reflected brilliance. Their skin is the color of weak tea, translucent at the temples where blue veins pulse with a slow, deliberate rhythm—sometimes only six beats to the minute. They wrap themselves in layer upon layer of textile until they become cylindrical, androgynous bundles of ochre and charcoal wool, their silhouettes indistinguishable from the loaded cedar beams of their houses. When they walk, which is seldom necessary in a space of a hundred paces, they place their feet with the care of those testing ice, rolling from heel to toe to minimize the concussion of their passage.

They speak in whispers that are less sound than shaped breath, a language of plosive consonants softened into clicks and glottal stops that die within inches of the speaker’s mouth. To communicate across the settlement, they use a system of weighted cords and copper bells wrapped in felt—vibrations felt through the floor rather than heard through the air. Their hands are their primary instruments: broad-palmed, with fingers shortened by cold but tipped with extraordinary sensitivity. They can distinguish between thirty-two grades of birch bark by touch alone, can feel the difference between ice formed yesterday and ice formed a century ago.

Beneath the settlement, in the warmest pockets where the ice lens meets the volcanic stone, dwell the **Root-Tenders**. These are not human, though the Lichen-Weavers claim they once were. They are pale, eyeless things with the approximate shape of starfish if starfish had evolved to grasp and weave. Five-limbed, soft-bodied, they secrete a enzyme that prevents the dwarf birch roots from freezing solid, allowing the trees to survive in soil that should be concrete. In exchange, the Weavers lower baskets of their finest textile scraps into the root-channels—offerings that the Root-Tenders wrap around themselves, creating cocoons that preserve their soft bodies against the occasional tremors that still shake the scarp. They communicate through pulses of cold; when a Root-Tender is distressed, the temperature in the cisterns drops perceptibly, and the Weavers know to cease their hammering.

The **Ptarmigan of the Hollow** are not truly birds but something between avian and mineral. Their feathers have evolved to incorporate silica from the quartz dust, creating a plumage that is genuinely white not through pigment but through structural color—like snow, like ice. They move through the settlement in absolute silence because their wingbeats have slowed to once every thirty seconds; they glide more than fly, navigating the narrow alleys between houses on currents of air so slight they would not disturb a candle flame. They eat the lichen that would otherwise obscure the quartz veins, and in doing so, they polish the stone. Where they roost, the granite shines with a higher luster. The Weavers consider them the custodians of the light, and when a ptarmigan dies—which happens rarely, for they live decades—the body is not buried but placed in a niche of the ice wall to desiccate into a white, feathered relic that continues to reflect brilliance long after the spark of life has faded.

The **Ice-Voles** are translucent. If you hold one to the light—though the Weavers never would, for to capture one would require unseemly haste—you can see the dark ribbon of its gut, the slow pulse of its heart. They have hollowed out networks of tunnels in the peat walls of the oldest houses, and their comings and goings aerate the structure, preventing the timber from becoming waterlogged. They are the only creatures permitted to make sound, a high-frequency scratching that the Weavers believe helps maintain the structural integrity of the ice by preventing the formation of stress fractures. In the deep winter, when the light fails to clear the scarp at all, the voles glow faintly with bioluminescent bacteria they cultivate in their cheek pouches, providing the only illumination in the darkness—a ghostly, moving constellation beneath the floorboards.

The **Day-Owl** is no larger than a sparrow, though its eyes occupy nearly half its skull. It hunts the voles not by sound but by heat-differential, detecting the warm mammalian bodies against the freezing substrate. Its feathers are edged with a fringe that breaks up turbulence, rendering its flight mathematically silent. The Weavers have domesticated them not for hunting but for their feathers, which they weave into the garments of the dying. It is believed that the owl’s silence transfers to the soul of the departing, smoothing their transition into the stillness of the Ancestors. These birds nest in the hollows of the Ancestors themselves, finding warmth in the residual metabolic heat of those who have slowed to the point of mineralization.

The **Ancestors** occupy the northern wall in tiers. They are not dead, though they have not moved in decades. Their metabolisms have slowed to the point where they require only the moisture that condenses on their skin, perhaps a mouthful of meltwater every season. They sit in alcoves carved to their specific measurements, wrapped in textiles that record their genealogies—copper threads for male lines, silver for female, birch-bark patterns indicating their trades in life. Their eyes remain open, clouded over with a white film that reflects the quartz light back into the hollow, creating the secondary illumination that allows the Weavers to work during the darkest months. They are the library and the oracle. When a Weaver seeks counsel, they sit before the appropriate Ancestor and watch for the subtle shifting of light on the quartz—a flicker that might indicate approval, a shadow that warns of ice-lens instability. The Ancestors do not speak, but their presence generates a pressure in the air, a thickness that the sensitive can interpret as memory.

There are also the **Tardigrades**, though the Weavers have no name for them, knowing them only as the "water-bears" that swim in the cisterns. In this closed system, they have grown large—some nearly visible to the naked eye, translucent cylinders that graze on the bacterial films coating the stone. When the cisterns freeze solid, as happens every few years, the tardigrades enter cryptobiosis, drying into husks that the Weavers carefully collect and store in leather pouches. When the thaw comes, these husks are returned to the water, where they rehydrate and resume their slow grazing. The Weavers view them as the ultimate expression of their philosophy: life reduced to its essential stillness, waiting without impatience for the return of flow.

The **Cushion Plants** that cover the roofs are colonial organisms, thousands of individual rosettes sharing a single root system and a common purpose. They bloom synchronously, triggered by some subtle change in light wavelength that occurs once every eleven years. When they do, the entire hollow turns gold for three days, and the air fills with a scent like burnt honey. During this time, the Weavers cease all labor and climb to the roofs to sit among the flowers, allowing the brief, violent warmth of the bloom to penetrate their wool and their skin, storing up heat against the decade of cold to come. It is the only time they are still by choice rather than necessity.

All of these—the Weavers, the Root-Tenders, the ptarmigan, the voles, the owls, the Ancestors, the tardigrades, the plants—form a single organism of extraordinary patience. They do not merely inhabit the Hollow; they are the Hollow, its consciousness distributed among cold-adapted flesh and mineral and fiber, dreaming together a dream of white light and perfect preservation, adding one stitch, one layer of fat, one polished stone at a time to the tapestry of a world that has nowhere else to go.
