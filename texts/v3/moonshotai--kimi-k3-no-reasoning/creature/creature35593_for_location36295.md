# The Lives of the Alto Seco

## The Cushion Communities

Begin with the cushions, because everything else begins there too.

A mature cushion is not a plant. It is a country. The largest of them — the six-foot discs that sit in the lee of the western ridges, the ones that have been growing since before the processing stations, before the terraces, maybe before the round house — these are less individual organisms than accumulated architecture. Each one is a lattice of woody stems so densely interwoven that you cannot push a finger between them. The outer surface, the part you see from standing height, is a crust of tiny, overlapping leaves, scale-like, pressed flat against the stems beneath, waxy and dark. They look dead. They are not dead. They are *armored*.

Beneath that armor, the cushion is hollow in places — not by design, but by the slow dying of interior stems that are shaded out, compressed, starved of light by the living outer layer. These interior voids are where the communities live. A large cushion might have a dozen distinct chambers within it, connected by narrow passages between dead stems, each chamber with its own microclimate: some dry and dusty, some humid with the plant's own transpired moisture, some warm from the metabolic heat of decomposition, some cool and still. The cushion is a building with many rooms, and the rooms are occupied.

The primary tenants are the beetles. There are at least four species that live exclusively within cushion plants, and probably more that no one has catalogued. The most abundant is a small, oval, matte-black beetle — the size of a lentil, flightless, with a hard shell that fits together like a clamshell when it retracts its legs. These beetles live in the dry chambers, feeding on the dead interior stems, breaking them down into fine, powdery frass that accumulates on the chamber floors. They are slow-moving and unremarkable to look at, but they are the foundation. Their frass is the soil of the cushion's interior — a fine, dark, nutrient-rich substrate that supports everything else.

In the frass live the larvae — not just of the black beetles, but of a second, smaller species, reddish-brown and soft-bodied, that feeds directly on the frass itself. These larvae are preyed upon by a flat, fast-moving centipede, pale yellow, that navigates the narrow passages between stems with fluid ease. The centipede is preyed upon by a small, dark spider that builds no web but hunts by ambush, waiting at the junctions of passages, its legs spread across the opening like a living trapdoor.

In the humid chambers — the ones where the living outer stems transpire moisture that condenses on the cooler interior surfaces — there are the aphids. Pale green, soft, clustered along the undersides of living stems where they tap into the plant's minimal sap flow. The aphids are tended by ants — not a ground-dwelling species, but a small, rusty-red ant that lives entirely within the cushion, never venturing to the outer surface except at night. The ants move the aphids from stem to stem, protecting them from the spider and the centipede, harvesting the honeydew they excrete. The relationship is ancient. The ant colonies within a single cushion can persist for decades, generations of workers tending generations of aphids in the dark, humid interior, neither species ever seeing open sky.

And then there are the frogs.

The cushion frogs are tiny — the length of a thumbnail, pale gray-green, almost translucent. They live in the humid chambers, wedged into the narrowest spaces where moisture concentrates, their skin permanently damp from the plant's transpiration. They do not leave the cushion. They cannot. The outside air would desiccate them in minutes. They are born in the cushion — as tadpoles, in tiny pockets of condensed water that collect in the deepest chambers after heavy dew nights — and they die in the cushion, and their entire world is the space between stems, the smell of frass and sap and ant-pheromone, the vibration of beetle movement through the woody lattice. They eat the smallest beetle larvae. They are eaten by the centipede, when the centipede can find them. Their eyes are large and dark and they sit motionless for hours, waiting for something small enough to come within range of their tongues.

When the afternoon heat hits a certain threshold — usually around two or three in the afternoon, when the sun has been baking the cushion's surface for hours — the plant undergoes a metabolic shift. The outer leaves open their stomata briefly, releasing a burst of volatile compounds. The cushion *smells* — a faint, sweet, almost floral scent, surprising in its delicacy. This scent attracts a species of small, solitary bee, metallic blue, that is one of the cushion's only pollinators. The bees land on the cushion's surface, crawl into the tiny flowers that bloom along the outer edge (so small and so brief-lived that you would miss them if you didn't know to look), collect pollen, and move to the next cushion. Each bee visits dozens of cushions in an afternoon. The bees nest in the ground between the cushions, in bare gravel, digging shallow burrows that they seal with pebbles.

The cushion is a world. It contains herbivores and predators, farmers and livestock, decomposers and pollinators, species that never leave and species that visit and species that pass through. It produces its own water, its own soil, its own climate. It is self-sustaining in a way that nothing else on the plateau is, and it persists for centuries, growing a fraction of a millimeter per year, its outer surface imperceptibly expanding, its interior slowly filling with the accumulated architecture of dead stems and the communities that inhabit them.

You could spend a lifetime studying a single cushion. Some people have.

## The Toads of the Pavement

The stone pavements are, from above, the emptiest part of the Alto Seco. Flat, dark, featureless expanses of interlocking pebbles, winnowed clean of everything fine and loose by centuries of wind. They look like dead ground. They are not.

The pavement toads are small — smaller than the cushion frogs, though bulkier, with thick, warty skin that is almost exactly the color of the dark upper surfaces of the pavement stones. During the day, they are invisible. They spend the daylight hours buried beneath the pebbles, wedged into the gaps between stones, their bodies flattened, their metabolism slowed to near-dormancy. They do not burrow — the pavement is too dense for that — but they have evolved to fit into the existing interstices, their bodies flexible enough to compress into spaces that look too small for them. A toad the size of a walnut can disappear into a gap the width of a finger.

They emerge at night, and the emergence is one of the strangest phenomena on the plateau. It begins an hour after sunset, when the temperature has dropped enough that the dew is beginning to form. The first sign is the sound — a faint, widespread clicking, like someone slowly, gently rolling a handful of marbles across a tile floor. This is the sound of pebbles shifting as hundreds of toads push up through the gaps, their backs lifting the stones above them, the stones rocking and settling as the toads squeeze through. The clicking builds over the course of twenty or thirty minutes, reaching a peak of soft, pervasive, mechanical noise — the sound of the pavement itself stirring — and then, gradually, it subsides, and the pavement is dotted with toads.

They sit on the surface and absorb dew through their skin. This is their primary source of water. They do not drink — they have no need to. Their skin is highly permeable, and on a heavy dew night, a toad can absorb enough moisture in a few hours to sustain it for days. They sit motionless, their skin glistening, their throats pulsing slowly, their eyes open but seemingly unfocused. They are not hunting. They are drinking, in the way that a plant drinks — passively, through osmosis, by simply existing in contact with moisture.

They also eat, but they eat on the move, and they move at night. After absorbing their fill of dew, the toads begin to hop — short, deliberate hops across the pavement, pausing frequently to sit motionless again. They are hunting the small insects that also emerge at night: the nocturnal beetles that scurry between the stones, the tiny moths that flutter low over the ground, the occasional ant that has ventured out of its cushion. The toads are not fast, but they are patient, and their tongues are quick. A toad might eat three or four insects in a night, which is enough. Their metabolism is so slow that a single beetle can sustain them for a week.

On rain nights — the rare, violent storms that sweep the plateau a few times a year — the toads' behavior changes dramatically. The rain triggers something in them, some deep, ancient imperative. They emerge en masse, not in the hundreds of a normal dew night but in the thousands. The pavements crawl. The clicking of displaced stones is continuous, a rolling, gravelly murmur that can be heard from a hundred yards away. The toads hop with purpose, converging on the temporary pools that form in the shallow basins, and there they mate — quickly, desperately, in the brief window before the pools drain or evaporate. The females lay their eggs in the pools — long, gelatinous strings of black eggs that sink to the bottom and adhere to the sediment. The males fertilize them. And then, within hours, the adults return to the pavement, burying themselves again before dawn.

The eggs hatch within a day if the pool persists — tiny, black tadpoles that swarm in the shallow water, feeding on algae and detritus, growing with frantic speed. They have perhaps a week before the pool dries. Most of them don't make it. The ones that do — the ones that reach metamorphosis before the water disappears — emerge as miniature toads, no larger than a fingernail clipping, and immediately bury themselves in the nearest pavement, beginning the cycle again.

The toads are silent. They have no call, no vocalization of any kind. In a landscape defined by its quiet, they are the quietest thing of all. Their presence is registered only by the clicking of stones and the faint, moist gleam of their skin in the moonlight. They are the plateau's most numerous vertebrate, and its least noticeable.

## The Shrimp That Wait

The fairy shrimp of the ephemeral pools are, in a sense, the defining organism of the Alto Seco. Nothing else so perfectly embodies the plateau's central principle: that life here is not about abundance but about *timing*.

The shrimp live most of their lives as eggs. The eggs are tiny — half a millimeter across, round, encased in a thick, dark shell that is resistant to desiccation, UV radiation, freezing, and the passage of years. They lie in the dust of the dry basins, mixed with sediment and organic debris, in concentrations that can reach thousands per square meter. They can remain viable for decades. There are eggs in the Alto Seco's dust that were laid before the processing stations were built. Some of them will still hatch.

The trigger is water — but not just any water. The eggs require a specific combination of temperature, osmotic pressure, and dissolved oxygen to break dormancy. A light dew is not enough. A seep is too constant, too mineral-rich. What the eggs need is exactly what the plateau provides a few times a year: a sudden influx of rainwater, filling a basin to a depth of a few inches, at a temperature between about fifty and seventy degrees, with low dissolved mineral content. When these conditions are met — when a storm dumps rain into a basin and the pool forms and the water is right — the eggs hatch within hours.

The shrimp emerge as nauplii — microscopic larvae, barely visible to the naked eye, that swim with a jerky, spiraling motion. They grow fast. Within a day, they are visible — tiny, translucent commas in the water, swarming in dense, flickering aggregations near the surface. Within three days, they are adults: an inch long, slender, with eleven pairs of leaf-like swimming legs that propel them upside-down through the water in smooth, undulating strokes. They are beautiful in a way that nothing else on the plateau is beautiful — their bodies are nearly transparent, and you can see their internal organs, the dark line of their digestive tract, the pulsing of their simple hearts. Their eyes are black dots on stalks. They swim with a kind of frantic elegance, as if they know their time is short.

It is. A typical pool lasts five to seven days. The shrimp must feed, grow, mature, mate, and lay eggs within that window. They feed on algae and bacteria that bloom in the nutrient-rich rainwater, filtering the water with their swimming legs. They grow visibly from hour to hour. By day three or four, the females are carrying egg sacs — dark, brooding clusters of eggs visible through their transparent bodies. The males pursue them in looping, tumbling courtship swims. Mating is brief and frequent. The females begin depositing eggs almost immediately, releasing them into the water where they sink to the sediment at the pool's bottom.

The shrimp's abundance is staggering. A single pool, no larger than a bathtub, might contain tens of thousands of individuals. The water seems to boil with them. And this abundance does not go unnoticed.

The birds come first. A species of small, gray plover — high-altitude specialists that nest on the bare gravel plains — descends on the pools in flocks of fifty or a hundred, their arrival sudden and raucous, a storm of wings and high, piping calls. They wade in the shallows and snap up shrimp with rapid, darting strikes of their bills, swallowing them whole, consuming hundreds in a feeding bout. Behind them come the sandpipers — smaller, browner, more deliberate, probing the sediment with their sensitive bills, extracting shrimp from the bottom where they shelter.

The lizards line the pool's edge — a species of medium-sized, tan-and-brown striped lizard that is common across the plateau but rarely seen in groups. At the pools, they congregate. A dozen or more might sit along a single pool's margin, motionless, their eyes fixed on the water, waiting for a shrimp to swim within range. Their strike is fast — a dart of the head, a snap of the jaws — and then stillness again, the swallowed shrimp a barely visible bulge in the throat. The lizards are patient. They will sit for hours. The shrimp keep coming.

Even the camelids visit the pools, though not to eat the shrimp. They come to drink, wading in belly-deep, their long legs rippling the water, sending the shrimp into panicked swarms. The camelids drink slowly, deliberately, raising their heads between swallows to scan the horizon for the cat. The water is muddy and warm and tastes of dissolved sediment, but it is water, and in the Alto Seco, water is water.

And the fox comes.

## The Fox

The Alto Seco fox is small — smaller than its lowland relatives, compact and lean, with large ears and a coat of grizzled gray-tan that blends perfectly with the gravel plains. It weighs perhaps eight pounds. It is the plateau's most versatile predator, and the one most often seen.

The fox is not a specialist. It eats everything. It eats the cushion beetles, digging them out of the frass-filled chambers with quick, scrabbling forepaws. It eats the toads, flipping pebbles with its nose to expose them during the day when they are torpid and helpless. It eats the lizards, when it can catch them — the lizards are fast, but the fox is patient, and it has learned to hunt them by waiting near their basking rocks in the early morning, before the lizards have warmed enough to move quickly. It eats the eggs of the ground-nesting plovers, when it can find them. It eats the fairy shrimp, wading into the ephemeral pools and snapping them up in great, splashing mouthfuls, a behavior that seems almost playful but is deadly efficient. It eats the carrion left by the cat's kills, picking the bones clean after the vultures have stripped the larger muscles.

It eats, in other words, whatever the plateau offers, whenever the plateau offers it. This flexibility is its survival strategy. The Alto Seco does not provide enough of any single food source to sustain a predator, so the fox has become a generalist, an opportunist, a creature that can extract calories from every niche, every transient abundance, every brief window of plenty.

The fox is solitary, but not aggressively so. Pairs come together briefly to mate, and females raise their kits alone, denning in shallow burrows dug into the talus slopes at the base of the western ridges. The kits are born in the season of most frequent rain, when the ephemeral pools are most reliable and the food supply — shrimp, emerging toads, insects — is at its peak. The female hunts constantly during this period, ranging across miles of plateau, returning to the den with her belly full, regurgitating partially digested food for the kits. By the time the dry season sets in, the kits are old enough to hunt on their own, and the family disperses.

The fox's most distinctive behavior is its relationship with the condensation traps. The fox has learned — or, more precisely, generations of foxes have learned, each one discovering the trick independently, because it is not taught but stumbled upon — that the mesh panels of the traps collect dew, and that this dew drips into the collection troughs, and that the troughs, if they are not cracked or clogged, contain water in the early morning. The fox visits the traps at dawn, moving from one to the next, lapping the thin layer of accumulated water from the troughs. The maintenance crews, when they visit, find fox tracks in the dust around every functional trap array. The fox is, in a sense, a beneficiary of the industrial water-harvesting system — a parasite on the infrastructure, drawing off a tiny fraction of the collected dew. The amount it takes is negligible. The stations' sensors do not register the loss. The fox drinks and moves on.

The fox is also the most vocal animal on the plateau. It has a range of calls — a sharp, yipping bark used as an alarm, a lower, guttural chitter used in close-range social interactions, a high, wavering howl that it produces at dusk, alone, for reasons that are not entirely clear. The howl carries for miles in the thin air. It is one of the few sounds that can make the Alto Seco feel lonely.

## The Camelids

The wild camelids of the Alto Seco are the largest herbivores on the plateau, and the most visible. They stand about five feet at the shoulder, with long, slender necks, large dark eyes, and thick, cinnamon-colored wool that insulates them against both the midday heat and the nighttime cold. Their faces are expressive in a way that reads almost as curiosity — they watch you approach with their heads held high, ears swiveled forward, nostrils flaring, assessing. At a certain distance — a distance they calculate with uncanny precision — they turn and run, and the sound of their hooves on the gravel is like a drumroll, a deep, rolling thunder that carries for miles.

They live in herds of twenty to thirty, composed mostly of females and their young, led by a single dominant male who is both protector and tyrant. The male is larger than the females, his wool thicker, his neck muscled and scarred from fights with rivals. He is vigilant, constantly scanning the terrain, his head swiveling in slow arcs as the herd grazes. He is the first to detect the cat. His alarm call — a high, nasal, trumpeting bleat — sends the herd into instant motion, a coordinated wheel-and-run that is as practiced and fluid as a flock of birds turning in flight.

The herd's movement across the plateau follows the water. They graze on the bunchgrasses that grow near the seeps and along the edges of the ephemeral pools, and they move from one water source to the next in a slow, looping circuit that takes them across the entire plateau over the course of a season. They know every seep, every reliable basin, every functional condensation trap (they have learned, like the fox, to drink from the collection troughs, though their larger muzzles require them to tilt their heads at awkward angles to reach the water). They know the schedule of the dew pools — which basins collect water reliably, which ones are too shallow to persist past midmorning. Their knowledge is not conscious, not mapped, but it is precise, accumulated over generations, encoded in the herd's collective behavior.

Their grazing is destructive in a small, sustainable way. They crop the bunchgrasses close to the ground, and they trample the cushion plants that lie in their path — not often enough to kill the cushions, but enough to damage their outer surfaces, creating openings in the armor that the interior communities must then repair or abandon. The camelids are not gentle with the landscape. They are large animals in a fragile environment, and their passage leaves marks: cropped grass, trampled cushions, piles of dung that dry quickly in the arid air and are colonized by dung beetles within hours.

The dung is its own ecosystem. The dung beetles — a large, metallic-green species, the size of a thumbnail — arrive at fresh dung within minutes, drawn by the smell. They burrow into the pile, excavating chambers where they lay their eggs, provisioning each chamber with a ball of dung for the larvae to feed on. The beetles are strong and industrious, and a fresh pile of camelid dung can be completely dismantled and buried within a day. The buried dung fertilizes the thin soil, providing nutrients for the bunchgrasses that the camelids eat. The cycle is tight and efficient. Nothing is wasted.

The young camelids — the calves — are the herd's vulnerability. They are born in the season of most frequent rain, when the grass is most abundant and the water most available. They can stand within an hour of birth and run within a day, but they are slow compared to the adults, and they are the cat's preferred prey. The herd protects them by surrounding them — when the herd runs, the calves run in the center, flanked by adults on all sides. But the cat is patient, and the plateau is vast, and not every calf survives.

## The Cat

The cat of the Alto Seco is not a true big cat, but it is big enough. It stands about two and a half feet at the shoulder and weighs perhaps sixty pounds — the size of a large lynx, which it resembles, with tufted ears, a short tail, and a coat of spotted gray-brown that matches the rocky terrain so perfectly that the animal seems to materialize and dematerialize as it moves. Its paws are large and soft-padded. Its claws are retractable. Its eyes are yellow-green and set wide apart, giving it an almost owlish face. It is, by any measure, a beautiful animal. It is also a killing machine, finely tuned to the plateau's specific challenges.

The cat is solitary. Each adult maintains a territory of perhaps fifty square miles — an enormous range, but necessary in a landscape where prey is dispersed and unpredictable. The territories overlap at the edges, and the cat marks its boundaries with scent — spraying urine on prominent rocks, scraping its claws on the trunks of the stunted pines. Encounters between adults are rare and usually nonviolent — a brief standoff, a mutual assessment, a withdrawal. The cat has no energy to waste on fighting. Every calorie matters.

It hunts by ambush. This is its defining behavior, and the one that makes it so difficult to observe. The cat does not chase. It does not stalk in the open. It finds a location where prey is likely to pass — a game trail between seeps, a narrow defile between ridges, the edge of an ephemeral pool where the camelids come to drink — and it waits. It waits for hours. It waits with a stillness that is almost geological, its body pressed flat against the ground, its eyes half-closed, its breathing slow and shallow. It is indistinguishable from the rocks around it. Lizards walk across its back. Beetles crawl over its paws. The cat does not move.

When prey comes within range — usually a camelid calf that has strayed from the herd's center, or a fox that is preoccupied with digging beetles out of a cushion — the cat explodes. The acceleration is startling. From complete stillness to full sprint in a fraction of a second, covering twenty feet in a single bound. The kill is quick — a bite to the throat, a suffocating hold, a brief struggle. The cat drags the carcass to a sheltered location — a rock overhang, a shallow cave, the lee of a large boulder — and eats methodically, starting with the internal organs, the most nutrient-dense tissues. A single calf can sustain the cat for a week.

The cat is not a picky eater. When camelids are scarce — during the cold season, when the herds retreat to the eastern foothills — the cat hunts whatever it can find. It digs toads out of the pavement, flipping pebbles with its paws. It raids cushion communities, tearing at the woody stems to expose the beetles and frogs within. It catches birds on the wing, leaping to snatch them out of the air as they rise from the ephemeral pools. It eats carrion when it finds it, competing with the vultures and the foxes for the scraps of its own kills and the kills of others.

The cat is almost never seen. It is nocturnal by preference, crepuscular by necessity, and its camouflage is so effective that you could walk past one at a distance of ten feet and never know it was there. The maintenance crews who visit the processing stations report occasional sightings — a flash of spotted fur on a ridge, a pair of reflective eyes in the headlights — but these are rare. More often, the cat's presence is inferred: a fresh kill, a scat pile on a prominent rock, a set of tracks in the dust around a condensation trap, circling, assessing, moving on.

The cat is the plateau's apex predator, and it is also its most fragile. The population is small — perhaps a few dozen individuals across the entire plateau — and it is entirely dependent on the camelid herds, which are entirely dependent on the seeps and the dew pools, which are entirely dependent on the water cycle, which is entirely dependent on the climate. Any disruption to this chain — a shift in rainfall patterns, a reduction in dew formation, a change in the condensation trap arrays that alters the availability of surface water — could cascade through the system and eliminate the cat within a generation. The cat is a specialist disguised as a generalist. It can eat anything, but it needs the camelids, and the camelids need the water, and the water needs the sky.

## The Vultures

The red-headed vultures are the most conspicuous animals on the Alto Seco. You cannot look at the sky without seeing them. They are enormous — wingspans approaching ten feet, bodies the size of turkeys, with bare, wrinkled heads and necks of deep, waxy red that looks almost artificial against the bronze sky. Their plumage is black, and in flight they are cruciform — the wings held flat, the tail fanned, the head tucked, the overall silhouette like a dark cross against the light. They are beautiful in the way that large, functional things are beautiful: not delicate, not graceful in the conventional sense, but *perfect* for what they do.

What they do is soar. The vultures do not flap their wings except to take off and land. Once aloft, they ride the thermals — the columns of warm air that rise off the sun-heated plateau — in slow, deliberate spirals, gaining altitude with each turn, ascending to heights where they are barely visible from the ground. From these heights, they can see the entire plateau. Their eyesight is extraordinary — they can detect a carcass from miles away, not by smell (unlike some vultures, they have a poor sense of smell) but by sight, scanning the ground for the telltale signs: a cluster of other vultures, a cloud of carrion beetles, a stillness in a place where there should be movement.

When a vulture spots a potential carcass, it begins to descend — not in a steep dive, but in a gradual, circling descent that conserves energy. Other vultures see the descent and follow, and within minutes, a carcass that was attended by one vulture is surrounded by a dozen, then twenty, then more. They land at a distance and approach on foot, walking with a waddling, side-to-side gait that is comical in contrast to their aerial majesty. At the carcass, they feed in a loose hierarchy — the largest, oldest birds eat first, tearing at the flesh with their powerful, hooked beaks, while the younger birds wait at the periphery, hissing and jostling. A camelid carcass can be stripped to bones in a few hours.

The vultures nest on the cliff faces of the northern mesas — the disconnected tablelands that pinch off the plateau's northern end. The mesas are inaccessible except by climbing, and the vultures nest on ledges and in shallow caves, in dense colonies that can number in the hundreds. The colonies are loud — a constant, raucous cacophony of hisses, grunts, bill-clacks, and wing-beats that carries for miles on the wind. The noise is startling in a landscape defined by its silence. It is as if the vultures have concentrated all the sound that the rest of the plateau has forgone.

The nesting colonies are also, frankly, foul. The ledges are whitewashed with droppings, the rock stained and streaked, the air thick with the smell of guano and regurgitated carrion. The vultures defecate on their own legs to cool themselves — the evaporating droppings act as a cooling system — and the accumulated waste creates a crusty, alkaline deposit that kills any vegetation that might otherwise grow on the ledges. The colonies are islands of biological intensity, concentrated and overwhelming, and they persist for generations, the same ledges occupied year after year, the accumulated droppings building up into deposits that are inches thick.

The young vultures — the fledglings — are covered in white down and look nothing like their parents. They stay on the nest for months, fed by both parents, who regurgitate partially digested carrion directly into their mouths. The fledglings' first flights are clumsy and dangerous — they launch from the nest ledge and plummet, catching the air at the last moment, flapping desperately until they find a thermal. Many fail. The rocks below the nesting colonies are scattered with the bones of young vultures that didn't make it. The survivors learn quickly. Within a few weeks, they are soaring with their parents, riding the thermals over the plateau, scanning the ground for death.

The vultures are the plateau's cleanup crew, its sanitation department, its morticians. They consume the dead, and in doing so, they prevent the spread of disease and recycle nutrients back into the system. A carcass that is not eaten by vultures is a carcass that rots, and a rotting carcass in a water-scarce environment is a breeding ground for bacteria that could contaminate the seeps and pools. The vultures keep the system clean. They are, in their way, as essential as the water itself.

## The Lizards

There are several species of lizard on the Alto Seco, but the most abundant — and the most visible — is the striped pool lizard, the one that congregates at the ephemeral pools to feed on fairy shrimp. It is a medium-sized lizard, perhaps eight inches long including the tail, with a pattern of alternating tan and brown stripes running the length of its body. It is fast, alert, and ubiquitous. You see it everywhere: on rocks, on gravel, on the trunks of the stunted pines, on the edges of cushions, on the rims of dew pools. It is the lizard you are most likely to see, and the one most likely to see you first.

The striped pool lizard is an ambush predator, like the cat, but on a smaller scale. It hunts by sitting motionless on a vantage point — a rock, a pebble, a slight rise in the gravel — and waiting for something edible to come within range. Its prey is small: beetles, ants, moths, small spiders, the occasional fairy shrimp when the pools are available. Its strike is a quick dart of the head, a snap of the jaws, and then stillness again, the prey swallowed whole. It eats several times a day during the warm months, building up fat reserves for the cold season, when it retreats into rock crevices and enters a state of torpor.

The lizard's most distinctive behavior is its relationship with the sun. It is a heliotherm — it regulates its body temperature by basking, and its daily schedule is dictated by the sun's position. In the early morning, it emerges from its overnight shelter (a crack in a rock, a gap between pavement stones, the interior of a cushion) and finds a flat, sun-facing surface to bask on. It flattens its body against the warm rock, spreading its limbs, maximizing its surface area, absorbing heat. As its body temperature rises, it becomes more active — it begins to hunt, to move, to interact with other lizards. By midday, when the rock surfaces are too hot to touch, it retreats to shade — the underside of a boulder, the interior of a cushion, the shadow of a condensation trap frame. In the late afternoon, as the rocks cool, it emerges again for a final bout of hunting before the sun sets and the temperature plummets.

The lizard is prey for many things: the cat, the fox, the vultures (which will snatch a lizard from a rock if the opportunity presents itself), the larger centipedes, the hawks that occasionally migrate through. Its defense is speed and camouflage. When threatened, it freezes, relying on its striped pattern to blend with the gravel and rock. If the threat persists, it runs — a sudden, explosive sprint to the nearest cover, zigzagging unpredictably. If caught, it can detach its tail, which continues to writhe and twitch on the ground, distracting the predator while the lizard escapes. The tail regrows over the course of several months, though the replacement is never as long or as perfectly patterned as the original.

## The Plovers

The gray plovers of the Alto Seco are small, unremarkable-looking birds — pale gray above, white below, with short bills and large, dark eyes. They are ground-nesters, laying their eggs directly on the bare gravel plains, in shallow scrapes lined with a few pebbles and bits of dried grass. The eggs are speckled brown and gray, and they are almost impossible to see against the gravel. This is the point. The plovers' survival strategy is crypsis — the eggs are invisible, the nest is invisible, and the incubating adult, sitting motionless on the nest, is nearly invisible too, its gray plumage blending with the stones.

The plovers are the first birds to arrive at the ephemeral pools. They seem to sense the pools' formation — perhaps by the sound of the rain, perhaps by the change in air pressure, perhaps by some subtler cue. Within hours of a pool forming, the plovers are there, arriving in flocks that descend from the sky in tight, wheeling formations, their high, piping calls filling the air. They land at the pool's edge and immediately begin feeding, wading in the shallows, snapping up fairy shrimp with rapid, precise strikes of their bills. They eat voraciously, consuming hundreds of shrimp in a feeding bout, their small bodies fueling up on the concentrated protein.

The plovers are migratory, in a loose sense. They do not leave the plateau, but they move across it, following the rain. When a storm hits one part of the plateau, the plovers in that area converge on the new pools. When the pools dry, the plovers disperse, returning to their breeding territories on the gravel plains. Their movements are irregular and unpredictable, dictated by the storm patterns, and they can cover dozens of miles in a day. They are, in their way, as nomadic as the camelids, but on wings instead of hooves.

The plovers' chicks are precocial — they hatch covered in down, with their eyes open, and they can walk within hours. They leave the nest almost immediately, following their parents to the nearest water source. The chicks feed themselves, pecking at small insects and crustaceans, while the parents stand guard, watching for predators. The fox is their primary threat, and the plovers have evolved a distraction display to deal with it: when a fox approaches the nest or the chicks, the adult plover feigns injury, dragging one wing on the ground, fluttering pathetically, leading the fox away from the vulnerable young. The display is convincing. The fox follows. The plover leads it a hundred yards, two hundred, then suddenly takes flight, leaving the fox confused and empty-mouthed.

## The Stunted Pines

The gnarled pines that grow near the larger seeps are the tallest living things on the plateau, and the oldest, and the most improbable. They should not be here. They are trees — large, woody organisms that require water and soil and shelter — in a landscape that offers almost none of these things. But they persist, and they have persisted for centuries, and their persistence is a testament to the power of adaptation.

The pines grow in small clusters, rarely more than five or six together, always within a few yards of a seep. Their roots tap into the seep's moisture, following the water through the porous sedimentary rock, extending deep into the substrate. The roots are the tree's lifeline, and they are enormous relative to the above-ground portion — a tree that stands ten feet tall might have a root system that extends thirty feet in every direction, a vast, subterranean network that far exceeds the visible structure.

The above-ground portion is stunted and twisted, shaped by the wind into forms that look more like sculpture than botany. The trunks are flattened on the windward side, the bark scoured away by windblown sand, the wood beneath polished to a smooth, silver-gray surface. The branches grow almost exclusively on the leeward side, reaching away from the prevailing wind like frozen flags, their needles clustered at the tips. The overall effect is of a tree that has been pressed sideways, compressed and deformed by the constant pressure of the wind, until it has become a kind of living weather vane, permanently indicating the direction of the force that shapes it.

The largest pines are several hundred years old. Their growth rings are incredibly narrow — a fraction of a millimeter per year, reflecting the extreme scarcity of water and nutrients. A tree that is four inches in diameter might be three centuries old. The wood is dense and resinous, and it does not rot. When a pine dies — usually from windthrow, the shallow root system finally losing its grip in a particularly violent storm — the trunk persists for decades, bleached silver by the sun, slowly being buried by windblown sediment.

The pines are landmarks. In a landscape with few vertical features, a cluster of pines is visible from miles away. The camelids use them as navigational references. The foxes den at their bases, digging burrows among the roots. The lizards bask on their windward trunks in the morning, when the wood is warm from the sun. The plovers sometimes nest in their sparse shade. The cat scratches its claws on their bark, marking its territory. The pines are, in their small way, centers of biological activity — oases of structure in a landscape of horizontality.

## The People

The people of the Alto Seco are the hardest inhabitants to describe, because they are the most elusive.

The maintenance crews are the most visible. They arrive every few weeks in rugged, dust-covered vehicles, following the rough dirt tracks that connect the three processing stations. They are employees of whoever operates the water-harvesting system — a company, a government agency, a cooperative; the specifics are unclear from the plateau itself. They wear practical clothing — heavy boots, wide-brimmed hats, layers that can be added or removed as the temperature swings. They carry tools and replacement parts. They work quickly and efficiently, servicing the filtration systems, repairing the condensation trap arrays, clearing sediment from the collection troughs, replacing worn mesh panels. They stay for a day or two at each station, sleeping in the small crew quarters attached to the main building, and then they move on. They do not linger. The plateau is a worksite to them, not a home.

But they are not the only people, and they are not the first.

The older structures — the stone walls, the terraces, the cairns, the round house — are evidence of a much longer human presence. Who built them is not recorded anywhere on the plateau. There are no inscriptions, no artifacts beyond the structures themselves, no oral histories that have been documented. The stone walls are dry-stacked without mortar, their construction technique similar to that of ancient pastoralist cultures in high-altitude environments worldwide. The terraces suggest agriculture — or at least the intention of agriculture — in a landscape that currently supports none. The cairns mark routes that no longer exist, connecting places that are no longer named.

The round house is the most enigmatic. Its construction is different from the stone walls and terraces — more deliberate, more refined. The walls are adobe and stone, smoothly finished, the dome expertly constructed without formwork. The petroglyphs inside are deeply carved and carefully executed — spirals, circles, anthropomorphic figures with outstretched arms. The building is in good condition, which suggests ongoing maintenance, but there is no evidence of recent use — no footprints, no offerings, no signs of fire. It stands on its low rise near the center of the plateau, facing east, waiting.

Someone maintains it. Or the plateau maintains it. The distinction may not matter.

There are reports — unverified, anecdotal, the kind of thing that maintenance crews mention over drinks in the lowland bars — of a person, or persons, seen at a distance on the plateau. A figure on a ridge. A fire at night, brief and small. Footprints in the dust around a seep that do not match any known boot pattern. These reports are not consistent enough to constitute evidence, but they are persistent enough to constitute a rumor. The plateau is vast. A single person, or a small group, could live on it indefinitely without being found, if they knew the water sources and the shelter locations and the seasons. The older inhabitants did it for centuries. There is no reason someone couldn't do it now.

The round house's door faces east. The petroglyphs depict figures with outstretched arms, oriented toward the rising sun. The building's interior is cool and dark and smells of earth and old stone. If you sit inside it, cross-legged on the packed-earth floor, with the door open and the morning light streaming in, you can hear the wind outside and the distant hum of the nearest processing station and, if you are quiet enough, the faint, layered hum of the insect communities in the nearby cushions. The round house is a place of listening. It may have always been a place of listening.

## The Interconnections

The lives of the Alto Seco are not separate. They are woven together in a web of dependencies and interactions so dense that removing any single thread would unravel the whole.

The cushion plants create habitat for the beetles, the ants, the aphids, the centipedes, the spiders, the frogs. The beetles break down the dead plant material and create soil. The ants farm the aphids and protect them from predators. The frogs eat the smallest larvae and are eaten by the centipedes. The bees pollinate the cushions and nest in the ground between them. The cushions' scent attracts the bees, and the bees' pollination produces the seeds that become new cushions.

The dew pools hatch the fairy shrimp, which feed the plovers, the sandpipers, the lizards, the foxes, and the camelids. The shrimp's eggs return to the dust and wait for the next rain. The plovers nest on the gravel plains, and their eggs feed the foxes. The plovers' distraction displays protect their chicks from the foxes, and the foxes' predation keeps the plover population in check.

The toads absorb dew and eat insects and are eaten by the cat and the fox and the larger centipedes. Their eggs hatch in the rain pools and their tadpoles feed on algae and detritus and are eaten by the shrimp (which are, in this one instance, predators as well as prey). The toads' emergence at night is timed to the dew formation, and the dew formation is timed to the temperature drop, and the temperature drop is timed to the sunset.

The camelids graze the bunchgrasses and trample the cushions and produce dung that feeds the dung beetles that fertilize the soil that grows the bunchgrasses. The camelids drink from the seeps and the dew pools and the condensation traps. The camelids are preyed upon by the cat, and their carcasses feed the vultures and the foxes and the carrion beetles.

The cat hunts the camelids and the foxes and the lizards and the toads and the birds. The cat's kills feed the vultures and the foxes and the carrion beetles. The cat's territory encompasses the entire water cycle — the seeps, the dew pools, the condensation traps, the ephemeral pools — and its survival depends on the integrity of that cycle.

The vultures eat the dead and keep the system clean. They nest on the mesas and their droppings fertilize the cliff-face ledges. They soar on the thermals that rise off the sun-heated plateau, and their presence in the sky is a barometer of the plateau's health: if the vultures are circling low, something has died. If they are high and drifting, all is routine.

The condensation traps harvest the dew and the processing stations filter the water and the pipes carry it to the lowlands, and the foxes and the camelids drink from the collection troughs, and the maintenance crews service the system and leave footprints in the dust that are erased by the wind within days.

The round house stands on its low rise, facing east, and the petroglyphs on its interior walls depict figures with outstretched arms, and the figures have been there for longer than anyone can remember, and they will be there for longer than anyone can imagine, and they are part of the web too — a human thread, woven into the fabric of the plateau, connecting the past to the present, the living to the dead, the visible to the invisible.

The Alto Seco is a machine for living, running on almost nothing, producing almost everything it needs from dew and dust and light. Its parts are its inhabitants — the cushions and the toads and the shrimp and the camelids and the cat and the vultures and the foxes and the plovers and the lizards and the pines and the people — and each part is essential, and each part is connected to every other part, and the whole thing runs with a quiet, persistent efficiency that has been going on for millennia and will continue for millennia more.

The machine is not perfect. It is not stable. It is constantly shifting, constantly adjusting, constantly negotiating the terms of its own existence. A drought year kills the cushion plants' seedlings. A late frost kills the bunchgrass seeds. A shift in the storm patterns eliminates the ephemeral pools for a season, and the fairy shrimp eggs lie dormant, and the plovers go hungry, and the foxes range farther, and the cat hunts harder, and the camelids lose calves, and the vultures soar higher, scanning a landscape that offers less. The machine adapts. It has always adapted. Adaptation is what it does.

And through it all, the dew forms and drips and pools and evaporates, and the wind moves across the gravel plains, and the vultures ride the thermals, and the cushions grow a fraction of a millimeter, and the toads shift beneath the stones, and the round house faces east, and the light changes from gray-blue to white-gold to bronze to red to black, and the plateau breathes, and the machine runs, and the lives within it persist, small and patient and extraordinarily efficient, making sense of a place that makes sense of them.
