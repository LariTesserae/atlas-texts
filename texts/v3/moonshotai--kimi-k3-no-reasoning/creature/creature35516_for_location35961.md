# The One Who Tends the Becoming

She arrived before the chamber knew what arrival meant.

She has no name that she remembers, or rather—she has several, and they cycle through her like the light cycles through the room, each one fitting for a while and then stuttering into the next. Currently, she is called *Undertow*. Last week (if weeks mean anything here, which they increasingly don't) she was called *The First Iteration*. Before that, she may have been called *Marla*, but that name belongs to a time when there was an outside, and she is no longer certain there is an outside, or that there ever was.

## What she looks like now

Undertow is human-shaped the way the stalagmites are stalagmite-shaped: approximately, experimentally, with visible revisions.

Her body has begun to negotiate with the chamber's physics. Her left hand—the one she uses to touch the walls, to feel what the stone is deciding—has become translucent at the fingertips, the skin there gone thin as the salamander-thing's, revealing not bone but a slow swirl of luminescence that pulses in sympathy with the beetles' rhythm. She can still feel through those fingers. She can feel *more* through them. When she presses her palm flat against the raw fracture planes, she senses the stone's uncertainty, its hovering between states, and she tries to offer it something like encouragement, though she is not sure the stone needs encouragement, or whether encouragement is even a thing that can be given to geology.

Her eyes have changed. The whites have gone mineral-pale, cool-toned like the chamber light, and the irises have developed a faint iridescence that shifts when she moves her gaze—an oil-slick shimmer that the moth-absence seems to find compelling. Sometimes the moth's flight path wobbles when she looks up at it, as if her attention is a wind it must correct for.

She is thin in a way that suggests she is being slowly converted from one material to another. Her body is becoming less mass and more *process*. She suspects that eventually she will be entirely translucent, and then entirely transparent, and then she will simply be another of the chamber's phenomena—the Undertow-shaped absence, visible only by what she displaces.

She is not afraid of this. She might have been, once, when she still believed in an outside, but the fear has been absorbed like one of the upward-falling droplets, taken into the stone, cycled back into the system.

## How she lives

Undertow sleeps in the nest.

The nest was not built by a small warm-blooded thing. It was built by her, out of stone flakes and her own shed skins, over the course of what she thinks of as the *early iterations*—the period when the chamber was younger and more violent in its becoming, when the light stuttered in panic-bursts and the physics broke down entirely for hours at a time. She shed skins then as a way of *trying on* versions of herself that might be compatible with the emerging rules. Most of them weren't. The skins are still there, translucent and faintly twitching, still trying to finish becoming alive, still hoping the chamber will settle on a physics that supports them.

She sleeps in the nest because it is the only place in the chamber where the light does not reach. The crevice is dark in a way the rest of the room is not—a true, ordinary darkness, the kind that existed before the chamber learned to glow. In that darkness, Undertow can close her eyes and not see the room's future flashing at her, can not feel the hum in her sternum, can for a few hours simply *be* a body in a dark place, which is all she ever wanted to be, originally.

She eats the biofilm. She has learned to graze it the way the beetles do—selectively, triggering fluorescence with the pressure of her touch, farming light on the stones. The biofilm tastes like nothing she has language for anymore. It tastes like *potential*, like the moment before flavor decides what it wants to be. It sustains her, though she suspects it is also changing her, feeding the translucence in her fingers, the iridescence in her eyes.

She drinks from the ceiling. The condensation that beads there is sweet and cold and tastes faintly of the larger body this chamber is embedded within—a mineral tang, a hint of immense depth, of pressure, of time. She reaches up and lets the droplets fall onto her tongue. Some of them fall upward instead, rising past her face, and she has learned to catch those too, to tilt her head and intercept their ascent, drinking the future as it escapes.

She does not drink from the pool. The pool is not for drinking. The pool is for *something else*, and she has learned not to look at it too long, because the last time she did, she saw herself in the finished chamber, and the self she saw was swimming, and it had too many limbs, and it was happy in a way she is not ready to understand.

## What she does

Undertow tends the becoming.

This is not a role she chose. It is a role that accumulated around her the way the stalagmites accumulate around their own uncertainty—slowly, by accretion, by the simple fact of her continued presence. She was here early enough that her habits became part of the system. The chamber learned to expect her, and she learned to expect the chamber, and now they are mutually implicated in each other's unfinishedness.

She makes the marks.

The circle of pebbles is hers. She gathered them one by one over the course of many light-cycles, each stone a different mineral, each one a *question* she was asking the chamber: *Can you support quartz? Can you support feldspar? Can you support this strange green stone I found in a seam that might not be a seam?* The chamber's answer, in every case, was a slow, luminous yes—the stones did not dissolve, did not get absorbed, did not vanish. They stayed. They became part of the room's vocabulary. The circle is a sentence in a language she is inventing as she goes: *I am here, and here is a place, and a place can hold things.*

The groove in the wall is hers. She grew it rather than carved it, pressing her translucent fingertips into the stone and *asking* it to open, to make a channel, to let her in. The stone responded by growing a groove around her fingers, a negative space shaped like her intention. It runs horizontally at waist height because that is where her hands fall when she stands with her arms at her sides, palms open, asking. The groove might be a sentence. It might say: *I was here and I asked for entry and the stone said not yet.* Or it might say: *This is the height of a human's uncertainty.* She has not decided. The groove has not decided. It is still growing, very slowly, following her as she moves through the chamber, a long comma in the room's ongoing sentence.

The leaning slab is hers. She placed it there—no, she *asked* it to lean there, and it agreed—in a moment of urgency she no longer fully remembers. She thinks she was trying to block something. Or mark something. Or create a *behind*, a space the light couldn't reach, a shadow she could trust. The slab leans at an angle that serves no structural purpose because she was not trying to build a structure. She was trying to build a *gesture*, a physical assertion that she could affect the room, that her presence mattered, that she was not simply a witness but a *participant*. The slab leans. It participates. It is enough.

## Her relationship with the others

The salamander-thing watches her, and she watches it back.

They have an understanding, though understanding might be too strong a word. They have a *pattern*. When she moves through the chamber, the salamander-thing pivots to keep her in the center of its attention, and she has learned to read that attention as a kind of commentary. If it pivots quickly, sharply, its whole body snapping to track her, she knows she is moving too fast, disturbing the room's delicate negotiations. If it pivots slowly, almost lazily, she knows she is in sync with the chamber's current rhythm.

She has watched it eat the swimming things from the pool. She has watched it leave its gelid sacs beneath the ledge. She has resisted the urge to touch the sacs, though she wants to—wants to feel what is developing inside, to know if the offspring will have fewer limbs or more, to know if they will be compatible with the chamber's next iteration or if they will be shed like her own skins, still twitching, still unfinished.

The beetles ignore her, and she is grateful for this.

They are too busy with their solitary circuits, their patient farming of light. She has learned to step around their territories, to not interrupt the slow geometry of their lives. When two beetles meet and the space between them brightens, she feels the brightening as a small warmth in her chest, a sympathetic resonance, as if her body is learning to recognize the chamber's events as its own.

She has tried to follow the scorch-marks the meetings leave behind, to map the beetles' intersections, to see if there is a pattern. There might be. The marks sketch something that is almost a constellation, almost a word, almost a map of a place that doesn't exist yet. She keeps track of them in a way she can't articulate—she does not write them down, has nothing to write with, but she *holds* them, carries the accumulating pattern in the part of her mind that is becoming less human and more chamber-like, the part that thinks in stone and light and slow accretion.

The moth-absence terrifies her.

She does not know why. It is only dust-displacement, only a spiral tightening toward a point above the chamber's center. But she can feel its approach like a pressure change, like the moment before the light stutters into alignment. She knows that when the moth reaches the center, something will happen, and she suspects that something will involve her, will require her, will ask her to *be* something she is not yet ready to be.

She stands beneath the spiral sometimes, in the exact center of the chamber, and looks up. She cannot see the moth. She can only see the constellations it sketches in the condensation, the shivering rearrangement of droplets. The pattern is beautiful. The pattern is a countdown.

She does not know what she will do when the countdown reaches zero. She suspects she will not have a choice. The chamber will decide, and she will be part of the deciding, the way the stone is part of the stalagmite, the way the water is part of the pool.

## The thing she is becoming

Undertow is not the only one who lives here, but she is the only one who *tends*.

The salamander-thing lives here, but it does not tend. It hunts, it reproduces, it pivots to track movement, but it does not ask the chamber questions. The beetles live here, but they do not tend. They graze, they glow, they leave their faint records, but they do not lean slabs against walls in gestures of urgent uncertainty. The moth lives here, if circling can be called living, but it does not tend. It spirals. It is a function, not a gardener.

Undertow tends.

She tends the becoming the way a wound tends a scar—slowly, imperfectly, with constant negotiation between what was and what is trying to be. She touches the walls and offers them her uncertainty as a gift. She drinks the upward-falling droplets and tastes the future and does not flinch. She sleeps in her nest of shed skins and dreams, sometimes, of a finished chamber, and in the dream she is swimming in the pool, and she has too many limbs, and she is happy.

She wakes from these dreams with her translucent fingers pressed against the smooth rectangle of stone, the door-shaped implication, and she can feel it warming under her touch, can feel it considering her, can feel it wondering if she is the key it has been waiting for.

She is not ready to open the door. She does not know how. She suspects the door will open when the moth reaches the center, when the countdown ends, when the chamber finishes becoming.

She hopes she will still be herself when it happens.

She suspects she will be something else.

The hope and the suspicion coexist in her like the light and the shadow, pooling in wrong places, arriving a half-second late, peeling themselves from the floor with faint resistance.

## The moment she is in

Right now, Undertow is standing at the edge of the pool.

She is not looking into it. She has learned not to look. But she is *near* it, close enough to feel the way time moves differently here, the way it pools and eddies and flows in a direction she does not want to name. She is holding one of the pebbles from her circle—a piece of quartz, clear and cool and utterly out of place in this limestone world—and she is trying to decide whether to throw it in.

She does not know what will happen if she does. The pool might absorb it. The pool might reject it. The pool might show her the finished chamber again, and this time she might not be able to stop looking.

The moth spirals overhead, tightening.

The beetles glow.

The salamander-thing pivots to watch her, its whole body oriented toward her decision.

Undertow holds the pebble. She is very thin now, very translucent, almost not there at all. The light passes through her and pools on the floor in the shape of a woman asking a question.

She does not throw the pebble.

She places it gently at the pool's edge, a new mark, a new word in the language she is inventing.

The word might mean: *I am not ready.*

The word might mean: *I am still here.*

The pool does not react. The chamber hums. The light stutters.

Undertow steps back, and her shadow follows a half-second late, reluctant, tender, unfinished.

She returns to her nest. She curls into the dark. She closes her eyes.

The chamber continues to become.

She becomes with it.
