There is a caretaker, singular and ancient, though not in the way humans count age. They are a person who has chosen to stay behind in the valley while the rest of their community has retreated to higher, drier ground. They move through the pools slowly, their fingers trailing through the water to feel the resonant frequencies, adjusting the shapes of the channels with the touch of a surgeon. They speak a language that is mostly gesture and sound—taps on stone, hums into the water, whistles that bounce off the cliffs in specific frequencies. They are not building alone; they are building with the mountain. They are the first note in the symphony, the one who remembers how the song is supposed to end, even if the ending is centuries away.

There are also the builders, the ones who sing the mountain into shape. They are not many—perhaps a dozen—but they are a single organism, a living instrument. They work in shifts, their voices rising and falling in a cycle that matches the rise and fall of the sun and the moon. They are not just laborers; they are composers, each one a specialist in a particular frequency, a particular note. They know which song will coax the rock to weep, which rhythm will make it rise. They are not building a city; they are building a memory, a record of their existence that will outlast them by millennia. They are not afraid of the time it takes; they are afraid of the silence that would come if they stopped singing.

And there is the bird, the white-feathered creature that glides between the pools in silence. It is not a pet or a symbol; it is a reminder. It is a reminder that even in a place where humans are shaping the very bones of the earth, there is still room for something wild and untamed. It is a reminder that the song of the mountain is not the only song that matters. It is a reminder that the world is still bigger than the human imagination. It is a reminder that even in a place where the boundary between craft and geology has dissolved, there is still a boundary between the human and the non-human, and that boundary is worth preserving.
