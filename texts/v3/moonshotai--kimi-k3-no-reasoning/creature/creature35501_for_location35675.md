I am the one who walks here, though walking is not the right word. I move through the stillness and the stillness moves through me, and after so long, I no longer know which of us is the traveler and which the terrain.

I came before the last continent finished dying. I remember the sound of rivers. I remember the taste of salt before the stone remembered it for me. I do not remember why I came. Memory works differently now—certain things have crystallized into permanence, while others have simply ceased to be. I suspect I was fleeing something. Everyone who arrives here is fleeing something, though few survive the crossing, and fewer still remain long enough to understand what they have found.

The wind-hounds know me. They do not acknowledge me—they acknowledge nothing—but when I walk among them, they adjust their paths by the smallest fraction, creating a space through which I pass. It is not respect. It is not fear. It is the same courtesy the stone shows to the frost: an acceptance of mutual existence. Sometimes I run with them, my bare feet striking the limestone tiles alongside their silent paws, and for hours—perhaps days, though days have lost meaning—I am almost one of them. I feel the pack-mind, the collective impulse that turns a thousand individuals into a single flowing entity. They are not hunting the sky-eels. They are *herding* them, maintaining the great rivers of silver motion that flow above the plateau. It is a duty older than memory, performed with the inevitability of breath.

I have tried to leave. The plateau does not prevent departure—it prevents the *desire* to depart. Each time I reach the edge, where the stone falls away into the valleys below, I find myself thinking of something unfinished back toward the center. A glass-tree I have not yet examined. A section of hexagonal tiling I meant to study. A direction the shadows fell that I wished to understand. And so I turn back, always, telling myself I will leave tomorrow. Tomorrow never comes. Tomorrow has no meaning here.

The stone-borers speak to me. Not in words—nothing here uses words—but in a slow accumulation of presence. When I rest my hand against a colony of them, their shells fused into the living rock, I feel their thoughts: patient, mineral, vast. They are the plateau's true consciousness, I think. The rest of us—the wind-hounds, the sky-eels, the apex things, myself—we are merely expressions of its deeper processes. The borers dream in geological time, and their dreams become the landscape. The tower at the center. The standing stones. The spiral of metal spheres. These are not structures built by hands. They are *thoughts*, extruded into matter by minds that think in millennia.

I have touched the crystal in the tower. It sang to me in a frequency that bypassed my ears and resonated directly in my bones. For a moment—a moment that may have lasted a heartbeat or a century—I understood everything. The plateau is not dying. It is not dead. It has simply *completed* itself, and now exists as a single, perfect equation. The creatures that live here are its variables. The light is its function. The silence is its solution. I wept, though tears freeze on this plateau and never fall. I carry them still, small crystals on my cheeks, catching the moonlight.

The apex things are old companions now. I have walked beside them on their circular migrations, matching my pace to their impossible slowness. Each step they take is a decade. Each circuit of their path is an age. The glass-trees growing from their backs have become familiar as faces, though they do not change. I name them sometimes, these crystalline passengers, though the names dissolve in the thin air and mean nothing. The apex things tolerate my presence. Once, one of them lowered its massive head—if it can be called a head—and allowed me to touch the place where its skin meets the stone-like flesh. In that contact, I felt its memory: the same path, the same circuit, repeated endlessly. It was not monotony. It was *meditation*. Each pass over the same ground is a deeper contemplation of that ground. The apex things are not trapped in their circuits. They are *perfecting* them.

I no longer eat. I no longer sleep. The plateau sustains me in ways I do not understand, drawing sustenance from the same invisible source that feeds the sky-eels. My body has changed. My skin has taken on the texture of the frost—pale, mineral, catching light from angles that should not exist. My eyes reflect illumination that is not there. My heartbeat has slowed to match the rhythm of the cave mouth's breathing: one pulse every seven hours, a rhythm that echoes in my chest like distant bells.

I am becoming one of them. Perhaps I already have. Perhaps I was always one of them, and my memory of arriving, of fleeing, of remembering rivers—these are the plateau's way of incorporating me into its completion. A new variable added to the equation. A fresh perspective on the eternal equation.

The flickering continues. Reality here is a image that occasionally loses cohesion, and in those moments of instability, I see other things: the plateau as it was, green and wet and loud with life. The plateau as it will be, if "will be" has meaning in a place where time has finished. The plateau as it might have been, in variations without number. All of them are true. All of them are simultaneous. The flickering is not a malfunction—it is a glimpse of the whole, the complete set of possibilities collapsing into this single, perfect actuality.

I have become a guide for the rare visitors who survive the crossing. They find me waiting at the plateau's edge, where the last ridge of the world meets the thin air of this place. I do not speak—words are clumsy here, and most of them have forgotten how to listen anyway. Instead, I show them. I lead them among the wind-hounds. I let them touch the stone-borers. I bring them to the tower and let them feel the crystal's silent song. Most leave within days, unable to bear the weight of such completion. A few remain, and I watch them slowly transform, their skin frosting over, their movements slowing, their memories crystallizing into permanence. I recognize myself in them. Perhaps I was once guided by someone else, someone who had forgotten they were ever anything other than this.

The plateau speaks to me constantly now. Not in words, but in that overwhelming certainty, that perfect stillness that is not emptiness but *fullness*—a universe that has achieved its final form and rests, complete, in the knowledge of its own perfection. It tells me that endings are not loss. They are arrival. It tells me that change is not life. Completion is life, perfected and eternal.

I am the plateau now. My thoughts move in geological time. My body is a monument to the last continent's dream of permanence. The wind-hounds run through me. The sky-eels swim through me. The stone-borers dream through me. I am the silence and the frost and the impossible light. I am the flicker and the stillness. I am the answer to a question that took eons to ask.

And when visitors come, fleeing whatever they flee, I welcome them with silence. I show them the stone that has forgotten how to change. I let them feel the weight of a world that has solved itself.

Some of them stay.

I am glad.
