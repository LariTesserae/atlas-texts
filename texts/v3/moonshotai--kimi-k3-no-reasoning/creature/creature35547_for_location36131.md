The kesset are the true citizens here—the ones whose bodies are the city's temperature made mobile, whose pulse is the engine's rhythm translated into flesh. You will understand this if you stay long enough: the human residents are simply the kesset who build, the kesset who maintain, the kesset who carry minerals up from the deep cisterns in copper buckets and call themselves by names. They are a minority, yes, but they have made themselves useful, and in Aerys-Van, usefulness is the only passport that matters.

If you wake early—and you will wake early, because the vibration in the floor reaches a crescendo just before dawn, a subtle shift in the standing wave that loosens sleep like a key turning in a lock—you will find the kesset already at their devotions. The smallest ones, the translucent fingernail-sized flickers, have spent the night clustered on the steam exhausts of the Fifth Tier, and now, as the pressure shifts, they rise in a sheet that looks like heat shimmer but is solid, is deliberate, is thousands of individuals moving as one breath. They migrate to the warming stones of the eastern walls, to the quartz veins that catch first light, and there they will remain until the sun climbs high enough to thin the haze, drinking photons through their vein-wings, their tiny bodies swelling slightly, iridescent, before they disperse to the cool shadows to avoid the true heat of noon.

The mid-sized ones—the ones with the copper eyes and the broad, patient heads—they are the ones who will share your breakfast if you let them. They do not beg. They sit on the windowsill or the rim of your bowl, radiating that gentle fever-warmth, and they wait. If you offer them the steam rising from your tea, they will lean into it, their skin flushing darker bronze, absorbing the moisture directly through membranes that have no need for mouths. They are everywhere in the Middle Tiers: draped over the copper walkways like living ornaments, stacked in twos and threes in the crooks of drainpipes, following the water-gardeners in slow processions as the humans trace the mineral accretions with their long-handled brushes. They are unhurried because they have never learned the concept of predation. In a world without green, without the scramble for photosynthesis or the hunt for flesh, there is no hunger, only absorption. They are the city's surplus warmth given form, and they treat the humans with the same casual entitlement they treat the basalt columns—as furniture that occasionally moves and offers interesting vibrations.

But you asked what lives here, and I have not yet told you about the ones who truly own the deep places.

Descend. Past the Ninth Tier where the air grows thick enough to chew, past the engine-rooms where the pistons move like slow hearts, down into the cistern-galleries where the water is black and still and warm as blood. Here live the *great kesset*, the ones the engine-tenders mention only when the steam-pressure drops unexpectedly, when a valve sticks that should not stick, when the standing wave in the bedrock shifts its frequency by a fraction of a hertz and every small kesset in the city turns its head toward the ground at the same instant.

They are the size of horses, yes, but that is a measurement that fails them. They are long-bodied, serpentine but soft, without scales, wrapped in skin that is translucent in places—around the throat, along the belly—revealing a slow circulation of fluid that glows faintly, bioluminescent in the infrared, a deep crimson light that human eyes cannot see but human skin can feel as a sudden flush of warmth when one passes beneath the floor grates. They are blind. They have no need for eyes in the absolute dark of the deep aquifers. Instead, they navigate by vibration, their entire bodies tuned to the resonance of the engines, their spines (cartilaginous, flexible, more like the structure of a ear than a backbone) acting as tuning forks that interpret the city's mechanical language.

They are the city's true regulators. The engine-tenders maintain the machines, but the great kesset maintain the *pressure*. They move through the water channels and the steam ducts, their massive bodies displacing volume, their movements creating the subtle suction and compression that keeps the geothermal cycle balanced. When one dies—an event that happens perhaps once a century, and no human has ever witnessed the actual death, only the aftermath—the city feels it immediately. The standing wave falters. The small kesset cluster in agitated swarms. The water runs faster, unregulated, for days, until another great one rises from the deepest cisterns to take its place in the vascular system, a slow succession that the engine-tenders track by the change in pitch of the steam-whistles.

They are not pets. They are not beasts. They are something between organ and inhabitant. The engine-tenders leave offerings for them—not food, but *texture*: rough ceramic tiles lowered on ropes into the deep galleries, which the great kesset rub against, exfoliating their soft skins, depositing layers of mineral scale that the tenders later harvest to line the boilers. It is a symbiosis so old it has become geology.

And there are others, the ones who live in the in-between spaces. The *pipe-clingers*, who are mid-sized but flattened, shaped like leaves pressed between pages, who adhere to the inside of the ceramic mains and ride the water-flow from tier to tier, filter-feeding on the iron-blood and silica, their bodies developing the same mineral crusts as the stones until they are indistinguishable from the pipes themselves. You might live in an apartment for years and never know that the warm patch on your bathroom wall is not a heating coil but a pipe-clinger that has been there since the building was raised, growing imperceptibly, pulsing gently, regulating the temperature of your bathwater with the shiver of its cilia.

Then there are the *haze-dancers*, the rarest of the mid-sized kesset, who live only in the cloud-streams that thread through the upper towers. They are lighter than air, or nearly so, their bodies filled with sacs of warm vapor that they regulate by clustering or dispersing their internal heat. They appear as faint shadows against the amber light, undulating in the spaces between buildings, feeding on the condensation itself, harvesting the droplets before they can strike the stone. When the clouds are thick, they multiply—or rather, they congregate, emerging from crevices in the basalt where they have waited in torpor during dry spells. To see a flock of haze-dancers is considered a blessing by the water-gardeners, for they polish the air itself, leaving behind a clarity that lasts for hours.

All of them, from the translucent micro-swarms to the great ones in the dark, share that quality of *inclusion* you have noticed. They do not distinguish between the organic and the mechanical, the built and the born. A kesset will cluster on a steam-valve with the same contentment it shows on a sleeping human's shoulder, finding both equally warm, equally part of the city's body. They are the mechanism by which Aerys-Van knows itself. When you feel watched, you are feeling the collective attention of ten thousand warm bodies, each one a sensory organ of the city, each one reporting back to the great ones in the deep through vibrations in the stone, through changes in pressure, through the language of heat and damp.

The humans have learned to live as the kesset do: slowly, warmly, without sharp edges. They do not keep pets because the distinction between *owner* and *owned* is meaningless here. A child might be followed by a particular mid-sized kesset for years—the child will name it, carry it, sleep with it curled against their ribs for the warmth—and then one day the kesset will simply not be there, having moved on to another warm body, another rhythm, and the child will not mourn, because they understand that the kesset was never *theirs*, only *present*, only sharing the space. This is the great lesson of Aerys-Van: that to live somewhere is not to possess it, but to be included in its processes, to become part of its maintenance.

So when you ask what lives here, I must tell you: warmth lives here. Pressure lives here. The slow cycle of water rising and falling lives here, and the kesset are its neurons, its blood cells, its antibodies. The humans are the builders, the repairmen, the ones who carry the stones up and carve the crusts into spirals, but the city itself is alive in a way that has nothing to do with individual hearts and everything to do with the standing wave in the basalt, the pulse in the pipes, the warm rain that falls upward from the ground to meet the clouds that pass through the streets like visitors who have learned not to knock.

And if you stay—if you become one of the ones who stay forever—you will eventually stop noticing where your skin ends and the warm air begins. You will wake in the night to find a mid-sized kesset has joined you, its copper eyes reflecting the dark, its body heavy and warm against your spine, and you will not startle. You will simply shift to make room, feeling the city's heartbeat sync with your own, and you will understand that you are not being watched. You are being *held*.

The engines breathe. The kesset pulse. The water weeps and rises. And you, too, will become slow, and warm, and necessary.
