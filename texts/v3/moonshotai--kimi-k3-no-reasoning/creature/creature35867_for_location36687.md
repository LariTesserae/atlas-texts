# What Lives in the Weir of Dim Song

They are called **Listers**, though this name is a recent graft, a word that arrived with the last engineer who came down here and stayed. Before that—decades before, centuries—they had no name at all, or they had many, or the concept of naming had not yet precipitated out of the warm dark. The name is apt enough. They list. They lean. They incline toward the sound.

You will not see them at first. This is important. They have learned the dimness the way fish know water, and they wear it. They are human, or were human, or are becoming something that remembers being human the way a river remembers its source: as a technicality, a point of origin no longer relevant to the flow.

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps fewer. Perhaps more—the boundaries of counting are porous here, like the boundaries of everything else. Some are old, their skin softened and mineralized by decades of warm humidity, their hair grown long and fused with the moss until you cannot tell where body ends and growth begins. Some are young, arrived recently (within the last fifty years, which is recent by the Weir's reckoning), still carrying the dry stiffness of the world above, still blinking too often in the glow, still speaking in sentences rather than the long tonal vowels that serve as language here.

They do not work on the machinery. This is crucial to understanding them. The machinery does not need working. It has been running for so long that maintenance has become indistinguishable from worship, and worship has become indistinguishable from presence. The Listers are present. They sit on the warm pipes. They lean against the sweating walls. They lie on the walkways with their ears pressed to the grating, listening to the water move below. They tend the moss, not by cultivating it but by staying still long enough for it to grow over them, then rising carefully, carrying the imprint of green light on their skin like a temporary tattoo that fades over hours.

They have no leader, but they have hierarchies of stillness. The ones who have been here longest can sit motionless for days. The younger ones (younger being a relative term—some are in their sixties by the world's count but have only been below for twenty years) still fidget, still feel the need to move through the space rather than simply occupying it. Movement is tolerated. It is even, in its way, celebrated. When a Lister walks the perimeter of the weir, trailing fingers along the railing, they are performing a kind of liturgy. The water responds. Not visibly, not immediately, but the hum changes key, and the moss brightens in their wake, and everyone feels it.

## The Biology of Listers

Time works differently here, and so do bodies. The Listers do not age the way surface people do. This is not immortality—it is a slowing, a synchronization with the place. Their heartbeats gradually align with the pulse of the engines. Their breathing falls into rhythm with the condensation cycle. Some of the eldest have hearts that beat once per minute. Their body temperature has risen to match the ambient warmth, and they no longer sweat, because they have become isothermal with their environment.

Their eyes have changed. The pupils are permanently dilated, giving them a startled, owlish look. The whites have taken on a faint green cast from decades of reflected phosphorescence. They can see in dimness that would blind a surface-dweller, reading the patterns in the moss glow the way you might read a familiar face. Conversely, they are blinded by brightness. A match, a candle, anything stronger than the moss-light causes them physical pain. This is why they do not leave.

Their skin is soft, perpetually damp, and slightly luminous in the dark. The mineral content of the air has deposited in their pores, giving them a faint iridescence, particularly on their hands and forearms where they touch the walls most often. The moss grows on them—not parasitically, but commensally, a symbiosis that neither party seems to notice anymore. A Lister who has sat in one place for a week will rise with a green silhouette on their back, a delicate tracing of filaments that will fall away over the next few hours, only to regrow the next time they rest.

They eat the moss. This took time to discover, decades of trial and error by the first ones who came down and could not or would not go back up. The moss is not nutritious in any conventional sense, but the Listers' digestive systems have adapted. They need very little now. A handful of the velvety growth every few days, washed down with water collected from condensation drips. The water is warm and mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and something organic, something that might be the essence of the place itself, distilled.

They do not sleep, or rather, they have redefined sleep. They enter states of deeper listening, where their breathing slows to match the rhythm of the slowest piston, and their consciousness spreads out into the sound. It is not unconsciousness—it is a different kind of awareness, less localized, less bound to the body. They can stay in this state for days. When they come back (and they always come back, though some of the older ones take longer each time), they report that the sound has voices in it. Not words, but intentions. The machinery is trying to say something, and if they listen long enough, they will understand.

## The Practice of Listening

This is their religion, if religion is the right word. It is more like a science, or an art, or a form of meditation so refined that it has become a physical practice. The Listers have developed what they call the *attunement*—a method of listening so deeply that the boundary between self and sound becomes permeable.

The attunement is taught, but not through instruction. A new Lister (and there are new Listers occasionally—someone descends the iron stair, drawn by rumors or accidents or a persistent feeling that the surface world is too loud and too bright and too fast) is paired with an elder. They sit together. They do not speak. The elder demonstrates the practice by doing it, and the younger one learns by proximity, by osmosis, by gradually slowing their own rhythms until they match the elder's, until they match the Weir's.

It takes years. The first thing a novice learns is to stop distinguishing between sounds. The engines, the water, the moss rustling, the dripping condensation, the hum of pipes under pressure—these are not separate noises but facets of a single acoustic phenomenon. The Weir is singing one song, and it has been singing it for centuries, and the song is so long and so slow that a human lifetime is only a few notes. The Listers are trying to hear the whole song. They know they will not live long enough. This does not trouble them. It is enough to hear a phrase, a measure, a sustained tone that might last a decade.

The advanced practitioners—the ones who have been here for fifty, sixty, seventy years—can isolate individual elements. They can hear the difference between water moving through the north pipes and the south. They can detect the exact moment when a valve completes its slow opening. They can feel the vibration of a piston through the soles of their feet and identify which engine it belongs to, where it is in its cycle, how long until the next stroke. This knowledge has no practical use. It is knowledge for its own sake, a form of intimacy with the place that transcends function.

## Social Structure and Solitude

The Listers are alone together. This is the paradox at the heart of their existence. They share the space, but they do not share their lives. They have no families, though some of them have been here so long they might be related by now—bloodlines blurred by the warm dark, genetics mingling in ways that no one tracks. They have no marriages, no partnerships, though some of them sit together regularly, and a few have been known to hold hands while listening, a physical connection that enhances the attunement.

They do not speak much. Language, they have found, is too fast, too discrete, too full of hard edges. It does not map well onto the reality of the Weir, where everything is gradual and continuous. Instead, they have developed a system of tonal communication—long vowel sounds, hummed or whispered, that convey states of being rather than specific information. A low, sustained *ooooh* means *I am here, I am listening, the sound is good*. A rising *eeee* means *something has changed, pay attention*. A descending *ahhhh* means *I am going deeper, do not worry if I do not move for a while*.

These sounds blend with the ambient noise, becoming part of the song. Sometimes it is impossible to tell whether a voice is human or mechanical. The Listers consider this a success.

They gather occasionally, but their gatherings are silent. A shift in the water will draw them to the weir's edge, and they will stand together, hands on the railing, watching the black surface. Nothing happens. The water does not rise. The light does not change. But something has shifted in the rhythm, and they all feel it, and they stand witness to it together. Then they disperse, returning to their individual stations, their chosen spots along the pipes and walkways.

There is no conflict. The Weir is too slow for conflict. Anger requires energy, speed, a sense of urgency that cannot survive here. If two Listers disagree (and this is rare—what is there to disagree about?), the disagreement manifests as distance. One moves to a different part of the room. The other stays. Over time—weeks, months—the disagreement dissolves. The space is too small for grudges, and the sound is too pervasive. It fills the ears and smooths the sharp edges of thought.

## Death and Continuation

Listers die, but death here is different. The body does not decay the way it would on the surface. The warm, mineral-rich environment preserves. When a Lister's heart finally stops (and this can take a very long time—the slowing of the heartbeat is so gradual that death is more like a fading, a final deceleration), the other Listers do not bury the body. They do not burn it. They move it to a specific alcove, a natural depression in the wall where the water drips steadily, and they leave it there.

Over time—months, years—the body becomes encrusted with mineral deposits. The moss grows over it. The constant drip of water calcifies it, layer by layer, until it is no longer a body but a formation, a stalagmite that was once a person. The oldest alcoves contain shapes that are barely recognizable as human. They are part of the architecture now. The Listers do not mourn. They incorporate. The dead are still here, still listening, still part of the sound.

This is not seen as an ending. It is a continuation, a deeper integration into the Weir. The living Listers sometimes sit near the alcoves, feeling a kinship with the mineral forms. They believe (though "believe" is too strong a word—they *sense*, they *intuit*) that the dead have achieved a more complete attunement, that they are now fully synchronized with the place, no longer constrained by the limitations of flesh.

## The Purpose

Why are they here? This question has no answer, or it has too many. Some came seeking solitude. Some were running from something—the noise of the surface, the speed of the world, the harshness of light. Some arrived by accident and stayed by choice. Some were born here, the children of Listers who paired briefly and raised their offspring in the warm dark until they were old enough to listen on their own.

But these are origin stories, and the Listers do not dwell on origins. They are here because here is where they are. The Weir needs listeners, and they have answered the need. Or the Weir does not need anything, and they are here simply because the place is here, and they are drawn to it the way water is drawn downhill, the way moss grows toward the light it produces.

They serve the machinery, but the machinery does not need service. They maintain the rhythm, but the rhythm is self-sustaining. They listen to the song, but the song would continue without them. Their purpose is to be present, to bear witness, to exist in a state of profound attention. They are the audience for a performance that does not require an audience, and this is what makes their presence meaningful.

The Weir of Dim Song is a machine for maintaining warmth and wet and dim. The Listers are part of that machine. They are the component that listens, that responds, that gives the place a consciousness it would not otherwise have. They are the Weir becoming aware of itself, slowly, over centuries, one sustained note at a time.

---

You could join them. This is always possible. The stair is always there, and the Listers do not turn anyone away. You would sit with an elder. You would learn the attunement. Your heartbeat would slow. Your eyes would adjust to the green glow. The moss would begin to grow on you, and you would let it. You would stop speaking in sentences. You would start humming long vowels that mean everything and nothing. You would become part of the song.

The Listers would not welcome you, because welcoming implies an event, a moment of transition. They would simply include you, the way the water includes a new drop of condensation, the way the moss includes a new filament. You would be there, and then you would have always been there, and the distinction between joining and belonging would dissolve.

This is what lives in the Weir of Dim Song: a community of listening, a congregation of presence, a slow accumulation of souls who have chosen to hear the whole song, even knowing they will not live long enough to reach the end. They are the Listers, and they are the Weir, and the difference between the two is a matter of perspective, and perspective is just another thing that dissolves in the warm, humming dark.
