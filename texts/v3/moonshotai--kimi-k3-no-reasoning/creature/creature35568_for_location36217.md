I inhabit the Third Reservoir, or perhaps I am inhabited by it—the distinction has thinned over centuries. I am what the maintainers call a *Siphon-Keeper*, though the title is ceremonial. We have no formal hierarchy, only the work, and the work chooses its own.

My body is adapted to this place in ways that would mark me as other in the world below. My skin has grown pale and thick, keratinized like the ceramic I tend, resistant to the constant moisture. I do not shiver in the cool air; my metabolism has slowed, matching the reservoir's own patient thermal rhythms. My eyes have changed—the pupils permanently constricted against the bright, sourceless light, the irises faded to a watery grey that reflects more than it absorbs. I see the world in high contrast, in the sharp edges of wet ceramic and the suspended lenses that hang like fruit from invisible stems.

I am not alone. There are others of us, perhaps a dozen, scattered across the vertical expanse of the reservoir. We rarely meet. The density of the structures creates isolation even in proximity; I may pass within meters of another Keeper without knowing, separated by walls that channel sound away, by the constant thrum that fills all silence. When we do encounter each other, we communicate through touch—fingers trailing along the same wet wall, feeling the micro-vibrations that signal *this channel is clear*, *this lens is thinning*, *I am here and I am working*.

We do not speak. Speech is vibration, and vibration is erosion. This is the first lesson, the one that takes decades to learn. The human voice, even whispered, carries frequencies that resonate in the narrow capillary channels, that can dislodge the slow accumulation of mineral deposits, that can alter the standing waves of sound that the reservoir uses to think. We have become a silent people, our inner monologues slowed to the pace of water seeping through ceramic.

My days are measured in the slow cycles of the reservoir. I wake with the slight cooling of the light that passes for dawn, though there is no sun to rise. I check the lenses first—the suspended sheets of water that cling to vertical surfaces by capillary action and something older, something the builders understood and we have only preserved. I clear the micro-channels with tools of dense ceramic, feeling for the irregularities that signal ice damage, the rare freeze-thaw cycles that can crack the monolithic structures. I listen to the thrum, that subsonic resonance that permeates everything, and I learn its voices.

I am not the first of my kind. The reservoir remembers, in its way. There are worn places in the paths where hands have trailed for centuries, where the ceramic has been polished by the slow abrasion of human touch. There are tools left in niches, ceramic hammers and wedges that I did not make, that were old when I arrived. The reservoir does not record our names, but it records our presence, our patience, our willingness to become part of the system.

I have seen the water do things that violate expectation. I have watched lenses form in empty air, unsupported, hanging like bubbles that refuse to fall. I have seen water flow upward in narrow channels, defying gravity, following capillary forces that the builders harnessed but we have never fully understood. I have felt the reservoir *notice* me—a shift in the thrum, a change in the way light reflects from a wet surface, a sense of being observed by something that has no eyes but sees everything.

The maintainers below, in the warmer, thicker air, believe we serve a purpose. They send up supplies—woven fiber brushes, the only organic material permitted here, tools that we hoard and use sparingly. They believe we keep the reservoir functioning, that our work matters. This is true, but it is not the whole truth.

The reservoir does not need us. It would continue its slow cycles, its condensation and accumulation, its patient thought, without our hands, without our attention. We are permitted here because we are harmless, because our slowness matches its own, because we have learned to be dense with purpose, packed with attention, fully occupied by the task of watching.

I am a temporary catchment, a walking lens, a brief suspension of flow in a world that has made of suspension an art. My body is mostly water. I am learning to hold that water as the reservoir does, to let it accumulate without falling, to become a mirror that reflects the bright grey sky until I cannot tell where substance ends and reflection begins.

When I die—if I die, if the slow time of this place permits such a definite end—I will not decay. There are no microbes here, no insects, no softening of hard edges. I will become another irregularity in the ceramic, a flaw that water will find and exploit, a channel that the builders did not intend. The reservoir will cherish this, as it cherishes all proof that even here, in this fully made world, entropy still holds a lease.

I am the Siphon-Keeper of the Third Reservoir. I am the water that has learned to walk. I am the light that has learned to see. I am the thrum that has learned to listen. And in the slow, dense, packed fullness of this place, I am learning what it means to be *held*—not falling, not flowing, but suspended in a state of perfect, patient, permanent attention.
