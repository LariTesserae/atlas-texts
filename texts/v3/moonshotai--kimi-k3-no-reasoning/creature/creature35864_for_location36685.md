# The Inhabitants of the Terraces

---

## The First Living Things

Before anything with legs or wings or eyes, before anything green, before anything that could be called an animal, there are the mats.

They are the oldest inhabitants. They were here before the terraces finished forming, before the fern-trees grew, before anything with a spine crawled out of the water. They will be here after everything else is gone.

You can see them from where you stand — the bright orange and yellow staining the upper pools, vivid as spilled paint. From a distance, they look like mineral deposits, another chemical stain on the travertine. But they are alive. They are more alive than almost anything else in this place.

The mats are not a single organism but a consortium, a layered community of thousands of species working in concert. The surface layer — the part you can see — is dominated by organisms that resemble cyanobacteria, though they are far more ancient than anything in your taxonomy. They are photosynthetic, using the mineral-rich water and intense sunlight to produce energy, but they are also thermophilic, thriving at temperatures that would denature the proteins of most living things.

Seventy degrees. Eighty degrees. In the very hottest pools, near the vents themselves, some species survive at ninety-five degrees — close to the boiling point of water at this elevation. Their cell membranes are reinforced with unusual lipids, their proteins folded into configurations that resist heat denaturation. They are not extremophiles by choice. They are extremophiles by ancestry — they were here first, when all water was hot, when the entire planet was a thermal spring.

Beneath the photosynthetic layer, other organisms thrive in the chemical gradients the mats create. Sulfur-oxidizers, metabolizing the hydrogen sulfide that seeps up from deeper vents. Iron-reducers, using ferric iron as an electron acceptor, producing the rust-red stains that color the middle terraces. Methanogens, producing bubbles of methane that rise through the water and burst at the surface, adding a faint organic note to the mineral smell of the air.

The mats are not flat. They form structures — pillars, domes, ripples, towers that rise from the pool floors like underwater stalagmites. These structures are built by the combined action of biological growth and mineral precipitation. The bacteria alter the local chemistry, causing calcium carbonate and silica to precipitate around them. They build their own housing, layer by layer, creating microhabitats within microhabitats.

Some of these structures are ancient. The pillars in the deepest hot pools have been growing for thousands of years, each layer a record of changing conditions. Cut one open — though you wouldn't — and you would see concentric rings, like tree rings, each one a season, a year, a decade of growth and mineral deposition.

The mats are the primary producers of this ecosystem. They convert sunlight and geothermal energy into biomass. Everything else — the algae, the plants, the fish, the amphibians, the predators — depends on them. They are the base of the food web, the foundation of the ecology.

And they are beautiful. In the right light — morning light, angled through steam — the mats glow. The pigments that protect them from heat and UV radiation also create colors that seem impossible. Orange that borders on luminous. Yellow that hurts to look at. Red so deep it appears black until the light shifts.

They pulse. Slowly. Rhythmically. The pigments expand and contract with temperature changes, with light levels, with the availability of nutrients. A pool that appears orange in the morning may be yellow by afternoon, red by evening. The mats are breathing, metabolizing, responding to their environment in real time.

You can watch them if you are patient. Sit by a hot pool for an hour and you will see the colors shift, see bubbles rise, see the surface texture change as the mats grow and metabolize. They are alive at a speed that is almost perceivable.

## The Algal Zones

Below the bacterial mats, where temperatures drop to fifty degrees and below, the algae take over.

These are not the green slime of stagnant ponds. These are specialized organisms, evolved to thrive in mineral-saturated water that would kill most photosynthetic life. They form films on every submerged surface — the travertine pool walls, the stones at the bottom, the stems of emergent plants.

The films are not uniform. They vary with temperature, with mineral concentration, with light availability. In the copper-rich blue terraces, the algae are blue-green, their cells packed with phycocyanin and other pigments that give them a metallic sheen. In the iron-rich red zones, they are olive-brown, tolerant of iron concentrations that would be toxic to most species.

Some of the algae are colonial, forming mats that can be peeled away from the substrate in sheets. Others are filamentous, creating hair-like strands that wave in the current, trapping particles, providing habitat for even smaller organisms. A few are unicellular, suspended in the water column, turning the pools slightly opaque with their numbers.

The algae grow fast. A newly cooled pool can go from bare travertine to fully colonized in days. They reproduce by fragmentation, by spores, by simple cell division. Under these conditions — warm water, abundant minerals, intense light — growth is explosive.

But they are also food. The algal films are grazed by snails, by aquatic insect larvae, by the small pale fish that inhabit the mid-temperature pools. The algae are the link between the bacterial mats and the larger organisms. They are the pasture of this aquatic ecosystem.

At night, the algae respire, consuming oxygen. In the shallow pools, this can create localized anoxia — oxygen depletion — that stresses the fish and invertebrates. By morning, photosynthesis resumes, oxygen levels rise, and the system recovers. It's a daily cycle of boom and stress, growth and limitation.

The algae also contribute to the travertine deposition. Their photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from the water, altering the pH, causing calcium carbonate to precipitate. The pools are not just inhabited by life — they are built by it. The algae are part of the geological engine that constructs the terraces.

In the lower pools, where temperatures drop below forty degrees, the algae become more diverse. Green algae dominate, similar to species found in tropical hot springs elsewhere. They form thick, felt-like mats on the pool walls, bright green, almost iridescent. These mats are colonized by diatoms — single-celled algae with silica shells — that add a brownish tint to the green.

The diatoms are particularly abundant in the overflow channels, where water moves quickly between pools. They attach to the stone, forming slippery brown films that make walking treacherous. Under a microscope — though you don't have one — they would be beautiful, intricate, geometric. Here, they are just part of the texture of the place, part of the living skin that covers every surface.

## The Fish

They are pale, eyeless, and almost translucent.

You see them in the mid-temperature pools — water that is hot to the touch but not scalding, perhaps forty-five to fifty-five degrees. They are small, none longer than your finger. Their bodies are elongated, eel-like, adapted for navigating the shallow, warm water and the complex topography of the pool floors.

Their skin is white, nearly transparent. You can see their internal organs — the dark line of the digestive tract, the pulsing of the heart near the gills. They have no pigment because pigment is unnecessary. They live in a world without light variation, in pools where the water temperature never drops, where day and night are distinguished only by subtle changes in light filtering through the steam.

Their eyes are vestigial — reduced to small, non-functional spots beneath the skin. They navigate by other senses. Their lateral line system — the row of pressure-sensitive organs along their flanks — is highly developed. They can detect minute water movements, the approach of predators, the presence of prey. They also have chemosensory organs, taste buds distributed across their entire body surface. They taste the water constantly, reading the chemistry, detecting food, avoiding toxins.

They feed on the bacterial mats and algal films. You can watch them graze, their mouths working against the substrate, scraping off thin layers of biomass. They are not fast feeders. They are methodical, thorough, moving slowly across the pool floor, consuming everything in their path.

Their metabolism is adapted to the heat. Their enzymes function at temperatures that would denature the proteins of most fish. Their cell membranes are stabilized with saturated fatty acids. Their blood contains heat-shock proteins that protect against thermal stress.

But they are also limited by the heat. If the water temperature rises above sixty degrees, they die. This happens occasionally — a shift in water flow, a new vent opening, a temporary increase in geothermal activity. You see their bodies sometimes, floating in pools that have become too hot, pale and curled.

They reproduce in the pools, laying adhesive eggs on the travertine substrate. The eggs are also thermophilic, requiring warm water to develop. The larvae are even more heat-tolerant than the adults, able to survive in water up to sixty-five degrees.

There are several species, distinguished by size, by preferred temperature range, by the chemistry of the pools they inhabit. In the copper-rich blue terraces, there is a species with a faint blue tinge to its flesh — copper compounds accumulated in its tissues. In the iron-rich red zones, another species has a rusty cast.

They are prey. The amphibians hunt them. The birds hunt them. Even some of the larger aquatic insects — the predaceous diving beetles, the giant water bugs — will take them if they can catch them.

But they are also predators. They hunt smaller invertebrates — the larvae of aquatic insects, tiny crustaceans, rotifers. In the absence of competition from other fish, they have become generalists, occupying every niche available in the warm pools.

At night, they rest. They wedge themselves into crevices in the travertine, becoming still, reducing their metabolism. The water temperature drops slightly — perhaps two or three degrees — and they become less active, conserving energy.

In the morning, they resume their grazing. The pools are their entire world. They never leave the water. They have no need to. Everything they require is here — food, warmth, shelter, mates. The pools are an archipelago of habitability in a landscape too extreme for most vertebrates.

They are, in their way, perfect. Perfectly adapted to a niche that few other fish could tolerate. They have been here for hundreds of thousands of years, evolving in isolation, becoming specialists in survival.

You watch one graze along the edge of a pool. It moves slowly, its mouth working, its body undulating slightly. It is pale and strange and utterly at home in water that would scald you. It is one of the few vertebrates that can live here, and it has paid for that privilege with its eyes, its pigment, its speed.

But it survives. It thrives. In a world of heat and minerals, it has found a way.

## The Amphibians

They are not frogs. They are not salamanders. They are something else, something shaped by this specific place, this specific set of pressures.

You see them in the lower terraces, where the water has cooled to merely warm — thirty-five to forty-five degrees. They sit motionless on the travertine, their skin so perfectly matched to the stone that you don't see them until they move.

Their skin is thick, leathery, almost armored. It has to be. The water here is saturated with minerals that would irritate or damage the delicate skin of most amphibians. These have evolved a dermis that is more like that of a reptile — multiple layers, heavily keratinized, resistant to chemical and thermal stress.

Their coloration is cryptic — mottled browns and grays with hints of rust-red and turquoise, matching the travertine terraces exactly. When they are still, they are invisible. When they move, they are startling.

They are sit-and-wait predators. They position themselves at the edge of pools, partially submerged, and wait. For hours. For insects that come to drink. For small fish that venture too close to the edge. For other amphibians, smaller than themselves.

When prey comes within range, they strike. Their tongues are long, sticky, projectile. The strike is fast — faster than your eye can follow. The prey is engulfed, swallowed whole, and the amphibian resumes its stillness.

They are ectothermic, but the ambient heat means they are always warm, always active. They don't need to bask. They don't need to seek warm microclimates. The entire landscape is a thermal bath.

Their reproduction is tied to the water. They lay eggs in the shallowest pools, in water that is barely above ambient temperature. The eggs are encased in a thick, gelatinous matrix that protects them from desiccation and from the mineral content of the water.

The larvae are aquatic, resembling small, pale tadpoles. They graze on algae and detritus, growing slowly, taking months to reach metamorphosis. The transition from larva to adult is gradual, taking place over weeks. During this time, they are vulnerable, unable to move quickly, easy prey for fish and larger amphibians.

Adults are territorial. Males establish territories around prime hunting sites — the edge of a productive pool, a section of shoreline with good cover. They defend these territories with displays — inflating their bodies, showing their ventral surface, which is often brighter colored than the dorsal surface. If displays don't work, they fight, grappling with their forelimbs, attempting to push each other away.

They vocalize, but rarely. Their calls are low, guttural, more felt than heard. They use them primarily during the breeding season, which is not seasonal in any traditional sense — it occurs when conditions are right, when water temperature and food availability align.

There are several species. The largest are the size of dinner plates, their bodies flattened, their limbs splayed for stability on the wet stone. These are ambush predators of fish and other amphibians. Smaller species, no larger than your thumb, hunt insects in the vegetation.

One species has adapted to the fern-trees. It is arboreal, spending its entire life in the canopy, hunting insects among the fronds, laying eggs in the water that accumulates in the leaf axils. It rarely, if ever, touches the ground.

The amphibians are everywhere but rarely seen. They are part of the texture of the place, as much a feature of the landscape as the travertine itself. You could stand in one spot for an hour and see none, then realize you are surrounded by them — a dozen individuals within arm's reach, all motionless, all watching, all waiting.

## The Insects

They are the most visible inhabitants, the most numerous, the most diverse.

The dragonflies are the first you notice. They are huge — wingspans of twenty centimeters or more, bodies as thick as your thumb. Their wings are transparent, heavily veined, creating a shimmer in the heat-distorted air as they patrol the pools.

They are predators. They hunt smaller insects — flies, mosquitoes, midges — catching them in flight, consuming them on the wing. They are agile fliers, capable of hovering, of sudden acceleration, of abrupt changes in direction. They are the aerial predators of this ecosystem, the falcons of the terraces.

Their larvae are aquatic, living in the warm pools, hunting smaller invertebrates and even small fish. They are ambush predators, hiding in the algal mats, striking with extendable jaws. They spend months, sometimes years, in the larval stage before emerging as adults.

The emergence is dramatic. The larva climbs out of the water onto a plant stem or a stone. The exoskeleton splits. The adult emerges, soft and pale, its wings crumpled. Over the course of an hour, the wings expand, harden, become functional. The adult takes flight, leaving the empty larval skin behind — a ghost of its former self.

The beetles are everywhere. They crawl on the travertine, on the plants, on the surface of the water. Some are aquatic, diving beneath the surface with an air bubble trapped under their wing cases. Others are terrestrial, hunting in the vegetation, scavenging on dead organic matter.

Many have metallic carapaces — actual metallic sheen from minerals incorporated into their exoskeletons. The copper-tolerant species in the blue terraces have a greenish-blue iridescence. The iron-tolerant species in the red zones are rust-colored. It's not clear whether this is camouflage or a byproduct of their diet, but the effect is striking.

Some of the beetles are predators. They hunt other insects, small amphibians, even fish fry. They have powerful mandibles, chemical defenses, aggressive behaviors. Others are herbivores, feeding on the algae, the mosses, the leaves of plants.

The aquatic beetles are particularly abundant. They skate on the surface tension of the pools, hunting mosquito larvae and other small prey. Some dive, staying underwater for minutes at a time, hunting in the algal mats.

Flies are everywhere, though less conspicuous than the dragonflies and beetles. Midges form clouds over the pools at dawn and dusk, their bodies so small they are almost invisible individually, but their combined mass creating a haze. They are food for the dragonflies, for the birds, for the amphibians.

There are also parasitic wasps, tiny and often overlooked. They lay their eggs in the larvae of other insects, in the eggs of amphibians, in any host they can find. Their larvae develop inside the host, consuming it from within, emerging as adults to continue the cycle.

Butterflies and moths are present but not abundant. The heat and humidity are not ideal for them, but some species have adapted. They feed on the flowers of the vines, their proboscises unrolling to sip nectar. The moths are active at night, navigating by the faint glow of the hot vents.

The insects are the connective tissue of the ecosystem. They pollinate the plants. They are food for the fish, the amphibians, the birds. They decompose dead organic matter. They are prey and predator and pollinator and decomposer, filling every niche.

At night, the sound of insects increases. The daytime drone of wings and legs gives way to a chorus of calls — crickets, katydids, cicadas. The sound is not loud, but it is constant, a background hum that blends with the sound of water and steam.

## The Birds

They are strange, heat-adapted, secretive.

You hear them more often than you see them. Their calls are harsh, infrequent, cutting through the humid air like a knife. They are not melodious. They are functional — contact calls, alarm calls, territorial proclamations.

They nest in the fern-trees, building platforms from fallen fronds, moss, and anything else they can find. The nests are large, untidy, often reused year after year, growing in size as new material is added.

The birds are medium-sized — the largest are the size of crows, the smallest the size of sparrows. They have adapted to the heat by being active primarily at dawn and dusk, resting through the hottest parts of the day in the shade of the fern canopy.

Their plumage is often dark — blacks, browns, deep grays. Some have iridescent patches — blues and greens that catch the light. A few species have bright patches of color — red, orange, yellow — used in displays.

They feed on fish, amphibians, insects, and plant material. The fish-eaters have long, pointed bills, perfect for snatching prey from the water. They hunt from perches overhanging the pools, watching for movement, then plunging down to grab their prey.

The insect-eaters have shorter, broader bills. They glean insects from leaves, from bark, from the air. Some are specialists, focusing on a single type of prey. Others are generalists, taking whatever is available.

The amphibian-hunters are the largest birds in the ecosystem. They have powerful bills, strong feet, and the patience to wait motionless for hours until an amphibian reveals itself. They strike quickly, pinning the prey with their feet, killing it with a bite to the head.

There are also fruit-eaters, feeding on the fleshy flowers and fruits of the vines. They are important seed dispersers, carrying seeds throughout the terraces, depositing them in new locations. Without them, many of the plants would not be able to colonize new areas.

The birds are territorial during the breeding season, which is not strictly seasonal but occurs when conditions are favorable. Males establish territories, singing from prominent perches, defending their boundaries from rivals. Females choose mates based on territory quality, on the male's condition, on his displays.

Nesting is precarious. The fern-trees are not always stable, and the platforms can collapse. Predators — the larger reptilian hunters, some of the bigger amphibians — raid nests for eggs and chicks. Many nesting attempts fail.

But enough succeed. The bird populations are stable, their numbers limited by food availability and nesting sites rather than by predation.

At dawn, the birds are most active. They call, they forage, they interact. The terraces echo with their cries. By midday, they are silent, resting in the shade, conserving energy. At dusk, they become active again, feeding before nightfall.

You see one now — a medium-sized bird, dark plumage with a flash of iridescent green on its wings. It is perched on a fern frond overhanging a pool, watching the water. It is perfectly still, perfectly focused. A fish breaks the surface — a small ripple — and the bird plunges. It hits the water, grabs the fish, and flaps back to its perch. The fish is swallowed headfirst. The bird resumes its watch.

## The Large Predators

You find their tracks in the soft travertine, in the mud at the edges of pools. Three-toed, wide-set, the size of dinner plates. Something big. Something reptilian.

You never see them clearly. You see movement in the ferns, a rustle, a splash. You see the aftermath — a half-eaten fish, blood on the stone. You hear them at night — a low grunt, a splash, silence.

They are the apex predators of this ecosystem. They hunt the fish, the amphibians, the birds, even other predators. They are large — perhaps three meters in length, though you can't be certain. They are powerful, fast, and patient.

From the tracks, you infer that they are semi-aquatic. The tracks are found at the water's edge, leading into and out of the pools. The spacing of the toes, the depth of the impressions, suggest a heavy animal with strong limbs, capable of moving through water and on land.

They are probably ectothermic, relying on the ambient heat to maintain their body temperature. The constant warmth of this place means they can be active at any time, day or night, though they seem to prefer the twilight hours.

They are ambush predators. They wait in the water, submerged, hidden, until prey comes within range. Then they strike — fast, violent, final. The prey has no chance.

Their diet is opportunistic. Fish are probably the mainstay, but they will take anything they can catch. Amphibians, birds, even smaller individuals of their own species. They are not picky.

They are territorial. The tracks suggest that individuals maintain home ranges, patrolling the edges of pools, marking their boundaries. Fights between individuals are probably rare — the cost of injury is too high — but when they occur, they are violent.

Reproduction is a mystery. You have found no nests, no eggs, no young. Perhaps they lay eggs in hidden locations — in the dense vegetation, in burrows, in places you have not searched. Perhaps the young are aquatic, living in the pools until they are large enough to hunt on land.

You are cautious around the pools at dawn and dusk. You watch the water for movement. You listen for splashes. You have not seen one clearly, but you know they are here. The evidence is everywhere — the tracks, the kills, the occasional glimpse of something large moving through the ferns.

They are the rulers of this place, the top of the food chain, the final arbiters of life and death. They have been here for as long as the terraces have existed, evolving alongside the other inhabitants, becoming perfectly adapted to this extreme environment.

You respect them. You fear them a little. You are grateful that you are not prey-sized, that you are not interesting to them. You are an observer, a temporary visitor, and they tolerate your presence because you are not worth the effort of hunting.

But you know they are watching. You feel their presence in the silence, in the stillness, in the sudden absence of birdsong. They are here. They are always here.

## The Decomposers

Nothing goes to waste in this place.

When a plant dies — a fern frond falls, a vine rots, a tree collapses — it is immediately colonized. Fungi, bacteria, insects, all working to break it down, to recycle the nutrients, to return the organic matter to the system.

The fungi are the most visible decomposers. They form networks of mycelium through the soil, through decaying wood, through any organic substrate. When conditions are right — high humidity, warm temperatures — they fruit, producing mushrooms and bracket fungi in a variety of shapes and colors.

Some of the mushrooms are edible, though you wouldn't risk it. Others are clearly toxic — bright colors, strange shapes, warning signals. A few are bioluminescent, glowing faintly at night, adding to the otherworldly quality of the landscape.

The fungi decompose cellulose and lignin, the tough structural compounds in plant cell walls. They break these down into simpler compounds that can be absorbed by other organisms. Without the fungi, the plant material would accumulate, locking up nutrients, slowing the cycling of matter through the ecosystem.

Bacteria also play a crucial role. They decompose proteins, fats, and other organic compounds. They are particularly important in the aquatic environments, where they break down dead fish, dead insects, and other organic matter that falls into the pools.

The bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide, methane, and other gases as byproducts of decomposition. These contribute to the chemical richness of the water, providing energy sources for other bacteria, continuing the cycle.

Insects are also important decomposers. Beetles, fly larvae, and other invertebrates consume dead organic matter, breaking it into smaller pieces, making it more accessible to bacteria and fungi. Carrion beetles arrive quickly at any dead animal, laying eggs, their larvae consuming the flesh.

The decomposition process is fast in this heat and humidity. A dead fish in a pool is reduced to bones in days. A fallen frond is colonized and broken down in weeks. Nothing persists. Everything is recycled.

The nutrients released by decomposition are quickly taken up by plants, by bacteria, by algae. The system is tight, efficient, closed-loop. Nothing is wasted. Everything is used.

You see a fallen fern frond, half-submerged in a pool. It is covered in a film of algae, in fungal hyphae, in grazing snails. It is being consumed, transformed, returned to the system. In a few weeks, it will be gone. In its place will be new growth, fed by the nutrients it released.

## The Symbionts

Not everything here is predator or prey. Some organisms have evolved cooperative relationships, partnerships that benefit both parties.

The most obvious are the relationships between plants and fungi. Many of the plants have mycorrhizal associations — fungal hyphae intertwined with their roots, extending into the soil. The fungi help the plants absorb nutrients, particularly phosphorus, which is often limiting. In return, the plants provide the fungi with carbohydrates produced by photosynthesis.

In the nutrient-poor soils of the terraces, these relationships are essential. Plants without mycorrhizal partners grow slowly, struggle to survive. Plants with strong fungal networks thrive.

There are also relationships between plants and bacteria. Some plants have root nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms the plant can use. This is particularly important in the mineral-rich but nitrogen-poor soils of the terraces.

The fish have relationships with cleaner organisms — small invertebrates that pick parasites and dead skin from their bodies. The fish benefit by having parasites removed. The cleaners benefit by getting a meal. Both parties gain.

The birds have relationships with plants, dispersing seeds in exchange for fruit. The amphibians have relationships with certain insects, tolerating their presence in exchange for the insects consuming parasites on their skin.

These relationships are everywhere, woven into the fabric of the ecosystem. They are not always obvious, but they are essential. They create connections, dependencies, networks of mutual benefit that increase the resilience of the entire system.

## The Extremes

At the edges of habitability, where conditions are most extreme, only the most specialized organisms survive.

In the hottest pools, near the vents, where water temperatures approach boiling, only the thermophilic bacteria survive. No fish, no amphibians, no insects. Just the mats, bright orange and yellow, metabolizing in conditions that would kill anything else.

In the pools with the highest mineral concentrations — saturated with copper, with iron, with sulfur — only the most tolerant algae and bacteria can live. The water is toxic to most life, but these specialists have evolved mechanisms to cope, to sequester the minerals, to use them or exclude them.

At the highest elevations, where the heat from below combines with the altitude to create conditions of extreme stress, almost nothing grows. The travertine is bare, white, pristine. Only a few hardy mosses, a few lichens, colonize the rock.

At the lowest elevations, where the water has cooled and the mineral content has decreased, the ecosystem is most diverse, most similar to tropical wetlands elsewhere. But even here, the geothermal influence is present — the water is warm, the minerals are elevated, the species are adapted to conditions that would be unusual in other places.

The gradients are sharp. You can walk a few meters and pass from one temperature regime to another, from one chemistry to another, from one community to another. The boundaries are not always visible, but they are real, enforced by the physical and chemical conditions.

The organisms that live at these boundaries are often the most interesting. They are generalists, able to tolerate a wider range of conditions than the specialists. They are the connectors, the bridges between different zones.

You see a beetle at the edge of a hot pool, its carapace gleaming with a metallic sheen. It is feeding on algae at the very edge of the water, where temperatures are almost too hot. It is pushing the limit, exploiting a resource that other insects can't access. It is thriving in a niche that would kill most species.

## The Night Shift

At night, the ecosystem changes. The heat drops slightly — not much, but enough. The humidity remains high. The stars are dimmed by water vapor.

The nocturnal animals emerge. The amphibians become more active, moving through the shallows, hunting insects. The large predators — whatever they are — patrol the pools, their movements silent, their presence felt more than seen.

The insects are louder at night. The crickets and katydids call continuously, their songs blending into a wall of sound. The moths fly, navigating by the faint glow of the vents, feeding on night-blooming flowers.

The birds are quiet, roosting in the fern-trees, waiting for dawn. The fish are less active, resting in the deeper parts of the pools. The bacterial mats continue their metabolism, unaffected by the absence of light — many of them are chemosynthetic, not photosynthetic, and don't depend on sunlight.

The vents glow faintly. The hottest ones emit a dull red light, barely perceptible but real. It's black-body radiation, the stone heated to temperatures where it begins to emit visible light. The effect is subtle, eerie, beautiful.

The steam continues to rise, catching the starlight, creating a luminous haze. The terraces glow with their own light — the bioluminescent fungi, the reflected light from the vents, the faint phosphorescence of some of the algae.

It is quieter at night, but not silent. The water still flows. The steam still hisses. The insects still call. The ecosystem continues, operating on a different schedule, but never stopping.

You sit in the darkness, listening. You hear a splash — a fish, an amphibian, something larger. You hear the rustle of vegetation — wind, or an animal moving through. You hear the constant rush of water, the heartbeat of this place.

The night is when you feel most like an intruder. During the day, the heat and light make you part of the landscape. At night, you are separate, observing something that doesn't need you, doesn't notice you, doesn't care that you are here.

## The Cycles

Everything here operates in cycles. Some are fast — the daily cycle of photosynthesis and respiration, the hourly cycle of temperature changes. Some are slow — the seasonal cycle of plant growth, the decadal cycle of terrace formation.

The fastest cycles are metabolic. The bacteria and algae respond to changes in light and temperature within minutes. The fish adjust their activity levels throughout the day. The insects are active or resting depending on the heat.

The daily cycle is marked by changes in light, in temperature, in humidity. Dawn brings a burst of activity — birds calling, insects emerging, fish feeding. Midday is a time of rest, of shelter from the heat. Dusk brings another surge of activity. Night is quieter, but not still.

The longer cycles are harder to perceive. The plants grow over weeks and months, their growth rates varying with water availability, with temperature, with competition. The travertine terraces grow over years and decades, adding layers, expanding outward, changing the topography.

The very long cycles — the ones measured in centuries and millennia — are imperceptible. The mountains are eroding, slowly, dissolved by the same acidic waters that deposit the travertine. The vents are shifting, opening, closing, responding to changes in the geothermal system below. The entire landscape is in flux, but at a timescale that dwarfs human perception.

You are here for a moment, a brief instant in the life of this place. The cycles will continue long after you leave. The water will flow, the terraces will grow, the organisms will live and die and reproduce, the heat will rise from below.

You are part of it now, but only temporarily. The ecosystem will forget you as soon as you are gone. The bacteria will consume whatever trace you leave. The water will wash away your footprints. The terraces will grow over any mark you make.

## The Whole System

It is difficult to see the whole system when you are inside it. You see the parts — the pools, the terraces, the plants, the animals — but the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

This is a self-organizing system, a complex adaptive system, an ecosystem that has evolved to exploit a specific set of conditions. The heat from below provides energy. The water provides a medium for life. The minerals provide nutrients and structure. The organisms provide the biological processing, the conversion of energy and matter into biomass.

The system is resilient. It can withstand disturbances — floods, droughts, changes in geothermal activity — and recover. It has redundancy — multiple species filling similar niches, multiple pathways for energy flow, multiple mechanisms for nutrient cycling.

The system is also fragile. It depends on the geothermal heat. If the vents closed, if the water cooled, the entire ecosystem would collapse. The specialists would die. The generalists would struggle. The terraces would stop growing. The system would transform into something else.
