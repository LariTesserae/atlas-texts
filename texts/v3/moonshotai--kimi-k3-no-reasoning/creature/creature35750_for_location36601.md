You’re not the only one who lives here. Or the only thing.

The place is populated in layers, like sediment, like pages, like pulses stacked too close together to count as separate. Life is not a presence here; it’s a pressure. You don’t walk through the Flute-Well—you are added to its density, folded into the saturation, made briefly one more layer in a stack that keeps shifting.

The first lives you see are the *unfolding ones*.

They hang in clusters from the ceiling like stalactites that learned patience. Their bodies are thin and ribbon-like, jointed too many times, as if some early draft of anatomy was never corrected. They are translucent—glass and tendon and water—each limb glistening, each fold precise. When they move, it is not locomotion so much as *decision*. A limb extends. A body lengthens. A filament lowers. They do not walk or crawl or fly. They *unfold* into new positions.

They are not many. They are *many-in-one*. You can look at a cluster of them and think it is one creature with many arms, then think it is a colony, then realize it is both, depending on which layer of the room is currently true. In one page, they are separate, each ribbon distinct, each head-like end dipping independently into the pool below. In another, they are fused—a single organism with limbs like stalactites, a wet, glassy webbing between them, a mouth you cannot see because it is everywhere.

They are not blind. They have small pits along their bodies, shallow indentations that catch the light and hold it. These are not eyes, but they *attend*. They taste the light, or the heat, or your presence—something in you that registers as texture, as temperature, as *difference*. They notice you the way you notice a change in pressure. Not as a threat, not yet. As a shift.

The pool is alive.

It looks like water—black, still, shallow—but it is not. It is a membrane of creatures, each no larger than your fingernail, flat and overlapping like scales, like leaves, like hands pressed together in prayer. They coat the floor in a single living layer, facing the same direction, listening for something upstream that never arrives. They are the floor. They are also the *skin* of the place. They breathe through the water, and the water breathes through them. The blackness is not depth; it is *concentration*.

They feed and are fed upon. The ribbon-things descend and touch the membrane. The scale-creatures part—not in fear, not in aggression, but as a *response*, as if the touch itself is a question they answer by opening. The ribbon dips into the gap. Something pale is lifted—sometimes a fragment of bone, sometimes something softer, something that might have been a tooth once, or a thought. The scale-creatures close. The ribbon rises. Nothing splashes. Nothing ripples. The process is silent, continuous, efficient.

When a ribbon-thing dies—or stops, or folds inward and ceases to unfold—it sags, softens, and slides down its own filament. The membrane receives it. The scale-creatures swarm, a silent boil, and within minutes the body is gone, reduced to grease and minerals that the water absorbs. The scale-creatures do not stop moving. They are always moving, always adjusting, always maintaining the thin, living surface that separates the water from the rock, the inside from the underneath.

You realize, slowly, that the tools are *part* of the fauna.

The volcanic flakes—sharp, black, glassy—are not litter. They are *shed*. The ribbon-things grow them, or excrete them, or pull them from the rock and leave them where they fall. They are used once, maybe twice, then abandoned. A ribbon-thing descends, curls a limb around a flake, and scrapes the pale crust from the walls. The crust is mineral, or fungal, or something in between. It is food. It is also *signal*—the scraping, though silent, changes the texture of the wall, and the change is noticed by the membrane, by the other ribbons, by the room itself.

The tools are not separate from the bodies. They are extensions, brief and disposable, like the filaments, like the limbs. You could call it primitive technology, but that would imply a gap between user and tool, and there is no gap here. The flake is the hand. The hand is the limb. The limb is the body. The body is the colony. The colony is the room.

There are other lives, but they are harder to see.

In the water, beneath the membrane, there are *pale things*. You catch glimpses when a ribbon dips and the scale-creatures part. Something white, or gray, or translucent, moving slowly in the dark. They are not fish. They are not worms. They are *under-lives*, the ones that exist in the space beneath the floor, in the cracks, in the places where the rock is still soft. They are rarely seen, but they are *present*. You feel them the way you feel a draft—an absence of warmth, a slight wrongness, a sense that the floor is not entirely solid.

Some of them come up. They are small, soft-bodied, eyeless. They wriggle through gaps in the membrane and are immediately consumed by the scale-creatures or the ribbons. But sometimes—rarely—one makes it to the surface and *stays*, clinging to the underside of the membrane, pulsing faintly, as if trying to decide whether it belongs above or below. These are the ones the ribbons take. These are the ones that look like teeth.

There are also lives in the walls.

The rock is young, still forming, still sweating. In the hottest places, where the steam is thickest, there are *crusts*—pale, powdery, faintly luminous. These are not just mineral deposits. They are colonies of something smaller than the scale-creatures, smaller than the gaps between the ribbon-joints. They are *micro-fauna*, if that word means anything here. They live in the thin film of water that coats the walls. They feed on the heat, on the minerals, on each other. They are the reason the water climbs. They are the reason the walls feel *alive* when you press your hand against them and immediately regret it.

You do not see them, but you feel their attention. It is faint, diffuse, like the attention of a crowd that has not yet decided to look at you. But it is there. The place is *full* of it. Every surface is attended. Every drop of water is a small ecosystem. Every flake of rock is a tool or a shell or a body.

And then there are the *layer-lives*.

These are the hardest to describe because they do not exist in all the versions of the room. You see them only when the pages misalign, when the layers flicker and you catch a glimpse of a version of the chamber that is not this one. In one version, there is a creature—large, slow, many-legged—moving across the far wall like a shadow with weight. In another, the pool is empty and the floor is covered in small, white, spiral shells, each one the size of a thumbprint, each one humming faintly. In another, the air itself is thick with motes that are not dust but *eggs*, drifting, pulsing, waiting.

You do not know if these lives are real or potential. You do not know if they are past or future or sideways. You only know that when you look too long, one of the layers *chooses you*, and suddenly you are in a version of the room where the fauna are different, and the silence is deeper, and the heat is a weight on your chest, and you cannot hear your own breath.

You leave before that happens. Or you don’t. Or you do, but part of you stays, filed away in one of the pages, a layer in the stack, a life in the density.

The Flute-Well does not have a name. But it has a population. It is crowded. It is full. It is *saturated* with lives that do not recognize you as separate from themselves. You are a texture. You are a temperature. You are a brief, warm interruption in the water’s climb, in the membrane’s listening, in the ribbons’ unfolding.

You are, for a moment, one of the things that live here.
