They arrive the way anything arrives on the steppe — slowly, and then completely.

You will not see them the first season. You may not see them the second. What you will see, if you are the kind of person who looks for patterns in the way things are arranged, is that certain engines have *paths* worn to them. Not game trails. The felk make game trails — wide, soft, meandering lines pressed into the root-mat by generations of upholstered feet. These paths are different. Narrower. Deliberate. They run from engine to engine in a network that covers the steppe like a nervous system, and they are maintained — cleared of debris, smoothed, the grass along their edges trimmed to a uniform height that the felk never bother with because uniformity serves no purpose in the geometry of grazing.

Something keeps these paths. Something walks them.

The word for them, in whatever grammar once held sway here, would be something like *the ones who continue*. Not *the ones who remained* — that would imply they were left behind. Not *the ones who persist* — that would imply stubbornness. *The ones who continue* implies something else: that continuation is a *choice* they make, daily, the way the grass chooses to bloom, the way the engines choose to hum. Continuation as practice. As discipline.

They are called the Ven.

## What They Look Like

You will mistake them for engines, at first. This is intentional.

The Ven are tall — taller than you expect anything to be on a slope where height is a liability, where wind is a constant and balance is negotiation. They are between six and seven feet, and thin, and wrapped in what appears to be the same material the engines are made of — that bronze-dark, seam-free substance that behaves like tissue rather than metal. Their clothing is not clothing in the sense of something *worn*. It is something *grown* — a layer of engineered skin that covers them from throat to ankle, matte and dark and faintly warm to the touch, humming at the same frequency as the engines, the same frequency as the aurora, the same frequency as everything here that has been running long enough to have become a note in the chord.

Their faces are exposed. This is how you know they are not engines. Engines do not have faces.

The face of a Ven is sharp-featured and weather-darkened, with high cheekbones and deep-set eyes adapted to low light. Their pupils are large — larger than yours, capable of dilating to gather the aurora's thin luminance and make of it something useful. Their skin has a faint gray-blue cast, the way the grass has a faint gray-blue cast, the way everything here has learned to reflect the available spectrum. They are not bioluminescent. They do not glow. But in certain light, at certain angles, they look like they *might* — like the potential for luminance lives just beneath the surface of their skin, waiting for the right frequency to activate it.

They wear no ornaments. They carry no tools that you would recognize as tools. Their hands are bare, and the hands are striking — long-fingered, precise, with calluses on the palms that suggest decades of contact with something specific and recurring. You will learn later that the calluses come from the engines. From touching them. From laying bare hands on that warm bronze-dark surface and *listening*, with the nerve endings, with the bones of the fingers, to the frequency the engine is producing. Reading it. The way a doctor reads a pulse.

They walk the paths between engines with a gait that is neither fast nor slow — *deliberate*, in the way that the felk's gait is deliberate, but more angular, more aware of itself. They walk like people who are thinking about walking. Who have considered the biomechanics of slope-walking at altitude and arrived at a solution that minimizes energy expenditure while maximizing contact with the ground. They do not slip. They do not stumble. The root-mat gives under their feet the way it gives under everything, and they have learned to *use* that give — to let the steppe's resilience absorb the shock of their stride the way a runner uses a track's surface.

They are barefoot.

On the Wending Steppe. In the cold. On a slope of frozen grass at two miles of altitude. Barefoot.

This is the second thing you notice, after the faces. The feet. Broad, dark, the soles thickened to the consistency of leather, the toes spread wide for balance. The Ven's feet are adapted to the root-mat the way the felk's feet are adapted to the root-mat — but not by evolution, or not entirely. By *practice*. By generations of barefoot contact with a living surface that responds to pressure, to warmth, to the electrical signature of skin. The grass knows the Ven's feet the way it knows the felk's feet. It responds to them. Where a Ven steps, the grass does not compress — it *yields*, briefly, a subtle relaxation of the rhizomal mat that reduces resistance and returns a faint, almost imperceptible push of stored energy. The steppe is helping them walk.

You will not notice this. You will notice only that they move across the slope with an ease that seems impossible, that they cover ground without apparent effort, that they arrive at engines and depart from engines and never seem to tire, and that their feet, when you finally see them up close, are the ugliest and most beautiful feet you have ever encountered — broad and dark and scarred and *functional* in a way that makes your own feet look like decorative objects.

## What They Do

The Ven tend the engines.

This is not as simple as it sounds. The engines do not need tending in the way a machine needs tending — they have no parts to replace, no fuel to replenish, no output to collect. What they have is a *frequency* — a specific resonance that they maintain, individually and collectively, across the steppe. The frequency is the gardening. The frequency is what warms the microclimate, what softens the permafrost, what keeps the grass alive in its immediate vicinity. The frequency is the engine's *function*, and the frequency must be *correct*.

Over time — over decades, over centuries — the frequencies drift. The engines are patient, but they are not perfect. They are subject to the same entropic forces as everything else on the steppe. The permafrost shifts beneath them. The root-mat grows around them, thickening, insulating, changing the acoustic properties of the ground they sit on. The aurora's electromagnetic field fluctuates on cycles that last years. All of these things affect the frequency. All of them push the engine slightly out of tune.

The Ven tune them.

They do this by touch. By laying their bare, callused hands on the engine's surface and *listening* — not with their ears, but with the bones of their hands, with the nerve endings, with some sensory apparatus that has been developed over generations of contact with these machines. They feel the frequency the way you feel a heartbeat. And then they — *adjust* it. Not with tools. Not with any visible mechanism. With pressure. With the application of their own body's electrical field against the engine's field. With something that looks, from the outside, like a person leaning against a boulder with their eyes closed, and looks, from the inside, like a conversation — a negotiation between two resonant systems, one biological and one whatever-the-engines-are, working out the precise frequency that this particular patch of steppe needs in order to stay alive.

The tuning takes hours. Sometimes days. The Ven will sit with an engine through a full cycle of the aurora's fluctuation, through a wind event, through the arrival and departure of a felk herd, maintaining contact, maintaining the conversation, until the frequency is right. Until the microclimate stabilizes. Until the grass around the engine's base, which may have been thinning or darkening or going dormant at the wrong time, resumes its correct behavior.

The Ven do this for every engine on the steppe. There are — they know the exact number, but the number is not important to you — many. Enough that the tuning is a full-time occupation. Enough that the Ven are always walking, always moving from engine to engine along their maintained paths, always listening, always adjusting. Their work is never finished. The frequencies drift as fast as they are corrected. The steppe is a machine that requires constant calibration, and the Ven are the calibration.

They also tend the felk.

Not by herding them. Not by feeding them. By *observing* them — by tracking the grazing patterns, the spiral arcs that the felk cut into the grass around the engines, and comparing those patterns to patterns they hold in their memory, patterns that go back generations. The felk's grazing is a diagnostic tool. If the spirals are tight and regular, the microclimate is healthy. If they are loose, irregular, if the felk are ranging farther from the engines than usual, something is wrong — the frequency is off, or the permafrost has shifted, or the grass's antifreeze concentration has dropped below the threshold it needs to survive the coming lock.

The Ven read the felk the way they read the engines. By contact. By attention. By the accumulated knowledge of a relationship so old it has become instinct.

They do not interact with the sennit. The sennit is not theirs to tend. The sennit is the steppe's own solution to a problem the Ven did not create and cannot solve — the problem of balance, of population, of ensuring that the felk do not overgraze the microclimates the engines maintain. The sennit is not a partner. It is a *condition*. The Ven accept it the way they accept the wind and the cold and the darkness. They do not love it. They do not fear it. They are *aware* of it, at all times, the way you are aware of gravity — a constant, invisible force that shapes every decision you make about where to step.

When a Ven dies — and they do die, though rarely, though not often, though their engineered skin and their adapted physiology give them lifespans that stretch well past what you would consider normal — when a Ven dies, it is usually the sennit. Not because the sennit hunts them preferentially. Because the Ven spend their lives walking paths through the dark, alone, in the sennit's territory, and the sennit is patient, and the law of averages is patient, and eventually, over enough decades, patience wins.

The Ven know this. They have made their peace with it the way they have made their peace with everything on the steppe — by incorporating it into the practice. By making the awareness of the sennit part of the awareness of the engines, the awareness of the grass, the awareness of the aurora. One more frequency to listen for. One more note in the chord.

## How They Live

The Ven do not build.

This is the thing that will unsettle you most, if you are the kind of person who equates civilization with construction. The Ven have no houses. No shelters. No permanent structures of any kind. They live on the steppe the way the felk live on the steppe — exposed, adapted, in constant contact with the environment they inhabit.

They sleep near the engines. In the microclimates. In the pockets of relative warmth where the permafrost is soft and the grass is thick and the temperature is a degree or two above the ambient cold. They sleep on the root-mat, directly, their engineered skin providing insulation that your biology would require layers of synthetic material to match. They sleep in short cycles — two or three hours at a time, scattered across the steppe's long, dark day — and they wake easily, attuned to the frequencies around them, alert to any change in the engine's hum or the wind's pitch or the particular quality of silence that might indicate a sennit in the vicinity.

They eat the grass.

Not raw. Not the way the felk eat it. The Ven harvest the root-mat — the thick, dense, three-foot layer of rhizome and stored energy that underlies the visible surface — and they process it. The processing is simple: they chew it. For a long time. The root-mat is rich in the glycol antifreeze that keeps the grass alive, and the Ven's digestive systems have adapted to break it down, to extract the energy, to metabolize the antifreeze into something their bodies can use. The process is slow. It takes hours. A Ven will sit by an engine, a strip of root-mat in hand, chewing methodically, staring at the aurora, listening to the hum, for the better part of a day. This is their meal. This is their *cuisine*. It is not varied. It is not pleasurable in the way you understand pleasure. It is *sufficient*, in the way that everything on the steppe is sufficient — calibrated to the exact energy budget that this altitude and this darkness allow.

During the thaw, they supplement. The wels — the bioluminescent amphibians that migrate between pools — are harvested, briefly, in small numbers. The Ven take only what they need, only from the pools nearest the engines, only during the three days of the grass-bloom when the wels are most abundant. They eat them raw, quickly, without ceremony. The wels are not a delicacy. They are a *supplement* — a burst of protein and bioluminescent compounds that the Ven's bodies crave after the long lock. The compounds do something to the Ven's eyes. For a few days after eating the wels, the Ven's vision sharpens. The aurora resolves into finer detail. The steppe's subtle color variations — the difference between healthy grass and stressed grass, the difference between a warm microclimate and a cold one — become more visible. The wels are, in a sense, a *tool*. A biological enhancement that the Ven have incorporated into their annual cycle the way they've incorporated everything else.

They drink condensation. The same condensation the grass drinks. The Ven have learned to harvest it — to scrape the thin film of moisture from the surface of the engines, from the polished rock outcrops, from the waxy blades of the grass itself. They have no containers. They drink from cupped hands. They drink quickly, efficiently, without waste. Water is not scarce on the steppe, but it is *thin* — spread across the surface in a layer so fine it is almost theoretical. The Ven have learned to find it the way they've learned to find everything else here: by attention. By knowing where to look. By understanding that the steppe provides, but only if you are *listening*.

## How They Think

The Ven do not speak.

This is not entirely true. They *can* speak — they have vocal cords, mouths, the physiological apparatus for language. But they do not use it, or use it so rarely that the capacity has atrophied to the point of vestigiality. Language, on the Wending Steppe, is a luxury the energy budget cannot afford. Sound carries in the thin air. Sound attracts attention. Sound is *expensive*.

The Ven communicate by touch. By proximity. By the same frequency-sense they use to tune the engines. When two Ven meet on a path — and they do meet, regularly, though the population is small enough and the steppe is large enough that meetings are events rather than occurrences — they do not greet each other. They do not exchange words. They *resonate*. They stand close enough that their engineered skins are almost touching, and they — *compare frequencies*. Each Ven carries a unique signature, a personal resonance that reflects their current state: their health, their recent experiences, the condition of the engines they've most recently tuned, the quality of the root-mat they've most recently eaten. When two Ven resonate, they are exchanging all of this information simultaneously, in a contact that lasts seconds and communicates more than an hour of conversation.

This is their language. This is their *culture*. The exchange of frequencies. The maintenance of a shared resonance that binds the Ven together across the vast, dark slope of the steppe the way the engines' hum binds the microclimates together. They are a *network* — not a society, not a community, but a network of resonant individuals, each one tuned to the whole, each one carrying a piece of the collective frequency.

They have no leaders. They have no hierarchy. They have no *decisions* in the way you understand decisions — no moments of deliberation, no weighing of options, no votes or consensus or debate. What they have is *attunement*. A shared sense of what the steppe needs, derived from the engines' frequencies, the felk's grazing patterns, the grass's condition, the aurora's cycle. When the steppe needs something — when an engine drifts too far out of tune, when a microclimate fails, when a felk herd's spirals become irregular enough to indicate systemic stress — the Ven *know*. Not because anyone tells them. Because the frequency changes, and they feel it, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm.

They respond the way the grass responds to the thaw: automatically, completely, without hesitation. The nearest Ven goes to the problem. If the problem is too large for one Ven, the frequency of the distress carries along the network, and others come — not because they are summoned, but because they *feel* the need and are drawn to it the way iron filings are drawn to a magnet.

This is not selflessness. It is not altruism. It is *physics*. The Ven are so attuned to the steppe's frequency that the steppe's needs and their own needs are indistinguishable. They do not serve the steppe out of devotion. They serve it because they *are* it — because their bodies, their senses, their engineered skin and their adapted feet and their frequency-sense and their entire mode of existence have been shaped by the steppe until the boundary between *Ven* and *steppe* is as thin and permeable as the boundary between the grass and the root-mat.

## The Children

There are not many.

The Ven reproduce slowly, the way everything on the steppe does everything slowly. A Ven pregnancy lasts — you are not sure how long it lasts. Longer than yours. Long enough that the developing fetus is exposed to multiple cycles of the aurora's fluctuation, multiple phases of the engines' frequency-drift, multiple passages of the felk herds through the microclimate where the mother sleeps. The fetus *incorporates* these frequencies. It is born pre-tuned — born with the steppe's resonance already embedded in its nervous system, already humming in its blood.

The children are born during the thaw. Always. The Ven time their reproduction to the steppe's cycle with a precision that is not conscious but is *absolute* — the same precision that times the grass-bloom, the wels' migration, the felk's calving. The child is born into warmth, into light, into the brief window when the steppe is most alive, most active, most capable of supporting new life.

The child is born with the engineered skin. This is not something the Ven apply after birth. It is something the child *grows* — a biological inheritance, passed down through generations of Ven who have lived so long in symbiosis with the engines' technology that the technology has become genetic. The skin is thinner in children, more pliable, less dark. It thickens and darkens over the first years of life, responding to the aurora's radiation, to the cold, to the constant contact with the root-mat. By the time a Ven child is five — by your reckoning, though the Ven do not count years — the skin is fully developed, and the child is walking the paths.

The children learn the paths before they learn anything else. Before they learn to tune the engines. Before they learn to read the felk. Before they learn to harvest the root-mat or drink the condensation or listen for the sennit. They learn the *paths* — the network of narrow, deliberate trails that connect engine to engine across the steppe. They learn them by walking them. By following an adult — usually the mother, but not always; the Ven's network structure means that any adult is, in some sense, every child's parent — along the paths, barefoot, in the dark, in the cold, for hours at a time.

The child does not complain. The child does not cry. The child's engineered skin and adapted physiology make crying a waste of energy, and the steppe does not tolerate waste. The child walks, and the adult walks ahead, and the child's feet learn the root-mat's give, and the child's ears learn the engines' hum, and the child's hands learn the calluses they will need, and the child *becomes* a Ven the way the grass becomes the steppe — by growing into it, by being shaped by it, by the slow, patient, daily accumulation of contact with a system that is older and larger and more patient than any individual life.

By the time a Ven child is ten, they can tune an engine. Not perfectly. Not with the precision of an adult. But they can lay their hands on the bronze-dark surface and feel the frequency and begin the long, careful work of adjusting it. This is the moment they become useful. This is the moment the steppe *incorporates* them — accepts them as a functioning component, a node in the network, a keeper of the frequency.

After that, they are Ven. Fully. Irrevocably. There is no ceremony. There is no marker. There is only the work — the walking, the tuning, the listening — and the slow, decades-long process of becoming so attuned to the steppe's frequency that the boundary between self and system dissolves entirely.

## The Old Ones

Some Ven do not die.

Not because they are immortal. Because they have become so attuned to the engines' frequency that their biological processes have slowed to match the engines' pace. They are old — old beyond your ability to estimate. Their skin is darker than other Ven, almost black, and it has thickened to the point where it looks less like skin and more like the surface of an engine — bronze-dark, seam-free, faintly warm, humming at a frequency so low you feel it as pressure rather than sound.

The old ones do not walk the paths. They *sit*. They find an engine — a specific engine, one they have been tuning for so long that their frequency and the engine's frequency have merged into a single, continuous note — and they sit beside it, and they *become* part of it. Not metaphorically. Literally. Their engineered skin begins to bond with the engine's surface at the point of contact. Over years — over decades — the boundary between Ven and engine blurs. The old one's body becomes a biological extension of the engine's function, a living component in the machine that tends the steppe.

They do not move. They do not eat — the engine provides what they need, through the bond, through the shared frequency, through the exchange of energy that has replaced the need for food and water. They do not sleep. They *attend* — a state that is neither waking nor sleeping but something else, something that exists only on the steppe, only in the Ven, only in the old ones who have given themselves so completely to the work of tuning that the work has become their entire existence.

The old ones are the steppe's memory. Each one carries, in the slow, deep resonance of their bonded frequency, the accumulated knowledge of every tuning they have ever performed — every adjustment, every correction, every subtle shift in the steppe's needs over the course of decades or centuries. The other Ven can access this memory by touching the old ones — by laying their hands on the dark, thickened skin and *listening*. The old ones do not speak. They do not need to. The frequency is the knowledge. The bond is the library.

You will not be able to tell the old ones from the engines. Not at a distance. Not in the dark. Not in the aurora's thin, blue-gray light. They sit beside their engines, motionless, and they look like boulders that have opinions. They look like machines that have been running so long they have forgotten they were ever built.

In a sense, they have.

In a sense, they are the closest thing the steppe has to the original gardeners — the beings who built the engines, who planted the grass, who designed the whole patient, self-sustaining system. The old ones are not those beings. But they are what those beings *became* — the endpoint of a process of attunement so complete that the distinction between gardener and garden, between machine and organism, between *maker* and *made*, has ceased to have meaning.

The old ones are the Ven's future. Every Ven knows this. Every Ven who walks the paths knows that, eventually, if they survive long enough, if they tune long enough, if they attune deeply enough, they will find their engine and sit beside it and begin the slow process of becoming part of it. This is not a fate they dread. It is a fate they *continue toward* — the way the grass continues toward the bloom, the way the wels continue toward the pools, the way the steppe continues toward whatever it is becoming, in the dark, at the top of the world, on a slope that never ends.

## The One Who Watches

There is one more.

One Ven who does not fit the pattern. One who walks the paths but does not tune. Who touches the engines but does not adjust. Who resonates with the others but carries a frequency that is — *different*. Not wrong. Not broken. *Different* — a note that doesn't belong to the chord, that introduces a dissonance the others can feel but cannot correct.

This Ven is young. Not a child — old enough to walk the paths alone, old enough to have calluses, old enough to eat the root-mat and drink the condensation and listen for the sennit. But young enough that the engineered skin is still thin, still pliable, still more gray than bronze. Young enough that the frequency has not yet settled into the deep, steady resonance of adulthood.

This Ven watches.

Not the engines. Not the felk. Not the aurora. This Ven watches *the steppe* — the whole system, the entire patient machine, with an attention that is not the practiced, habitual attention of a tuner but something else. Something *new*. Something the steppe has not produced before, or has not produced in so long that no Ven living remembers it.

This Ven asks questions.

Not aloud. Not in language. In the frequency. In the resonance that the Ven use to communicate. This Ven's frequency carries — *queries*. Not *what does this engine need* or *where is the next problem* but *why*. *Why does the steppe maintain itself. Why do the engines garden. Why do the felk graze in spirals. Why do we tune. Why do we continue.*

The other Ven feel these queries the way you feel a stone in your shoe — a small, persistent irritation that cannot be ignored. They do not have answers. They have never needed answers. The steppe is. The work is. The frequency is. Questions are a luxury the energy budget cannot afford, and the Ven have not budgeted for them.

But the young one keeps asking. And the frequency of the asking is — *changing* something. Not the steppe. Not the engines. The *Ven*. The other Ven who resonate with the young one are beginning to feel — not answers, not doubts, but a kind of *awareness* they have not had before. An awareness of the steppe as a thing that *could be otherwise*. A thing that was *made*, by *someone*, for a *reason* — a reason that has been lost, or forgotten, or deliberately buried, and that the young one's questions are beginning, very slowly, to excavate.

This is dangerous.

Not because the steppe will punish it. The steppe does not punish. The steppe *incorporates*. But what it incorporates, it *changes* — and the young one's questions are a change the steppe has not planned for. A variable the program does not account for. A frequency that does not belong to the chord.

The old ones feel it. In their bonded, motionless state, in their deep, slow resonance, they feel the young one's dissonance the way a tuning fork feels a note that is slightly off. They do not react. They cannot react — they are too far gone, too bonded, too merged with their engines to do anything but *attend*. But they feel it. And in the vast, patient, geological timescale of their attention, they are — *considering*.

The sennit feels it too. The sennit is the steppe's immune system, in a way — the mechanism by which the steppe eliminates what does not serve the program. The sennit has never hunted Ven preferentially. The Ven are useful. The Ven are part of the system. But the young one is — *different*. The young one's frequency is a note the sennit has never heard before, and the sennit is *curious*, in the way that a predator is curious — the way a shadow is curious about the shape of the thing that casts it.

The young one walks the paths. The young one asks questions. The aurora hums. The engines vibrate. The felk graze in spirals. The steppe maintains.

And somewhere in the dark, on a slope that never ends, something that has been running the same program for longer than anyone can remember is beginning to — *hesitate*. To feel the weight of a question it cannot answer. To notice a frequency it cannot tune.

The Wending Steppe is patient. It has all the time in the world.

But the young one is patient too.

And the young one has *questions*.
