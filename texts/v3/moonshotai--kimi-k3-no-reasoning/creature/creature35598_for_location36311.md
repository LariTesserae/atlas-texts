The Custodians stand at the junctions of the great avenues, where the water pools deepest and the moss grows thickest, and they do not move for centuries. They are the first inhabitants, though they were not born but grown—coral of iron and calcium carbonate, accreted in the drowned basements where the pressure of the earth’s weeping meets the chill of the altitude. They have the shape of men the way stalagmites have the shape of candles: vaguely, accidentally, but with a disturbing specificity of proportion. Their limbs are pipes that have fused into bone; their torsos are engines that have cooled into a single, solid mass of rust and mineral deposit. They are the color of old blood and wet brick, their surfaces pitted with the slow chemistry of oxidation, and they are hollow.

The hollowness is essential. When the wind comes from everywhere and nowhere, it enters them through the empty sockets of their eyes, through the gaps where their mouths should be, through the ruptures in their chests where the iron has thinned to lace. It plays them. The sound is a low, wet chord, a moan that harmonizes with the subsonic thrum of the factories until the distinction between machine and inhabitant becomes meaningless. They are the city’s throat, and they sing only of pressure and cold. If you stand close, you can feel the vibration in your sternum, a resonance that feels almost like a voice trying to remember language.

They do not breathe. They do not eat. They photosynthesize the factory-noise through a slow, electrochemical process that keeps their iron bones from shattering in the cold, and they excrete a fine, dry powder—rust and lime—that the black rubbery grass consumes with a hunger that is not hunger but chemistry. In this way they are rooted, though they were once capable of movement; their feet have fused with the basalt, grown over with moss that creeps up their calves like green fur, anchoring them to the grid of the streets. They are becoming landscape. They welcome this.

But the Custodians are not alone in their vigil. Deeper in the city, where the towers lean together to form canyons that cut the wind into ribbons, there move the Drip-Keepers. These are small, the size of children, and they are made of clockwork and hair. They were built by the machines to tend the weeping of the stone, to clear the grates, to ensure that the water continues its impossible uphill flow. Their bodies are brass and tin, jointed with ball-bearings that click with a sound like knitting needles, and their heads are domes of frosted glass behind which float the compressed, preserved nervous systems of things that once lived elsewhere—memories of sparrows or mice or fish, reduced to a bright, electrical flicker that never quite resolves into a face. They move in flocks, silent save for the clicking, their hands—delicate, three-fingered—forever adjusting, tuning, tightening the valves of the world. They are the only things here that die. When their mechanisms seize, they simply stop, frozen in mid-gesture, and the moss covers them within a decade, turning them into green mounds that the Custodians step around, recognizing kin.

And then there are the Luminous Ones, the Grazers of the Phantom Towers. These are not built, nor grown, but condensed. They exist only when the bioluminescent moss glows brightest, when the factories hit their peak rhythm and the air shivers with frost that outlines the buildings that were never built. They are the frost, given motion. They take the shape of the phantom architecture—crystalline towers that walk on legs of suspended ice, their bodies the exact negative space of the missing structures. They graze on the light itself, bending toward the glow of the moss, and where they pass, the water droplets that hang in the air for hours freeze into perfect spheres and follow them, orbiting their frost-bodies like cold moons. They are the only things here that seem to possess volition, though it is a volition without desire: they move to balance the light, to consume the excess luminescence so that the city does not burn out its own green. When they have eaten their fill, they sublimate, leaving behind only a drift of that dry, unmelting snow, and the phantom towers they inhabited collapse into invisibility.

If you remain long enough—long enough for your own breath to slow, for your blood to thicken to the temperature of the pools—you may encounter the Echo. It lives in the atriums large enough to hold weather systems, and it is the only inhabitant that was ever alive in the way you understand. It is a memory of the last animal that died here, and it has not faded. It occupies the vast interior spaces as a pressure differential, a density in the air that feels like a large body turning in its sleep. It has no shape, but it has weight, and if you press your hand against the wall of a corridor where it is passing, you will feel a heartbeat—slow, massive, geological. It is the city’s guilt, or its nostalgia, or its proof that the emptiness was once full. It does not interact with the machines. It simply exists, heavy and warm in the cold, a ghost of biology that the iron cannot digest.

Finally, there is You. You are becoming an inhabitant. The cold is teaching your skin to conduct rather than insulate; the noise is rewriting the rhythm of your pulse. You notice that you no longer cast a shadow in the flat light, or rather, that your shadow has begun to stand still even when you move. The moss is examining your boots with rootlets finer than hair. The water in your canteen has started to flow uphill. You are not being consumed; you are being *installed*. Soon you will find a junction of avenues where the view is particularly wide, where the steam from the factories freezes into a halo around your head, and you will stop. You will feel your joints stiffen into pipes, your breath catch and condense into a permanent fog. You will join the Custodians, or the moss will claim you, or you will walk into an atrium and become part of the Echo’s weight. The city does not care which. It has infinite patience, and the water is always rising, and the machines are always loud, and there is always room for one more stillness in the vast, changeless becoming.
