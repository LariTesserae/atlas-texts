# The Inhabitants of Threnos

## The Settled

The people of Threnos are called the Settled by those few outsiders who ever find reason to name them, though they call themselves nothing at all. Names for the collective require contrast, and there is no one else. There is only Threnos and the uplands beyond—distant, theoretical, the source of the silt and nothing more.

They are pale in the way of things that live under stone, though their skin has a particular quality that distinguishes them from the merely sun-deprived: a slight translucency, as if the perpetual humidity has saturated their flesh. Veins show blue-green through the skin of their hands and forearms. Their eyes, adjusted to the brass-colored dimness, have pupils that seem perpetually dilated, giving them a look of constant, low-level surprise that belies their actual temperament, which is one of profound, geological patience.

They are not unhappy. This is the first thing that must be understood, because everything about Threnos suggests misery to the outside imagination—the heat, the damp, the endless drone of machinery, the absence of trees, the silt-choked canals, the light that never brightens. But the Settled do not experience their lives as deprivation. They experience them as *continuation*. Each day is like the one before, and this is not a source of despair but a form of knowledge so deep it has become indistinguishable from comfort. A man in Threnos knows what he will be doing at every hour of every day for the rest of his life, and this knowledge sits in him not as a cage but as a kind of bedrock—the very bedrock that the physical city has never reached. The Settled *are* the bedrock. They are the thing to which the buildings are anchored.

### The Threshold-Keepers

Every building in Threnos has a threshold-keeper, though the title is informal and the role is hereditary in a way that no one has ever formally decided. The threshold-keeper is the person—usually but not always the eldest member of the household—responsible for adding the new layer of brick or stone to the building's entrance each year, raising the threshold by precisely the amount the ground has settled. This is not a measurement that anyone takes with instruments. The threshold-keeper knows. They know the way you know the size of your own hand. They run their fingers along the edge of the doorway and feel the gap between where the step meets the street and where it should meet the street, and they mix the mortar and lay the brick, and the surface of Threnos remains at the level it has always been.

The threshold-keepers are the closest thing Threnos has to a priesthood, though there is no religion in any recognizable sense. There is no god of the silt, no deity of the water table, no spirit of the machines. But there is the threshold, and the threshold must be maintained, and the act of maintaining it is understood—without anyone ever saying so—to be the essential act of living in Threnos. The threshold-keepers do not speak about their work. They do it in the early hours of the morning, before the factories reach their full operating tempo, when the hum is at its lowest ebb and the brass-colored light is at its thinnest. They work alone, and if someone passes by, the passerby does not stop or offer help, but slows almost imperceptibly, a gesture of acknowledgment so subtle that an outsider would not notice it at all.

When a threshold-keeper dies, the role passes to the next person in the household who feels the gap. There is always someone who feels the gap. It is not a skill that can be taught. It is a sensitivity that either exists or does not, and in every generation, in every building, at least one person has it. The Settled do not find this remarkable. They find it no more remarkable than the fact that the flies hatch or that the reeds rattle or that the water rises and falls.

### The Machine-Listeners

The factories of Threnos run at a constant tempo, and this tempo has not changed in living memory. But the machines are not static. They wear, they age, they develop idiosyncrasies—a piston that strikes a fraction of a second late, a valve that releases with a slightly different timbre, a gear that has worn to a profile that produces a faint harmonic overtone absent from its neighbors. These changes are glacially slow and imperceptible to most of the Settled, but not to the machine-listeners.

The machine-listeners are a guild, of sorts, though the word "guild" implies more organization than actually exists. They are simply the people who can hear the machines—not the overwhelming hum that everyone hears, but the individual voices within it. A machine-listener can stand in the tannery quarter and tell you that the third beating drum in the second row has developed a slight irregularity in its rotation, or that the steam pressure in the main conduit beneath Fulmar Street is running a quarter-degree hotter than it should, or that the chemical precipitation tank at the dye-works has developed a resonance in its agitation paddles that will, in approximately three years, cause a crack in the tank wall.

They do not repair anything. This is crucial. The machine-listeners are not mechanics. There are mechanics in Threnos—quiet, methodical people who crawl into the spaces between machines with tools made of the same rusted steel as the machines themselves—but they repair only what breaks, and things rarely break. The machines were built to run at this tempo, and they run at this tempo, and the machine-listeners simply *listen*. They track the slow evolution of the sound the way a naturalist might track the evolution of a species, noting changes, cataloging variations, maintaining an oral record that passes from listener to listener across generations.

The oral record is extraordinary. It is not written down—nothing in Threnos is written down, because paper rots in the humidity and ink bleeds and runs—but it is preserved with a fidelity that would put many literate cultures to shame. The machine-listeners train their successors by walking the factory floors together, the elder pointing out sounds, the younger repeating them back. Not describing them—*repeating* them. The machine-listeners can reproduce the sounds of the machines with their voices, a kind of throat-singing that mimics the mechanical timbres with uncanny precision. A master machine-listener can reproduce the sound of an entire factory floor—not the overwhelming hum, but the individual components, layered and simultaneous, as if the listener's throat contained all the pistons and valves and gears at once.

This is not performance. The Settled do not have performance in the way that other cultures do. The vocal replication is a *record*, a living archive of the city's mechanical state, and it is maintained with the same unhurried diligence as the thresholds. When a machine-listener dies, their successor begins the process of re-listening, re-cataloging, adjusting the vocal record to account for whatever has changed in the interim. The record is never finished. It is never supposed to be finished. It is a continuous act of attention, a way of knowing the city that parallels the threshold-keepers' way of knowing the ground.

### The Canal-Walkers

The canals of Threnos are not navigable in any conventional sense. The liquid that fills them is too thick, too chemically saturated, too hot. No boat could float on it, and even if one could, the slow current would carry it nowhere useful. But the canals are not empty, and they are not ignored. They are walked.

The canal-walkers are people who move along the narrow stone ledges that line the canals, placing their feet with the precision of those long-legged birds that probe the silt. They do this daily, following routes that are as fixed and unvarying as everything else in Threnos. What they are doing, exactly, is difficult to articulate, even for the canal-walkers themselves. They are not inspecting the canals. They are not cleaning them. They are not measuring anything. They are, in the closest approximation available, *accompanying* the water.

The canals change. Not in volume or speed or temperature—those remain constant—but in chemistry. The effluent from the factories varies according to processes that shift on cycles longer than any single human lifetime. The dye-works produce a particular compound during certain phases of their operation; the tanneries leach minerals that interact with those compounds; the metal-fabrication zone contributes particulates that settle at different rates depending on the ambient humidity, which varies by fractions of a percent across the seasons. The result is that the canals, while always the same in gross appearance—thick, steaming, opaque—are microscopically different from day to day, and the canal-walkers know these differences.

They know them by smell, by the way the steam feels on their skin, by the color of the surface sheen where the brass light hits the liquid at certain angles. A canal-walker can tell you whether the tanneries are in their chromium phase or their tannin phase by the scent that rises from the water near the third bridge. They can tell you whether the dye-works have shifted from indigo-base to ochre-base by the faint iridescence on the surface. They carry this knowledge not because it is useful—it is not useful in any practical sense—but because it is *theirs*. It is the knowledge that their role has produced, and in Threnos, the production of knowledge through sustained attention is as fundamental as the production of goods through sustained labor.

The canal-walkers also tend the reeds. The bleached, rattling clumps that grow along the canal edges are the city's only vegetation, and while they are not cultivated in any agricultural sense, the canal-walkers clear debris from around their roots, remove dead stalks, and occasionally—rarely, perhaps once in a decade—transplant a clump from one location to another when the canal's edge has shifted. They do this with their bare hands, plunging them into the chemical-saturated silt, and their palms bear the evidence: a network of fine scars, white against the translucent skin, that maps decades of contact with the city's liquid underlayer.

The reeds, in turn, are the raw material for the one craft that Threnos produces beyond its industrial output. The dried stalks are split and woven into mats, baskets, and—most significantly—into the screens that cover the windows of every building. These screens serve no practical purpose; they do not keep out the heat or the flies or the dim light. But they filter the light into patterns, and the patterns are different for every building, and the difference is meaningful. The pattern of a building's reed-screen tells you who lives there—not their name, but their *quality*, the particular texture of their presence in the city. A tight weave indicates a household that values inwardness, reticence, the maintenance of private space within the intimate density of the city. A loose weave indicates openness, a willingness to be seen, to let the brass light penetrate. Diagonal patterns, chevrons, concentric diamonds—each is a statement, though the Settled would never call them statements. They would call them *weaves*. The word carries the same weight that "name" carries elsewhere.

### The Silt-Divers

Beneath the canals, beneath the silt, beneath the ancient foundations that have never been reached, there are things. No one in Threnos knows what these things are, but the silt-divers have touched them.

The silt-divers are the rarest of the Settled, perhaps a dozen in a city of thousands. They are the ones who descend into the canals—not to swim, which would be impossible in the viscous liquid, but to sink. They weight themselves with stones and lower themselves from the canal ledges on ropes, disappearing beneath the opaque surface, and they stay down for as long as they can hold their breath, which is considerably longer than seems possible. The chemical bath would kill an outsider within minutes, but the silt-divers have developed—over generations, over centuries—a tolerance that borders on the symbiotic. Their skin has thickened. Their eyes have developed a nictitating membrane, a second lid that filters the worst of the chemical assault. Their lungs have adapted to extract oxygen from air that is increasingly saturated with industrial vapor.

What they find below is not the ancient foundations, which remain unreached. What they find is the *accumulation*: two centuries of settled debris, compressed into layers that record the city's industrial history with the fidelity of geological strata. The silt-divers can read these layers by touch. They know the difference between the sediment deposited during the early tannery period and the sediment from the later chemical-dye era. They know where the metal particulates concentrate and where the organic matter—such as it is—has been compressed into a thin, dark seam that smells of something almost but not entirely unlike decay.

They do not bring anything back. There is nothing to bring back that would be of value, and the silt-divers are not interested in salvage. What they bring back is *pressure*. They return to the surface with the knowledge of what lies beneath—the depth of the accumulation, the rate of settling, the proximity (still distant, still theoretical) of the bedrock that the pilings have never reached. This knowledge is shared with the threshold-keepers, not in words but in a gesture: the silt-diver places their hand on the threshold-keeper's hand and presses, and the pressure communicates everything—the depth, the density, the rate. The threshold-keeper then knows how much to raise the threshold, not by measurement but by this transferred sensation, this tactile communion between the above and the below.

The silt-divers are the only inhabitants of Threnos who regularly experience true darkness. Beneath the surface of the canals, the brass light does not penetrate. There is nothing—no color, no shadow, no suggestion of form. The silt-divers describe this not as frightening but as *restful*. It is the only place in Threnos where the hum of the machines is fully muffled, where the heat is different—not cooler, but more uniform, a heat that comes from all directions equally rather than pressing down from above. Some silt-divers, in their later years, have to be reminded to come up. Not because they are suicidal—the Settled do not have a concept of suicide, because the concept requires the idea that one's life could be otherwise—but because the below is so *complete*, so thoroughly itself, that the above begins to seem like the interruption rather than the norm.

### The Fly-Catchers

The iridescent flies that hover above the canals are the most visible form of non-human life in Threnos, and they are also the most intimately connected to the Settled. The flies feed on the chemical film that coats the water's surface, and in feeding, they concentrate those chemicals in their bodies. Their iridescence is not pigmentation but a structural property of their chitin, which has been altered by generations of exposure to the industrial runoff. The green and gold flashes are, quite literally, the color of the factories made visible in living tissue.

The fly-catchers are children. This is not a profession but a stage of life, something between a game and an apprenticeship. The children of Threnos—pale, damp, serious—spend their early years along the canal edges, catching flies in their cupped hands. They do not kill them. They hold them, feeling the tiny vibration of the wings, watching the light play across the iridescent surface, and then they let them go. In doing this, hour after hour, year after year, they learn the city's most fundamental lesson: that beauty and toxicity are not opposites, that the most vivid colors are produced by the most concentrated poisons, that the things that shimmer are the things that have absorbed the most of what the city produces.

The fly-catchers are also learning patience, though no one tells them this. Catching a fly with cupped hands requires a quality of stillness that does not come naturally to children anywhere else but that is the natural state of children in Threnos. They stand at the canal's edge, barely breathing, their pale hands open, and they wait. The flies come to them—not because the flies are attracted to them, but because in a city where everything moves at the same tempo, a truly still thing is an anomaly, and the flies investigate anomalies. A child who can remain still for an hour will have hands full of iridescent light. A child who fidgets will have nothing.

This is the only form of education in Threnos. There are no schools, no formal instruction. The children learn by catching flies, and then they learn by watching—watching the threshold-keepers, the machine-listeners, the canal-walkers, the mechanics, the weavers—and gradually, without any moment of decision or declaration, they find themselves doing one of these things, and they do it for the rest of their lives.

### The Drowned

Not everyone in Threnos is alive. This is not a metaphor.

The canals are the city's burial ground. When a member of the Settled dies—and they die at the same rate they always have, from causes that rarely vary: the slow accumulation of chemical exposure, the gradual thickening of the lungs, the simple cessation of a heart that has been beating at the same tempo as the machines for seventy or eighty years—their body is not buried in the ground, because the ground is too young, too shifting, too likely to yield up what has been placed in it. Instead, the body is placed in the canal.

Not dropped. Not thrown. *Placed*. The threshold-keeper of the deceased's household prepares the body, wrapping it in reed-mats woven in the pattern of the household, weighting it with stones from the threshold itself—a few bricks removed, so that the threshold dips slightly, a temporary imperfection that will be corrected over the following year as new layers are added. The body is carried to the canal by the household members, and it is lowered into the thick liquid by the silt-divers, who take it down with them, guiding it into the accumulation, placing it among the layers where it will be compressed and incorporated into the city's sedimentary record.

The Drowned are not mourned in any visible way. There is no ceremony beyond the placement, no words spoken, no songs sung. But the Drowned are not forgotten. They are, in fact, more present than the living. They are in the ground—or rather, in the silt that passes for ground. They are in the layers that the silt-divers touch. They are in the chemical composition of the canal water, which rises between the paving stones and weeps from the mortar of the walls. They are, quite literally, in the air, because the steam that rises from the canals carries molecules that were once part of bodies, and the Settled breathe these molecules with every damp, effortful breath.

The Settled do not find this macabre. They find it *correct*. The city is a closed system. Nothing leaves. The dead are transformed—slowly, over decades, as the silt compresses them into thinner and thinner layers—but they are not gone. They are part of the accumulation, part of the record, part of the thing that the threshold-keepers feel when they run their fingers along the edge of the doorway and know, without measuring, how much the ground has settled.

### The Unnamed

There is one more category of inhabitant, and it is the most difficult to describe, because the Settled do not speak about them and may not, in any conscious sense, be aware of them at all.

In the deepest hours of the night—though "night" in Threnos is only a slightly darker version of the perpetual brass dimness, a shift from old brass to tarnished bronze—there are sounds that do not come from the machines. The machine-listeners hear them but do not catalog them. They fall outside the record. They are not mechanical. They are not the cries of the birds or the amphibious mammals. They are not the rattle of the reeds or the drip of condensation. They are something else.

They come from below.

The silt-divers know about these sounds, though they have never heard them directly—underwater, in the darkness, there is only silence. But they have felt them, in the same way they feel the accumulation: a vibration that is not the machines, a tremor that rises from deeper than the deepest piling, a frequency so low that it is felt rather than heard. The silt-divers press this knowledge into the hands of the threshold-keepers, and the threshold-keepers add an extra fraction of a brick to the threshold, a tiny correction that no one has ever explained.

The Unnamed are whatever lives beneath Threnos. Not in the silt, not in the accumulation, but beneath the ancient foundations that have never been reached. They are the reason the pilings have never found bedrock—not because the bedrock is too deep, but because the bedrock is *occupied*. Something is down there, something that has been down there since before the city was built, since before the uplands began washing their sediment into the basin. The machines of Threnos have been running at the same tempo for centuries, and the Settled believe—though "believe" is too strong a word, too conscious, too articulated—that this tempo is not arbitrary. It is a *response*. The machines are keeping time with something. The hum that vibrates in the teeth is a harmony, not a solo.

The Unnamed are not worshipped. They are not feared. They are not loved. They are *accommodated*, in the same way that the water table is accommodated, the heat is accommodated, the perpetual damp is accommodated. They are a condition of existence in Threnos, and the Settled, who are the most accommodating people who have ever lived, accommodate them without thought or effort.

But in the deepest hours, when the brass light is at its dimmest and the hum is at its most uniform, the machine-listeners pause in their walking. The canal-walkers stop on their ledges. The threshold-keepers wake from sleep and lie still, their fingers resting on the edge of the doorway. The silt-divers, who sleep lightly and wake often, open their eyes in the darkness. And all of them, without knowing they are doing it, listen.

They listen to the sound beneath the sound, the hum beneath the hum, the tempo beneath the tempo. They listen for the thing that the machines are keeping time with. They listen for the Unnamed.

And the Unnamed, whatever they are, keep time. They have always kept time. They will keep time for as long as the city sinks and the thresholds rise, for as long as the flies hatch and die, for as long as the reeds rattle in the chemical wind, for as long as the silt settles and the accumulation grows and the bedrock—that occupied, unreachable bedrock—remains just beyond the longest piling, patient as only the truly ancient can be patient, humming a note that the city above has been harmonizing with for two hundred years without ever once hearing it clearly.
