The maintenance-caste lives here, and they are not entirely what they were.

They are human-shaped because their ancestors were human, but the word *human* has been through too many translations, too many deliberate adjustments. They average two meters tall, elongated, with skin that has adapted—been adapted—to the perpetual amber dimness. Their eyes are large, not for light-gathering but for pattern-recognition in low-contrast environments; they see the difference between one shade of brown-green and another with an acuity that would seem supernatural to their ancestors. Their skin has a faint greenish cast, not from symbiosis but from generations of exposure to the volatile-rich air, which deposits terpenoid compounds in their tissues. They do not photosynthesize, but they are slowly becoming the color of the world.

They live in the spaces between function. A chamber that was once a control-room, now half-filled with a root-buttress that grew through the floor eight hundred years ago and has since become structural. They live in the half of the room that remains, their possessions arranged on the buttress itself, in niches carved or grown into its surface. They do not build. Building would imply a separation between maker and made that no longer exists here. Instead, they *cultivate*—they encourage certain growths, discourage others, redirect, prune, graft. Their tools are not tools in the ancient sense but instruments of suggestion: nutrient-spikes that release specific compounds, electrode-prods that trigger growth-responses, sonic devices that resonate with the gel-pressure in particular root-systems and convince them to thicken here, not there.

They number perhaps eleven thousand, distributed across the continental hump in a loose network of settlements that are not villages but *nodes*—places where the world's density creates a temporary habitable pocket. They move often. The world changes, and they change with it. A node that was stable for fifty years might become uninhabitable in a decade as a new trunk system expands, as a vault-arch collapses upward, as the ceramic sky heals a crack and in doing so seals off a ventilation shaft. They do not mourn the loss of a node. They do not have a concept of *loss* that maps cleanly onto territory. What they have is a concept of *attention*: a node is a place where the world's processes have temporarily aligned in a way that permits human habitation, and when the alignment shifts, they shift with it.

They are organized, loosely, by what they call the *reading*. This is not religion, though it has rituals. It is not science, though it requires expertise. It is the practice of interpreting the world's behavior, of understanding what it is doing and why, of predicting—not controlling, never controlling—what it will do next. The best readers are elders, not because they are wise in an abstract sense but because they have *seen more*. They have watched the same root-system for two hundred years. They know its rhythms, its preferences, its responses. They can look at a patch of fungal-shrub and tell you that a turbine is failing three hundred meters below, that the gel-pressure is building, that a new chamber will open within the year, that it will be habitable for perhaps a decade before the world decides to close it again.

The reading is transmitted through direct apprenticeship, through a lifetime of walking the same routes, touching the same surfaces, learning the specific grammar of a specific region. There is no written language that adequately captures it. They have tried—glyphs carved into bark, data-drives sealed in ceramic capsules, even genetic encoding into symbiotic mosses. But the world changes too fast, too locally. What is true here is not true a hundred kilometers away. What is true now may not be true in ten years. The reading is oral, embodied, performed. It is a way of being in the world, not a body of knowledge about it.

They do not have names in the way their ancestors did. They have *indicators*—a combination of the node where they were born, the elder who trained them, the specific subspecialty of their reading. A person might be called *Third-Node-Trained-By-Vael-Gel-Pressure-Specialist*, but in practice they are addressed by whatever aspect of their reading is currently relevant. In a discussion about a failing turbine, you would call the turbine-reader. In a discussion about a new growth, you would call the growth-reader. Identity is functional, situational, dissolved into the work.

They reproduce slowly, carefully. A child is a significant investment of resources and attention, and the world does not always permit the stability that child-rearing requires. Most nodes support no more than three or four children at a time. The children are raised communally, taught to walk on the mat almost before they can stand on flat ground, taught to recognize the subtle give of a root that is about to thicken, the sound of gel-pressure building in a conduit, the smell of a chamber that is about to seal. They learn to move through the density the way their ancestors might have learned to swim—constantly, automatically, with an awareness that is more bodily than conscious.

They do not have animals, but they have *companions*. These are not pets—they are domesticated plant-machines, or perhaps undomesticated ones that have learned to tolerate human presence. A common one is a drone-hybrid, a machine that was once autonomous but has been overgrown, its chassis now serving as a substrate for a slow-growing moss that glows faintly. The drone still moves, still follows its ancient patrol-routes, but it has been trained—by nutrient-rewards, by gentle redirection—to carry small loads, to scout ahead, to emit a chime when it encounters an obstruction. They are not intelligent, but they are *reliable*, and in a world where reliability is rare, they are valued.

They eat the world. Not all of it—most of the vegetation is structural, load-bearing, or toxic—but certain tissues are edible, or can be made edible through processing. They harvest the tender tips of new growth, the gel-rich nodules that form at root-junctions, the fruiting bodies of the fungal-shrubs. They cultivate small patches of fast-growing, nutrient-dense species in the spaces between larger trunks, training them into shapes that maximize surface area. They do not cook. Cooking requires fire, and fire is unthinkable here—the air is oxygen-rich, the vegetation is dense, a single spark could trigger a cascade that would consume a node in hours. Instead, they ferment, they culture, they use the world's own metabolic heat to slow-process their food. A meal might be a gel-nodule that has been buried in a warm compost-pocket for three days, or a strip of leaf-tissue that has been inoculated with a bacterial culture and allowed to partially digest itself into something more bioavailable.

They do not sleep in the way their ancestors did. The world does not have a day-night cycle—the amber light is constant, the heat is constant, the low groan of growth is constant. Instead, they sleep in shifts, in short cycles, attuned to the rhythms of the world around them. A reader might stay awake for thirty hours to monitor a critical system, then sleep for six, then wake again. They sleep in hammocks woven from fiber-bearing vines, suspended in the spaces between trunks, gently swaying with the world's slow movements. They dream, and their dreams are full of pressure and growth and the sound of the world deciding.

They do not die in the way their ancestors did. Death here is not an event but a *process*, a gradual incorporation. When a body is no longer viable—when the reading fails, when the body can no longer keep up with the world's demands—it is placed in a nutrient-vat, a chamber where the world's decomposers have been cultivated to break down organic matter quickly and cleanly. Within a week, the body is gone, its compounds returned to the gel-stream, its minerals incorporated into new growth. The person's indicator is retired, added to the oral history, recited at the next gathering of readers. They do not grieve in the way their ancestors did. Grief would imply a separation between the person and the world, and that separation is not available here. The person has become the world, and the world continues.

They have art, but it is not art in the ancient sense. They do not make objects. Instead, they *compose*—they redirect growth to create patterns, they tune the resonance of a chamber to produce specific harmonics, they cultivate fungal-shrubs to glow in particular sequences. A master composer might spend fifty years training a single grove to produce a visual pattern that can only be seen from a specific vantage point at a specific time of year (though there are no years here, there are cycles, and the cycles are tracked meticulously). The art is not the object but the *attention*, the sustained focus required to convince the world to become, temporarily, something beautiful.

They have no concept of *nature*. The word does not exist in their language. There is only the world, and the world is what it is, and they are part of it. They do not romanticize it. They do not fear it. They *read* it, and in reading it, they participate in it, and in participating in it, they become it, slowly, over generations, their bodies adapting, their senses sharpening, their minds reshaping themselves to fit the contours of a reality that has no outside.

They are waiting, though they would not use that word. They are waiting for the world to finish deciding what it is. The engineers who maintain the under-vaults—who are not a separate caste but a subspecialty of the reading, the ones who descend into the deepest strata where the root-mats meet and the pressure is highest—they report that the world is still changing, still growing, still *becoming*. The accumulated decision is not yet complete. The world is still deciding whether to be a machine that grows or a plant that computes, whether to be a habitat or an organism, whether to be a ruin or a garden.

The maintenance-caste does not have an opinion on this. They will read the world, whatever it becomes. They will cultivate their nodes, raise their children, compose their groves, incorporate their dead. They will continue, because continuation is what they do, because the world continues, and they are the world's way of paying attention to itself.

You encounter one of them—an elder, a gel-pressure specialist, her skin so deeply greened that she seems to be made of the same stuff as the leaves around her. She is sitting on a root-buttress, her hands resting on its surface, her eyes closed. She is reading. You wait, because interrupting a reading is unthinkable. After a long time, she opens her eyes and looks at you, and her gaze is not unfriendly, but it is *assessing*, the way you might look at a new growth, a potential obstruction, a temporary feature of the landscape.

"You are from the outer nodes," she says. It is not a question. She can tell by the way you walk, by the way you hold yourself, by the fact that you are still trying to *navigate* the world rather than *read* it.

You nod.

"The chamber you are walking toward," she says, "will close in six days. There is another, three kilometers east, that will open in eight. It will be habitable for perhaps a year." She pauses. "You are welcome to stay here until then. The node is stable. The reading is clear."

You thank her, and she nods, and closes her eyes again, and returns to her reading, and you understand—suddenly, completely—that you are not a visitor here, not an observer. You are a temporary feature of the world, and the world is deciding, gently, patiently, whether to incorporate you.

And you will let it.
