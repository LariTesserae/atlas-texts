They move in the interstitial hours—the grey hour when the streetlights flicker and the canals reverse their flow, when the limestone plates sweat condensation thick enough to drip. You find them first in the basement of the old Bessemer foundry, where the cooling channels still hold six inches of oily water warmed by geothermal pipes. They are the size of foxes, or perhaps smaller—perspective shifts in the humid gloom—and they carry themselves with the furtive, low-slung gait of creatures evolved to slip beneath machinery.

They are not quite crabs, though they possess a carapace of layered chitin that glimmers with a iridescent black sheen, like oil on basalt. Not quite rats, though patches of oily, waterproof fur grow in tufts along their spinal ridges and between the joints of their segmented legs. They have become something else entirely: a synthesis of vermin and crustacean, warm-blooded and scaled, their metabolisms running at a perpetual fever that matches the ambient heat. Their blood is hot—hotter than the cobblestones in summer—and when they move, they steam faintly in the cool evening air, exhaling plumes of visible moisture through respiratory vents that line their flanks.

You observe them drinking. They gather at the condensate traps beneath the cooling towers, their mandibles clicking against metal as they lap up the mineral-rich runoff that pools in the catch-basins. Their mouths are complex—triple-jawed, with grinding plates suited for scraping rust from iron and lichen from stone, but also delicate, needle-like proboscides for probing the cracks in the pavement where condensation beads. They have learned to harvest the city's water without needing the sun. They lick the dew from the undersides of bridges, tap the copper pipes with specialized fore-claws to release droplets, and filter the humid air through spiracles that expand and contract like bellows.

In the flooded subway tunnels, the eyed creatures become blind. There, where the water is deep and black and smells of ozone and wet cement, the species has split. A pale variant dominates—larger, slower, their carapaces bleached white or translucent, revealing the network of bio-luminescent vessels beneath. They navigate by the vibration of the tram lines overhead, their antennae flattened into broad, feathery appendages that taste the electrical currents in the air. These are the Deep-Dwellers. They nest in the corroded junction boxes of decommissioned dynamos, weaving their silken, heat-resistant egg-sacs around the copper coils. The eggs pulse with a faint blue glow, visible through the murky water, like drowned stars.

But most remain in the vertical city. They have claimed the attics and the ventilation shafts, the warm spaces behind brick facades where the heat pools. You see their leathery wings unfurling from beneath the eaves at dusk—wings that are not for true flight, but for gliding between rooftops, membranes stretched between elongated limbs, patterned with camouflage that mimics the texture of weathered limestone. They roost upside down in the smokestacks, hanging in clusters that resemble vast, shifting colonies of bats, though their chittering is softer, a dry clicking that sounds like typing, like code being transmitted through the metal bones of the city.

They are social, though their society is opaque. You watch a procession cross the central plaza—a rustling tide of thousands of individuals moving in a wave that parts around the tram tracks and flows over the statue bases. They carry objects: fragments of glass, bones (whose bones?), twisted bits of wire, shells of their own molted exoskeletons. They are building something in the abandoned factories, or perhaps merely sorting the debris of the vanished humans into meaningful categories known only to them. Their nests are intricate, multi-chambered structures that fill the interiors of warehouses, constructed from salvaged metal and a cement-like secretion that they produce from glands at the base of their tails.

Their young are born live, soft and pink and mammalian, though they develop their chitinous armor within weeks. You find the molts everywhere—perfect, ghostly replicas of the adults, clinging to pipes and doorframes, translucent and empty. The creatures eat their own shed skins when food is scarce, but mostly they graze. They have adapted to consume the mineral crust of the city itself. You see them gnawing on the efflorescent salts that bloom on brick walls, scraping the verdigris from copper gutters, digesting the very stone through symbiotic bacteria in their guts that break down silicates and extract trace nutrients. They are the city's gardeners, though their garden is rust and lime.

There are no predators left to control them, save for the occasional ancient dog that has gone feral and turned to hunting the slower individuals, and the cold snap that comes once a decade when the estuary freezes. They have filled every niche. Some are scavengers, cleaning the carcasses of their own dead and whatever else the tide brings in. Others are hunters, taking the pale Deep-Dwellers that venture too close to the surface, or fishing the canals for the blind, albino fish that swim in the flooded basements. A few have become specialized cleaners, moving through the empty tenements in organized bands, devouring the leather and paper and fabric that the humans left behind, converting the last organic material of the old world into their own hot, wet biomass.

At dawn, they retreat. The rustling subsides. The city returns to its mineral silence, the stone cooling slightly under the pale, hazy sun. You walk the quays and find only their traces: the wet trails where bodies have dragged across the dust, the clicking echoes still vibrating in the metal railings, the faint scent of ozone and copper that lingers in the air. They are waiting in the walls, in the sewers, in the bellies of the foundries. They are the current that runs through the grid now, the living static that fills the empty rooms. The city is not abandoned; it is merely inhabited by a new scale of life, one that has learned to drink the moisture from the air and find sustenance in stone, that has traded the green world for the gray and found it sufficient.
