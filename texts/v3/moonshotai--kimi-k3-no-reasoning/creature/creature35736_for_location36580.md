The inhabitants of this place are not merely numerous; they are *fabric*. To stand on the plateau is to be standing on a skin that is also a throat, and the throat is full of voices.

In the perpetual twilight, sight is a secondary sense. The first thing that lives here is sound, and it shapes the bodies that produce it. There are the **stridulators**—crustacean-limbed creatures the size of fists, their carapaces fused with the cured resin of the substrate, rubbing hind-leg against ridged abdomen in frequencies that travel through the honeycomb rather than the air. They live in clusters, barnacle-tight, and their clicking forms a standing wave that stabilizes the temperature of the plates beneath them. When you step near, they do not flee; they *attune*, shifting their rhythm to include your footfall, incorporating the anomaly into the chord.

Deeper in the vegetation move the **chewers**—soft-bodied, segmented grazers with jaws like milling stones. They do not bite; they rotate, grinding the waxy leaves into a paste that they secrete back into the microchannels. Their digestion is slow, communal, and it feeds the substrate directly, returning cured matter to the ancient biological shell. They are blind, navigating by the vibration of their own teeth, and they leave behind a trail of glistening, amber secretion that hardens into new growth medium.

Hanging from the edges, where the plateau meets the thinning air, are the **thread-bearers**. They are difficult to categorize as individual organisms or colonies; perhaps they are both. Each is a rope of translucent tissue, thickened at the root where it plugs into the built structure, tapering to a hair-fine filament that dangles meters into the updrafts. They glow only when touched—a bioluminescent shudder that runs the length of the strand, signaling to others of their kind that the boundary has been breached. They feed on atmospheric moisture and the occasional drifting spore, filtering the high thin air. When the shadow passes overhead, they retract slightly, dimming to near-invisibility, then relax again into their slow, bright droop.

In the pockets of pooled water, where dew has gathered into still, dark lenses, live the **mirror-folk**. They are small, amphibious, and organized in a way that suggests a distributed intelligence. Individually, they are frog-like, with skin that shifts between the amber of the substrate and the deep green of the leaves. But they rarely act alone. They link hands—or the appendages that serve as hands—forming chains that siphon water from one pocket to another, regulating flow, preventing stagnation. They sing, too, a low, vibrating call that resonates in the hollow cavities beneath the plates, maintaining the structural humidity. They are the closest thing this place has to engineers, though their engineering is purely hydraulic and acoustic.

Above all, moving through the dense, intertwined canopy, are the **whistlers**. These are avian only in the loosest sense—membranous, hollow-boned, with wingspans that stretch three meters yet weigh almost nothing. They do not fly so much as float, riding the thermal breath of the plateau. Their calls are long, flute-like notes that bend around the heavy leaves, serving as mapping signals. They are the heralds of the place; their voices are the first to hush when the great shadow passes, and the first to resume, defiant and clear.

And then there is the **shadow itself**. It comes from above, or perhaps from the deeper cavities below—no one has tracked it to a lair. It is vast, winged, but its wings are not membranes; they are more like the trailing edges of the plateau itself, grown mobile. It feeds, if that is the word, on the accumulated static of the chorus, drawing in the noise, the vibration, the excess energy of the living mass. When it passes, the fauna do not hide from predation; they *conserve*, pulling their sounds inward, allowing the shadow to siphon off the pressure that builds in the resonating body. It is a necessary exhalation. When it departs, the surge of renewed noise is not panic, but relief, and reassertion.

Beneath all these, in the honeycombed cavities where the old growth folds down to rot, live the **settling ones**—microscopic, patient, the true architects of decay. They are not seen, but their work is felt in the slow settling of the plates, the smoothing of edges, the way a protruding strut gradually becomes a rounded swell. They process the dead, the molted carapaces, the fallen leaves, transforming them back into the cured, once-living matter of the structure. They are the memory of the place, ensuring that nothing is lost, only translated.

Together, these inhabitants form a single, breathing entity. The plateau does not host them; it *is* them, in the same way a body is its cells. The buzz, the click, the whistle, the chew—all are the sound of a built thing that has learned to live, and in living, has become a world.
