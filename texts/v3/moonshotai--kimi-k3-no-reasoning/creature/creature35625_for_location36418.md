The mountain carries a wound where its roof should be, and at the center of that wound sits a single eye—open, unblinking, wet. What lives here lives inside that eye. Not beside it, not around it, but inside. The pool is not water. It is sorrow made liquid, and the creature in the slick black feathers is not a bird that swims. It is the grief itself, given shape and appetite.

The fish are not fish. They are the mountain’s memories, tarnished silver thoughts swimming in endless circles because they cannot remember the way out. The bird eats them, slowly, one by one, and as it eats, the mountain forgets what it was. When the last fish is gone, the mountain will not die. It will simply stop being anything at all. The bird knows this. It has always known. It paces itself, watches, waits, because there is nothing else to do at the end of everything. The yellow eyes are not cruel. They are patient. They are the eyes of something that was here before the river had a name, and will be here after the name is forgotten.

The succulents are not plants. They are the mountain’s slow attempt to scab over, to close the wound. Dark green and burgundy, thick with stolen moisture, they crawl across the stone in a deliberate, desperate silence. Their roots reach not into soil but into the cracks between what is real and what is not. They drink from the pool not because they are thirsty but because they are trying to dilute the sorrow. They will fail. The pool is too deep, too old, too cold. But they will keep trying, because that is what life does at the rim of the world. It tries, knowing it will fail, because trying is all that remains.

The stone circles are not ruins. They are the fingerprints of the ones who came before, the ones who built the spiral not to reach the pool but to slow themselves down as they approached it. They knew that to stand at the edge of Acheron was to feel the loneliness of the mountain enter your bones like frost. They built the circles to delay that moment, to give themselves time to prepare. It did not help. They are gone, and the circles remain, shifting when no one watches because the mountain is restless and does not like to be reminded of what it lost.

The silence is the loudest thing here. It is the mountain breathing, and it is the sound of time passing without witness. The wind does not blow. It sighs. The water does not lap. It weeps. And the sun, that colorless disc, is not a star. It is a hole in the sky where the warmth escaped, and what is left is only light without comfort, illumination without grace.

Whoever stands here becomes part of the wound. The loneliness does not belong to the mountain alone. It is contagious. It seeps into the lungs with the thin air, and by the time you understand that, it is already too late. You are not visiting the last pool of Acheron. You are being visited by it. The bird watches you now, not because you are prey, but because you are the next memory it will carry. And when it finally takes you, it will not be with violence. It will be with the same slow, patient certainty with which it takes the fish. You will swim in circles. You will forget the way out. And the mountain, in its dying, will be a little less alone.
