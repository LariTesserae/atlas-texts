They live in layers, and the layers have tempers.

At the very bottom, where the floor bowls into its three black lenses and the damp is most reliable, are the **pachyderms of the crust**—the burden-bearers, mastiff-sized and slow as poured tar. Their bodies are broad, low, leather-armored things with too many legs and none in a hurry. They graze on what falls: flakes of skin shed from above, spent exoskeletons, the occasional corpse that has lost its grip and dropped from the heights. They do not look up. They do not need to. What reaches them has already been filtered through five hundred vertical meters of hunger, and what arrives is rich enough. Their hides are studded with a moss of symbiotic glow-fungus, pale mint-green, and when they move—which is rarely, and only to shift weight from one set of legs to another—the light moves with them like swamp-fire drifting. They are the oldest continuous residents. Some of them may be centuries old. No one has checked.

Above them, clinging to the lower walls in thickening bands, the **velvet eoliths** spread themselves like living tablecloths across the stone. They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are *patient*. Each one is a sheet of muscle and cilia perhaps as wide as a man’s outstretched arms, thick as a folded blanket, colored in tones that match the wet rock so perfectly they are visible only when they ripple—and they ripple often, in slow peristaltic waves that draw moisture across their surfaces and into the thousands of tiny mouths that stud their undersides. They cluster thickest around the Fall Spot, where the hair-thin ribbon of water descends, and there they have formed something like a colony, or a single enormous organism, or a religious order—no one can tell. They receive the water with what looks like reverence. They may be farms. They may be priests. They are certainly ancient, and they do not leave.

The **chets** are everywhere in the middle zones. They are small, bat-adjacent, bird-adjacent, neither; they swing from stalactites and ledges on hooked limbs and chitter constantly, a sound like rain on dry leaves multiplied by ten thousand. Their calls carry meaning. Their calls carry *history*—the alarm-notes against two-foot entry are still passed mother to offspring, still sound when a human silhouette crosses the Mouth, even though no human has troubled them in living memory. They are the Hollow’s heralds and its gossips. They glow faintly, cold sapphire, in patterns that shift as they move, and when they swarm—which they do in tight, coordinated bursts—the effect is of a single large creature breaking apart and reassembling. They eat the smaller darting things. They are eaten by almost nothing. They are the noise of the place.

The **sket-runners** live in neat rows along the wet seams. They are finger-length, six-legged, armored in something that looks like wet ceramic and behaves like skin. They move in single file, pecking at microorganisms in the condensation lines, and they maintain their hierarchies with absolute rigor. The lead runner eats first. The second waits. The third waits longer. If a gap opens in the condensation—if the seep shifts, if a stalactite above dries out—the entire line re-forms within hours around the new source, and the hierarchy reasserts itself as though nothing has changed. They are the Hollow’s accountants. They keep the ledgers of moisture.

In the highest vault, where the dome curves inward and the air is driest, live the things too small to name. They are darting, translucent, barely more than organized moisture themselves. They eat what the seep brings down from the rock—minerals, perhaps, or something carried in the water from deeper in the mountain. They glow violet, faintly, inconstantly, and from the floor they look like a second set of stars behind the first.

And then there are the **Ancients**.

The Ancients are not a species. They are not several species. They are a *condition*. Any creature in the Hollow that lives long enough without being eaten, without falling, without drying out, becomes an Ancient. They grow. They slow. They thicken. Their outlines soften as fungal symbionts colonize their hides, as mineral deposits accrete in their folds, as the distinction between body and substrate blurs. An Ancient that was once a pachyderm becomes a shelf. An Ancient that was once a velvet eolith becomes a curtain. An Ancient that was once a chet—if any chet has ever survived long enough—would become something no one has ever seen, because chets do not stop moving until they die.

The Ancients are recognizable only by their stillness. In a chamber where everything trembles, chitters, pulses, the Ancients do not. They sit. They wait. They are mistaken for rock formations until they breathe, which happens perhaps once an hour, and then the mistake is corrected with some urgency. They are not aggressive. They are not *aware*, in any sense that maps onto animal cognition. But they are enormous, and they are *present*, and when the Migration comes, they move.

The **Migration** is the Hollow’s one act of collective will. It happens without warning, without pattern, without any detectable trigger. One day the lower half of the Assemblage simply *decides*—if decision is the word, which it is not—and tears itself free. The sound is indescribable. It begins as a rustle, becomes a roar, becomes a shriek that vibrates in the teeth and makes the nose bleed. Thousands of bodies detach from the wall in a coordinated rush, and the floor—for those few minutes—is a chaos of falling, crawling, flying things. The Ancients rise from their stillness and lumber toward the far wall. The pachyderms trundle. The chets swarm in shrieking spirals. The sket-runners abandon their hierarchies and run *anywhere*. The velvet eoliths peel themselves from the stone like wet bandages and flow across the floor in a manner that should not be possible for creatures of their anatomy.

And then it is over. The far wall explodes with colonization. The abandoned wall gapes, raw, pale, empty—and within days the fungi arrive, the leathery fans in maroon and bone and ashy teal, spreading across the vacancy like a second tide. The fungi are not part of the Assemblage. They are its successor, its undertaker, its heir. They do not compete with the fauna; they simply *wait*, and when the fauna leave, they take what is left. Some of the fans are old—older than any living creature in the Hollow, older than the oldest Ancient. They have been through many Migrations. They remember, in whatever way fungi remember, every abandonment.

The **Grand Assemblage** is the name for all of it together—the crust, the tapestry, the quivering textile of living meat. It is not a superorganism. It is not a colony. It is simply the word for what happens when you look at the wall and cannot see stone. It breathes. It chatters. It glows in patches of mint and sapphire and violet. It is the densest concentration of animal life on Earth, and it is invisible from outside, because outside there is only a dark ridge against a dark sky, and a crack the width of three men, and silence.

Nothing lives at the Mouth. Nothing guards it. Nothing needs to. The Hollow does not advertise itself. It does not defend itself. It simply *is*, and what enters either adapts or leaves or is eaten, and the Assemblage does not notice the difference.
