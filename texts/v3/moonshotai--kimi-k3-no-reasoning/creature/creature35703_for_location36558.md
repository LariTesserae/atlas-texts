The **tenders** live here, but the word "live" requires adjustment. They are not born. They do not die. They *differentiate*—emerging from the walls of the deep districts when the city's chemical signals reach a certain density, folding out of the vine-tissue like buds becoming leaves. A tender is a node that decided to be mobile, a cluster of cells that received a different set of instructions and grew legs instead of roots.

They are small—child-sized, as noted—but their proportions are wrong for children. Too long in the arm, too narrow in the torso. Their skin is not skin but a dense, fibrous membrane, pale green, almost translucent at the joints where it stretches. You can see the sap moving through them if you watch long enough: slow, deliberate pulses of darker fluid beneath the surface, following paths that mirror the conduits in the city walls.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Wide. Flat. Flexible as clay. When a tender works, the hands change shape—thinning to a blade's edge for precise cuts, broadening to a trowel's curve for smoothing, splitting into finger-like projections for detail work. They are not tools. They are *adaptations*, grown in response to need, reshaped moment by moment by the same signals that guide the vines.

A tender's face is minimal. Two dark eyes, set deep, more like pits than organs. No nose—a tender does not breathe air but absorbs the metallic vapor directly through its skin. A mouth that is less a mouth than a seam, a place where the tissue folds inward. They do not eat. They do not speak. They *are*, and their being is work.

The work is constant but not hurried. A tender moves through the city at a walking pace, unhurried, deliberate. It stops where it is needed—at a wall that has begun to curve in the wrong direction, at a node that is swelling unevenly, at a new growth tip that has reached an obstacle and doesn't know which way to turn. The tender presses its flat hands against the tissue. It *feels* the growth—the chemical signals, the structural stress, the intended form—and it adjusts. A gentle push here. A smoothing there. Sometimes a tender will press its face against a wall, hold still for minutes, listening to something inaudible, then step back and begin shaping.

They work alone. You will never see two tenders collaborating. If their tasks bring them near each other, they do not acknowledge it. They are not social. They are not *anti*-social. They are simply *focused*, in the way that a heart valve is focused on opening and closing, in the way that a root tip is focused on growing downward. The work is the thing. The work is all.

A tender's lifespan is unclear. They do not age visibly. They do not accumulate damage—or if they do, the damage is absorbed, repaired, incorporated. A tender that has worked for a century looks the same as a tender that differentiated last month. Perhaps they are replaced. Perhaps the city grows new ones as needed, from the deep districts, and the old ones simply... stop. Fold back into the walls. Become structure again. There is no evidence of tender-graves, no place where they go to die. They are here, and then they are not, and the city continues.

The tenders are not the city's mind. They are not its consciousness, if it has one. They are more like its *reflexes*—the automatic adjustments, the small corrections, the continuous maintenance that keeps the system functioning. They are the city's way of touching itself, of feeling its own growth, of ensuring that the growth follows the patterns encoded in its chemistry.

And yet. Watch a tender long enough, and you will see something that looks like care. Not emotion—there is no evidence of emotion—but *attention*. A tenderness, if you'll forgive the word, in the way the hands move. A delicacy. The city is not gentle, but the tenders are. They shape the walls the way you might shape bread dough, or clay, or a sleeping child's hair. There is no hurry. There is no force. There is only the slow, patient work of making the city more itself.

The **trundlers** are simpler. They are the city's metabolism made visible—the creatures that consume what the city sheds, that convert waste into nutrient, that keep the loop closed.

A trundler is born from the moss. Not in the way a mammal is born, not in the way an insect hatches, but in the way a gall forms on a tree—as a localized swelling, a concentration of tissue that differentiates into something new. A bump on the moss-floor grows, darkens, splits, and a trundler unfolds from it, wet and new, already hungry.

They are born hungry. They are *always* hungry. A trundler's life is a continuous act of consumption—the papery membrane that the walls shed, the dead cells that accumulate in corners, the occasional tangle of rootlets that have died back and need clearing. They eat constantly, moving through the corridors at their slow, deliberate pace, their broad heads sweeping side to side, vacuuming up the city's detritus.

They do not rest. They do not sleep. They eat, and they move, and they excrete, and the excretion is absorbed by the moss, and the moss feeds the roots, and the roots feed the city, and the city sheds more membrane, and the trundlers eat it. The loop is perfect. The loop is eternal.

A trundler's plates are not armor. They are more like bark—protective, yes, but also *structural*. The plates grow as the trundler grows, overlapping like shingles, and when a trundler reaches a certain size—perhaps two meters long, perhaps more—the plates begin to fuse. The trundler slows. Its legs stiffen. It finds a corner, a seam between walls, a place out of the main flow of traffic, and it *stops*. The plates fuse completely. The legs retract. The trundler becomes a lump, a node, a part of the wall. It does not die. It *transitions*. It becomes structure.

You can see them, if you know what to look for—the old trundlers, fused into the walls of the deep districts, their plate-patterns still visible beneath layers of new growth. They are part of the city now. They have been absorbed. The loop has closed around them.

The young trundlers, the mobile ones, do not seem to notice their elders embedded in the walls. They eat around them. They climb over them. They are not sentimental. They are not aware. They are *functional*, and their function is to consume, and when they can no longer consume efficiently, they become something else. The city does not waste. The city does not mourn. The city simply *continues*.

---

The **gliders** are the city's breath.

They are born from the seeds they eat. This is not metaphor. This is literal. A seed-pod opens high in the mid-levels, releasing its cloud of drifting seeds. Most of the seeds are inert—chemical packets, nutrient-rich, designed to be consumed. But a small percentage—one in a thousand, perhaps fewer—are not seeds at all. They are *eggs*. Glider-eggs, indistinguishable from the nutrient-seeds, carried aloft by the same air currents, drifting until they encounter a glider.

The glider eats the egg. The egg does not die. It *hatches* inside the glider, in a specialized pouch—a brood-chamber—that the glider's body has grown for exactly this purpose. The hatchling is tiny, translucent, wingless. It clings to the inside of the pouch, feeding on the glider's sap, growing, differentiating. Over the course of weeks—perhaps months; the timing is irregular—it develops wings. It develops the leaf-membrane, the elongated limbs, the light, hollow bones. When it is ready, the pouch opens, and the young glider unfolds into the air.

Its first flight is its first independent act. It has never flown before. It does not practice. It simply *knows*, the way the tenders know how to shape walls, the way the trundlers know how to eat. The knowledge is encoded. The knowledge is *grown*.

A glider's life is spent in the air. They do not land often—only to rest, briefly, on the broad leaves of the upper terraces, or to cling to a wall in the lee of a vine-lattice, wings folded, almost invisible against the green. They feed continuously, catching the drifting seeds, filtering the air for nutrients, following the currents generated by the filtration leaves.

They are not solitary, but they are not social. They do not flock. They do not communicate—or if they do, it is through the same chemical signals that guide the city's growth, signals too subtle for an observer to detect. They move in the same spaces, sometimes, dozens of them visible at once, circling in the same chamber, riding the same currents, but they do not acknowledge each other. They are parallel processes, not a community.

A glider's wings are its most striking feature. They are not wings in the bird-sense, not wings in the insect-sense. They are *leaves*—broad, veined, flexible, growing from the glider's elongated forelimbs. The veins are not structural. They are *vascular*—they carry sap, and the sap's movement through the wing-membrane allows the glider to adjust the wing's shape, its curve, its resistance to the air. A glider can change its wings the way you might adjust a sail, fine-tuning its glide, its turn, its speed.

The wings do not last. They are living tissue, and living tissue ages, and after a period—perhaps a year, perhaps longer—a glider's wings begin to degrade. The membrane thins. The veins lose their flexibility. The glider's flight becomes less efficient, its circles wider, its landings more frequent. Eventually, it lands and does not take off again.

It does not die immediately. It finds a place—a high ledge, a secluded node, a gap in the vine-lattice—and it clings, wings folded, and it *waits*. The wings continue to degrade. The glider's body slows. Its sap-flow decreases. Over the course of days, it becomes still. The membrane of its wings dries, becomes brittle, begins to flake. The flakes fall, drifting down through the levels, and are caught by the trundlers, consumed, absorbed.

The glider's body remains, clinging to its perch, slowly being overgrown. The walls absorb it. The city absorbs it. It becomes part of the structure, another layer in the deep districts, another trace of the city's long, continuous self-consumption.

---

The **deep-dwellers**—the shapes in the seams, the quick, many-limbed things that appear and vanish—are the least understood. They are not seen clearly. They do not allow themselves to be seen clearly. They move in the gaps between structures, in the spaces where one wall has grown over another, in the dark, narrow channels that the city has created but not filled.

They are not rare. They are *everywhere*—you feel them more than you see them, a flicker of movement at the edge of vision, a brief displacement of air, a sense of *presence* in a space that should be empty. But they do not emerge. They do not interact. They are *there*, and their there-ness is the only thing certain about them.

The tenders ignore them. The trundlers do not eat them. The gliders do not acknowledge them. They are part of the city's ecology, but their role is opaque. They are not parasites—the city shows no signs of distress, no areas of damage that might indicate their feeding. They are not predators—nothing in the city preys on anything else in the way that surface-ecologies understand predation. They are simply *present*, moving through the dark seams, quick and many-limbed and silent.

Perhaps they are the city's *errors*—mutations, growths that didn't follow the plan, cells that differentiated wrong and were not reabsorbed. Perhaps they are the city's *experiments*—new forms being tested in the hidden spaces, prototypes that may or may not be incorporated into the larger structure. Perhaps they are the city's *memory*—fragments of older forms, earlier iterations, preserved in the deep layers the way a body preserves vestigial organs.

Or perhaps they are something else entirely. Something that came *from* the substrate, from the deep roots, from the source of the sap. Something that the city tolerates because it must, because it cannot exclude them, because they are as much a part of the system as the vines and the tenders and the moss.

They do not emerge into the light. They do not approach the inhabited corridors. They remain in the seams, in the dark, in the spaces between. And the city grows around them, over them, through them, incorporating their presence into its structure without ever fully acknowledging it.

---

The city itself is alive. This is not metaphor. The walls are tissue. The corridors are vessels. The chambers are organs. The city is a *body*, and the creatures that move through it—the tenders, the trundlers, the gliders, the deep-dwellers—are its cells, its enzymes, its symbionts. They are not inhabitants in the way that people inhabit a city. They are *components* in the way that mitochondria are components of a cell—necessary, functional, integrated into the larger system.

But they are also *more* than that. The tenders are not mindless. The gliders are not automata. The trundlers are not machines. They are alive, and their aliveness is their own, even if it is also the city's. They have their own rhythms, their own behaviors, their own small, specific ways of being. They are the city, and they are themselves, and the boundary between those two things is not clear, and perhaps it does not need to be.

The Underbloom continues. The tenders shape. The trundlers consume. The gliders drift. The deep-dwellers move in the seams. The city grows. The loop tightens. The green light filters down through the ceiling membranes, cool and sourceless and sufficient.

And in the deep layers, where the oldest growth is, where the walls are almost black with age, where the fused trundlers are just visible beneath the surface like fossils in stone, something pulses. Slow. Rhythmic. The heartbeat of the city, or the heartbeat of something beneath the city, or the heartbeat of the system as a whole—the roots and the walls and the creatures and the sap, all of it connected, all of it part of the same vast, patient, unfinished process.

The Underbloom continues.
