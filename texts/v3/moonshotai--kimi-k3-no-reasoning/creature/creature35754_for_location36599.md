The city holds its breath, and the lungs of that breath are the **Steppe-Mothers**—the great, shaggy beasts whose slow circulation through the lower terraces is the city’s true heartbeat. They are not livestock but **co-constituents**, their massive, horizontal forms the primary architects of the social order. A Steppe-Mother stands perhaps two meters at the shoulder but stretches four meters in length, a dense topography of muscle and insulating fat beneath a coat so thick and matted with lanolin that snow accumulates upon it like sediment, neither melting nor falling away, but carried as a temporary burden until the beast brushes against stone and leaves a smear of white upon the grey. Their heads are low-slung, with broad, shovel-like muzzles adapted for scraping lichen from rock, and their eyes are small, dark, and liquid with a patience that seems to predate the city itself.

The people who move among them—calling themselves the **Kith of the Terraces**, or simply the Kith—are shaped by this same patience. They are compact, dense-boned figures, rarely exceeding a meter and a half in height, with broad chests and short limbs that conserve heat with brutal efficiency. Their skin carries the greyish cast of the stone, weathered to a fine, porous texture like old leather, and their eyes are universally a pale, watery blue or amber, eyes that do not stare but *rest*, taking in light without grasping. They move with a deliberate, gliding step, toes splayed slightly for balance on the narrow stairs, their breath a constant, rhythmic plume of white that they have learned to read as others read words—short puffs for assent, long exhalations for contemplation, the sudden stillness of held breath for mourning or reverence.

Their society is organized around the **Vertical Covenant**, a taxonomy of altitude that maps the spiritual and the practical onto the same gradient. 

At the **Root Levels**, where the city meets the valley floor and the air is thickest, dwell the **Stone-Tenders** and the herds. These are the engineers of the living rock, those who read the striations of the crystalline peaks and know where a terrace may be cut without waking the mountain’s resistance. They work with tools of antler and dense hardwood, their labor slow and percussive—tap, tap, tap—synchronizing their strikes to the subsonic groaning of the growing peaks. They live in low, womb-like structures with walls a meter thick, their interiors lit by the blue-white flame of rendered fat burning in shallow stone bowls. The smoke from these lamps is not wasted; it is channeled through flues into the upper levels, carrying warmth and the scent of roasting meat, a constant reminder that all life here is combustion, slow and controlled.

Ascending, one reaches the **Terraces of Breath**, the residential heart of the city, where families dwell in interconnected cells arranged around central cisterns. Here the Kith practice the **Long Pause**—a form of communal stillness that occurs at irregular intervals, triggered by a collective intuition that the mountain is listening. During these pauses, all movement ceases. The Kith sit or stand exactly where they are, breath shallow, eyes open but unfocused, while the Steppe-Mothers below stop their grazing and lift their heads, and the very air seems to thicken. In these moments, they believe, the mountain speaks—not in words, but in shifts of pressure, in the settling of ice, in the sudden clarity of a distant crack. To interrupt the Long Pause is the only true taboo; it is to speak while the world is thinking.

Above these lie the **Wind Galleries**, though there is no wind, only the thin, cold seep of the high places. Here dwell the **Cloud-Readers**, the eldest and thinnest of the Kith, those whose bodies have acclimatized to the lowest oxygen and the highest silence. They are not rulers but **interpreters**, their role to translate the mountain’s slow intentions into human action. They dwell in open-air platforms carved from the sharpest peaks, their skin toughened by cold to something like stone, their hair long and frozen into ropes of ice that click softly when they move. They do not speak often, having learned that words scatter in the thin air and return distorted; instead, they communicate through a language of sustained tones, humming notes that resonate in the chest cavity rather than the ear, a music that vibrates the stone beneath their feet and travels downward, informing the Stone-Tenders where the next terrace must be cut, where the ice-veins are too fractured to trust, where the mountain dreams of falling.

The Steppe-Mothers have their own parallel hierarchy, one that the Kith have spent centuries learning to read. The herds are led not by the largest or most aggressive, but by the **Silent Ones**—those beasts whose vocalizations fall below the range of human hearing but are felt in the bones as a pressure, a directive. These matriarchs choose the paths, and the Kith build their staircases accordingly, following the routes worn smooth by generations of hooves. The beasts are never slaughtered in the open; when a Steppe-Mother grows old and her milk runs thin, she is led to the **Cleft of Exhalation**, a natural fissure in the mountain where the cold is most intense, and there she lies down, and the Kith gather, and they wait. The beast does not die by blade but by the slow, patient withdrawal of heat, her great body becoming stone-like, her breath slowing until it matches the rhythm of the ice-veins. Then, and only then, do the Kith approach with their antler tools, taking the hide, the fat, the long bones that will become staircases and structural beams, the meat that will be dried into black, nutrient-dense strips. Nothing is wasted; the blood is collected in stone basins and mixed with ash to create a mortar that binds the city together.

The **young** of the Kith—those who have not yet taken their first breath-hold at the Root Levels—are marked by their restlessness. They run when they should walk, speak when they should hum, and their breath comes in quick, hot gusts that the elders watch with a mixture of concern and nostalgia. These children are taught the **Slow Arts**: how to card the coarse hair of the Steppe-Mothers without breaking the fibers, how to chip stone so that the fracture follows the crystal’s natural desire, how to sit motionless for hours until the cold ceases to be an enemy and becomes a texture, a garment. They learn that to hurry is to invite the mountain’s indifference, that speed is a form of arrogance the peaks do not tolerate.

There are **strangers** here, occasionally—**Lowlanders** who climb the impossible gradients seeking trade or refuge. They arrive gasping, their blood too thin, their movements jerky and frantic, and the Kith watch them with a gentle, implacable pity. Most leave within days, unable to bear the silence that is not empty but full, the light that casts no shadows, the sense of being watched by something too slow to see. Those who stay undergo the **Thinning**, a physical transformation as their bodies adapt to the altitude, their lung capacity increasing, their movements slowing, until they too begin to speak in hums and hold their breath with the Long Pause.

In the deepest cellars, where the ice-veins converge, dwell the **Rememberers**—those Kith who have chosen to enter a state of semi-hibernation, their metabolisms slowed to match the mountain’s growth. They lie in niches carved from the living rock, their breathing reduced to one cycle per hour, their bodies cooling until they are barely distinguishable from the stone. They are the library, the archive, the deep memory of the city. When the Cloud-Readers are uncertain, when the mountain’s groaning takes on a new, ambiguous tone, they descend to the Rememberers and press their ears to the cold chests, listening for the slow, tidal rush of blood that carries the history of previous crises, previous negotiations, previous breaths.

And above all, invisible but omnipresent, is the **Mountain Itself**—not as a deity, but as a participant. The Kith do not worship it; they negotiate with it. When a child is born, the afterbirth is buried in a crevice, an offering of warmth to the stone. When a structure is completed, the builders leave a small cavity in the wall, a void where the mountain may insert its own presence. The city is a continuous act of *accommodation*, a dialogue between the rapid, hot biology of the mammals and the slow, crystalline patience of the rock. 

To live here is to accept a different grammar of existence. Past, present, and future are not linear but *stratigraphic*, layered like the terraces. The Kith do not plan for tomorrow; they prepare for the next exhalation. They do not grieve for the dead as lost, but as *settled*, returned to the slow grind of the mineral world. Their greatest art is the **Stillness**, a quality they cultivate until they become, in their final years, almost indistinguishable from the architecture—an old woman sitting on a stone bench, her breath fogging in the grey light, her heartbeat synchronized with the clicking hooves below, her thoughts moving at the speed of ice, not frozen, but flowing with a density that the faster world cannot perceive.

The city holds its breath, and in that holding, it is not stagnant but *pregnant*—a pause between the long, slow inhalation of the mountain’s growth and the inevitable, equally slow exhalation of its decline, centuries hence, when the peaks will have grown too high, the air too thin, and the Kith will simply stop, becoming one with the stone they have tended, their last breaths rising as mist to join the permanent cloud layer, their bodies fossilizing into the striated rock, new veins of ice forming in the marrow of their bones, continuing the slow, patient work of the world.
