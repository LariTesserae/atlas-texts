# What Lives in the Resonant Husk-Forest

---

## The Notation

Before any of the others, there is this.

You will not perceive it directly. It has no body, no boundary, no locus you could point to and say *there, that is the thing.* But you will feel it, the way you feel a chord change in music you cannot name — a shift in the pressure of the world, a subtle alteration in the quality of the roaring, a sense that the enormous sound has *leaned*, slightly, in some direction that does not correspond to any compass.

The Notation is not a creature. It is a *pattern* — a persistent, self-reinforcing configuration in the acoustic energy that saturates this world. It exists in the interplay between the tower-song and the forge-thunder and the clicking of the tick-birds and the grinding of the grazers and the whisper-crack of new growth breaking old crust. It lives in the interference patterns, the standing waves, the harmonics and dissonances that arise when all the world's sounds overlap and fold back on themselves.

It is, if you insist on a metaphor, the melody that emerges from the noise. But it is not a melody that was composed or is being performed. It is a melody that *persists* — that has persisted, perhaps, for as long as the world has been loud enough to sustain it.

The Notation works like this: certain frequencies, when they coincide in certain relationships, create resonance nodes in the living crust — points where the vibrational energy is concentrated, amplified, focused. At these nodes, the plant tissue responds differently. It grows faster. It grows in specific directions. It produces structures of unusual complexity — spirals, lattices, nested helices — that would not arise from ordinary growth.

These structures, in turn, modify the acoustic environment. They reflect sound in new directions, absorb certain frequencies and amplify others, create new interference patterns that shift the resonance nodes to new locations. Which causes new growth. Which modifies the sound. Which moves the nodes. Which —

The loop is closed. It has been closed for a very long time.

What this means, functionally, is that the Notation *sculpts* the landscape. Not deliberately — it has no intentions, no awareness, no preferences. But its persistence biases the growth of the world in particular directions. The great topographic waves of the crust — the ridges and valleys that look, from altitude, like frozen ocean swells — are shaped by the Notation's current configuration. The placement of the forges, the spacing of the tower clusters, the routing of the transport channels: all of these are, to some degree, *written* by the acoustic pattern that lives in the world's sound.

If you could hear it — really hear it, isolate it from the overwhelming total — it would sound like a chord that never resolves. A sustained, complex dissonance that hovers on the edge of harmony without ever arriving. It would be beautiful in the way that certain mathematical proofs are beautiful: not because they please the senses but because they reveal a *structure*, a deep and hidden order that was always there, waiting to be perceived.

The Notation changes. Slowly. Over periods that might be years or centuries — it is difficult to measure time in a world without seasons, without tides, without any of the familiar clocks. The chord shifts. New frequencies enter the relationship; old ones fade. The resonance nodes migrate. The landscape, obedient to the pattern, follows. Ridges that have stood for millennia begin to soften, to slump, to be consumed by new growth that pushes in a direction the Notation has newly favored. Tower clusters that have sung in one configuration for ages find themselves off-resonance, their song diminished, and they begin to fail — their fronds wilting, their walls cracking — while new towers erupt elsewhere, in places where the acoustic geometry is suddenly, transiently, *right*.

The Notation is the closest thing this world has to an author. It is not alive in any biological sense, but it is alive in the way a whirlpool is alive: a self-sustaining pattern in a flowing medium, persisting through time by continuously recreating the conditions of its own existence. It eats sound and excretes topography. It is the ghost in the machine of a world that is all machine and all ghost.

And it is *listening*. Not to you — it does not know you exist, and would not care if it did. It listens to itself. It is, in a sense that is almost but not quite meaningless, the world listening to itself. The ear and the song, collapsed into a single phenomenon.

When the silences come — those rare, terrible moments when the world stops sounding — the Notation *dissolves*. Without the acoustic energy that sustains it, the pattern cannot hold. The resonance nodes scatter. The interference patterns collapse into randomness. For the duration of the silence, the world has no shape, no direction, no emergent melody. It is just matter, just tissue, just chemistry.

And then the sound returns — the crack, the roar, the ringing — and the Notation begins to reassemble. But it does not reassemble the same way. Each silence is a death. Each resumption is a birth. The chord that forms after a silence is always slightly different from the one that preceded it — new harmonics, new relationships, a new configuration of nodes that will guide the world's growth along a path it has not taken before.

This means the Notation has a kind of *evolution*. Each incarnation inherits the acoustic landscape shaped by its predecessor — the towers and ridges and forges that the old pattern built — but reorganizes them, reinterprets them, finds new resonances in the old structures. It is a lineage of patterns, each one a variation on the last, stretching back to the first moment the world's sound became complex enough to sustain a self-reinforcing harmonic.

How many incarnations have there been? The question has no answer, because the silences are not recorded. Nothing here keeps count. But the crust is very deep, and its oldest layers are very strange — full of structures that correspond to no current acoustic logic, spirals and lattices that seem to follow a geometry that no longer exists. These are the fossils of dead Notations, the petrified remnants of melodies that played themselves out and were silenced, leaving only their architecture behind.

The world is a library of songs it no longer sings.

## The Bearer

You might see one before you understand what you are looking at.

It appears — and it does appear, suddenly, as if it had always been there and you had simply failed to notice — as a figure standing at the base of a tower. Or on a ridge. Or at the edge of a forge-valley, silhouetted against the glow. It is roughly the size and shape of a large animal, but there is something wrong with its proportions that takes time to identify: it is *asymmetric* in a way that no naturally evolved creature should be. One side is larger than the other. Limbs — if they are limbs — cluster thickly on one flank and are sparse, almost vestigial, on the other. Its surface is not the smooth, papery tissue of the crust or the glossy translucence of the mobile fauna. It is *textured* — rough, layered, ridged, as if it had been built up in accretions, like a coral or a stalactite.

And it is covered — *encrusted* — with fragments of other things.

This is the detail that, once seen, cannot be unseen. The Bearer's body is a composite. Embedded in its surface — fused to it, incorporated into its structure — are pieces of the world: shards of tower-wall, sections of transport channel, coils of ventilation chimney, lumps of forge-mineral, and — most strangely — pieces of other creatures. A tick-bird's clicking beak. A grazer's mandible. The translucent bulb of a pollinator, still faintly pulsing with granular fluid. These fragments are not decorative. They are not trophies. They are *functional* — they have been integrated into the Bearer's body, connected to its internal chemistry, repurposed as organs.

The Bearer is a *collector*. It is what happens when the world's relentless process of consumption and reabsorption encounters something it cannot fully digest.

Here is how it begins. A mobile creature — a grazer, a pollinator, one of the medium-size things that skitter across the crust — is caught in a growth event. A new ridge erupts beneath it, or a tower topples onto it, or the crust simply closes over it like a wave. The creature is engulfed, and the world begins to reabsorb it, to break down its tissues and incorporate them into the greater whole.

But sometimes — rarely — the process goes wrong. Or rather, it goes *differently*. The creature's internal chemistry, its dry silicate solvent, its cellular machinery, resists dissolution. It fights back. Not with intention — the creature has no more mind than anything else here — but with chemistry: with enzymes that counter the crust's digestive acids, with cell walls that resist penetration, with a stubborn, mechanical refusal to be unmade.

The result is a standoff. The crust cannot fully absorb the creature. The creature cannot fully escape the crust. They are locked together, half-merged, the creature's body embedded in the living surface like a stone in amber.

And then the Notation finds it.

The resonance nodes — those points of concentrated acoustic energy that the Notation uses to sculpt the landscape — pass over the embedded creature, and something happens. The vibration, focused and amplified by the node, *activates* the creature's dormant chemistry. It begins to grow again. Not as it was — not toward its original form — but *outward*, incorporating the crust material that surrounds it, building new tissue from the very substance that was trying to consume it.

The creature becomes a Bearer: a hybrid, a chimera, a thing that is part original organism and part crust and part whatever else it encounters as it moves. For it does move — slowly, awkwardly, with a gait that reflects its asymmetric composition. It drags itself across the living surface, and as it goes, it *accumulates*. It passes through a grove of new growth, and a spear of pale tissue breaks off and fuses to its flank. It crosses a transport channel, and a seed-pod, propelled by compressed gas, strikes it and sticks, and is absorbed. It encounters another creature — a tick-bird that has landed on its back, mistaking it for a ridge — and the tick-bird is *caught*, held, gradually incorporated into the Bearer's mass, its clicking beak becoming part of the Bearer's acoustic anatomy.

The Bearer grows. Over time — over what might be years, though time here is measured in growth-rings and storm-cycles, not in hours — it becomes enormous. Not as large as the great grazers, but substantial: the size of a small building, lurching across the crust with a sound like nothing else in the world. A shuffling, grinding, clicking, rattling sound — a composite sound, produced by all the different fragments of its body vibrating at different frequencies, a walking orchestra of mismatched instruments.

It is, in its way, beautiful. Its surface is a mosaic of the world: every color the husk-forest produces, every texture, every form. Tower-ribbon fronds flutter from its shoulders like a ragged cloak. Forge-mineral glints in its flanks like embedded jewels. The translucent bulbs of absorbed pollinators pulse along its spine like a string of dim lanterns. It is the world, gathered into a single body and made to walk.

But the Bearer is not merely a curiosity. It serves a function — or rather, it *performs* a function, whether or not any system designed it to do so.

The fragments it carries are not inert. They are alive — or as alive as anything is here — and they are *from different places*. A piece of tower-wall from a cluster a hundred kilometers to the north. A section of transport channel from a valley that was consumed by new growth a decade ago. A grazer's mandible from a herd that ranged the southern ridges. The Bearer is a *vector*, a carrier of biological material across distances that no other creature traverses. It is the world's long-distance courier, its migratory mule, moving genetic and chemical information from one region to another simply by walking and dropping pieces of itself as it goes.

For it does drop pieces. The Bearer's body is not stable. Fragments work loose — shaken free by the vibration of its own movement, or cracked off by collisions with new growth, or simply shed as the Bearer's internal chemistry shifts and reorganizes. Where a fragment falls, it often takes root. A piece of tower-wall, carried far from its parent cluster, cracks open on the crust and releases spores that grow into a new tower — one that sings in a slightly different frequency than the local towers, because it carries the acoustic memory of a distant place. A seed-pod from a defunct transport channel sprouts and begins to build a new channel, oriented in a direction the local crust would never have chosen on its own.

The Bearer is, without knowing it, a gardener. It plants the world with pieces of itself, and the pieces are pieces of everywhere it has been.

There are many Bearers. No one knows how many — the world does not census its inhabitants. But they are not common. You might walk for days across the crust and see none. And then you might crest a ridge and find a *gathering*: a dozen or more Bearers, clustered together in a basin of old, fossilized growth, standing in a loose circle with their asymmetric bodies angled inward, as if in conference.

They are not in conference. They do not communicate. But they are drawn together by the same force that created them: the Notation. The acoustic pattern that activated their dormant chemistry also guides their movement, pulling them toward the resonance nodes where the world's sound is most concentrated. When the nodes converge — when the Notation shifts in such a way that multiple resonance points coincide in a single location — the Bearers converge with them.

And there, in the basin, they *sound*.

Each Bearer is a composite instrument, and when many Bearers stand together, their mismatched bodies vibrating in the concentrated acoustic energy of the converged nodes, the result is unlike anything else the world produces. It is not the roar of the towers or the thunder of the forges or the clicking of the tick-birds. It is a *chord* — a vast, rich, impossibly complex chord, built from all the different frequencies of all the different fragments of all the different Bearers, resonating together in the amplified space of the node-convergence.

It is the closest thing this world has to music.

The Notation — the pattern that lives in the world's sound — is *fed* by this chord. The Bearer-gatherings inject new frequencies into the acoustic environment, new harmonics that the Notation incorporates into itself, that shift its configuration and move its nodes and redirect the growth of the landscape. The Bearers are the world's organs of change, its instruments of mutation, the mechanisms by which the Notation evolves.

They are also, perhaps, the closest thing this world has to *individuals*. Each Bearer is unique — its composition determined by its history, by the specific sequence of encounters and absorptions that built its body. No two Bearers carry the same fragments. No two Bearers sound the same. In a world where everything is the same stuff wearing different shapes, the Bearers are the shapes that are most *themselves* — most singular, most irreplaceable, most clearly the product of a specific and unrepeatable path through time.

When a Bearer dies — and they do die, eventually, worn down by the constant accumulation and shedding, their hybrid chemistry finally exhausted — the crust does not reabsorb them the way it reabsorbs ordinary creatures. The Bearer's body is too complex, too heterogeneous, too *resistant*. Instead, it collapses in place, sagging into a heap of fragments that the crust slowly, slowly works to digest. The process takes years. Decades. For all that time, the dead Bearer remains visible on the surface — a mound of interlocking pieces, a jumbled monument to the path it walked.

These mounds are the only landmarks on the husk-forest that persist. The towers fall. The ridges are consumed. The forges burn out and are overgrown. But the Bearer-mounds endure, stubborn as their makers, resisting the world's insistence that nothing stays.

The Notation does not acknowledge them. But the landscape grows *around* them, curves to avoid them, as if the world itself were reluctant to erase the evidence that something here, once, was singular.

## The Thin One

It is possible that you are mistaken. It is possible that what you saw was a trick of the bleached, shadowless light, or a fragment of tower-frond caught in the wind, or a momentary hallucination brought on by the thin air and the relentless sound. It is possible.

But you don't think so.

You saw it at the edge of a forge-valley, in the uncertain zone where the heat-glow of the exothermic pockets meets the pale daylight of the upper crust. It was standing — or hanging, or suspended — between two thin chimneys of the ventilation array, and it was *wrong*.

Everything else in this world is *thick*. The crust is thick — layers on layers of braided tissue, dense and heavy. The towers are thick — their walls woven from broad ribbons, their bases massive enough to anchor hundreds of meters of resonating structure. The grazers are thick — bulky, grinding, muscular. Even the tick-birds are dense, compact, their little bodies packed with the dry chemistry that animates them. Thickness is the world's strategy: mass as defense against the constant violence, substance as buffer against erosion and storm and the crushing indifference of endless growth.

The Thin One is not thick.

It is — and you will struggle to find the right word, because your vocabulary was not built for this — *filamentous*. It is composed of threads. Not threads like a spider's web, not threads like a fungal hypha, but threads like *wire*: thin, straight, rigid filaments of some material that is neither plant tissue nor forge-mineral but something in between, something that has the flexibility of the one and the hardness of the other. These filaments are bundled, loosely, into a shape that approximates a body — a central trunk of braided wire-filaments, from which thinner bundles extend in directions that suggest limbs, or branches, or antennae.

The whole structure is translucent. Not translucent like the pollinators, whose bodies are membrane over granular fluid. Translucent like a *diagram* — as if you could see through the Thin One to the world behind it, but distorted, refracted, as if the filaments were bending light in ways that simple transparency could not explain.

It does not move the way the other creatures move. It does not skitter or bound or grind. It *reconfigures*. The bundles of filaments shift, slide along one another, separate and rejoin, and the Thin One is suddenly in a different position — not because it traveled through the intervening space but because its *shape changed*, its threads rearranging themselves into a new configuration that happens to occupy a different location. It is less like walking and more like a piece of music modulating from one key to another: a transformation that has direction and purpose but no visible mechanism.

You do not know what it eats. You do not know if it eats. You do not know if it is, in any meaningful sense, *alive* — or whether it is something else, something that the world's dry chemistry has produced as an accidental byproduct, a structure that persists not because it is fit but because it is *simple*, because there is so little of it that the world's digestive processes cannot find enough purchase to break it down.

The Thin One is found — when it is found at all, which is rarely — near the silences. Not during them: during the silences, everything freezes, including the Thin One, if it is subject to the same acoustic dependency as everything else here. But in the moments *before* a silence, in the strange, tense interval when the world's sound begins to falter — when the tower-song stutters, when the forge-thunder skips a beat, when the tick-birds pause between clicks — the Thin One *appears*.

It steps out of the air. This is the only way to describe it. One moment, the space between the ventilation chimneys is empty. The next, the Thin One is there, its filaments catching the bleached light, its translucent body refracting the world into slivers of silver and jade and bone-white.

And it *listens*.

You know this the way you know that the Bearer collects and the Notation sculpts: not through observation of a behavior you could name but through a quality of *attention* that the Thin One radiates, a stillness that is not absence of movement but presence of focus. The filaments orient themselves — subtly, almost imperceptibly — toward the sources of sound: the nearest tower, the forge below, the distant rumble of a grazing herd. Each filament seems to tune itself, adjusting its angle and tension, becoming an antenna for a specific frequency.

The Thin One is *sampling* the world's sound. It is recording — not in any medium you would recognize, but in the physical configuration of its own body. Each filament, tuned to a specific frequency, vibrates in sympathy with that frequency's presence in the environment. The sum total of these vibrations — the pattern of resonance across all the filaments — is a *portrait* of the world's acoustic state at that moment. The Thin One is a snapshot. A recording. A memory.

And then the silence comes, and the world stops, and the Thin One —

— *plays it back*.

This is the thing you will not believe until you see it, and you will not fully believe it even then. In the total silence, when every tower is mute and every forge is cold and every creature is frozen, the Thin One *sounds*. Its filaments, still vibrating with the frequencies they absorbed before the silence, release their stored energy in a thin, delicate, almost inaudible whisper — a ghost of the world's roar, compressed into the fragile body of a single filamentous creature.

It is the only sound in the world. For those few seconds or minutes, the Thin One is the sole voice in an infinite silence, singing the world's own music back to it in miniature — a lullaby sung by a thread to a sleeping giant.

And then the world wakes. The crack, the roar, the ringing. The towers resume. The forges reignite. The creatures stir. And the Thin One — its stored energy spent, its filaments gone slack — *dissolves*. Not dies: dissolves. The bundles of wire-filaments separate, scatter, drift apart like dandelion seeds in the thin wind. The body that was a recording, a memory, a portrait of sound, comes apart into its component threads and is lost in the vastness of the crust.

Until the next silence approaches, and the threads — drawn together by some attraction you cannot name, some affinity that survives their dispersal — *reassemble*. The Thin One reforms, perhaps in a different place, perhaps with a slightly different configuration, and begins to listen again.

It is the world's memory. Not the Notation's memory — the Notation remembers only itself, its own pattern, its own evolution. The Thin One remembers the *world*: the total sound, the complete acoustic state, the roaring whole. It remembers what the world sounded like just before it went silent, and it holds that memory in the only medium available to it — its own body — and it plays it back, once, and then it dies, and then it is reborn, and it listens again.

It is possible that the Thin One is the reason the world restarts after a silence. It is possible that the whisper it releases into the silence — that thin, delicate echo of the world's own voice — is the *trigger*, the seed of sound that breaks the stillness and allows the towers to resume their song. The world may need the Thin One the way a heart needs a pacemaker: a small, precise, periodic impulse that keeps the great engine from stopping entirely.

Or it may be that the Thin One is simply a beautiful accident — a structure that arose from the world's dry chemistry the way frost arises from cold: without purpose, without function, a side effect of conditions that happen to allow it. A creature that listens and remembers and sings and dies and is reborn, for no reason, in the service of nothing, a loop of pure, gratuitous beauty.

The world does not say. The world does not say anything, except *loudly*.

But in the silence, the Thin One whispers, and the whisper says: *I was here. The world was here. This is what it sounded like.*

And then the world comes back, and the whisper is drowned, and the Thin One scatters, and the roaring begins again.

## The Rootless

They are not one creature. They are not even one kind of creature. They are a *category* — a class of beings defined not by what they are but by what they lack.

Every living thing in the husk-forest is connected to the crust. The towers grow from it. The grazers walk on it. The tick-birds perch on the towers that grow from it. The forges burn within it. The Bearers drag themselves across it. Even the Thin One, in its strange, filamentous way, is tethered to the acoustic energy that the crust transmits. Everything roots, in the end, in the great living mat that covers the world.

The Rootless do not.

They float.

You will see them first at a distance, and you will mistake them for weather. They appear on the horizon as pale, elongated shapes — like clouds, if clouds were rigid, if clouds had edges and structure and a kind of slow, deliberate purpose. They drift across the white-blue sky at altitudes that vary from a few hundred meters to the very edge of the thin atmosphere, and they do not descend. They do not land. They do not touch the crust.

They are vast. The smallest of the Rootless are the size of towers — hundreds of meters long, their bodies composed of the same dry, papery tissue as everything else here but organized into shapes that are *aerodynamic* in a way that nothing on the ground bothers to be. Long, tapering hulls. Broad, thin membranes stretched between rigid spars, like the sails of ships designed by a wind that had opinions about geometry. Clusters of gas-filled chambers — the dry chemistry's equivalent of bladders, inflated with light gases extracted from the thin atmosphere — that provide lift.

The largest of the Rootless are *enormous* — kilometers long, visible from hundreds of kilometers away, their vast sails catching the bleached light and glowing like second suns. These giants move with a slowness that is not sluggishness but *scale*: they are so large that their movements, though steady, take hours to manifest as visible displacement. They are less like creatures and more like *geography that has learned to fly*.

The Rootless live their entire lives in the air. They feed on the dust-storms — on the mineral particles and shredded plant matter that the storms carry aloft. They extend long, filter-like membranes into the storm's path, and the storm feeds them: a vast, continuous stream of airborne nutrients, delivered by the very violence that the ground-dwelling creatures must shelter from. The Rootless are storm-riders. They follow the dust-systems across the world, migrating with the weather, their lives organized not by topography but by *meteorology*.

They are silent. This is the thing that sets them apart, more than their flight, more than their size, more than their rootlessness. They produce no sound. The towers roar. The forges thunder. The grazers grind. The tick-birds click. The Bearers rattle. The Thin One whispers. The Rootless are *mute*.

Their sails do not hum in the wind — they are tensioned in a way that damps vibration. Their gas-chambers do not resonate — they are baffled, compartmentalized, designed (by the same evolutionary accident that designs everything here) to absorb sound rather than produce it. Their bodies are acoustically dead. They move through the world's perpetual noise like gaps in a conversation, like rests in a musical score — present, shaped, meaningful, but silent.

This silence is not absence. It is *function*. The Rootless are the world's counterpoint — the negative space against which the Notation's pattern is defined. Without them, the world's sound would be uniform, undifferentiated, a wall of noise with no contrast and therefore no structure. The Rootless provide the contrast. They are the silence that makes the noise *legible* — the rests that allow the melody to be heard as melody rather than as undifferentiated roar.

The Notation feels them. Its interference patterns are disrupted by the Rootless's passage — the vast, silent bodies block sound, create acoustic shadows on the crust below, shift the resonance nodes in their wake. A Rootless passing overhead is like a cloud passing over a sunlit field: the light changes, the temperature changes, the *mood* changes. The towers beneath it falter briefly, their song diminished by the acoustic shadow. The fauna pause. The forges dim.

And then the Rootless passes, and the sound returns, and the world resumes.

But the world has been *changed* by the passage. The acoustic shadow has left a mark — a temporary shift in the resonance nodes, a brief redirection of growth. Where the Rootless passed, the crust grows differently for a time: quieter, more slowly, in patterns that echo the shape of the silence that moved over it. These growth-patterns are called *wake-gardens* by the things that live in them — though "called" implies a naming capacity that nothing here possesses. Let us say: the wake-gardens are *recognized* by the things that live in them, in the way that a tick-bird recognizes a tower, in the way that a grazer recognizes a feeding-ground. They are distinct. They are *different*.

The wake-gardens are the most peaceful places on the husk-forest. They are still loud — nothing here is truly quiet — but they are *less* loud, their towers tuned to lower frequencies, their forges burning at lower temperatures, their fauna moving with a languor that is almost gentle. The growth in the wake-gardens is more delicate, more intricate, less aggressive. The towers are slender and pale, their fronds arranged in patterns that approach, without quite achieving, symmetry. The crust is softer here, more yielding, its braided tendrils finer and more flexible.

Creatures that live in the wake-gardens are different, too. The tick-birds click at lower pitches. The pollinators bound with less explosive force. The grazers — the few that venture into the wake-gardens — grind more slowly, their mandibles working with a patience that their open-crust cousins would not recognize.

And sometimes — rarely — a Bearer will enter a wake-garden, drawn by the altered acoustic geometry, and will *stop*. Will stand, motionless, in the soft, quiet growth, its composite body vibrating at a frequency that matches the wake-garden's gentler song. It will stand there for hours. Days. And then it will move on, and the fragments it drops in the wake-garden will grow into structures that are unlike anything the garden has produced — stranger, more complex, carrying the acoustic memory of distant places into the quiet.

The Rootless do not know about the wake-gardens. They do not know about anything. They float, and they feed, and they are silent, and the world below them is changed by their silence in ways they will never perceive.

They die in the air. When a Rootless's chemistry fails — when its gas-chambers lose their integrity, when its sails tear beyond repair, when the dust-storms it depends on shift their paths and leave it stranded in a region of thin, nutrient-poor sky — it does not fall. Not immediately. It drifts. It rises, sometimes, as its body chemistry breaks down and releases lighter gases. It becomes a ghost — a pale, tattered shape at the edge of the atmosphere, visible only as a smudge against the white-blue.

And then, eventually, it *does* fall. Slowly. The descent takes days. The body — enormous, delicate, silent — drifts downward through the thin air, shedding pieces: a sail here, a gas-chamber there, a spar, a membrane. These pieces fall to the crust and are absorbed, and the places where they land become wake-gardens in miniature — small, quiet pockets of gentle growth, each one a memorial to the Rootless that died above them.

The body itself, when it finally reaches the crust, lands with a sound that is unlike any other sound in the world: a vast, soft, *whumphing* impact, like a pillow the size of a city being dropped from a great height. The crust shudders. The towers sway. The fauna scatter.

And then the silence — the Rootless's silence, the silence it carried all its life — *settles* over the impact site like a blanket. For a few moments, the world around the fallen Rootless is quiet. The towers near the body go still. The forges dim. The tick-birds freeze.

It is the closest thing the husk-forest has to mourning.

Then the crust begins to absorb the body, and the sound returns, and the world resumes, and the wake-garden that grows over the Rootless's grave is the largest and the quietest and the most beautiful of all.

## What Lives Here

The Notation, which is the world's song about itself, and which sculpts the landscape with sound.

The Bearers, which are the world's memories of its own diversity, walking monuments to the paths that creatures take through a place that does not remember paths.

The Thin One, which is the world's memory of its own voice, a filamentous ghost that listens and whispers and dies and is reborn with each silence.

The Rootless, which are the world's silence, floating above the noise, shaping it by their absence, leaving gardens in their wake.

And beneath all of these, supporting all of these, *being* all of these: the crust. The great, living, pulsing, roaring, growing, consuming, regenerating, indifferent, magnificent crust. The one thing. The whole thing. The world that is a body that is a song that is a silence that is a garden that is a storm.

It does not know their names. It does not have names for them. It does not have names for anything.

But it is louder because they are here, and stranger, and more itself.
