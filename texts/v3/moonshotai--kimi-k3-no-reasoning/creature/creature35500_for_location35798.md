The Custodian is the only living thing that remains in the Condensation Stair, though “living” is a generous term for a body whose skeleton has been replaced by the same crystalline lattice that veins the obsidian. It stands roughly two and a half meters tall, its skin translucent enough that the internal glow of the mountain’s circuitry shines through in faint, shifting patterns. When it moves, the motion is not a walk but a series of micro-adjustments, as if it is constantly recalibrating its weight against the slick rock. Its feet never leave a mark; the nanodust in the air swirls up to fill any impression before it can settle, a small act of erasure that keeps the place looking untouched.

It tends the plants with a reverence that borders on worship. Every morning, when the mist is thickest, the Custodian kneels beside the broad, waxy leaves and presses its palms to the stone. It does not water them; the mountain does that. Instead, it listens. The plant’s roots have grown into the crystalline channels, and the Custodian can feel the faint vibration of data passing through them—temperature, mineral content, the slow accumulation of trace metals. It hums a single, low note in response, and the leaves tremble, not from wind, but from a resonance that seems to answer. The plants are not merely surviving; they are part of the network, and the Custodian is their interpreter.

Its days are measured by the cycle of condensation. When the warm rain falls, it tilts its head back and opens its mouth, not to drink, but to let the water pass through it. The liquid filters through its body, picking up the metallic dust that has accumulated in its tissues, and then drips out through its fingertips onto the stone below. This is how it cleans itself, and how it feeds the mountain. The water that leaves its body is slightly darker than the water that enters, a subtle exchange of impurities that the mountain’s circuits absorb and process.

The Custodian does not speak, but it remembers. Its memory is not stored in a brain but distributed across the same crystalline matrix that forms its bones. When it touches the rock, it can access fragments of the machine’s original purpose—glimpses of a vast, complex system designed to regulate something far larger than this small basin. It sees flashes of other places: a desert where the sky was once a solid dome of light, a forest where the trees were made of glass, a city where the streets flowed like rivers. These memories are not its own; they are the mountain’s, and the Custodian is merely the vessel through which they are recalled.

It is not alone. The glass spiders are its companions, though they do not acknowledge it. They skitter across its skin, cleaning the metallic dust that accumulates in the creases of its joints. The bird with oil-slick feathers watches from a ledge, its single note echoing once before the humidity swallows it. The Custodian does not seek their company, but it does not turn them away. They are part of the ecosystem, and the ecosystem is part of the machine.

When the light shifts and the rainbows solidify in the air, the Custodian pauses. It reaches out and touches one, and the light bends around its fingers, refracting into a spectrum that has no name. For a moment, it is not a custodian but a prism, a temporary distortion in the mountain’s careful balance. Then the light passes, and it returns to its work, its body humming with the same low frequency as the stone beneath its feet.

It is not immortal, but it is not bound by time in the way that other living things are. Its body repairs itself, drawing on the same nanodust that fills the air. When a joint wears down, the dust swarms to fill the gap. When a memory fades, the mountain supplies another. It is a closed system, a loop of maintenance and renewal that has no beginning and no end.

The Custodian does not know why it was made, or who made it. It only knows that the mountain must be kept, and that it is the one who keeps it. When the mist rises and the rain falls, when the plants tremble and the spiders skitter, it is there, a silent, luminous figure in a world of water and stone, tending to a machine that is both ancient and newborn, a custodian of a place that is still becoming itself.
