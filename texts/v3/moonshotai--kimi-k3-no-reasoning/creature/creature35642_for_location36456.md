They were a culture that treated friction like a toxin. To live in the Arteries was to accept that nothing was ever finished, and so the inhabitants—descended from the first survey corps and the tectonic engineers who refused to abandon the crustal stations—had organized their entire existence around the maintenance of the immediate. They were called Line-Dwellers, though they rarely used the term for themselves; they simply called themselves *Kith*, a word derived from the low-frequency hum of the grid that they felt in their teeth and their bones. They were not explorers of the void or farmers of the earth; they were custodians of the pause between heartbeats.

Physically, they had been shaped by the thin air and the pervasive, bone-deep vibration. Generations of living at altitude had given them broad chests and oxygen-saturated blood that was nearly as dark as the iron-rich stone they walked upon. Their skin had a pale, waxy quality, etched with fine, silvery lines—micro-scars from the static discharges that frequently arced between the city-grid and the atmosphere. These marks were considered beautiful, a record of survival and proximity to the raw power of the planet. They wore clothing that was less about protection and more about grounding: heavy, insulated suits woven with copper-threaded mesh that helped bleed off excess electrical charge. To touch a Line-Dweller without this mesh was to receive a sharp, stinging shock, a literal manifestation of their isolation.

Their settlements were not towns, but *Nests*. Because the ground was inherently unstable, they did not build upward; they built inward and outward, sprawling across the flat, shielded shelves of the plateau where the tectonic sensors had stabilized the rock. Their architecture mimicked the nervous system of the city: modular, interconnected pods made of the same self-repairing ceramic as the main lines. A Nest was a cluster of living quarters, hydroponic bays, and communal heat-sinks, all tethered to the central transit conduits by thick, insulated cables. If a seismic event shifted the shelf, the Nest would simply sway, its internal dampening systems compensating for the movement, like a bird’s nest rocking in a gale.

Socially, they were defined by the *Duty-Cycle*. Time was not measured by the sun, which was often obscured by mist or the violet haze of the upper atmosphere, but by the rhythmic pulsing of the energy-dampening fields. There were no days, only *Intervals*. During a "Deep Interval," the grid throttled down to a low, resonant hum, and the inhabitants retreated to their pods to sleep in the warmth of the thermal vents. During a "High Interval," the city hummed at a higher pitch, signaling a period of intense maintenance. Every member of the Kith was a technician, a monitor, or a caretaker. There were no children in the traditional sense; there were only Apprentices, who began their training at seven, learning to feel the vibrations of the crust through the soles of their boots before they were taught to read or write.

Their relationship with the land was one of profound, cautious symbiosis. They viewed the emerging mountain not as a resource, but as a temperamental god. They practiced a form of "Geologic Stewardship," where they manually cleared mineral build-up from the vent-sensors and checked the integrity of the carbon-lattice bridges that spanned the growing canyons. They did not tame the thermal-furred grazers, but they managed them. The herds were seen as biological sensors; if the grazers grew restless or migrated toward the higher ridges, the Kith knew that the magnetic levitation tracks were experiencing harmonic interference. They followed the herds, living in a slow-motion migration that mirrored the shifting of the tectonic plates.

The most striking aspect of their existence was their relationship with sound. In the Arteries, silence was dangerous—it meant a break in the line, a loss of power, a failure of the grid. Consequently, the Kith lived in a state of constant, low-level auditory awareness. Their speech was clipped, rhythmic, and often punctuated by the physical act of tapping—tapping on pipes, on stone, on each other’s shoulders—to confirm that the vibration was still present, that the planet was still being held together. Their music was not melodic; it was harmonic, a series of low-frequency drones played on instruments that resembled large, tensioned wires. To an outsider, it sounded like the wind or the hum of a transformer. To the Kith, it was a conversation with the mountain, a way of saying: *We are here. We are holding. Do not shake.*

They were a people who lived with the knowledge that their home was being built beneath them, and that at any moment, the slow-motion violence of the planet’s growth could render them obsolete. Yet, they did not live in fear. They lived in a state of intense, hyper-aware presence. They were the stewards of the transient, the keepers of the cold, and the only ones who truly understood that in a world of rising stone and glowing wire, the only thing that mattered was the integrity of the connection.
