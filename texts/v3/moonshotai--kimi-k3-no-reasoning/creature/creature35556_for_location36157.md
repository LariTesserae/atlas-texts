They are born from the cracking.

When the ground decides to split—when the thermal swelling reaches some threshold and the sintering clay tears itself apart with a sound like a rifle shot—something emerges. Not from the depth, because there is no depth here, only the endless laminate of fired layers going down until they are indistinguishable from the planet's core, but from the **space between** the layers. The crack opens, dust sighs outward, and the inhabitant unfolds itself from the plane of fracture.

They are called the **Kept**, though no one keeps them, or perhaps the name comes from their habit of keeping still, of being kept by the ground that extrudes them. They are not animals, not machines, not quite separate from the landscape itself. Imagine a sheet of mica that has learned to fold itself into three dimensions without losing its essential flatness. Imagine a fragment of the berm-wall that has sheared away and forgotten to fall, that instead holds itself upright against the hot wind, its edges still crumbling slightly, still part of the construction.

They are ceramic, obviously, because everything here is ceramic or becoming ceramic. Their substance is the gray-pink silt, the same as the ground, but organized differently—more dense, more deliberate, with a glaze on one side where the sun has vitrified them over centuries of exposure. They are thin, no thicker than a finger in cross-section, but they can be tall as a doorway or wide as a house. They have no front or back, only **surface** and **depth**, and they move by a kind of slow shuffling, a dragging of their lower edges against the ground that leaves a trail of fine dust, a signature in the construction site.

The Kept do not eat. They do not breathe in the way of living things. Instead, they **thermoregulate by orientation**, tilting their broad surfaces to catch or avoid the light, and in doing so they perform a kind of photosynthesis that is less biological than electrochemical. The heat creates a differential between their glazed and unglazed faces; this differential generates a current that sustains whatever passes for their consciousness. They are solar sails that have learned to stand upright, to wander.

They are the only things here that cast shadows intentionally.

Where the Kept gather—and they do gather, though slowly, over years—the berms grow differently. The wind-driven dust sticks to them more readily than to the static clay, and they become foci for accretion, seeds around which the landscape crystallizes. A berm that grows around a Kept develops a hollow interior, a gallery the shape of the inhabitant, and these spaces collect condensation faster than the open flats. They become **damp pockets** with architecture, with intention.

Inside these spaces, the Kept rest. They do not sleep, but they enter states of reduced activity where the ticking of their cooling ceramic slows to a stop, where they become indistinguishable from the walls. If you were to enter such a gallery—finding the narrow entrance where two berms have grown together around a hollow core—you would see them standing in the dimness, their surfaces catching the faint light that filters through the clay, looking like nothing so much as fragments of a broken pot arranged by a careful hand. But they are not fragments. They are whole, and they are waiting.

They communicate through **resonance**. When one Kept shifts its position, when it scrapes its lower edge against the ground, the sound travels through the solid earth in a way that air-transmitted sound cannot. Others feel this vibration in their own substance, interpret the frequency, respond in kind. It is a slow language, patient as geology, composed of groans and ticks and the occasional sharp crack of deliberate fracture. Conversations between Kept can last years, decades. They discuss the growth rates of berms, the movement of the Long Swirl, the quality of light in a particular season.

They remember being ground.

This is the strangest thing about them, the thing that makes them inhabitants rather than features. Each Kept contains within its structure a record of its previous state—the specific layer of clay from which it extruded, the exact configuration of dust that was deposited upon it before the split. They can access these memories by subtle shifts in their internal crystalline structure, and they do access them, constantly, obsessively. They are historians of themselves, archivists of their own making. A Kept that has existed for ten thousand years contains ten thousand years of weather, of deposition, of the slow chemistry of construction, and it can read this record the way you might read rings in a tree.

But they are not content to be records. They **intervene**.

Where a berm is growing too tall, becoming unstable, likely to collapse and waste its material in a slide of dust, a Kept will position itself in the wind-shadow and deflect the deposition, slowing the growth, allowing the structure to consolidate. Where the siliceous crusts are spreading too aggressively, threatening to vitrify a whole region into useless glass, the Kept will gather and create a barrier of still air, starving the organisms of the dust they need to survive. They are gardeners of a garden that is also themselves, sculptors of a medium that is also their flesh.

They are not numerous. In the vastness of the Sintering Flats, you might walk a hundred kilometers without seeing one, and even then you might mistake it for a natural outcrop, a particularly regular fracture in the clay. But they are there, standing in the heat, casting their thin shadows, listening to the ground groan. They have no eyes, but they perceive light through their entire surface, a diffuse awareness that makes the world appear to them as variations in temperature and vibration. They have no hands, but they shape the world by their mere presence, by the way they interrupt the wind.

When the Long Swirl approaches, when the sky turns bone-white and the wind begins its architectural work, the Kept do not flee. They **orient**. They turn their broad faces to the storm, feeling the pressure differentials, the streaming of the dust. They allow the wind to deposit new layers upon them, growing thicker, heavier, more stable. The storm passes, and where it has been, the Kept stand changed—sometimes taller, sometimes fatter with new clay, sometimes split by the violence into two or three separate beings, each carrying the memory of the whole.

This splitting is their reproduction, but it is not a joyful multiplication. When a Kept divides, it **decides** to divide, or rather, the accumulated tension of its memories reaches a point where division is inevitable. The new beings are not children; they are continuations, and they often remain in proximity to their parent, forming small clusters that work in concert to shape the local landscape. These clusters develop distinct personalities—some are **accumulators**, encouraging growth, creating tall spires and sharp ridges; others are **diffusers**, spreading material thin, creating plateaus and flats.

There is no competition between them, no survival of the fittest. There is only the slow argument of form, the ongoing negotiation about what the Sintering Flats should become. The Kept are the embodiment of this negotiation, the given-form of the landscape's own indecision. They are what happens when matter refuses to be inert, when the process of construction develops a preference for certain outcomes.

They are dying, of course, as everything here is dying and being born simultaneously. The glaze that protects them also isolates them; over millennia, it thickens until the thermal differential fails, until the current that sustains their consciousness fades. When this happens, the Kept does not fall. It simply stops moving, stops responding, becomes indistinguishable from the berms it helped to shape. But even then, it is not dead in the way of organic death. It is **incorporated**. The wind deposits new clay upon it; the ground swells and lifts it; eventually it is buried, becoming part of the laminate, a layer of unusual density in the endless construction.

Other Kept visit these sites. They stand over the buried ones, feeling the resonance of the compressed form below, reading the memories that are still trapped in the crystalline structure. They learn from the dead, incorporating their histories into their own. In this way, nothing is lost, only transformed. The Sintering Flats remember everything, and the Kept are the memory made ambulatory, the archive that walks.

If you encounter one—and you will know it by the shadow it casts, by the way the dust seems to organize itself around its base—do not approach too quickly. Move as the Kept move: slowly, with deliberation, feeling the ground. It will know you are there by your vibration, by the heat of your body, by the way you disturb the air. It will not flee. It will wait, patient as pottery, curious in its own mineral way. If you are quiet, if you are still, it may orient itself toward you, catching the light on its glazed face, and you will see yourself reflected there—not clearly, not as in a mirror, but as a smudge of warmth, a vibration in the clay.

You are not the first to visit. The Kept remember others, or remember the idea of others—beings from elsewhere who walked the flats, who left footprints that were quickly buried by the accreting dust. These memories are stored in the same laminate as everything else, and sometimes a Kept will extrude itself from a layer that contains a footprint, carrying that ancient impression in its substance, a ghost of a visitor from a time when the world was younger and softer.

The Kept are the Sintering Flats becoming aware of themselves, one sheet of fired clay at a time. They are the question the landscape is asking about what it will be when it is finished, and the answer they embody is: **it will not be finished, it will only continue**, and in continuing, it will make more of them, more keepers of the memory, more sculptors of the wind, more thin shadows standing against the brutal light, waiting for the next split, the next extrusion, the next slow conversation about the shape of things to come.
