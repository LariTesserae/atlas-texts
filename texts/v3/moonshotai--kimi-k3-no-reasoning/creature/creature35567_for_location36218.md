They rise before the light does—not from sleep, because sleep is not quite the word for what they do, but from a suspension so complete it might be mistaken for death if death were not so much noisier. The first warmth touches the eastern ridges, the basalt beginning its daily exhalation, and the cushion plants release them: the breathless ones, the drift-born, the small soft weightless proofs that the mountain is not finished.

They are the Moths of the Hollow, though no one names them. They have no need of names, being so numerous that individual identity is a concept that has never evolved here. They are a family of perhaps forty species, each tuned to a slightly different thermal, a slightly different hour, a slightly different wavelength of the ordinary light. Their bodies are miracles of subtraction: abdomens hollowed to chambers, thoraxes reduced to scaffolding, legs so fine they serve only as anchors for the brief hours of rest. Their wings are not the dusty, opaque things of the lowlands. They are membranes stretched over vacuoles, translucent as shed skin, some refracting light into colours that have no names—violent violets, ambers that seem to burn without heat—others drinking the light so completely that they appear as holes in the morning, fluttering absences, negative spaces given agency.

They do not fly so much as they *are flown*. The thermals rise from the ancient stone in slow, spiraling columns, and the moths enter them the way water enters water, becoming part of the current. They drift for hours, for kilometres, their wing-beats so slow and shallow they appear to be gliding even when they are climbing, their bodies so light that the boundary between creature and atmosphere blurs into a fine, warm suspension. To stand among them at dawn is to feel the air itself become animate—not buzzing, not loud, but *populated*, textured with soft collisions that never quite happen. A wing brushes your lip. A body settles in the hollow of your throat, weightless as a thought, and is gone. They do not fear hands. They do not recognize hands as distinct from the wind.

At the density they achieve, they are less a population than a condition of the air, a second, softer weather. They feed on the mist that condenses on the cushion plants, sipping through proboscises so fine they can drink from the film of water on a single lichen lobe. They mate in the drift, two bodies rising together in the same thermal for hours, wings almost touching, until they descend as one onto a dinner-plate of ancient vegetation. They die there, often, their wings folding into the green-black cushions, adding their chitin to the soil that will feed the next century’s growth. They are the mountain’s breath made visible—exhaled in the morning, inhaled at dusk, silent, warm, and full.

And where they gather, the Yellow-Eyed Ones wait.

They are lizards, taxonomically, though the word suggests a quickness and a dryness that does not suit them. They are broad-headed, low-slung, their skin the colour of wet basalt, their eyes the impossible, saturated yellow of the lichens that are older than the stones they sit upon. They do not hunt. Hunting implies a narrative, a sequence of desire and fulfillment, and the Yellow-Eyed Ones have evolved beyond narrative into pure arithmetic. They position themselves on the warm rock faces where the thermals converge, where the moths are thickest, and they become geological. They do not tense. They do not crouch. They simply *persist*, with a stillness so absolute that it becomes a form of motion—a movement through time rather than space, a patience that wears the same texture as the mountain itself.

You will not see them unless you know to look for the quality of stillness that is too complete to be stone. They are not camouflaged. They are *continuous*. They are the mountain growing eyes in the places where the moths are likely to pass, the bedrock developing a taste for wings. When a moth drifts within range—and one always does, eventually, because the air is full of them—the lizard moves. It is not a strike so much as a *correction*, a brief adjustment of the world’s state. The tongue is fast as a whip-crack but silent, a pink flicker that restores the proper ratio of moth to air. Then the lizard returns to its stillness, unblinking, its yellow eyes holding the same light as the lichens, digesting the small soft body with the same patience with which it waited for it.

They are common, but they are not *present* in the way the moths are present. They are the punctuation to the moths’ endless sentence, the periods that fall without sound. They breathe slowly, their sides swelling once a minute, twice, their hearts beating with the unhurried rhythm of a place that has stopped needing to hurry. They live for decades, perhaps longer, spending their lives on the same outcrop, the same face of warm basalt, watching the same thermal patterns repeat with variations until the variations themselves become familiar. They are the memory of the mountain, the long view, the slow regard.

Higher, rarer, move the birds—the punctuation made visible. There are three species, perhaps four, all high-altitude specialists with wings built for the thin air and voices that seem embarrassed to break the silence. They are not the dominant presence here; they are too large, too hungry, too *loud*, their wing-beats audible as a thin whistle that seems to apologize for existing. They move through the moth-laden air the way large fish move through plankton, present and necessary but operating at a different scale, casting shadows that briefly dim the refractions of a thousand wings. They are the tourists of the Hollow, the visitors who have not yet learned the local custom of silence.

But the true owners, the inhabitants who define the place by their saturation, are the ones who do not move at all.

The Lichen-Saints are not animals, though they are alive in a way that makes the moths seem hasty. They are the oldest residents, the original settlers, the ones who were here before the walls were built and will be here when the walls have finished melting back into the soil. They grow on the basalt and on the dry-stone walls and on the monolith that leans to the east, in colours that violate the mountain’s palette: a yellow so intense it generates its own light, an orange like burnt time, a blue that exists only in the moment before dawn. They grow at rates measured in fractions of millimetres per year, a single colony the size of a hand representing a continuous act of persistence that predates language, predates agriculture, predates the concept of history.

They are not individuals. They are *processes* given form, a slow, crystalline breathing that exchanges gases with the thin air, that drinks the mist directly through their surfaces, that weathers the rock and is weathered by it in a reciprocal erosion so gradual it has become a kind of conversation. They hold the walls together even as they consume them, their root-like hyphae threading through the stones, binding the deliberate arrangements of the forgotten builders into a new, biological geometry. They are the mountain’s skin, its memory, its slow, colorful thought.

When the mist rolls in—and it does, most afternoons, rising from the lowlands like a held breath released—the Lichen-Saints drink. They swell imperceptibly, their colours deepening, the yellows becoming more yellow, the oranges burning with a cold fire. The moths settle among them, folding their wings, becoming indistinguishable from the rock. The Yellow-Eyed Ones close their eyes, which are the same colour as the lichens, and for a moment the entire ecosystem becomes a single organism: the stone, the plant, the insect, the reptile, all breathing the same wet air, all holding the same pattern steady against the long erosion.

There are others, perhaps. Things that live in the pools, in the shallow, still water that reflects the sky with such fidelity it seems like a hole in the world. Microscopic lives, crustaceans the size of dust motes, larvae that have never known a river. They are there, in the warm, mineral-rich water, doing their small work, but they are not the *presence* of the place. They are the details, the footnotes. The text is written in wings and patience.

At night, when the thermals die and the moths settle and the Yellow-Eyed Ones become one with the dark basalt, the Hollow of Breathless Wings belongs to the persistent ones—the lichens continuing their millennial growth in the dark, the cushion plants holding their moisture, the stone itself radiating the day’s warmth back into the thin, star-filled sky. The hum begins, or becomes audible, that low vibration that may be the mountain’s metabolism or may be the sound of time itself passing through a place that has learned to let it pass without resistance.

And in the morning, the breathless ones rise again, the air fills with silent colour, and the Yellow-Eyed Ones open their eyes, which are the colour of the oldest light, and wait for the world to correct itself into their mouths. It has always been thus. It will continue. The wings are breathless because the air is thin, and because they have no need to breathe as we understand breathing—they are sustained by the warmth, by the drift, by the sheer, unreasonable density of life in a place that should be empty, and is instead full, and silent, and ancient, and alive.
