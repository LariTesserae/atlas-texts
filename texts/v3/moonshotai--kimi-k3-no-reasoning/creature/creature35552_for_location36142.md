They call themselves the **Lowborn**, though not with shame—just as a statement of altitude. They are the ones who came down when the surface mines played out, who stayed when the coal seam slumped into this shallow bowl and the rain began to pool. They are broad-shouldered people with permanent dampness in their collars, their skin mapped with heat rash and the fine grey dust that the kilns exhale. They speak in a dialect flattened by humidity, words clipped short as if conserving breath in the thick air, and they move with a distinctive carefulness, a economy of motion learned from navigating slick brick and rusted iron.

The Lowborn live in the interstitial spaces between the kilns, their homes built from the same firebrick as the ovens, their windows perpetually fogged. They have developed a culture of **vertical patience**: because the heat rises but cannot escape the bowl, they have learned to live in layers, sleeping in hammocks slung close to the floor where the air is coolest, working at mid-level, and storing their few dry goods—salt, tobacco, ammunition—in clay pots hung from rafters where the condensation cannot reach. Their skin carries a permanent sheen, not of oil, but of a microscopic algae that thrives in the humid air, giving them a faint greenish cast in certain lights. They consider this a mark of belonging.

Among them, but not of them, moves the **Condensation Herd**—a population of feral dogs that has diverged from their street-dwelling ancestors into something almost amphibious. These are lean, grey-muzzled creatures with paws webbed slightly more than their ancestors, capable of navigating the flooded lower tiers without hesitation. They have learned to hunt the rats that cluster around the warm kiln walls, and the Lowborn tolerate them, even leave out bowls of the rendered fat that drips from the coal-processing machinery. The dogs have developed a peculiar respiratory adaptation: a third eyelid that filters the soot, and lungs that extract oxygen with violent efficiency from the moisture-heavy air. They bark rarely, conserving breath, and when they do, the sound is wet, truncated, echoing off the bowl's walls with a strange, submarine quality.

The **Rust Priests** occupy the middle tiers, tending to the spiritual needs of a population that sees the divine in entropy. They are not ordained in any traditional sense, but are self-appointed keepers of the **Oxidation Liturgies**—rituals that celebrate the slow transformation of iron to rust, of dry to damp, of solid to slurry. They wear vestments of layered burlap that have absorbed so much moisture they hang like lead, and they carry censers that burn not incense, but handfuls of damp moss and coal dust, producing a thick, white smoke that clings to the skin. The Rust Priests tend the **Weeping Walls**—sections of brickwork where the condensation is particularly heavy—and collect the mineral-rich runoff in copper vessels, using it to anoint the foreheads of the newborn and the foreheads of the dying. They believe the city is not dying, but *distilling*, slowly concentrating into its essential self.

Below them, in the flooded alleys of the lowest tier, dwell the **Sump-Singers**, though "dwell" is perhaps too static a word. These are outcasts, madmen, or visionaries—depending on which Lowborn you ask—who have given themselves over entirely to the moisture. They live half-submerged in the stagnant pools, their bodies hosting colonies of leeches and water beetles that they cultivate deliberately, believing that the parasites purify their blood of the coal-toxins. They communicate in a whistling language that mimics the sound of steam escaping valves, and they have developed an intimate knowledge of the city's hydrology, able to predict which basement will flood next based on the behavior of mosquito larvae. The Lowborn fear them but consult them when the drainage channels back up, for the Sump-Singers can navigate the clogged pipes with their bodies, clearing blockages that machinery cannot reach.

The **Kiln-Masters** are the aristocracy of this place, though their wealth is measured in sweat-equity rather than gold. They are the descendants of the original industrialists who recognized that the bowl's natural humidity could be harnessed to create a specific grade of slow-cured brick, prized for its density and resistance to thermal shock. They live in the largest structures, their homes built directly atop the kilns to capture the waste heat, and they have developed a physiology adapted to constant high temperature: enlarged sweat glands, veins that sit close to the skin surface, and a tolerance for carbon monoxide that would kill a surface-dweller. They are pale, almost translucent, with eyes adapted to the dim light, and they rule through control of the **Draft Rights**—the carefully managed airflow through the chimneys that prevents the entire bowl from becoming a gas chamber. They are rarely seen without their **breathing masks**, complex apparatuses of leather and copper mesh that filter the air, though within their own homes they remove them to reveal faces scarred by years of heat exposure.

And then there are the **Others**, the non-human citizens who have claimed the city as their own. The **Rats of the Third Tier** have grown unusually large, not through mutation but through abundance—feeding on the constant spillage of grain used to temper the kiln fires. They have organized into complex warrens within the walls, their scratching synchronized with the machinery, creating a percussive undercurrent that the Lowborn use to tell time. The **Cockroaches of the Cooling Racks** have evolved a metallic carapace, iridescent and heat-resistant, allowing them to survive temperatures that would kill their surface cousins. They are harvested by the Lowborn children, who crack them open to extract a protein-rich paste used as a condiment.

The birds—**Soot-Pigeons** and **Kiln-Starlings**—have developed a symbiotic relationship with the city's industry. They nest in the warm updrafts of the chimneys, their feathers grey with ash, and they have learned to follow the steam vents to catch insects driven upward by the heat. Their droppings, acidic and copious, are collected by the Rust Priests as a component in the anointing oils.

Finally, there are the **Mosquito-Born**, though this is a name the Lowborn give to a phenomenon rather than a true people. In the deepest, stillest pools of the sump-bottoms, the mosquitoes have grown so dense, so persistent, that they form a kind of living mist. The Sump-Singers claim that if you stand in these clouds long enough, allowing yourself to be bitten ten thousand times, you begin to hear the city's true voice—the hum of the kilns translated into insect frequencies, the slow, wet thoughts of the descending bricks made audible. Whether this is madness or transcendence, none can say, but occasionally a Lowborn will disappear into the lowest tier for three days and return with bloodshot eyes and a new, whistling cadence to their speech, claiming to have heard the future in the whine of wings.

All of them—human, dog, rat, priest, and singer—are bound by the **Great Damp**, the understanding that they are living in the pauses between waterdrops, in the breath between rain and evaporation. They do not dream of dryness. They dream of slower rust, of deeper pools, of the day the bowl finally fills completely and they become, at last, aquatic.
