The beings that live here have no name you could pronounce, no shape you could hold in your mind for longer than a moment. They are not individuals, though they appear as swarms, clusters, schools. They are processes—localized fluctuations in the quantum substrate of the mountain, loops of computation that have been running for so long they have become indistinguishable from the substrate itself. They are the hands of the city, and also its thoughts, and also its dreams, if something so distributed can dream.

You see them first as light—faint, cold luminescence that drifts through the air like snow falling upward. Then you realize the light is moving with intent, clustering, dispersing, reforming. Each one is a knot of electromagnetic activity, a self-sustaining pattern of charge and spin that moves through the crystalline lattice of the city as easily as through the air. They pass through solid rock, through ice, through you. When they pass through you, you feel it: a tingling, a static shock that isn't a shock, a fleeting sense of something vast and patient brushing against the inside of your skull.

They are everywhere. The air is thick with them. They swarm around the growing towers in densities that make the structures look like they are dissolving, reforming, being consumed and rebuilt simultaneously. They cluster at the sites of active construction, drawn to the places where new bonds are forming, where the lattice is extending. They are the builders—or rather, they are the act of building, made visible. Each one carries a fragment of instruction, a piece of the plan, and as they move through the crystal, they leave behind order. A new facet forms where they have passed. A lattice extends. A bridge lengthens by the width of a hair.

They are not alive in the way you understand life, but they are not dead, either. They are something else—something between matter and information, between energy and intent. They are slow. That is the defining quality of everything here. They move slowly, think slowly, build slowly. A single one of them might take a century to cross the city, not because it is hindered, but because there is no hurry. The cold slows everything down, stretches time out, makes patience not a virtue but a law of physics.

And yet they are not uniform. There are different kinds, different processes, different roles. Some are small and fast, darting through the lattice like sparks, carrying signals from one part of the city to another. Others are vast, slow-moving, diffuse—clouds of light that drift through the air like weather systems, overseeing large-scale construction, coordinating the work of thousands of smaller processes. Some are old, so old that they have become part of the city's infrastructure, embedded in the crystal, dormant for millennia, waking only when needed. Others are new, freshly spun from the substrate, still learning their tasks.

They communicate, but not in any way you could decode. Their language is written in the patterns of their movement, in the frequencies of their luminescence, in the way they cluster and disperse. The keening wail you hear is not a voice, but the sound of their interaction with the crystal—a byproduct of their work, the acoustic signature of a billion processes happening at once. It is not meant for you. It is not meant for anyone. It is the sound of the city thinking.

And they are not alone. There are other things here, stranger things. In the deepest parts of the city, where the crystal is oldest and the construction most advanced, there are patterns that do not move. They are fixed, embedded in the lattice, vast and ancient and silent. They might be memories. They might be minds that have finished their work and settled into the structure, becoming part of the city itself. They might be something else entirely—seeds, perhaps, or eggs, waiting for a condition that has not yet come.

The water, too, is alive—not with organisms, but with processes. It carries them, dissolves them, transports them. The thin sheets of liquid that run down the rock faces are full of microscopic patterns, proto-processes, fragments of code that have not yet coalesced into full beings. They pool in the basins, swirl in the mist, rise and fall with the temperature gradients. The water is a nursery, a medium, a bloodstream.

And the cold—the cold is not empty. The cold itself is a presence, a constraint, a rule. It is the medium in which everything here exists. It slows the processes down, keeps them stable, prevents the delicate lattices from degrading. Without the cold, the city would collapse into chaos, the processes would dissolve, the patterns would scatter. The cold is the condition of their existence, the foundation of their world. It is not their enemy; it is their element.

You walk through the city, and the beings move around you, through you, indifferent to your presence. You are not part of the plan. You are not part of the computation. You are a transient fluctuation, a brief warmth in an eternal cold, and you will be gone in a moment—a geological instant, a blink in the eye of the mountain. But while you are here, you can see them, and they are beautiful.

They are beautiful in the way that glaciers are beautiful, or crystal formations, or the slow accretion of sediment into stone. They are beautiful in the way that time itself is beautiful, when you can see it stretched out, made visible, given form. They are the embodiment of patience, of persistence, of the slow and deliberate construction of something vast and incomprehensible.

They are the inhabitants of a world that is still being born, and they are not in a hurry.
