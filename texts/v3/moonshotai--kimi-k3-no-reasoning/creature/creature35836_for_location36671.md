# Those Who Dwell in the Hollow City

The city breathes, and what lives within it is inseparable from that breathing — each creature a different rhythm laid over the same vast pulse, none of them synchronized, all of them necessary.

## The Drifting Membranes

They are the most numerous, though "numerous" here means you might see five or six in an hour if you stand still and watch the streets. The city calls them into motion with its own breath — when a building exhales, when a cluster of orifices dilate and release the displaced air from within, the membranes catch that faint current and ride it through the streets like fallen leaves riding a stream. Except there is no stream. There is only the slow respiration of stone-that-is-not-stone, and these translucent sheets that have learned to travel on it.

They are not flat, not quite. They billow slightly, convex and concave by turns, their edges rippling in slow waves that help them steer — though "steer" may be too intentional a word. They respond. They adjust. When one approaches a building wall, it will sometimes deflect away, riding the pressure differential, and sometimes it will turn toward the wall deliberately, spreading itself wide as it approaches, and then — contact.

The settling is gentle. The membrane drapes itself across the building's surface like a cloth laid over a table, conforming to the contours, finding the shallow ridges of the growth-ring bands and fitting into them. Then it holds. For hours. Sometimes for most of a day.

While it holds, it feeds. Or it cleans. The distinction may not exist here.

The veins inside it — those faint branching lines, barely darker than the surrounding tissue — begin to pulse more rapidly. You can see this if you stand close. The pulse is visible as a slight thickening and thinning of the vein-lines, a rhythmic engorgement that moves in waves from the edges of the membrane toward its center. Something is being drawn in. Something is being extracted from the building's surface, some fine layer of biological residue — dead cells, secreted compounds, the accumulated film of the city's slow metabolism. The membrane takes this in, digests it in its slow, distributed way, and when it finally releases — peeling away from the wall edge-first, curling into the air like a page turning — the surface beneath is changed.

It is smoother. The growth bands are clearer, more distinct, their colors slightly more saturated. The building looks healthier, if that word applies. The membrane-creature is plumper, its veins more visible, its color shifted almost imperceptibly toward a milky opalescence. Both have benefited. Or neither has, and this is simply what they do — an exchange with no purpose beyond its own continuation.

They have no eyes, no mouth, no limbs. They are sheet and vein and pulse. They are, in their way, perfect for this place — light enough to ride the city's breath, broad enough to cover surface area, simple enough to persist in an environment that offers no water, no soil, no rich organic debris. They live on the city's skin, and they keep the skin clean, and they drift, and they pulse to their own tempo, and that tempo never matches the city's, and so they exist in a permanent state of near-harmony that never resolves.

There is something almost unbearable about watching them for long. The dissonance of their rhythm against the buildings' rhythm creates a tension in your own chest, as though your heartbeat is trying to reconcile two incompatible tempos and failing. You find yourself holding your breath without realizing it, waiting for synchronization that never comes.

The membranes do not notice. They drift. They settle. They feed. They release. They are the city's hands, wiping its own face.

## The Stem-Walkers

You will not see one on your first day. You may not see one on your fifth. They are rare, and they are reclusive in the way of things that have never needed to hide but have also never needed to be seen.

When you finally do see one, it will be at a distance, moving down the length of a long street, and your first impression will be that the light is playing tricks — that a shadow (though there are no shadows here) has detached itself from a doorway and is walking away. Then you will understand that it is tall, and thin, and structured, and that it is moving with a gait that is not quite like anything you have seen — not the scuttle of an insect, not the stride of a mammal, but something more deliberate, more measured, as though each step is placed with the precision of a surveyor marking boundaries.

They are roughly two meters tall, though some are taller — you will see one that is closer to three, its upper bundle swaying slightly as it walks, and it will look fragile and inevitable in equal measure. The body is a collection of jointed segments, each the color of dry grass, of old paper, of bone left in the sun. The segments are bound together at the top in a loose cluster — not fused, not knotted, simply gathered, as though the creature were a sheaf of wheat tied with a single loose band. Below this binding point, the segments separate into legs. Many legs. A dozen, perhaps more, each one thin as a reed and articulated at multiple points, so that the walking motion is a complex ripple of coordinated joints, a wave of careful placements that carries the creature forward in absolute silence.

They do not hurry. They have never hurried. Speed is not part of their function, and their function is the oldest thing in this city — older, perhaps, than the buildings themselves.

They enter the structures. This is what they do. A stem-walker will approach a building, pause before an orifice — a doorway-shape, a window-shape, one of the tall archways that span the narrower streets — and wait. If the orifice is dilated open, it will step through immediately, folding its many legs slightly to pass under the lintel, disappearing into the dim interior with no hesitation. If the orifice is closed or nearly closed, the stem-walker will stand before it, motionless, waiting. It will wait for hours if necessary. It will wait for the building to breathe, for the sphincter-edges of the opening to relax and retract, and then it will enter.

What they do inside is not visible. The interiors of the buildings are hollow — you have looked through open orifices and seen this — vast chambers with ribbed walls, the cartilage-stone rising in curved vaults like the inside of a lung, like the nave of a cathedral built by something that had never seen a cathedral but understood resonance and space. The stem-walkers move into these spaces and are gone. You cannot track them. You cannot follow — the orifices often close after they enter, sealing shut behind them with the slow inevitability of a wound healing.

Hours pass. Days, sometimes. Then the orifice will open again, and the stem-walker will emerge. Sometimes from the same opening it entered. Sometimes from a different one, on a different face of the building, as though it has passed through some internal passage, some corridor that connects one side of the structure to another. It will step out into the street, pause for a moment as though reorienting, and then walk on — to the next building, to the next task, to the next phase of a cycle you cannot perceive in its entirety.

They are the city's maintenance. Or its communication system. Or its reproductive mechanism. You suspect they carry something from building to building — information, chemical signals, spores, packets of genetic material — but you have never seen them holding anything. They are empty-handed, if "hands" is even a concept that applies. They are simply themselves, walking from one breathing structure to another, entering, waiting, emerging, moving on. The city's buildings do not touch each other. They are separate organisms, separate colonies, and the stem-walkers are the threads that stitch them together, the messengers that make the city a city rather than a collection of isolated towers.

They do not acknowledge the membrane-creatures. When a membrane drifts across a stem-walker's path, the walker simply adjusts its gait, stepping around or over the translucent sheet, and continues. There is no hostility. There is no interaction at all. They occupy different layers of the city's life, and those layers do not intersect.

They will not acknowledge you, either. You can stand directly in a stem-walker's path, and it will pause, consider you with whatever sensory apparatus it possesses (you have never found eyes, or antennae, or anything that resembles a face), and then step around you and continue. You are not part of its function. You are furniture. You are weather. You are irrelevant.

This should feel dismissive. It does not. It feels correct. The stem-walkers are performing a task that predates your arrival and will continue after your departure, and the fact that they do not pause for you is not rudeness but purity of purpose. They are the most honest thing in this city.

## The Yellow Threads

They are the smallest life here, and the most persistent.

In the gaps between the floor tiles — those knife-edged cracks that descend a hand's width into hollow darkness — something grows. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus, though it resembles both. It is a thread, a filament, a single continuous strand of pale, waxy material the thickness of coarse string, and it rises from the darkness below in clusters that seem random until you have walked enough streets to recognize that they are not.

The threads grow upward. This is their defining motion. Over the course of a morning — though "morning" here is only a slight brightening of the already-dim light, a shift from gray to slightly-lighter-gray — the threads emerge from the gaps and extend. A centimeter. Two centimeters. Sometimes more. They are stiff, not flexible, holding their vertical posture with the rigidity of dried grass. They are smooth to the touch, and dry — so dry that touching them feels like touching something that has never known moisture, something that was born in desiccation and will die in it, if they die at all.

They are pale yellow. Not gold, not amber, but a faded, waxy yellow like old candle tallow, like the color of teeth. In the sourceless dim light of the city, they are almost white, but when you hold one close — when you crouch down and look at it directly — you see the yellow, faint but definite, and you understand that this is the color of something that does not photosynthesize, does not use light as energy, but perhaps uses it as a signal.

They are not evenly distributed. Some streets have them in abundance — dense tufts rising from every gap, clusters of a dozen threads or more, so that the floor looks bristly, upholstered in dry yellow hair. Other streets have none at all. You will walk for an hour and see nothing but bare, cracked tile, and then turn a corner and find a street where the threads grow so thickly that you have to step carefully to avoid breaking them.

You do break them, sometimes. Accidentally. A foot placed wrong, a stumble, and a cluster of threads snaps with a dry, papery crack — the only sharp sound you will hear in this city, and it dies instantly, absorbed by the walls, leaving no echo. The broken threads do not bleed, do not leak sap. They simply break, and the broken ends retract slowly back into the gap, and the next morning new threads rise in their place.

The retraction is their other defining motion. At night — or in what passes for night, the dimming, the deepening of the gray — the threads withdraw. You can watch this happen if you are patient. Over the course of an hour, the threads shorten, pulling back down into the gaps between tiles, descending into the darkness below. They do not wilt. They do not curl or collapse. They simply lower themselves, segment by segment, like periscopes retracting into a submarine, and then they are gone, and the streets are bare again, and the gaps in the floor are just gaps.

You have looked into those gaps. You have crouched and peered down into the narrow darkness, trying to see where the threads go, what they are connected to, what they are growing from. You have seen nothing. The darkness is absolute. The gaps descend farther than your light can reach — if you had light, if you brought a lamp or a torch, but there is nothing to burn here, nothing that produces flame, and so you have only the dim gray ambient light, and it does not penetrate the gaps. The threads descend into mystery.

What are they? You have theories. They are roots — but roots grow downward, not upward, and these threads clearly spend half their cycle above the floor. They are sensory organs — the city's way of feeling the air, tasting it, detecting changes in temperature or chemistry. They are reproductive structures — spore-dispersal organs that rise to catch currents that do not exist, releasing seeds too small to see into air too still to carry them. They are communicative — a signaling system, sending chemical messages from one part of the city to another through a network that exists below the floor, hidden, connecting all the separate buildings into a single distributed organism.

Or they are simply growing because growth is what they do. The city is young. The ground is young. Perhaps the threads are the first stage of something larger, something that will one day rise from the gaps and become new buildings, new structures, new extensions of the city. Perhaps the city is still being born, and the threads are its first tentative reach into the air.

You do not know. You will not know. The threads grow, and retract, and grow again, and the cycle repeats with a regularity that feels less like biology and more like ritual.

## The Thing at the Center

You reach it on your fourth day, or your seventh — time moves strangely here, measured not in hours but in the slow cycling of the buildings' breath, the dilation and closing of orifices, the growth and retraction of the yellow threads. You reach it by following the curve of the streets, the subtle inward spiral that you did not notice at first but now cannot unsee. Every street bends. Every intersection is slightly askew, angled just enough to lead you inward, and you follow, and the buildings grow closer together, and the streets narrow, and the air — already cool and dry — becomes cooler, drier, as though you are approaching a place where even the city's slow metabolism pauses.

And then you are there. At the center. At the dome.

It is small. This surprises you. After the scale of the city — the towering row-house structures, the long streets, the vast basin — you expected the center to be monumental, a cathedral, a palace. Instead it is a dome no taller than your shoulder, perhaps three meters across, sitting flush with the tile floor, rising from it without seam or joint, as though the floor itself swelled upward and solidified into this shape.

It is made of the same cartilage-stone as the rest of the city, but it is different. Denser. Darker. The growth-ring bands that stripe the buildings' facades are absent here — the dome's surface is uniform, unbroken, so smooth it is almost reflective. You can see yourself in it, faintly, a distortion of gray and shadow, and when you touch it — you cannot help but touch it, your hand flat against the cool surface — you feel nothing. No pulse. No breath. No slow contraction and release. The dome is still.

It has no openings. You walk around it, tracing its circumference with your fingertips, and the surface is seamless. No doorways. No windows. No orifices that dilate and close. The dome is sealed, entire, complete. It is the only structure in the city that does not open itself, does not breathe, does not change.

The tile floor around it is different, too. You notice this as you step back, looking at the ground. The hexagonal tiles here are flush, level, perfectly aligned. They do not shift. They do not rise or subside. You press your hand to the floor and feel only stillness, the cool mineral unyielding, and you understand that this is the one place in the city where nothing moves.

The membrane-creatures do not come here. You have watched them for days, tracking their drifting paths through the streets, and you have never seen one approach the dome. They avoid it — not with any visible swerve or hesitation, but simply by never being here, by riding the city's breath along routes that curve around this central point without ever touching it.

The stem-walkers do not enter it. You have seen them pass nearby, walking the last few streets before the center, and they do not pause before the dome. They do not wait for an opening that will never come. They walk past, unhurried, deliberate, and they do not look at it — if "look" is even applicable to creatures with no visible eyes — and they continue on their rounds, entering the breathing buildings, emerging, moving on.

The yellow threads do not grow near it. The gaps in the floor tiles here are empty. No pale filaments rise from them. The ground is bare, clean, uninterrupted. The threads grow throughout the rest of the city, in their irregular clusters, their daily cycle of emergence and retraction, but here — in a radius of perhaps ten meters around the dome — there are none. It is as though the ground itself knows not to disturb this place.

And so you stand at the center of the Hollow City, beside the dome that does not breathe, and you feel the tension that defines this place — the tension between motion and stillness, between the constant, layered, multi-rhythm activity of the city and the absolute, unyielding silence of this single point.

What is it? You circle the dome again, slowly, your hand trailing along its surface. It is the same temperature as the air, neither warm nor cold. It does not vibrate. It does not hum. It simply exists, sitting at the center of the spiral, and the city grows around it — or grows from it, or grows toward it, you cannot tell which.

Is it the heart? If so, it is a heart that does not beat. Is it the seed? If so, it is a seed that has not yet germinated, or one that germinated long ago and has now gone dormant, its work complete. Is it the city's destination — the thing the whole breathing, shifting, opening-and-closing organism is growing toward, the final form it will one day achieve? Or is it the city's origin — the thing that was here first, before the buildings, before the streets, before the membrane-creatures and stem-walkers and yellow threads, and everything else grew outward from it like rings from a stone dropped in still water?

You will never know. The dome does not speak. It does not change. It sits in the dim light, in the dry cool air, in the silence, and it waits — or it does not wait, because waiting implies anticipation, and the dome does not anticipate. It simply is.

And the city breathes around it, and the membranes drift, and the stem-walkers walk, and the yellow threads rise and retract, and all of it happens in reference to this point, this stillness, this held breath at the center of the spiral.

You want to leave. You want to stay. The dome makes you want both, simultaneously, and the wanting is the thing you will carry with you when you finally walk back out through the breathing streets — the memory of a silence so complete it became a presence, a stillness so total it became a kind of motion, a thing that does nothing and yet holds an entire city in orbit around it.

The city is alive. The dome is not. And the relationship between those two states is the defining mystery of this place, the question that has no answer, the thing that will bring you back here even after you have left.

The city breathes. The dome does not. Everything that lives here — the membranes, the stem-walkers, the yellow threads — exists in the space between those two truths.
