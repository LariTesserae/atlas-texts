The things that live in the Clagwall Basin have all made their peace with the noise, though peace is perhaps too gentle a word for it—they have been hammered into shape by the sound, reformed under its relentless press.

The most visible inhabitants are the **Knuckle-Crabs**, which scuttle across the cracked clay flats in armored congregations. They are not true crabs at all, but something older and more ambiguous—thick-shelled arthropods the size of a man’s fist, their carapaces the color of old blood and pitted with rust-pox. They feed on the bacterial films that grow in the warm pools, and they have evolved internal chambers lined with flexible membranes that resonate *against* the drone of the Heart-Engine, creating pockets of silence inside their own bodies. Without this adaptation, the pressure would liquefy their organs. They move in slow, deliberate waves, hundreds at a time, clustering around the bases of the leaning gantries where the condensation drips most steadily. When threatened, they do not flee—they simply clamp down, locking their limbs into rigid braces, becoming small, immovable stones until the danger passes or forgets to exist.

Living alongside the crabs, though rarely seen by them, are the **Pipe-Wardens**. These are not human, though they may have been once, long ago. They are tall, attenuated figures, seven feet or more, their limbs elongated and slightly bent as though grown to fit the curvature of the great copper conduits where they make their homes. Their skin is grey-green, smooth, faintly luminous in the mist, and covered in a slick, protective mucus that smells of ammonia and hot brass. They wear no clothing—clothing would rot in the damp—and their heads are small, narrow, with vestigial eyes buried beneath translucent folds of skin. They do not need sight. They navigate by vibration, feeling the thrum of the Heart-Engine through their bones, mapping the basin by its harmonics and dissonances.

The Pipe-Wardens tend the machines, though “tend” is too purposeful a word. They are more like symbiotes, scraping mineral buildup from the vent openings, clearing blockages in the steam-lines, performing small, instinctual maintenance that has no memory of its original purpose. They do not speak. They communicate through low-frequency pulses emitted from chambers in their throats—sounds so deep they are felt rather than heard, resonating in the chest cavity like a second heartbeat. They are not hostile, but they are territorial in a passive, inevitable way. If you stand too long near one of their access-tunnels, a Pipe-Warden will simply appear, silent and dripping, and stand there until you leave. They do not threaten. They simply *exist* in your space with the weight of someone who has all the time in the world.

In the deepest pools, where the water is warmest and stained amber with dissolved iron, there are the **Weep-Eels**. These are long, ribbon-thin creatures, translucent as wet glass, that coil through the steaming shallows in slow, hypnotic spirals. They have no eyes, no fins, only a lateral line of sensory pores that detect the faintest tremor. They feed on the Knuckle-Crabs when they can, but mostly they filter the water for the microscopic organisms that thrive in the chemical runoff—bacteria that metabolize rust and excrete oxygen in tiny, shining bubbles. The Weep-Eels are beautiful in a way that feels accidental, their bodies catching what little light filters through the mist and refracting it into soft, wavering halos. They are utterly indifferent to anything larger than themselves. If you step into their pool, they will slide around your ankles, cool and slick, and continue their slow, eternal feeding.

High above, in the skeletal frames of the gantries and the fluted chimneys, there are the **Rust-Rooks**—though “rook” is a generous term, as they barely qualify as birds. They are small, hunched creatures, their feathers more like scales, stiff and overlapping, colored in gradients of ochre and black. They have adapted to the noise by becoming nearly deaf; their auditory canals are narrow, calcified, filled with a waxy secretion that deadens the roar to a tolerable murmur. They nest in the hollow spaces of the machinery, building crude cups of woven wire and shredded insulation. They feed on the lichen, pecking it from the metal in slow, methodical strikes, and on the occasional Knuckle-Crab that strays too far from its colony. They do not sing. They do not call. They simply sit, motionless for hours, their heads tucked against the heat radiating from the pipes, waiting.

And then there is the **Heart-Engine itself**, which is alive in the only way that matters here. It is not sentient—not in any way that can be mapped or understood—but it is *aware* in the way a mountain is aware, in the way a river knows the shape of its own bed. It has been running for so long that its purpose has eroded into pure function, stripped of intention. The pistons rise and fall. The boilers churn. The vents exhale. It does not want anything. It does not dream. But it *persists*, and in its persistence, it has become the gravitational center of everything that lives here. The Pipe-Wardens orbit it. The Knuckle-Crabs cluster in its shadow. The Weep-Eels thrive in the heat it sheds into the water. The Rust-Rooks nest in its warmth. It is not a god. It is not a machine. It is simply the thing that makes the Clagwall Basin what it is—a slow, thundering heart that does not know it is beating, and will not stop.

The people who built the Weepworks are gone. Perhaps they became the Pipe-Wardens. Perhaps they simply left, or died, or were absorbed into the damp and the rust. It does not matter. What lives here now has no interest in origins. The Knuckle-Crabs do not remember. The Weep-Eels do not wonder. The Pipe-Wardens do not grieve. They simply continue, locked into the rhythm of the drone, existing in a state of profound, unexamined equilibrium—wet, loud, and eternal.
