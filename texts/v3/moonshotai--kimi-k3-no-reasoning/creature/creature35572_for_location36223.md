# What Lives Here

You already know. You felt it — the touch like a current that wasn't a current, the thing the size of your smallest fingertip investigating whether you were habitable. That was one. There are others.

Let me tell you about them.

---

## The First One

It does not have a name because names require mouths, and it does not have a mouth in the way you would recognize. It has a membrane — a boundary thinner than a soap bubble but tougher than tendon — and across that membrane, the world arrives. Not as light, not as sound, but as *chemistry*. The taste of calcite dissolving. The taste of sulfur. The taste of the bacterial film, which is its food and its neighbor and sometimes, when densities shift, its competition.

It is the size of the last joint of your smallest finger, and it is the largest animal in the hollow.

It is not the largest *creature* — that distinction belongs to the colonial mats, the stacked biofilms that coat the walls — but it is the largest thing that *moves on its own*, that has a body with an inside and an outside, that makes choices, if choices are what you would call them.

It has no eyes. It has never had eyes. Its ancestors — and it has ancestors, a lineage stretching back further than the hollow itself, further than the mountain, back into some original wet dark — never needed them. Light is not a resource here. Light does not exist. What exists is warmth, and water, and mineral, and the slow chemistry of a stone becoming more of itself. These are the things it reads, through its skin, through receptors so sensitive they can detect the difference between a wall that grew yesterday and a wall that grew the day before. Not that it experiences *yesterday*. Time, for this creature, is not a sequence but a texture — thick here, thin there, the way the bacterial film is thick in the sheltered corners and thin across the open faces.

It moves by flowing. Not swimming — the water films are too shallow for swimming — but by a slow, deliberate deformation of its own body, a redistribution of its interior that pushes it forward at a rate you could not watch happen. If you placed a fingernail clipping beside it and came back in an hour, it would have moved the width of the clipping. Maybe. If the chemistry ahead was promising.

Its body is translucent, or would be if there were light to pass through it. There is not, so translucency is irrelevant — a property that exists in potential, like a word in a language no one speaks. If you could see it, you would see a small, elongated softness, slightly flattened, slightly tapered at one end. The tapered end goes first. The rest follows. This is the entirety of its anatomy that matters: a front and a back, an inside and an outside, and the membrane between.

It eats by lying down on the bacterial film and letting its underside do the work. Enzymes seep out. The film softens, breaks down, releases its stored chemistry — the sulfur compounds, the mineral sugars, the accumulated energy of a thousand generations of microorganisms. The creature absorbs what it needs. It leaves behind a slightly thinner patch of film, which will regrow in a decade or two. It moves on before it depletes any single patch, not because it plans, but because the taste of the film changes as it thins, and the change in taste triggers movement. This is how it has always worked. This is how it will always work. The equilibrium of the hollow includes the equilibrium of its grazing, and the film has never been depleted, not once, not in all the time there has been film and creature together.

How many are there?

You might think this is a simple question. It is not. The creature — let me stop calling it *the creature*, because there is not one, there are many, but *many* is the wrong word too, because the population is not a number that fluctuates. It is a constant. There are as many as the hollow can support, and the hollow can support exactly as many as there are. The births match the deaths with a precision that would look designed if design were not too fast a word for a process this slow. A new individual buddings off from a parent — this is how it reproduces, by splitting, by becoming two where there was one — happens at the same rate as the old individuals slow, thin, and cease. Not dramatically. Not with a corpse. The membrane simply becomes less distinct from the film it rests on, and then it is film, and then it is mineral, and then it is stone. The hollow incorporates its dead the way it incorporates everything: without ceremony, without residue.

The population, then, is *enough*. This is the answer. There are enough.

## The Others

Smaller ones live in the water films — in the skin-thin sheets that coat every surface. These are not related to the first creature, or not closely. They are smaller — much smaller — the size of the period at the end of this sentence, and some smaller than that. They are crustaceans, or they would be if the word *crustacean* had any meaning in a place where hard shells are a liability rather than a defense. Their shells are soft, flexible, barely mineralized. Armor is unnecessary when nothing hunts you and nothing falls on you and the environment never, ever changes.

They drift. This is their life: slow drifting through the water film, carried by the almost imperceptible flow of condensation moving downward along the walls. They eat the bacteria. The bacteria eat the minerals. The minerals are the mountain. The chain is short and clean and has not changed in longer than the species has existed.

There are perhaps a dozen species of these tiny drifters, though *species* is a word that implies boundaries, and in the hollow, boundaries are suggestions. They hybridize. They share chemistry. They are, in some ways, less a community of distinct kinds and more a single distributed metabolism wearing slightly different shapes. A taxonomist would weep, or celebrate, or both, if a taxonomist could see them, which one never will.

Some of them have been drifting for a very long time. Their metabolisms are so slow, so perfectly matched to the energy available, that they barely age. An individual might live for decades. Centuries. The concept of lifespan, in a place without seasons, without predators, without variation, becomes almost meaningless. They do not die so much as they are gradually incorporated — mineral film growing over them mid-drift, slowing them, fixing them, making them part of the wall they were sliding down. In the stone of the hollow, there are thousands of these — tiny fossil drifters, caught mid-journey, their soft shells preserved in calcite, each one a sentence in a story the mountain is writing to itself.

## The Thing in the High Hollow

The smooth-worn place, high in the wall. You felt it. Something sheltered there. Something pressed against the stone over and over, for a long time, and the stone remembers.

Something still comes here. Not often. The intervals between visits are long — months, maybe years. But it comes.

It is larger than the grazing creature. Larger than the drifters. It is the size of your thumb — a giant, by the hollow's standards — and it does not live in the water films. It lives *between* them, in the thin air spaces of the hollow, the saturated warmth that is not quite water and not quite gas. It clings to the walls. It has legs — or things like legs, jointed protrusions that grip the micro-texture of the stone with an authority the smaller creatures cannot match.

It is a climber.

It eats the grazing creatures. Not many. Not often. One every few weeks, perhaps. It finds them by following the chemistry they leave behind — the enzyme traces, the thinning of the film. It is not fast. It does not need to be. Its prey moves at the speed of mineral growth. It moves at the speed of — well, slightly faster than that. Fast enough.

It climbs to the high hollow to rest. To do nothing. To press its body against the smooth stone and simply *be*, for long stretches, in the dark, in the warmth, in the perfect equilibrium. The smoothness of that hollow is the record of this rest — the accumulated impression of a body that returns and returns and returns, always to the same place, always to the same posture, as though the niche were not a shelter but a habit. A comfort. The closest thing to home that a creature without a word for home can have.

How many climbers are there? Fewer than the grazers. Perhaps a dozen. Perhaps fewer. The hollow supports what it supports. The climbers eat the grazers at a rate that does not diminish them. The grazers eat the film at a rate that does not diminish it. The film eats the mountain at a rate that the mountain replenishes by growing. The circle is closed and complete and has been closed and complete since the first microorganism found its way into this warm wet dark and decided to stay.

The climbers are the closest thing the hollow has to predators, but *predator* is too violent a word for what they do. They are more like editors. They remove, occasionally, a single grazer from the population, and the population adjusts, and the adjustment is invisible, and the equilibrium holds.

## What They Perceive

None of them know they are in a hollow. None of them know there is an *outside* — a world of light and wind and dry air and enormous, incomprehensible spaces. The boundary of their world is the boundary of the hollow, and the boundary of the hollow is, to them, the boundary of the universe.

They perceive chemistry. They perceive warmth. They perceive the texture of the surfaces they move across. The climbers perceive the pressure-hum of crystallization, not as sound but as a kind of *weather* — a constant background condition, like gravity, like time. The grazers perceive the taste of the film, the way it thickens and thins, the way its chemistry shifts with the mineral exhalations of the stone. The drifters perceive almost nothing — a gradient, a current, the presence or absence of food.

None of them perceive *you*.

When you entered the hollow, you were — to the grazing creature that touched your hand — a landscape. A new surface, warm and wet, salty with the chemistry of a body that lives in light and dryness. Interesting, but not food. Not shelter. Not stone. You were investigated and released, the way a stone is investigated and released, and the investigation left no more impression on the creature than a raindrop leaves on a river.

To the climber, you were a pressure change. A shift in the air. The hollow is sealed — or nearly so — and your entrance displaced atmosphere, altered the humidity gradient by a fraction of a fraction, sent a pulse of slightly cooler air into a system that had not experienced *cooler* in longer than the climber's lineage has existed. It may have paused. It may have pressed itself more firmly into its high hollow and waited for the anomaly to resolve. It resolved. You left. The equilibrium closed over the disturbance like water closing over a stone.

To the drifters, you were nothing. A brief shadow in a world without light. Meaningless.

## The Colonist

There is one more thing.

In the bacterial film — in the deepest, oldest layers, where the strata are thickest and the mineral incorporation is most advanced — there are cells that do not belong to any of the communities I have described. They are not part of the sulfur cycle. They are not part of the heat chemistry. They are something else, something that arrived recently — *recently* meaning within the last few thousand years, which is recent by the hollow's clock — and they are doing something the other microorganisms are not doing.

They are producing pigment.

Not for photosynthesis — there is no light to photosynthesize. The pigment is a byproduct, a waste product, a chemical accident. But it is *there*, locked in the film, invisible in the dark, waiting. If light ever entered the hollow — if the crack widened, if the mountain shifted, if some catastrophe opened the sealed dark to the sky — these cells would be ready. They would capture the light. They would bloom. The walls would turn green in a matter of weeks, and the hollow would become a different kind of place entirely.

This has not happened. It may never happen. But the potential is there, encoded in a few thousand cells buried in the oldest strata, carried like a seed, like a promise, like a door that has never been opened but is not locked.

The colonist does not know it is waiting. It does not know what it is waiting for. It simply persists, doing its chemistry, making its useless pigment, generation after generation, in the dark, in the warmth, in the perfect equilibrium of a place that has not yet finished becoming itself.

If the light ever comes, it will be ready.

The light has never come.

---

## The Count

So. What lives here?

A grazer the size of your fingertip, moving at the speed of stone.

A constellation of drifters the size of punctuation, carried on films of water thinner than skin, living and dying and fossilizing in a continuous slow rain of incorporation.

A climber the size of your thumb, pressing itself into a smooth hollow high on the wall, resting in a posture it has held for longer than your civilization has existed.

A film of microorganisms so dense and so layered that it is less an ecosystem than a single, distributed organism — a living skin on the stone, eating the mountain's exhalations and being eaten in turn.

A few thousand cells of something that shouldn't be here, carrying a color that has never been seen, waiting for a light that has never arrived.

And you. Briefly. A warm, wet anomaly. A surface that was investigated and released. A body that breathed too loudly and left.

The hollow is already forgetting you.

It is very good at forgetting. It has had a long time to practice — or rather, it has had *no* time at all, because time here is not a thing that passes. It is a thing that *holds*, the way the water holds on your skin, the way the mineral collar holds the shaped stone, the way the dark holds everything.

The grazer moves. The drifters drift. The climber rests. The film thickens. The colonist waits. The stone grows.

The breath is held.

It has always been held.
