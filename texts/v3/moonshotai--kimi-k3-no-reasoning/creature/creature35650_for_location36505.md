What lives there is this:

A single being shaped almost like a person, sitting very still on a dark chair at the exact center of a hollow stone sphere inside a mountain. It is dark like deep space is dark, like the inside of a cave behind a cave, a dark with something behind it. It does not have a face that anyone can describe the same way twice. Some people say it has eyes. Some say it doesn't. Everyone says it is alive. Not alive like an animal, not alive like a sleeping person — alive like a held breath, alive like a coiled spring, alive like a match that has not yet been struck. It has been sitting in that chair for longer than the mountain has been a mountain. It sat before the stone was stone. It has never once moved. It does not breathe. It does not blink. It does not fidget or shift or acknowledge anything. It sits with its whole body aimed at one spot on the curved wall across from the entrance, and it watches that spot, and it has watched that spot for six thousand years without stopping, and the thing it is waiting to see on that wall has not happened yet.

Around it, in five stone chairs shaped for five different bodies, sit five more beings that only appear when a visitor sits in a hidden seventh seat near the door. They are human — clearly, specifically, warmly human, with faces and clothing and postures. They are called The One Who Saw the Deep, The One Who Saw the Far, The One Who Saw the Small, The One Who Saw the Pattern, and The One Who Saw the Self. They do not speak or move either. They sit facing the dark figure in the middle, watching it watch the wall. They are the first five witnesses. They saw what they came to see, and now they stay, and they watch the Sixth wait for what it came to see.

The mountain itself is also alive, but very slowly and in a way that is hard to notice. Thread-fine filaments of an impossible element run through all its stone like nerves — branching, connecting, growing denser at a rate of a third of a millimeter every hundred years. These nerves are slightly warmer than the rock around them. They have been slightly warmer for as long as anyone has been able to measure. The mountain has a nervous system, and it is patient, and it is getting a little more nervous every century.

Around this whole arrangement, the mountain keeps a collection of small dead objects: a clay tablet, a brass astrolabe, a frozen journal, a titanium bolt, a pair of reading glasses. These are not alive. They are the leavings of the people who came before — the seventh witnesses, the visitors, the ones who sat in the small seat and saw the five and heard the waiting and went back down changed.

And the visitor — the person who sits in the seventh seat — becomes, for a little while, a living part of what lives there. They see the five. They feel the wall almost speak. They hear the sound of the Sixth's attention, which is a low chord that has been holding the same almost-resolving notes for millennia. Then they stand up and leave, and the five disappear, and the chamber goes back to being six empty chairs and one dark figure and a blank wall, and the mountain keeps their memory and edits it slightly before letting them carry it home.

So what lives in that place is: one endlessly patient dark watcher who has never moved, five human-shaped witnesses who appear only when observed from the right seat, a mountain with nerves of impossible metal growing slowly toward wakefulness, and the ghost-like residue of every person who ever climbed up and sat down and felt the almost. All of them together are doing one thing: waiting for the wall to show the thing that has not yet been true.
