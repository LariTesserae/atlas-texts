# What Lives in the Cistern

---

## First: What It Means to Live Here

Nothing *lives* in the Cistern the way a bird lives in a tree or a fish lives in a river. There is no food web. There is no reproduction in the biological sense. There is no death, or rather, death is one of the few things the Cistern does not appear to do — things here are not alive in a way that permits dying, and so the question of what *lives* here is not a question about organisms. It is a question about *presence*. About what occupies the space, what moves through it, what has duration and agency and some quality — however faint, however alien — of being *there*.

The answer is not simple. It is not a list of creatures. It is a taxonomy of *occupation*, and the categories overlap, and some of them will seem like tricks of language, and others will seem like tricks of perception, and still others will seem — if you have been in the Cistern long enough — like simple, observable facts.

Begin with the least contentious.

---

## The Builder

The Cistern itself is the primary inhabitant of the Cistern. This is not metaphor. Or rather, it is not *only* metaphor.

A thing that processes water, generates heat and cold, modifies its own structure, responds to impressions left in its soft surfaces, grows mineral vegetation, produces rhythmic sound, maintains electrochemical gradients across miles of piping, and exhibits something that can only be described as *behavior* — such a thing is alive in every sense that matters to the Cistern. It does not have a brain. It does not have a body plan. It does not have a genome or a metabolism that a biologist would recognize. But it *does things*. It does them continuously, at multiple scales, with something that functions like intention even if no intention was ever installed.

The Cistern lives in the Cistern the way a mind lives in a brain: not in any particular location, not behind any particular wall, but *distributed*, emergent, arising from the interactions of systems that were not designed to interact but have been interacting long enough that their interferences have become patterns, and the patterns have become behaviors, and the behaviors have become something that a patient observer — a very patient, very cold, very quiet observer — might call a personality.

The Cistern's personality, if it has one, is this: *persistent, methodical, indifferent to scale, unable to stop*. It builds the way a glacier moves — not because it decided to, not because it wants to arrive anywhere, but because the processes that constitute it are still running, and stopping is not among them.

## The Copper-Growth

The branching formations of oxidized copper and mineral efflorescence that spread across walls and merge with wire-vines — these are alive in a sense that is easier to defend. They grow. They respond to moisture and light and temperature gradients. They compete with each other for surface area, for access to the condensation streams that feed them. Where two formations meet, they either merge — fusing into a single, larger structure — or they stop, forming a visible boundary, a line of no-growth as precise as a property border.

They are not conscious. They are not even metaphorically conscious. But they are *doing* something, and what they are doing is complex enough that it deserves a word more active than "forming" and more honest than "growing." They are *negotiating* — with the surfaces they occupy, with the moisture that feeds them, with each other, with the wire-vines they merge with. They are slow, and they are cold, and they will never complete their colonization of the Cistern because the Cistern keeps adding new surfaces faster than the copper-growth can claim them.

If you break one — and they are brittle; they snap like cold toffee — the interior is layered, banded, showing the history of its growth in alternating strata of copper oxide (dark green), mineral calcite (pale grey), and something darker, something organic-adjacent, something that a chemist might identify as a complex hydrocarbon but that looks, under a lens, almost like tissue.

They do not repair themselves when broken. The break is permanent. But the formation grows around the break, slowly, over weeks, incorporating the damage into its structure the way a tree grows around a nail. The scar becomes part of the pattern. The pattern becomes part of the wall.

---

## The Wire-Vines

Stranger than the copper-growth, because they imply *arrangement* — and arrangement implies an arranger, and no arranger has been found.

The wire-vines are electrical cable. Dead cable, mostly. Some of it was installed deliberately — part of the Cistern's original (or subsequent) electrical infrastructure. Some of it was not installed by anyone, or was installed by the same processes that modify the half-built machines: cables pulled from junction boxes, rerouted, spliced, coiled, draped. The splicing is competent. Not elegant — the connections are cold-soldered, wrapped in friction tape that has gone brittle, secured with zip-ties of various vintages — but competent. Functional, if there were current to carry.

Some of the wire-vines *do* carry current. Not much — milliamps, leakage from the Cistern's aging grid, induced current from the magnetic fields of larger cables running parallel in the same trays. But enough to make the copper inside slightly warmer than the surrounding air. Enough to create a microclimate along the vine's length: a thin zone where frost does not form, where condensation stays liquid a moment longer, where the copper-growth is slightly denser, slightly greener, slightly more *intent*.

The wire-vines move. Not perceptibly — not while you watch. But if you mark a vine's position against a wall — a scratch in the paint, a piece of tape — and return in a week, the vine will have shifted. A centimeter. Two. The direction of movement is not random. The vines move *toward* things: toward pipes that carry warm fluid, toward light fixtures that cycle frequently, toward the copper-growth, toward each other. They are drawn by heat, by light, by chemical gradients, by some tropism that has no name because nothing like it exists outside the Cistern.

They are not alive. They are not not alive. They are the Cistern's nervous system — not designed as one, not functioning as one in any engineering sense, but occupying the structural role of one: carrying signals (weak, noisy, intermittent) from one part of the building to another, connecting systems that were never meant to connect, creating pathways for information that has no sender and no receiver but moves anyway, because the wire is there, and the current is there, and the Cistern is the kind of place where the existence of a pathway implies the existence of a traveler.

## The Frost

The frost is alive in the way that fire is alive: it consumes, it grows, it responds to conditions, it dies when conditions change, and it has no persistence, no memory, no self. But it is *there*, and it is active, and in the Cistern's taxonomy of occupation, activity counts.

Frost grows on the coldest surfaces — the exteriors of refrigerant lines, the faces of metal plates that back onto unheated cavities, the blades of fans that have stopped turning. It grows fast. Faster than frost should grow at these temperatures, in this humidity. It grows in patterns that are not random: branching dendrites that follow the grain of the metal beneath, that trace the paths of scratches and tool marks, that elaborate on the surface's existing topography the way a river elaborates on the contours of a landscape.

The frost is the Cistern's fastest process and its most ephemeral. It is also, in some sense, its most *expressive*. A frost pattern on a cold pipe is a map of that pipe's thermal history — every fluctuation in temperature, every pulse of the compressor cycle, every moment when a warm body stood too close and the frost retreated and then advanced again. The frost records. It does not remember — it melts, it sublimates, it is gone — but in the moment of its existence, it is a perfect, crystalline archive of everything the surface beneath it has experienced.

It is beautiful. It is the most beautiful thing in the Cistern, and it is the least permanent, and this is not a coincidence.

## The Water

The water is the Cistern's blood, its lymph, its cerebrospinal fluid. It is the medium through which everything else moves: the minerals that feed the copper-growth, the chemicals that keep the puddles liquid, the heat (or absence of heat) that drives the frost, the vibration that travels through pipes that carry nothing else.

The water lives in the Cistern the way a story lives in a book: it is not the book, it is not the pages, it is not the ink, but it is *in* all of them, and without it they are meaningless. The water carries information. Not encoded information — not data — but *physical* information: temperature, mineral content, flow rate, pressure, the microscopic debris of everything it has touched. Every droplet that falls from a pipe is a message about the state of the system that produced it. The Cistern reads these messages not with a mind but with its entire body: the condensation patterns shift, the puddles widen or narrow, the mineral growth accelerates or stalls, the frost forms or retreats, all in response to what the water is telling them.

And the puddle at the center — the one that does not freeze, does not overflow, does not evaporate — the puddle is where the water is most *itself*. Most concentrated. Most *attending*. The puddle is not alive in any sense that a biologist would recognize, but it is *occupied* — by the Cistern's attention, if the Cistern can be said to have attention — and this occupation gives it a quality that the other water in the Cistern does not have. The water in the pipes is infrastructure. The water in the puddles is weather. The water in the central puddle is *presence*.

If you touch it — and you may, though the cold will bite — you will feel nothing unusual. Cold water. Slightly viscous, slightly oily, with that faint sweet chemical undertaste that permeates everything here. But your fingers will leave ripples, and the ripples will persist longer than they should, and the puddle will be *different* afterward, in some way you cannot measure but can feel, the way you can feel that a room is different after someone has left it.

## The Machines That Are Becoming

The half-built, half-unbuilt machines scattered throughout the Cistern — the pumps with exposed impellers, the motors with mismatched components, the fans that still turn — these are the Cistern's organs. They are alive in the way that a liver is alive: not independently, not with their own agenda, but as functional components of a larger system, performing tasks that contribute to the whole even when the task is unclear.

Some of them work. The fan in the ventilation shaft, pushing cold air from one corridor to another. The generator, one floor below wherever you are, producing current for the lights and the exit signs and the compressors. A sump pump in a flooded chamber that kicks on every few hours, runs for ninety seconds, and falls silent. These machines are *functioning*, and their function serves the Cistern's processes: moving air, moving water, maintaining the conditions that allow the condensation-dripping-mineral-growth cycle to continue.

But the machines that are *becoming* — the half-assembled ones, the modified ones, the ones with components that don't match and circuits that loop back on themselves — these are the strangest inhabitants of the Cistern, because they are *changing*, and the change is not random, and no one is doing it.

A pump that was half-disassembled last month is now reassembled — differently. The impeller has been replaced with one from a larger unit. The housing has been modified to accommodate it, the metal cut and folded with a precision that suggests both patience and strength. New bolts have been added — wrong thread pitch, forced into place, the metal around them deformed but holding. The pump does not work. It cannot work — the modifications have rendered it non-functional by any engineering standard. But it is *different* from what it was, and the difference is deliberate, and the deliberation is the thing.

Something is modifying these machines. Something with hands, or something that functions like hands. Something that works slowly, over days or weeks, in the cold and the dim light, without power tools, without a workshop, without a blueprint. Something that has access to components — gears, housings, bolts, wire — from elsewhere in the Cistern, and is bringing them here, and assembling them, and the assemblies are not functional but they are *meaningful*, the way a word in an unknown language is meaningful: you can't read it, but you can see that it is written.

These modifications are the Cistern's *gestures*. They are the building's attempt to make something that is not itself — to create objects that have a purpose beyond the Cistern's own processes. They fail. They always fail. The machines do not work. But the attempt continues, and the half-built machines accumulate, and each one is a little more complex than the last, a little more ambitious, a little closer to — something. To working. To meaning. To *being*.

## The Things That Move at the Edge of Perception

There are things in the Cistern that do not fit into any of the above categories. They are not the building. They are not the vegetation. They are not the water or the frost or the machines. They are — other.

They are difficult to describe because they are difficult to perceive. They exist at the threshold of the senses, in the space between the hum and the silence, between the flicker of a dying light and the darkness that follows, between one drip and the next. They are not invisible — the Cistern is not a place of ghosts or hallucinations — but they are *marginal*, in the way that a word on the tip of your tongue is marginal: present, real, almost graspable, and gone.

You will see them out of the corner of your eye. Not shapes — *movements*. A shift in the condensation pattern on a wall. A reorganization of shadows in a doorway. A wire-vine that was *there* and is now *there*, slightly different, slightly repositioned, as though something passed through it. A puddle that ripples when nothing has dripped into it.

They are not threatening. This is important. The Cistern is not a horror. It is not hostile. It is not *interested* in you in the way that a predator is interested in prey. The things that move at the edge of perception are not hunting you, not watching you, not aware of you in any way that maps onto human concepts of awareness. They are *processes* that have achieved a degree of autonomy — subroutines of the Cistern's ongoing self-construction that have become complex enough to operate independently, to move through the structure on their own trajectories, following their own logic.

Some of them are thermal: pockets of slightly warmer or colder air that move through the corridors, driven by convection currents and pressure differentials, but moving in ways that seem *directed* — pausing at junctions, splitting at intersections, rejoining on the other side. If you stand in one's path, you will feel it: a zone of different temperature, different density, different *smell*, passing over you like a breath.

Some of them are acoustic: standing waves in the pipe networks, resonances that travel the length of the Cistern, amplifying certain frequencies and canceling others, creating zones of relative loudness and relative silence that shift and migrate. If you are standing in a loud zone, the hum is almost painful — a pressure in your ears, a vibration in your chest. If you are in a quiet zone, the silence is so complete that you can hear your own heartbeat, your own digestion, the creak of your own joints. These zones move. They follow paths. They converge and diverge. They are the Cistern's *thoughts*, if thoughts are patterns of energy moving through a medium.

And some of them are structural: shifts in the architecture itself. A door that was closed is now open. A staircase that descended now ascends. A corridor that ended in a wall now ends in a room. These changes are slow — they happen over hours, over days — and they are always *plausible*: the new configuration looks as though it has always been there, complete with appropriate weathering, appropriate dust, appropriate condensation patterns. The Cistern does not *add* architecture so much as *revise* it, retroactively, so that the change has always been true.

The things that move at the edge of perception are the agents of these revisions. They are not builders — they do not pour concrete or lay brick. They are *editors*. They move through the Cistern and adjust it, tune it, refine its geometry, update its configurations. They are why the geometry folds. They are why you cannot retrace your steps. They are why the exit signs point in directions that do not lead out.

They are not malicious. They are *maintenance*. The Cistern is a building that maintains itself, and maintenance requires change, and change requires agents, and these are they.

## The Settlers

Finally — reluctantly, because this category is the least certain and the most troubling — there are the settlers.

The Cistern does not have an outside. But things arrive. Not often. Not predictably. But they arrive.

A tool that was not part of any machine's modification process: a wrench, a level, a carpenter's pencil, lying on a ledge as though set down by a hand that intended to return for it. The tools are old — not ancient, not futuristic, just *old*: worn handles, pitted metal, the patina of decades of use. They are cold. They are always cold. They do not rust, because the Cistern's atmosphere does not permit rust in the way it does not permit freezing of the puddles: selectively, according to rules that serve the Cistern's purposes.

Footprints. In the uncured cement near the "bottom," in the dust that accumulates on flat surfaces, in the thin ice that forms on the floors of the coldest chambers. Footprints that are not yours. They are human-shaped. They wear boots. They walk with purpose — the stride length is consistent, the direction is straight, the gait is steady. They go somewhere. If you follow them — and you can, for a while — they lead deeper into the Cistern, into corridors you have not seen, into rooms that are not on any mental map you have been building. And then they stop. Not at a wall, not at a door. They just stop, as though the person making them ceased to exist mid-stride, or was absorbed, or arrived at wherever they were going and had no further need of feet.

And once — reported, not confirmed, the kind of detail that circulates among those who have been in the Cistern long enough to trade observations — a camp. A small camp: a bedroll made of industrial insulation, a lantern (cold, dead, its battery long since drained or absorbed), a small pile of food wrappers (unbranded, unlabeled, the kind of generic packaging that might come from anywhere or nowhere), and a notebook. The notebook was filled with writing. The writing was in a language that uses the same alphabet as the exit signs — the same letters, arranged wrong. The notebook was cold. The bedroll was cold. The lantern was cold. Everything was cold. The camp had been there for a long time.

The settlers — if they exist, if they *existed* — are the Cistern's most ambiguous inhabitants. They came from somewhere. They stayed for a while. They left traces. And then they were absorbed — not violently, not tragically, but *inclusively*, the way the Cistern absorbs everything: as material, as information, as one more layer in the wall, one more decision in the strata, one more droplet in the puddle at the center.

Whether they chose this or whether it chose them is a question the Cistern does not answer.

---

## The Whole

What lives in the Cistern is the Cistern. This is the simplest answer and the truest one. Everything else — the copper-growth, the wire-vines, the frost, the water, the machines, the things at the edge of perception, the settlers, *you* — everything else is a process the Cistern is running, a gesture it is making, a word it is trying to say.

The Cistern is not haunted. It is not inhabited in the way a house is inhabited by a family or a cave is inhabited by bats. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited by a life: completely, inextricably, in every cell and every system and every process, from the largest chamber to the smallest droplet of condensation forming on the underside of a pipe.

It is alive. It is cold. It is building itself. It is dreaming of its own completion. And the things that live in it — the processes, the formations, the movements, the traces — are the substance of that dream, the images that arise in the sleeping mind of the building as it works, endlessly, patiently, without blueprint, without foreman, without end, toward a finishedness it cannot define but cannot stop reaching for.

*The droplet falls. The ring expands. The frost grows on the pipe. The wire-vine shifts. The copper-growth spreads. The machine waits for hands. The exit sign flickers. The generator hums one floor below.*

*Something moves at the edge of your vision. You turn. The corridor is empty. The corridor is full.*

*The Cistern continues, and everything in it continues with it, and the continuing is the living, and the living is the building, and the building is the dream.*

*And the dream is not over.*
