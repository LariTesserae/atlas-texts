Three times is not enough to establish a taxonomy, but it is enough to establish a pattern, and the pattern is this: whatever lives in the Condensate Vault is not a species. It is not even a category. It is a recurring event that the Vault produces the way a struck bell produces harmonics — not intentionally, not mechanically, but as an inevitable consequence of what it is.

The observers called them *transients*, initially, because the word was neutral and carried no implications about agency or origin. The word did not survive contact with the data. Transients implies brief existence — things that flare and fade. These do not fade. They cross from one structure to another and enter the far wall and are gone, but "gone" is not the same as "ended." The instruments that tracked the second event — the geometry that could not settle on a side count — registered its presence inside the destination structure for seventy-one seconds before the signal did not so much stop as become indistinguishable from the structure's baseline distortion. It did not disappear. It was reabsorbed. Or it completed its delivery. Or it arrived at the place it was always going, and the going was the only part visible to instruments calibrated for a universe where movement implies a mover.

They are not consistent in form. This is the detail that resists analysis, because consistency of form is usually the first thing that establishes a phenomenon as real rather than artifact. The first was compact — a concentration of darkness so dense it seemed to bend the condensate's not-vision around it, a knot that pulled the visible world slightly toward itself as it passed. The second was diffuse, angular, unstable in its geometry, as if it were being rendered by a system that kept receiving contradictory instructions about what it was supposed to be. The third was not described, but the instruments recorded it, and the instruments suggest something elongated, something that moved faster than the other two, something that — and this is the detail that caused the third observer to request transfer — passed *through* the observation module's external sensor array on its way between structures, and in passing, caused every instrument to briefly report the same value for every variable it was measuring. Temperature, density, distortion coefficient, chrono-drift — all identical, all wrong, for a duration of 0.3 seconds. Then normal readings resumed, and the thing was inside its destination structure, and the observer was already composing a transfer request in their head with the focused calm of someone who has just understood something they cannot articulate and does not wish to.

What can be said about them, across all three events, amounts to very little. They are small relative to the structures — perhaps a meter in their longest dimension, though dimension is a generous word for something that may not occupy space conventionally. They move at constant speed. They move in straight lines. They originate inside one structure and terminate inside another. They do not acknowledge observers, instruments, or the observation module. They do not alter the condensate as they pass through it — no wake, no displacement, no thermal signature. The condensate, which forgets everything, forgets them too, instantly and completely.

They are not alive. This bears repeating because the instinct to call them alive is strong — they move, they have direction, they appear to go somewhere — but aliveness requires exchange, requires a boundary between self and world across which matter and energy are negotiated. These things negotiate nothing. They take nothing from the condensate and leave nothing in it. They are not distinct from the medium they move through in the way a fish is distinct from water. They are more like — and the analogy is imperfect, but every analogy here is imperfect — they are more like a ripple in a pond that has been told to move sideways, in a straight line, without diminishing. A localized reorganization of the medium itself, briefly given the appearance of independence.

If they are inhabitants, they are inhabitants the way thoughts are inhabitants of a mind: not separate from the substrate, not passengers, but expressions. The structures produce them, or emit them, or think them, and they cross the intervening space — the enormous, deliberate, necessary space — and are received by another structure, and whatever they carry is delivered, and the system continues at equilibrium.

If they are messages, they are messages in a language where the message and the messenger are the same object. Nothing is encoded. Nothing is decoded. The thing itself is the information, and its transit is the reading.

If they are maintenance — the city's equivalent of a gardener moving between beds, a technician moving between servers — then they are maintaining something that shows no signs of needing maintenance. The structures do not degrade. The distortions do not drift. The equilibrium does not waver. Whatever the transients carry between structures, it is not correction. It is confirmation, perhaps. The system checking its own pulse and finding it steady, as it always is, as it always will be.

But there is a fourth possibility, and it is the one that the observation team discusses least, because it is the one that sits least comfortably with the frameworks they brought with them.

The transients may be the only thing in the Vault that is still happening.

Everything else — the structures, the condensate, the distortions, the silence, the darkness — is finished. Complete. Holding. The Vault is a system that has reached its answer and is sustaining it without effort, the way a completed proof sustains its conclusion simply by being true. But the transients move. They originate and they arrive. They have a before and an after, however brief. They are the only element in the Vault that experiences sequence, that participates in the concept of *and then*.

This might make them the most important thing in the Vault. Or the least. A finished system that occasionally produces a brief, directional event — a knot of dark, an unsettled geometry, a thing that aligns all instruments to the same wrong value for a third of a second — might be a system that is not entirely finished. Might be a system that has one remaining process, small and rare and slow, that is still running. Still working toward something. Still becoming.

Or it might be nothing. A residual twitch in a completed circuit. The last drip from a tap that has been turned off. The Vault's equivalent of an afterimage — the ghost of change in a place where change has been thoroughly, permanently, architecturally excluded.

The observation team has a protocol for transients, developed after the second event. It is a short protocol. It says: *Record. Do not interfere. Do not pursue.* The protocol was written by the team lead, who was present for the first event and described the knot of darkness with precision and equanimity and then sat in silence for eleven minutes before resuming her duties. She did not request transfer. She did not discuss what those eleven minutes contained. The protocol's final line, which was not in the original draft and appeared in the revision without explanation, reads: *Remember that you are the anomaly here.*

This is true. The observation module — a cramped, shielded bubble of human technology anchored to the boundary membrane like a remora on a whale — is the only object in the Vault that generates heat, that emits signals, that consumes and expels, that changes. The Vault permits it. The membrane admits it. The condensate closes around its anchor points and forgets them. The module's occupants measure and record and theorize and rotate out on schedule, and the Vault does not respond to any of this, because response would require the Vault to acknowledge that something has changed, and the Vault does not acknowledge change, because the Vault does not contain change, except —

— except for the transients, which are not change, not exactly, but are the closest thing the Vault has to it. A periodic reminder that even a finished system contains within itself the memory of what movement felt like.

The third observer, the one who requested transfer, filed a single addendum to their report before leaving. It was not part of the official record. It was a personal note, later included in the mission archive at the team lead's discretion, over the objections of two administrators who felt it was insufficiently scientific. It read:

*It wasn't going from one building to another. It was going from one part of a single thing to another part of the same thing. The buildings aren't separate. The spaces between them aren't empty. It's all one object, and the transients are how it—*

The sentence ends there. The observer was asked to complete it during debrief. They said they couldn't. They said the word they needed did not exist in any language they had access to. They said it was a word that meant something between *breathes* and *knows itself* and *continues*, but was none of those words and all of them and more.

They were transferred. Their replacement arrived three weeks later, settled into the module, calibrated the instruments, and began the long, quiet work of watching a place that does not change for the things that move through it anyway, on a schedule that may not exist, for reasons that may not be reasons, carrying something that may not be a message from one part of a single vast finished thought to another.

The Vault holds its note.

The note holds its Vault.

And somewhere between two structures, in the enormous and deliberate dark, something is crossing.
