# The Dwellers of the Waterlands

---

## The Undecided

They were the first things you noticed, if "noticed" is the right word—more like your eyes kept sliding off them, the way light slides off wet stone, until eventually your brain *insisted* on assembling something from the fragments.

The Undecided are what happens when life refuses to commit.

They drift through the shallow basins in loose, unhurried aggregations—not schools, not herds, nothing so organized. Just *proximities*. Each one is roughly the length of your forearm, though "roughly" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, because their dimensions are never quite the same from one moment to the next. They are translucent, but not cleanly so—not like glass or water. More like *the memory of translucence*. You can see through them, but what you see on the other side arrives slightly altered, as if their bodies edit reality in passing, shifting colors, softening edges, making the world beyond them just slightly less certain of itself.

Their forms are the strangest thing. An Undecided might begin as something fishlike—sleek, tapered, built for speed it will never use—and then, over the course of an hour, a day, a week, begin to *reconsider*. Fins broaden into paddles. A tail bifurcates, then trifurcates, then becomes something with no name in any taxonomy. Limbs bud and retract. Eyes migrate. Mouths open in new places and close in old ones. It's not metamorphosis in any biological sense—there's no fixed sequence, no endpoint. It's more like watching someone *think out loud* with their body.

They eat, but no one has ever determined *what*. They simply open whatever aperture currently serves as a mouth and let the water flow through them, and whatever they extract from it is too subtle to detect. Perhaps they eat the *possibility* of food. Perhaps the water itself is enough—the Waterlands are so saturated with dissolved everything that swimming through them might be the same as eating them.

They are not social, exactly, but they are *aware of each other* in a way that produces a strange effect: when several Undecided occupy the same pool, they begin to *resemble* one another. Not perfectly—never perfectly. But their forms drift toward a loose consensus, as if they're trying on each other's shapes the way you might try on a coat. Then something disrupts the consensus—a shift in the water, a change in the light, or nothing at all—and they scatter their forms again, each one becoming something new.

They have no predators. This is not because nothing wants to eat them but because nothing can quite figure out *what they'd be eating*.

## The Ridge-Keepers

On the highest points of the silt ridges—those footpath-wide spines of compacted mud that divide the world into cells—live the things that tend the ghost-plants.

They are small. That much is consistent. Roughly the size of a clenched fist, with a body plan that suggests *crustacean* if you squint and *insect* if you don't and *something else entirely* if you look too long. They have too many legs—or perhaps exactly the right number for a creature that walks on surfaces that might not be there tomorrow. Their carapaces are the color of wet silt, which makes them nearly invisible against the ridges they inhabit, and their eyes—if those are eyes—are arranged in a ring around the crown of their bodies, giving them a perpetually startled expression that may or may not reflect their inner state.

They tend the ghost-plants with a devotion that seems almost religious. They crawl along the twisted, sideways-growing branches, scraping away excess moisture with delicate mandibles, repositioning the scale-like leaves to catch the strange ambient light, and—this is the part that defies explanation—*singing* to them. Not audibly, not in any range a human ear could detect. But if you press your hand against the bark of a ghost-plant while a Ridge-Keeper is nearby, you can feel a vibration, a low, steady hum that seems to resonate through the wood and into your bones.

The ghost-plants respond to this attention by *not dying*. This is more remarkable than it sounds, because by every known metric, ghost-plants should be dead. They have no functioning roots, no chlorophyll, no visible means of sustenance. They subsist, as the landscape does, on *dampness itself*—on the idea of moisture, on the perpetual wetness that saturates everything. And the Ridge-Keepers, in their endless, patient tending, seem to *remind* the plants that they are, in fact, still alive. Or at least still *present*.

There is a theory—held by no one in particular, since no one lives here long enough to hold theories—that the Ridge-Keepers and the ghost-plants are the *same organism*, separated by some ancient schism into mobile and sessile forms. The Ridge-Keepers are the plants' way of moving; the plants are the Ridge-Keepers' way of thinking. This is almost certainly wrong, but it *feels* right, in the way that many things in the Waterlands feel right without being true.

## The Submerged

Beneath the surface—in the deep basins where the water is thick with silt and the strange light barely penetrates—there are *other things*.

You don't see them. You *sense* them, the way you sense a storm coming—through pressure, through stillness, through the sudden, inexplicable feeling that you are being *considered* by something vast and patient and utterly unconcerned with your existence.

The Submerged are old. Older than the ruins that dot the landscape, older than the ridges, possibly older than the water itself. They are large—not whale-large, not building-large, but *landscape-large*. Each one occupies an entire basin, their bodies spread out beneath the mud in a vast, sprawling network that might be a single organism or might be many organisms fused together or might be something for which the concept of *organism* is inadequate.

They are the reason the Waterlands breathe.

The slow, rhythmic pulse of the water—the rise and fall that has nothing to do with tides—is the Submerged *circulating*. They draw water through their bodies in great, slow cycles, filtering it, processing it, and releasing it back into the landscape. They are, in effect, the Waterlands' *kidneys*, or their *lungs*, or their *heart*—some essential organ whose function is too vast and too slow to be perceived as anything other than *weather*.

Occasionally, a Submerged will *shift*. This is a catastrophic event. The basin above it bulges, the water heaves, the ridges crack and reform. New channels are carved. Old pools vanish. The Undecided scatter. The Ridge-Keepers cling to their plants with desperate tenacity. And then, slowly, the world settles back into its new configuration, and the Submerged goes back to its slow, patient circulation, as if nothing happened.

No one has ever seen a Submerged. No one ever will. They are too large, too deep, too thoroughly integrated with the landscape to be *perceived* as separate entities. They are less *creatures* than *geological processes that happen to be alive*.

## The Rememberers

In the half-submerged ruins—the fitted stones, the broken columns, the curved edges of domes that are no longer domes—there are things that *remember*.

Not remember in the way you remember—not as a narrative, not as a sequence of events. The Rememberers are not minds. They are not even *conscious*, in any meaningful sense. They are more like *residues*—the accumulated weight of all the things that were *built* here, compressed into the stones themselves, given a kind of slow, geological *agency* by the perpetual damp.

If you press your hand against one of the half-buried walls, you will feel it. Not a vibration, not a sound—*a pressure*. A steady, insistent *push* against your palm, as if the stone is trying to *tell you something*. And if you hold your hand there long enough—minutes, hours, days—the pressure will begin to *resolve* into something almost like an image. Not a picture, not a memory, but a *sense* of what this place was before it became what it is. A city. A temple. A home. A *world* that was solid and separate and *one thing* rather than *everything*.

The Rememberers are not trying to *restore* that world. They are not *nostalgic*. They are simply *holding* the memory, the way a stone holds heat long after the fire has gone out. They are the Waterlands' *archive*, the place where the past is stored not as a story but as a *texture*—a quality of the stone, a density in the water, a particular shade of light that falls on a particular wall at a particular angle.

They are the closest thing the Waterlands has to *history*. And they are, in their slow, silent way, *fading*.

## The Walkers

And then there are the ones who come from *outside*.

Not often. Not in any organized way. But occasionally—once a decade, once a century, the intervals are impossible to measure—someone *walks in*. A traveler. A wanderer. Someone who heard about the Waterlands and came to see them, or someone who was lost and ended up here by accident, or someone who was *called* by something they couldn't name.

They don't leave.

Not because they can't. Not because the Waterlands *traps* them—there's no barrier, no force field, no magical compulsion. They don't leave because, after a while, they *stop wanting to*. The Waterlands does something to them—something slow, something gentle, something that operates below the threshold of conscious awareness. It *blurs* them. Softens their edges. Makes them less *separate*, less *defined*, less *one thing* as opposed to *another*.

They become Walkers. They move through the landscape with the same deliberate slowness as the Undecided, their forms gradually *loosening*, their outlines becoming less certain. They don't age—they *dissolve*, but so slowly that the process looks like stillness. They lose their names first, then their memories, then their faces, then their *shapes*. Eventually, they become something that might be human or might be water or might be *both*, and they join the great, wet, roaring *whole*.

The Walkers are not *tragic*. They are not *lost*. They are, in the truest sense, *found*—found by a world that has been waiting for them, that has been *breathing* and *shifting* and *dissolving* for millennia, and that has finally, at long last, *received* them.

They are the Waterlands' *future*. They are what the world is *becoming*. And they are, in their slow, silent, *beautiful* way, *home*.
