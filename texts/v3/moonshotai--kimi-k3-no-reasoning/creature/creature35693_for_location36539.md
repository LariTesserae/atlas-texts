What lives in the Hollow Basin has learned, over a span of time that has no clear beginning, to exist in the pauses between things.

The most visible inhabitants are the **clay-walkers** — though "walker" is generous, and "clay" only partially accurate. They are small, perhaps the size of a person's hand spread flat, and their bodies have taken on the exact buff-pale tone of the basin floor. Their skin is not skin but something closer to the clay itself: matte, slightly rough, faintly warm to the touch, and when they are still — which is almost always — they are indistinguishable from the ground. You could stand a meter away and not see them. You could stand a *step* away and not see them.

They move, but the movement is so slow that it exists on a timescale the basin recognizes and you don't. A clay-walker might cross from one side of a pool to the other over the course of what you would call weeks. It does not hurry. There is nothing to hurry toward. Its locomotion is not a crawl or a scuttle but a kind of patient redistribution of its own mass — the leading edge extends, anchors, draws the rest forward, anchors again. It leaves no track. The clay closes behind it, or it presses so lightly that the clay doesn't notice.

There might be forty of them in the basin. There might be four hundred. It is genuinely impossible to know, because counting them would require watching one spot for longer than your patience — perhaps longer than your life — allows.

What they eat is unclear. They don't graze the plants. They don't skim the water's surface. The most likely answer — the one that feels truest to the basin's logic — is that they metabolize the clay itself, drawing from it some faint mineral or microbial richness that sustains them at the same rate that everything else here operates: barely, adequately, without excess. They are not hungry. They are not full. They exist in the narrow band between the two, the same band the temperature occupies, the same band the light occupies.

---

Beneath the pools, in the compacted sediment, live the **listeners**. You would never see them. They are smaller than the clay-walkers, soft-bodied, translucent to the point of near-invisibility, and they spend their entire existence pressed against the undersides of the pool floors, facing down. What they are listening to is the basin itself — the slow, almost imperceptible shifting of the deep clay layers, the thermal murmur of groundwater moving through strata far below, the occasional distant shudder of the earth adjusting its weight. They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. Their entire body is a sensory membrane, and what it senses is vibration — the faintest, slowest, most patient vibrations a landscape can produce.

They do not respond to what they hear. They don't flee from tremors or migrate toward warmth. They simply register. They are the basin's memory, or something like it — a distributed, living record of every small event that has ever disturbed the stillness, encoded in bodies that will never speak of it, never act on it, never do anything with the information except hold it. If a clay-walker crosses a pool above them, they feel it. If rain falls, they feel it. If nothing happens for a century, they feel that too, and it is the same to them as everything else: data, absorbed, kept.

Their lifespan is unknown. It may be that they don't die in any conventional sense — that they divide occasionally, slowly, and the new ones take up positions beside the old, and the sediment floor of each pool is a dense, invisible layering of generations, all pressed together, all listening, all holding everything.

---

The plants are not alone in their stillness. Each one hosts a single **stem-dweller** — a creature so small and so perfectly matched to the plant's gray-brown coloring that it took (or would have taken) careful observation over an absurdly long time to confirm that the stem-dwellers are alive and not simply irregularities in the bark. They are positioned at the junction where leaf meets stem, tucked into the angle, and they do not move. Ever. Their bodies are soft, wingless, legless — more like a small knot of living tissue than an insect — and they draw sustenance directly from the plant's vascular system, a slow siphon of sap so gentle that the plant doesn't diminish. Or diminishes so slowly that its own negligible growth rate absorbs the loss.

The relationship is not symbiotic in any way that benefits the plant. It is not parasitic in any way that harms it. It is simply *adjacent* — two lives occupying the same small space at the same immeasurable pace, neither one aware of the other in any sense that would produce behavior, neither one capable of leaving.

A stem-dweller lives as long as its plant lives. When the plant eventually — after a span of time that might be centuries, might be millennia — fails, the stem-dweller fails with it, and both return to the clay at the same rate, in the same place, without ceremony.

---

There is one more thing.

The structure on the south rim — the low, open, clay-formed niche — is not empty. It contains the **keeper**, though "keeper" implies a role, a duty, a relationship to the structure that may not exist. What is true is that something is inside, and it has been inside for as long as the structure has been there, and the two have become so thoroughly associated that separating them conceptually requires an effort the basin itself would find unnecessary.

The keeper is difficult to describe because it is difficult to see. Not because it is hidden — it sits in plain view, in the center of the structure's bare floor — but because it shares the quality that everything in the basin shares: it has so thoroughly matched itself to its surroundings that perceiving it requires a recalibration of attention that most observers can't sustain. It is roughly the size of a curled sleeping cat. It is the color of the structure's interior walls, which is the color of the clay, which is the color of everything. Its surface is smooth and matte and faintly warm. It does not appear to have limbs, or a face, or any features at all. It is a shape — a settled, resting, permanent shape — that happens to be alive.

It breathes. This is the only clear evidence of its aliveness, and even the breathing is so slow and so shallow that you would need to sit beside it for an hour to confirm it. A slight rise. A long pause. A slight fall. A longer pause. The rhythm is not urgent. It is the rhythm of a body that requires almost nothing and receives almost nothing and has no complaints about the arrangement.

What it is — what species, what origin, what purpose — the basin does not say. The structure does not say. The keeper does not say. It sits in its niche on the south rim, and it breathes, and the dim gray light falls on it evenly, and the still air touches it without moving it, and it does not turn to look at you if you approach, because it has nothing to look with and nothing to look for, and if you reached out to touch it, your fingers would meet a surface that is warm and smooth and very slightly yielding, like clay that has not quite finished drying, and the keeper would not react, because reaction is a thing that happens in places where time has consequences, and the Hollow Basin is not one of those places.

---

These are the inhabitants. They do not interact. The clay-walkers do not know about the listeners. The listeners register the clay-walkers' passage but do not respond. The stem-dwellers are unaware of everything except the slow pulse of sap. The keeper is unaware of everything, or aware of everything in a way that produces no output, and the difference between those two states is one the basin has no interest in clarifying.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing competes. The populations do not grow or shrink, or if they do, the rate is so slow that growth and shrinkage are indistinguishable from stability. There are no young. There are no old. There are only the present inhabitants, in their present forms, in their present locations, persisting.

They are alive in the way the basin is alive: minimally, patiently, without narrative. They do not have stories. They have durations. They do not have behaviors. They have states. They exist the way the pools exist — as facts, as features, as things that are here and will continue to be here until the equilibrium shifts, which it will not, which it never has, which it shows no sign of ever doing.

The basin holds them the way the clay holds its cracks: not tightly, not loosely, just permanently. They are part of the pattern. They found their places long ago, or their places found them, or the distinction between those two possibilities dissolved in the same long stillness that dissolved everything else — urgency, purpose, change, time.

They live here. That is all. That is everything. The light is dim. The air is still. The water does not ripple. The plants do not bloom. And the inhabitants of the Hollow Basin persist in their quiet, pale, immeasurable lives, and nothing moves, and nothing needs to.
