# The Inhabitants of Cavreth

---

## The Ones Who Are the City

Before anything else, there are the ones who are the city itself.

The tower-skin organisms have no individual names because they have no individual selves worth naming. Each is a polyp — a small, translucent animal no longer than a grain of rice, anchored to the mineral lattice of its tower, connected to its neighbors by threads of shared tissue that carry nutrients, chemical signals, and the faint electrical impulses that coordinate the tower's light. A single tower might be composed of two hundred million such polyps, and they are, in every meaningful sense, one organism — one vast, distributed, sessile creature shaped like a building.

They are not intelligent. They are not unintelligent. They are something for which those categories are inadequate. They respond to stimuli — the touch of a resident entering a chamber, the chemical signature of a predator passing in the current-street, the slow oscillation of the drifters' gatherings — and their responses are coordinated across scales that would be impressive in a brain. A disturbance on one side of a tower can produce a change in luminescence on the other side within seconds, a wave of adjusted brightness that ripples through the polyp network like a shrug.

They live a long time. Individual polyps die and are replaced, but the colonial network — the shared tissue, the chemical memory encoded in the matrix of the tower's skin — persists for as long as the tower stands. The oldest towers carry, in some sense, a hundred thousand years of accumulated response-patterns, a library of stimuli and reactions written into the colony's distributed biology. They have seen everything that has happened in Cavreth, and they have responded to all of it, and the responses have shaped the towers' growth, and the growth has shaped the city, and the city is, in this way, a record of its own history — not a record that can be read, exactly, but a record that can be lived in.

They glow. This is their most visible function and their most important one. The bioluminescence is produced by symbiotic bacteria housed in specialized light-organs within each polyp's body — tiny chambers where luciferin meets luciferase in the ancient chemical reaction that converts energy into photons. The polyps feed these bacteria, shelter them, regulate their light-output with a precision that suggests long co-evolution. The light they produce is blue-green, the wavelength that travels farthest through seawater, and its intensity varies according to patterns that the colony controls.

What the patterns mean is not simple. A tower's glow is not a single signal but a layered one: a baseline intensity that indicates occupancy-density, a slow pulse that reflects the thermal rhythm of the seeps, a faster flicker that encodes local information — a predator in the vicinity, a vacancy in a chamber, a disturbance in the current. The residents of the tower read these patterns the way you read the expression on a familiar face: not by decoding individual signals but by perceiving the whole, the gestalt of light and rhythm that tells you what you need to know.

The tower-skin organisms are not aware of being read. They are not aware of being lived in. They are aware, if the word applies at all, of light and chemistry and pressure and the slow accretion of time. They are the city's body, and they go about the business of being a body with the same unreflective competence that your own cells bring to the business of being you.

## The Cleaners

The cleaning stations of Cavreth's squares are staffed by a species of shrimp that has no name in any human language and needs none. They are small — the largest barely two centimetres — and they are transparent, their bodies so clear that they are visible only when their internal organs catch the ambient light: a thread of digestive tract, a pulse of heart, the dark specks of their eyes floating in a body made of nothing.

They work in aggregations. A cleaning station is a specific location — a flat expanse of basalt in a square, a particular strut of a tower's lattice, a shelf of mineralized sediment — where the shrimp congregate in densities of several hundred per square metre. They wave their antennae in a specific pattern, a slow, beckoning semaphore that is universally understood in Cavreth: *I am available. Come to me. I will tend you.*

And the residents come. Fish arrive and hover, orienting themselves to present specific surfaces — gills, flanks, the sensitive skin around the eyes. The shrimp swarm over them, picking at parasites, dead tissue, the bacterial films that accumulate on any surface in warm water. They are thorough and they are fast and they are absolutely fearless: a shrimp will climb into the open mouth of a predator and clean between its teeth with the confidence of a creature that knows its services are more valuable than its meat.

The relationship is ancient. The cleaning stations have been operating in the same locations for thousands of years, and the shrimp that staff them are the descendants of shrimp that cleaned the ancestors of the fish they now clean, in an unbroken chain of mutual benefit that stretches back to the city's youth. Neither species is conscious of this history. Neither needs to be. The behaviour is inscribed in their biology, refined by selection until it is as reliable as any instinct, as automatic as any heartbeat.

But there is something in the way the shrimp work — a thoroughness, a precision, a quality of attention — that resists the word "automatic." They are not machines. They make choices: which parasite to remove first, how long to spend on a particular wound, whether to accept a client whose chemical signature suggests it has recently eaten a cleaner (it happens; the shrimp have ways of knowing). They are professionals, in the way that evolution produces professionals: not through training or intention but through the simple, brutal process of doing a job well or dying.

The cleaning stations are also, incidentally, information hubs. The shrimp touch everything. They taste the chemical signatures on their clients' skin — where the fish has been, what it has eaten, what it has encountered. Whether the shrimp process this information or simply carry it is unknown, but the stations are nodes in Cavreth's communication network, places where the chemical data of the city's population is concentrated and, potentially, exchanged.

## The Eels

The narrow passages of the towers — the tubes and channels that run between chambers, connecting the interior spaces like hallways — are the territory of the eels.

They are not true eels, or not all of them. The most common species is a elongated fish that has converged on the eel body-plan: long, sinuous, scaleless, with reduced fins and a jaw structure that allows it to strike with surprising speed in confined spaces. They are dark — black, or a brown so deep it reads as black in the tower-light — and they are almost entirely blind, navigating the narrow passages by touch and by the lateral-line system that detects the faintest water displacement.

They live in the walls. This is the simplest way to put it. The tower lattices contain channels that were not designed for habitation — structural voids, gaps between struts, spaces that exist because the tower's growth pattern produces them incidentally. The eels have claimed these spaces. They occupy them the way rats occupy the walls of a human building: not as residents in any formal sense but as a presence, a rustle in the architecture, a movement in the spaces between spaces.

They hunt by waiting. An eel will position itself in a passage that connects two chambers — a transit route, a hallway — and it will wait, motionless, for hours or days, until something passes. The strike is fast and usually fatal. The prey is swallowed whole, if it is small enough, or dragged back into the eel's recess to be consumed at leisure.

The tower-skin organisms respond to the eels' presence by dimming the luminescence in their immediate vicinity. Whether this is a defensive response — reducing the light that might attract prey to the eel's ambush site — or simply a reaction to the eel's chemical signature is unclear. The effect, from the perspective of the tower's other residents, is a patch of darkness in the wall, a zone of reduced visibility that experienced residents learn to avoid. The towers, in this way, warn their inhabitants about the predators within them. The warning is not deliberate. It is simply what the tower does.

The eels are not social. They do not cooperate. They tolerate each other's presence in adjacent recesses, and they mate when the chemistry of the water tells them it is time, and beyond that they are alone. They are the city's quiet danger, the threat that lives inside the walls, and they are accepted as such — not welcomed, not resisted, simply factored into the calculations of every creature that navigates the tower's interior.

## The Octopuses

They deserve more than a paragraph, and they would be the first to tell you so, if they could tell you anything.

The octopuses of Cavreth are large — the largest species has an arm-span of nearly three metres — and they are old, by octopus standards. Surface octopuses live fast and die young: two years, sometimes three, a brief blaze of intelligence and appetite and then senescence and death. The octopuses of Cavreth live longer. The deep cold slows their metabolism; the steady warmth of the thermal seeps moderates it. They live five years. Seven. Perhaps more. Long enough to learn. Long enough to remember.

They are the most cognitively complex creatures in the city, and they behave as though they know it. Not with arrogance — octopuses do not do arrogance, or if they do, it looks like something else — but with a quality of deliberateness, of considered action, that sets them apart from the city's other residents. A fish moves through Cavreth the way a commuter moves through a subway station: purposefully, but on rails, following patterns so deeply ingrained they are indistinguishable from instinct. An octopus moves through Cavreth the way a person walks through a neighborhood they know well: attentively, with preferences, with opinions.

They have opinions. This is the thing about them that is hardest to convey without resorting to anthropomorphism, and perhaps a little anthropomorphism is warranted here, because the octopuses of Cavreth are, in ways that matter, persons. Not human persons. Not persons in the way a chimpanzee or a dolphin might be a person — persons built on a completely different neurological architecture, persons whose intelligence is distributed through their arms as much as concentrated in their central brain, persons who think with their skin.

They choose their chambers. This is not trivial. A vacant chamber in a tower is assessed by multiple octopuses — sometimes simultaneously, sometimes in sequence — and the assessment is thorough. The octopus enters, explores, touches every surface. It tests the entrances: are they defensible? Can they be blocked? It evaluates the light: is the tower-skin's luminescence in this chamber adequate? Too bright? Too dim? It checks the current: does water flow through the chamber, bringing oxygen and carrying away waste? Is the flow too strong, too weak?

And then it decides, or it doesn't. An octopus that rejects a chamber moves on. An octopus that accepts a chamber begins, immediately, to modify it.

The modifications are the thing. The octopuses of Cavreth are interior decorators. They collect objects — shells, stones, fragments of mineralized tower-material, the shed exoskeletons of crustaceans — and they arrange them. The arrangements are not random. They are not functional in any obvious way: they do not improve the chamber's defensibility or its water flow. They are aesthetic, or they are territorial, or they are something that maps onto no human category. The octopus places a shell here, a stone there, a fragment of old lattice at the chamber's entrance, and it maintains this arrangement with a consistency that borders on obsession. If the arrangement is disturbed — by a current, by another organism, by the slow shifting of the tower itself — the octopus restores it. Exactly. Precisely. As though the arrangement matters.

Perhaps it does. Perhaps the arrangements are a form of communication — a signal to other octopuses, a marker of occupancy and identity. Perhaps they are a form of memory — a physical record of the octopus's experiences, encoded in the placement of objects. Perhaps they are simply what an octopus does when it has a large brain and a long life and a chamber to itself. The octopuses are not explaining.

They hunt at the edges of the city. This is a pattern, not a rule, but it is consistent enough to be noticeable: the octopuses of Cavreth prefer to hunt outside the tower clusters, in the open sediment beyond the city's luminous core. They hunt crabs and shrimp and small fish, and they hunt with a patience and a creativity that is characteristic of their kind: reaching into crevices, probing with arm-tips, using jets of water to flush prey from hiding. They are good hunters. They are also, when the opportunity arises, cannibals — smaller octopuses are fair game, and the larger ones know it.

Within the city, they are restrained. They do not hunt in the towers. They do not harass the cleaning stations. They move through the current-streets with a fluid, boneless grace that is unsettling to watch — an octopus in open water is not the clumsy crawler of the seafloor but something else entirely, a creature that flies through the water with slow pulses of its mantle, arms trailing like ribbons, changing color and texture as it passes through zones of different light.

They watch the drifters. This has been mentioned, and it bears repeating, because it is the strangest thing the octopuses do, and they do it consistently. When the drifters gather in the squares — those slow, purposeless, humming congregations — the octopuses of the nearby towers emerge from their chambers. They position themselves at openings. They orient toward the squares. They remain motionless for the duration of the gathering — hours, sometimes — and then they return to their chambers and resume their lives.

They are watching. That much is clear. Their eyes — large, complex, structurally similar to vertebrate eyes through a coincidence of convergent evolution that still startles biologists — are fixed on the drifters. Their chromatophores are still. Their arms are curled beneath them. They are, in every observable way, paying attention.

What they perceive, what they understand, what they conclude — if they conclude anything — is unknown. The octopuses of Cavreth keep their own counsel.

## The Drifters

They are the city's mystery, and they are numerous enough that the mystery cannot be dismissed as an anomaly.

A drifter, observed at close range — if anything in Cavreth can be said to observe anything else at close range, in a world where vision is bioluminescent and distance is measured in chemical gradients — is a translucent, roughly ovoid body, perhaps two metres in its longest dimension, filled with structures that are clearly organs but whose functions are not obvious. There is a central mass — denser, less transparent — that pulses with a slow, regular contraction, like a heart but more complex, its rhythm varying in ways that suggest it is doing more than moving fluid. Around this central mass, distributed through the drifter's body, are smaller structures: nodes, filaments, chambers that fill and empty, areas of greater or lesser density that shift over time.

The drifters are faintly luminous. Their bioluminescence is different from the tower-glow — dimmer, warmer in tone, and distributed unevenly across their bodies, so that a drifter appears as a shape made of soft, shifting light, like a lantern seen through fog. The light does not appear to be communicative — it does not flash or pulse in patterns. It simply is, the way body heat simply is: a byproduct of metabolism, of the chemical processes that keep the drifter alive.

They move by adjusting their buoyancy. They have no fins, no jet propulsion, no cilia. They rise and sink by changing the density of their internal fluids, and they steer — if steering is the right word for something so slow — by asymmetric contraction of their outer membrane, a gentle, almost imperceptible reshaping that nudges them in one direction or another. In the current-streets of Cavreth, they drift with the flow, adjusting their position with small corrections that keep them in the center of the channel, away from the towers, away from the seafloor.

They are everywhere in the city, but they are not dense. A current-street might carry one drifter per hundred metres — a slow procession of faint, glowing shapes moving through the dark water like thoughts through a mind. They do not interact with each other during these transits. They do not interact with the towers, the residents, the current. They are, in the most literal sense, passing through.

Until they gather.

The gatherings are the thing that makes the drifters more than a curiosity. They happen in the squares — always in the squares, never in the towers, never in the open sediment — and they involve anywhere from a dozen to several hundred drifters, converging from all parts of the city over a period of hours. They arrive slowly, individually, each drifter adjusting its buoyancy and position until it finds its place in the configuration.

The configurations are not random. This is the part that is difficult to explain without resorting to language that implies intention. The drifters arrange themselves in three-dimensional patterns — not geometric, not symmetrical, but structured. There are clusters within clusters. There are spaces between individuals that are maintained with precision. There are layers — some drifters higher in the water column, some lower — and the layering follows a logic that is consistent from gathering to gathering, though the specific configuration is never quite the same.

And then they hum.

The sound is produced by the central mass — the pulsing organ — but it is not the sound of a single drifter, amplified. It is the sound of all the drifters, synchronized. Their individual pulses, normally asynchronous, fall into alignment within minutes of the gathering's formation, and the result is a single, low-frequency oscillation that fills the square and resonates through the surrounding towers. It is a sound that is felt more than heard: a vibration in the water, in the sediment, in the mineral lattice of the towers, in the bodies of every creature in the vicinity.

It lasts for hours. Two, three, sometimes five or six. During this time, the drifters do not feed, do not mate, do not do anything that can be identified as biologically functional. They simply hold their configuration and hum.

And then they disperse. Slowly, individually, each drifter adjusting its buoyancy and drifting away from the square, resuming its solitary transit through the city's current-streets. The gathering ends as gradually as it began, and within an hour the square is empty again, and the city returns to its baseline rhythm, and the towers resume their asynchronous glow.

The octopuses watch. The tower-skins brighten, fractionally, during the gatherings — a response to the vibration, perhaps, or to the chemical signals that the drifters release during their humming. The cleaning stations continue their work. The eels remain in their walls. The city accommodates the gatherings the way a body accommodates a heartbeat: as something that happens, something that is part of the rhythm, something that does not require explanation because it simply is.

The drifters are not intelligent in the way the octopuses are intelligent. They do not solve problems, do not learn, do not display the behavioral flexibility that characterizes cephalopod cognition. But they are not simple, either. Their internal structures are complex — more complex than they need to be for a creature that drifts and feeds and occasionally hums. Their gatherings are coordinated — more coordinated than they need to be for a creature with no apparent social structure. And their relationship with the city is peculiar: they are the only large organisms in Cavreth that do not interact with the towers, the only residents that seem to exist in parallel with the city's economy rather than within it.

They are the city's mystery, and the city, being a city, contains them the way all cities contain their mysteries: without resolving them, without needing to.

## The Sixgills

They are not residents, exactly. They are visitors — regular, predictable, and significant.

The sixgill sharks of Cavreth are old. Not old as individuals — though they are that, too, some of them, with the slow growth and long lifespan of deep-water sharks — but old as a lineage. Sixgills are among the most ancient shark species, virtually unchanged for tens of millions of years, and they carry that antiquity in their bodies: the extra gill slits, the primitive jaw structure, the eyes that are green in the light of a submersible's lamps, though no submersible has ever been here.

They patrol the city's perimeter. Not in any coordinated way — they are not guards, not watchdogs — but because the perimeter is where the hunting is. The outer edges of the city, where the tower clusters thin and the current-streets dissolve into open sediment, are where the city's residents are most vulnerable: away from the complexity of the tower interiors, exposed, visible in the ambient glow. The sixgills cruise these margins with the unhurried confidence of apex predators, their massive bodies — four metres, five, sometimes more — moving through the water with a slowness that is not lethargy but patience.

They are not fast. They do not need to be. A sixgill's hunting strategy is based on proximity and opportunity: it drifts close to its prey — close enough that the prey's lateral line should be screaming, close enough that any reasonable creature would flee — and then it lunges, a short, explosive burst of speed that covers the last metre in a fraction of a second. The prey is usually something that has strayed too far from the towers: a fish, an octopus caught in the open, a drifter that has wandered too close to the city's edge.

The drifters are taken, occasionally. A sixgill will bite one — a slow, deliberate bite, the shark's multiple rows of teeth sinking into the gelatinous body — and the drifter will not resist. It cannot resist. It has no escape response, no defensive mechanism, no ability to flee. The sixgill consumes what it can and moves on, and the remaining drifters do not react. They do not flee, do not scatter, do not alter their behavior in any observable way. If a drifter is taken from a gathering, the remaining drifters close the gap in their configuration and continue humming.

The sixgills produce their low-frequency sound — the one that resonates through the tower lattices — when they pass close to the city. It is not clear whether this sound is deliberate: a call, a signal, a territorial announcement. It may be a byproduct of their movement, the displacement of water by a large body, amplified and modulated by the unique acoustics of the deep, warm, pressurized environment. Whatever its origin, the city's residents respond to it. The towers dim. The cleaning stations pause. The eels retreat deeper into their walls. The octopuses, if they are outside their chambers, return to them.

The sound of a sixgill passing is, in Cavreth, what the sound of thunder is in a surface city: a reminder that the world is larger and more dangerous than the structures we build to contain it.

## The Multitudes

And then there are the others. The ones who are too numerous to name, too varied to categorize, too essential to omit.

The copepods and krill-like creatures that fill the water column between the towers, feeding on the marine snow and the bacterial films, forming the base of the food web that supports everything else. They are the air that Cavreth breathes — invisible, ubiquitous, taken for granted by every creature large enough to eat them.

The amphipods that burrow through the sediment, maintaining the streets, processing the city's waste, turning the seafloor over with the patient industry of earthworms in a garden. Without them, the streets would silt up. The currents would slow. The city would choke on its own metabolism.

The medusae that drift through the upper reaches of the tower clusters, trailing tentacles that glow with their own faint light, feeding on the copepod swarms. They are beautiful, in the way that deep-sea medusae are beautiful: not decoratively but structurally, their bodies organized with a geometric precision that seems impossible in something so fragile.

The crabs — dozens of species, from tiny commensals that live in the chambers of larger organisms to the big, slow-moving scavengers that patrol the seafloor between the towers, picking at whatever the city discards. They are the sanitation department, the recycling center, the morgue.

The fish — hundreds of species, most of them small, most of them dark, most of them carrying their own light. They fill the city's middle layers, the spaces between the towers and the seafloor, moving in schools or alone, hunting or hiding or simply existing in the warm dark water.

The bacteria — the true foundation of everything. The chemosynthetic mats that colonize the warm rock around the seeps, converting hydrogen sulfide and methane into organic matter, feeding the grazers that feed the predators that feed the city. They are the primary producers, the ones who turn the earth's heat into life, and without them Cavreth would be a basin of warm water and empty basalt.

And the viruses. The phages that infect the bacteria, the pathogens that cycle through the city's population, the genetic material that moves between organisms in the constant, invisible exchange that drives evolution. They are the city's unconscious, its dreaming, its capacity for change.

## The Whole of Them

The inhabitants of Cavreth are not a community. They are not a society. They are a web — a dense, interconnected, mutually dependent web of organisms that have evolved, together, in this warm dark basin, over a span of time that makes human civilization look like a sneeze.

They do not cooperate in the way humans cooperate: with intention, with agreement, with shared goals. They cooperate in the way that cells cooperate in a body: because they must, because the alternative is death, because the selective pressures of a hundred thousand years have eliminated every lineage that did not fit.

And yet. There is something in the city that exceeds the sum of its biology. The towers that synchronize their light. The drifters that gather and hum. The octopuses that watch. The cleaning stations that have operated in the same locations for millennia. The rules about light and space and current that are not written and not spoken but are real.

The inhabitants of Cavreth have built a city, and the city has built them, and the process is ongoing — a slow, patient, reciprocal construction that has no architect and no blueprint and no end. They are the city's residents and its workers and its infrastructure and its mysteries, and they go about their lives in the warm black water with the unselfconscious competence of creatures that have never known any other way to live.

The light glows. The currents flow. The drifters hum. The octopuses watch. And Cavreth continues — not because anyone decided it should, but because everything in it, from the smallest copepod to the largest sixgill, is doing exactly what it has always done, and the sum of all that doing is a city, warm and dark and alive.
