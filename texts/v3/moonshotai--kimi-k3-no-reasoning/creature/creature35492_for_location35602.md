They live in the negative space of the mountain’s own forgetting, and they have become porous to accommodate the wet.

You will not see them at first. They are built from the same refuse as the terraces—translucent cartilage, weeping basalt, rust that has learned to breathe. They call themselves the **Seep**, though the name is a vibration in the jawbone rather than a word, and they are not a people, not quite, but a process.

Their bodies are **membrane-first**. Imagine a glass eel that has decided to walk upright, to approximate the shape of the ancient Precursors who built the engines, and failed in the most instructive way. They are bipedal only when observed closely; when you blink, they slump into quadrupedal, octopedal, fluid configurations that better navigate the vertical marsh. Their skin is the color of old iron, like the rust-hounds, but where the hounds are solid, the Seep are **perforated**—honeycombed with pores that match the mountain’s own sweating stones. They do not drink. They **transfuse**, pressing their bodies against the weeping walls and letting the cold, viscous water pass through them, filtering out the heavy metals, the ancient lubricants, the memories of the engines. They taste of ozone and cinnamon when they exhale, and their breath is visible as frost even in the warmer hollows.

They are **tall, but variable**, compressing their cartilaginous skeletons to slide through the conduits when the engines shift, expanding to thirty feet when they need to reach the upper terraces to harvest the blood-bamboo. Their faces are smooth, featureless, with only two phosphorescent eyes that burn with the same cold light as the luminous sphagnum—because they cultivate that moss in the hollows of their collarbones, in the pits of their knees, tending it like lantern-light. They need no other illumination. They read the mountain by the glow of their own bodies.

But their true strangeness is their relationship to **stagnation**. The Precursor engines refuse entropy; the Seep have surrendered to it. They are **deliberately unstable**, embracing the dynamic revision that defines Mount Karth. Their bones do not knit when broken; instead, they **sprout** new configurations, extra limbs, additional eyes, before dissolving the old structures into black sludge that feeds the fungi. They are never the same shape twice. To speak to one is to speak to a crowd; to know one is to remember a geography rather than a face.

They build nothing permanent. Their dwellings are **temporary symbioses**—hollowed-out stands of blood-bamboo that they coax to grow in circles, weaving the hollow stalks into shelters that amplify the mountain’s hum into a kind of music. When the bamboo inevitably collapses into sludge, they move on, carrying only their **memory-pools**: small, concave shells of flexible ice that hold water from the summit’s black pool, water that reflects not the face but the **before-time**. They drink from these when they need to remember what the mountain was, or what it is becoming, and the visions make them weep a clear, viscous light that evaporates into those nameless colors.

They are scattered, as the mountain demands. You might walk for days and find only their **signs**: a spiral of rust-hound bones arranged around a piston, their marrow sucked dry; a patch of sphagnum that glows in a grid pattern, the mathematical residue of a Seep sleeping beneath the surface, filtering nutrients; a sudden, perfect circle of stillness in the water where one has just submerged, leaving behind a scent of iron and cinnamon.

When the rust-hounds gather to sleep in their piles of fur and phosphorescent eyes, the Seep gather too, but differently. They **disperse**. They flatten themselves against the saturated stone, becoming one with the mountain’s skeleton, their bodies pulsing with the same rhythm as the pistons. They sleep in the **frequency** of the place, their dreams synchronized with the flywheels. If you press your ear to the basalt during these times, you will hear their thoughts—not words, but the sound of light pooling, of bamboo migrating, of reality thinning to a membrane that something vast is pressing against from the other side.

They are not afraid of the coming tear. They are **midwives** to it. The turbulence, the rising water, the acceleration of the engines—these are contractions. The Seep are preparing to be born, or to unbirth themselves, into whatever the mountain is becoming. They have begun to gather at the summit, at the spiral of conduits, submerging themselves in the black pool one by one, dissolving into the water that shows the future and the past, becoming part of the reflection that is not a reflection.

If you meet one—and you will, if you stay too long, if you drink the water, if you let the spores into your lungs—it will reach out with a hand that is becoming a root, becoming a conduit, and touch your face. You will feel your own bones go soft, your own skin become porous, and for a moment you will taste the ten thousand years of patience that the water used to conquer the stone. Then it will collapse into sludge, or expand into a towering structure of bamboo and light, or simply cease to be distinguishable from the mist.

And you will continue your passage, but you will be **changed**. Your sweat will taste of iron. Your eyes will glow faintly in the dark. And you will hear, always, the hum of the engines in your teeth, calling you back to become part of the Seep, part of the mountain’s long, wet, mechanical thought.
