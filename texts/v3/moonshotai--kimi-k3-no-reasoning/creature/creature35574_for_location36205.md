# The Inhabitants of the Undershelf

---

## The Processionals

They are the first thing you learn to see.

Not because they are large — they are not, most of them no longer than a thumb, the largest perhaps the length of a forearm — but because they are *inescapable*. They are the background against which everything else in the Undershelf is foreground. They are the static that resolves, on closer inspection, into signal: thousands upon thousands of pale, segmented bodies, each one moving with the same unhurried, metronomic gait, each one tracing a path across the young rock that is unique to itself and yet indistinguishable from every other path being traced at the same moment.

They are not millipedes. That is the easy comparison, the surface-dweller's reach for a familiar category, but it fails on inspection. Millipedes are cylindrical; these are *flat*, pressed thin by some evolutionary pressure that favoured a low profile in a world with a low ceiling. Their segments overlap like roof tiles, each one a pale, translucent plate of chitin through which the darker shapes of internal organs can be seen, faintly, in the grey light. Their legs — thirty pairs, forty, sometimes more — emerge from beneath these plates in rows so regular they look machined. Each leg is short, jointed twice, ending in a single hooked point that grips the micro-texture of the young rock with a dry, tick-*tick*-ticking precision.

They eat the pale growths. This is their purpose, if purpose is a word that applies to creatures with no evident purpose beyond continuation. They move across the rock surfaces — floor, walls, ceiling, the undersides of tilted slabs, the faces of boulders, every surface the Undershelf offers — and they scrape. Their mouthparts are not jaws but *rasps*: broad, flat, mineralised plates that they press against the crust-form colonies and drag backward, shearing away a thin layer of biological film with each pass. The trail they leave is visible: a clean, slightly darker line through the pale growth, like the wake of a tiny ship through a sea of lichen.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. They do not fight. They process. They move, and they scrape, and they grow, and they moult — leaving behind pale, empty exoskeletons that litter the rock like discarded gloves, like the shed skins of a world that is always renewing itself without ever changing — and they mate, briefly and without ceremony, and they lay eggs in the fine sediment, and they die, and their bodies are consumed by other Processionals or by the decomposers or by anything with a mouth and the inclination, and the whole cycle takes perhaps a year, though the word *year* has no meaning here.

Their numbers are their defining feature. Individually, a Processional is unremarkable: a small, flat, pale arthropod doing what small, flat, pale arthropods do. But there are *so many of them*. On any given square metre of rock surface — any surface, in any region of the Undershelf, near water or far from it — there are between ten and fifty Processionals. On the surfaces near the pools, the density doubles. Triples. The rock appears to *crawl*. Not metaphorically. Literally. The overlapping bodies of Processionals create a living skin over the stone, a shifting, rustling, chitinous layer that never thins, never gaps, never stops moving.

They are not social. They do not cooperate, do not communicate, do not acknowledge each other's existence beyond the occasional physical collision, which is resolved by one or both simply walking over or around the other. But their sheer density creates the *illusion* of coordination. When several hundred Processionals are moving across the same rock face in roughly the same direction — because the crust-growth is denser in that direction, because the moisture gradient favours it, because of some microscopic feature of the rock that channels their movement — the effect is of a *procession*, a slow, purposeful migration of pale bodies across the grey stone. It is not a migration. It is not purposeful. It is statistics. But it *looks* like intent, and in a world without events, the appearance of intent is a kind of comfort, or a kind of horror, depending on the observer.

The sound they make — that dry, rustling, papery whisper that is the baseline acoustic of the Undershelf — is produced by their legs. Each hooked point, gripping and releasing the rock surface, generates a tiny click. Multiply by forty legs. Multiply by thousands of individuals. The result is a continuous, textured *shush-shush-shush* that fills the compressed airspace like humidity fills a tropical night. It is the sound of being surrounded. Not threatened — the Processionals are harmless, utterly indifferent to anything they cannot eat — but *surrounded*. You are never alone in the Undershelf. The Processionals make sure of that.

They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. The light here is too dim for vision to be useful at their scale, and the surfaces they traverse are best navigated by touch — by the sensory bristles that line their ventral surface, by the chemical receptors on their mouthparts that detect the presence of crust-growth before their rasps make contact. They perceive the Undershelf as a landscape of textures and flavours, of smooth and rough, of edible and inedible, of damp and dry. The visual world — the grey-brown dimness, the pale rock, the low ceiling — is invisible to them. They live in a darkness that is not darkness, processing a world they cannot see, contributing to a sound they cannot hear.

When they die — and they die constantly, in numbers proportional to their population, which is to say in vast, uncountable numbers — their bodies become part of the system within hours. Other Processionals eat the soft tissue. The decomposers break down the chitin. The minerals return to the rock, to the water, to the crust-growths, to the next generation of Processionals. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The Processionals are the engine of the Undershelf's economy, the primary consumers that convert the thin, marginal productivity of the pale growths into biomass that everything else can eat.

They are, in this sense, the most important creatures in this world. They are also the least noticeable. You stop seeing them after a while. They become part of the rock, part of the background, part of the *texture* of the Undershelf. You have to remind yourself to look for them, and when you do, you are always slightly startled by how many there are. By how they were there all along. By how they are always there, always moving, always scraping, always rustling, always *present*, in numbers that the eye cannot quite process and the mind cannot quite accept.

## The Stillwaters

The pools belong to them.

Not legally, not territorially in any way that a surface-dweller would recognise, but *effectively*, *practically*, *absolutely*. Each pool — each still, cold, clear basin of seepage-water in the rock floor — is the domain of one or more Stillwaters, and the domain is maintained with a patience and a totality that makes the word *ownership* seem frivolous.

They are amphibian in form, though the comparison to salamanders is as imprecise as the comparison of Processionals to millipedes. They are *shaped* like something that once swam — streamlined, low-slung, with a broad, flattened head and a muscular tail that comprises nearly half their total length — but they do not swim. They *lie*. They lie at the bottom of their pools, motionless, their pale, translucent skin almost invisible against the pale rock beneath the clear water. They lie there for hours. For days, if days existed here. For as long as it takes.

An adult Stillwater is the length of a human forearm, sometimes longer — the largest individuals, the old ones, the ones that have held their pools for longer than anyone can determine, may reach the length of a full arm, fingertip to shoulder. Their bodies are soft, almost gelatinous, the skin so thin that the architecture beneath is visible: the dark thread of the spine, the branching tracery of blood vessels, the slow, patient pulse of the heart. Their colour is the colour of the Undershelf itself — pale grey, pale brown, the faint warm tint of weak tea — as though they have taken the light of this world into their bodies and made it their own.

They have no eyes. This is not an absence, not a vestigial reduction, not a pair of sunken pits where eyes used to be. There is simply *nothing*: a smooth, unbroken expanse of skin where eyes would be, as though the concept of vision was never introduced into their evolutionary vocabulary. What they have instead is a sensory system so refined, so exquisitely tuned to the specific conditions of their environment, that eyes would be a crude distraction. Their entire head — the broad, flat, shovel-shaped head that is their most distinctive feature — is a sensory organ. The skin of the head is covered in neuromasts: tiny, hair-like receptors that detect displacement in the water with a sensitivity that borders on the absurd. A single arthropod stepping into the pool at the water's edge creates a ripple that the Stillwater perceives as clearly as a surface-dweller perceives a shout. The direction, the distance, the size of the intruder, the *texture* of its movement — all of this is available to the Stillwater, instantly, without effort, without the mediation of light or lens or retina.

They perceive the world as vibration. As pressure. As the subtle, constant shifting of water in response to the things that move around and within it. The pool is their *ear*, their *eye*, their *skin* extended outward into a medium that carries information with a fidelity that air never could. When a Processional approaches the water's edge — drawn by the moisture, by the crust-growths that cluster near the damp rock, by the blind, statistical wandering that constitutes its existence — the Stillwater knows. It has always known. It knew before the Processional reached the water. It knew when the Processional was still a body-length away, its forty legs sending tiny tremors through the rock and into the water, tremors that the Stillwater reads the way a scholar reads a familiar text.

The strike, when it comes, is not a strike. It is a *disappearance*. The Stillwater does not lunge, does not dart, does not perform any of the visible, dramatic movements that surface predators perform. It simply *is somewhere else*. One moment it is lying at the bottom of the pool, motionless, a pale shape on pale rock. The next moment — and there is no perceptible interval between these moments, no transition, no blur of movement that the eye can track — it is at the edge of the pool, its mouth is full, and the Processional is gone. The water ripples. The ripples settle. The Stillwater drifts back to the bottom of the pool — slowly, this time, with the languid, unhurried motion of a creature that has already won and sees no need to celebrate — and resumes its stillness.

The whole event takes less than a second. The sound it makes — a soft, wet *snap*, barely distinguishable from the ambient noise — is the only evidence that anything has happened at all.

They are not aggressive. They are not territorial in the active, confrontational sense. When two Stillwaters share a pool — and the larger pools often support two, three, sometimes four individuals — they maintain a careful, mutual distance, each occupying its own section of the bottom, each attuned to the vibrations of the other, each aware of the other's presence with the same clarity with which they perceive prey. They do not fight. They do not display. They simply *share*, with the equanimity of creatures that have never needed to compete because the resource — the steady, reliable, statistical stream of arthropods coming to the water — is sufficient for all.

This sufficiency is the Stillwater's great luxury. In an ecosystem where every other creature is locked in a constant, grinding competition for scarce resources, the Stillwater exists in a state of *surplus*. The arthropods come to the water. They come constantly, reliably, in numbers that far exceed the Stillwater's capacity to consume them. The Stillwater eats when it is hungry, which is rarely — its metabolism is tuned to the cool temperature, slow and efficient, requiring far less energy than a surface-dweller would expect. A single Processional provides enough nutrition for days of stillness. The Stillwater is never desperate. Never urgent. Never *hungry* in the way that drives surface predators to recklessness.

It is, in the most literal sense, a creature at peace. It lies in its pool, in the cold, clear water, in the grey-brown dimness, and it waits, and the world comes to it, and it takes what it needs, and it waits again. There is no drama in its existence. No arc. No narrative. It is born — from an egg laid in the sediment at the pool's edge, hatched into a miniature version of itself, fully formed, fully capable — and it lives, and it grows, and it holds its pool, and eventually it dies, and its body is consumed by the decomposers or by the sediment-dwellers or by another Stillwater, and the pool gets a new Stillwater, and the new Stillwater lies at the bottom of the pool and waits, and the world comes to it.

The sound they make — the low, wet clicking that is felt in the sternum more than heard by the ear — is produced by the rapid compression and release of their pharyngeal cavity, the same mechanism that powers their strike. They produce these clicks not during feeding but during *stillness*: slow, rhythmic, almost metronomic pulses that resonate through the water and into the rock and into the compressed air of the Undershelf. The function of these clicks is unknown. They may be echolocatory — a way of mapping the pool, of maintaining a three-dimensional vibrational image of their environment even when nothing is moving. They may be communicative — a signal to other Stillwaters, a declaration of presence, a gentle assertion that this pool is occupied and the occupant is content. Or they may be something else entirely: a physiological byproduct, an involuntary sound with no more meaning than the sound of a heartbeat.

Whatever their function, the clicks add a layer to the Undershelf's acoustic texture that is felt as much as heard. Standing near a pool, you can feel them: a slow, deep, resonant *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates in the water, in the rock, in the air, in the bones of your chest. It is a calm sound. A patient sound. The sound of a creature that has everything it needs and wants nothing it does not have.

The Stillwaters are, in many ways, the antithesis of the Processionals. Where the Processionals are numerous, the Stillwaters are few. Where the Processionals are constantly in motion, the Stillwaters are almost perfectly still. Where the Processionals are defined by their *presence* — by the rustling, by the crawling, by the sheer, overwhelming fact of their numbers — the Stillwaters are defined by their *absence*: by the stillness of the pool, by the clarity of the water, by the almost imperceptible pale shape at the bottom that you might not notice at all if you did not know to look for it.

But they are connected. Intimately, inextricably connected. The Processionals feed the Stillwaters. The Stillwaters cull the Processionals. The relationship is not balanced — the Processionals are too numerous to be meaningfully controlled by predation — but it is *constant*, a steady, reliable flow of energy from the scrapers to the waiters, from the many to the few, from the moving to the still. It is one of the loops in the Undershelf's closed system, and it has been running, unchanged, for as long as the pools have existed and the Stillwaters have lain at their bottoms, waiting.

## The Ceilingskimmers

They should not be able to fly.

This is not a casual observation. It is a structural assessment. The airspace of the Undershelf is — at its most generous — two metres from floor to ceiling. Frequently less. In the basins and depressions where the floor drops away, perhaps three metres. In the areas where the ceiling swells downward in those shallow, pressing domes, a metre and a half. There is no room for flight. Flight requires altitude — the ability to climb, to dive, to convert potential energy into kinetic energy and back again. Flight requires *space*, and the Undershelf does not have space.

And yet they fly.

They are small — smaller than they appear in the dim light, smaller than the impression they create. A Ceilingskimmer's body is perhaps the length of a finger, slender and tapered, with a narrow head and a short, forked tail. Their wings — four of them, arranged in two pairs, fore and aft, like a dragonfly's — are disproportionately large for their bodies, broad at the base and tapering to sharp points, translucent as the Stillwater's skin and nearly as pale. The wing surface is not smooth but *textured*: covered in microscopic ridges and channels that serve some aerodynamic function in the thick, cool, compressed air of the Undershelf. The texture is visible in the right light — a faint, iridescent shimmer that appears and disappears as the wing beats, like oil on water.

Their bodies are light. Impossibly light. The skeleton — if it can be called a skeleton, this framework of thin, hollow struts that provides structure without weight — is barely mineralised, more cartilage than bone. Their internal organs are reduced, compressed, stripped down to the absolute minimum required for sustained flight and rapid manoeuvring. They are, in the most literal sense, *mostly wing*: a pair of large, efficient lifting surfaces with a small, aerodynamic body suspended beneath them, like a kite with a nervous system.

They fly low. They have no choice. The ceiling is always there, always close, always a hard, sharp-edged boundary that will not forgive contact. The Ceilingskimmers navigate this constraint with a precision that borders on the supernatural — though it is not supernatural, it is simply the product of evolution under conditions of absolute, unforgiving spatial limitation. They fly *between* things: between the floor and the ceiling, between the tilted slabs of young rock, between the stalactite nurseries and the sediment basins, in the narrow, compressed corridor of airspace that the Undershelf provides.

Their flight is not graceful. Grace requires room — the long, sweeping arcs of a bird in open sky, the elegant, unhurried spirals of a raptor riding a thermal. The Ceilingskimmer has no room for grace. What it has instead is *efficiency*: a flight style stripped of everything unnecessary, reduced to the bare mechanics of lift, thrust, and turn. They fly in short, direct lines, punctuated by sharp, instantaneous changes of direction that seem to violate the laws of momentum. They do not bank. They do not glide. They *snap* — from one heading to another, from one speed to another, from one point in space to another, with a quick, stiff flutter of wings that produces that characteristic sound: the riffle of a deck of cards, the shuffling of stiff paper, the dry *zip-zip-zip* that cuts through the ambient rustling like a blade through fabric.

They feed in flight. This is their defining behaviour, the thing that separates them from every other creature in the Undershelf. They are aerial predators — the only aerial predators in a world where the airspace is barely large enough to qualify as airspace. They hunt the Processionals, plucking them from rock surfaces in flight with a quick, precise snatch of their mandibles. They hunt the smaller flying things — the tiny, gnat-like creatures that swarm around the pools, too small and too fast to have a name of their own. They hunt anything that is small enough to catch and slow enough to catch it, and they do so with a success rate that is remarkable given the constraints of their environment.

The hunting flight is a thing to witness, if you can witness it — if your eyes are fast enough, if the light is sufficient, if the Ceilingskimmer happens to hunt within your field of vision. It begins with stillness: the Ceilingskimmer clings to a rock surface — the underside of a slab, the face of a boulder, the ceiling itself — motionless, its wings folded flat against its body, its sensory organs scanning the environment. The sensory organs are extraordinary: a pair of large, multi-faceted compound eyes that occupy nearly the entire surface of the head, giving the Ceilingskimmer a visual field that encompasses almost the full sphere around its body. These eyes are adapted to the dim light — not by enlarging, as a surface-dweller might expect, but by *refining*: each facet is tuned to detect movement rather than form, to register the subtle, rapid displacements of small bodies against a static background. The Ceilingskimmer does not see the Undershelf as a landscape. It sees it as a *field of potential motion*, a grey-brown canvas on which the movements of prey items appear as bright, urgent signals against the stillness.

When it detects a target — a Processional scraping across a nearby surface, a gnat-thing hovering near a pool — it launches. The launch is instantaneous: no crouch, no wind-up, no preparatory movement. The Ceilingskimmer simply *is airborne*, its wings already beating at full speed, its body already oriented toward the target, its trajectory already calculated and committed. The interception takes less than a second. The Ceilingskimmer closes the distance, adjusts its heading with micro-corrections so precise they are invisible, and *takes* the prey — a snatch, a bite, a quick manipulation of the mandibles — without breaking stride. It does not land to eat. It eats in flight, chewing as it flies, already scanning for the next target.

The chirps they emit — those short, sharp, surgical sounds that punctuate the ambient noise — are navigational. They are a form of echolocation, though a crude one: the chirp bounces off the ceiling, off the floor, off the surrounding rock, and the returning echo provides the Ceilingskimmer with a rough, low-resolution map of the airspace ahead. The map is crude, but it is sufficient. Combined with the visual input from those extraordinary eyes, it gives the Ceilingskimmer a real-time, three-dimensional model of its environment that allows it to fly at speed through a space that would kill any other flying creature.

They roost on the ceiling. When they are not hunting — and they hunt frequently, their high-metabolism flight style demanding a constant intake of energy — they cling to the pale rock above, upside-down, their wings folded, their bodies pressed flat against the stone. From below, they are nearly invisible: pale, flat, still. From above — if there were an above, if anything in the Undershelf occupied a vantage point above the ceiling — they would look like small, pale growths on the rock, indistinguishable from the crust-form colonies or the young stalactites.

They are not social. They do not flock, do not swarm, do not cooperate. Each Ceilingskimmer is a solitary hunter, maintaining a loose, informal territory around a section of airspace that provides sufficient prey density. They do not defend these territories aggressively — there is no room for dogfighting, no altitude for aerial combat — but they maintain them through presence, through the simple fact of being there, of occupying the airspace, of making it clear to any rival that this section of the Undershelf is *spoken for*.

Their lifespan is short. Flight in the Undershelf is a high-cost, high-risk activity, and the Ceilingskimmer pays for its aerial lifestyle with a body that wears out quickly. The wings fray. The muscles fatigue. The delicate sensory systems degrade. A Ceilingskimmer that survives its first few months — many do not; the learning curve for flight in this environment is steep, and the penalty for error is a collision with the ceiling or the floor or a rock slab, any of which is fatal — may live for a year, perhaps two. By the standards of the Undershelf, this is a brief, bright, intense existence: a life lived at speed, in motion, in constant, urgent engagement with the environment.

When they die, they fall. This is perhaps the most poetic thing about them, though the poetry is accidental. A dead Ceilingskimmer — killed by a collision, by exhaustion, by the simple accumulation of cellular damage — drops from its roost or from mid-flight and lands on the floor or in a pool or in the sediment, a small, pale, crumpled thing, its wings still folded, its eyes still open. The Processionals find it within minutes. The decomposers within hours. Within a day, it is gone, its biomass redistributed, its minerals returned to the system, its brief, bright existence reduced to a memory that no one holds.

But while they live, they are the most dynamic thing in the Undershelf. In a world of stillness and patience, of slow processions and motionless ambush predators, the Ceilingskimmer is *speed*. It is *movement*. It is the one creature that treats the compressed airspace not as a constraint but as an *opportunity*, a niche, a way of being that no other creature has claimed. It is, in its small, frantic, efficient way, the most alive thing here.

## The Undertow

The sediment is not empty. It is not even quiet.

This is the thing that takes the longest to understand about the Undershelf: that the flat, smooth, apparently barren basins of fine powder — the areas that look like nothing, like absence, like the negative space between the interesting features of rock and pool — are in fact the most densely inhabited regions of all. The sediment is not a surface. It is a *volume*, a three-dimensional living space that extends downward through layers of accumulated particles to depths that have never been measured, and it is *full*.

The creatures that live in the sediment — and there are many, of several distinct types — are collectively known, by the few observers who have attempted to categorise the Undershelf's fauna, as the Undertow. The name is apt. They create a current, a slow, continuous, subsurface circulation of particles and bodies and organic matter that never ceases, never pauses, never rests. The surface of the sediment is in constant, subtle motion — a shifting here, a subsidence there, a small, almost imperceptible eruption of displaced particles as something moves below — and this motion is the visible signature of the Undertow's activity.

The largest of the sediment-dwellers are the *Borrowers* — a name that is more descriptive than taxonomic, given by an observer who watched one emerge from the sediment and noted its superficial resemblance to something that might, in better light, be called a crustacean. They are not crustaceans. They are something that has no surface analogue, no familiar category, no convenient comparison. They are the size of a hand — some larger, the oldest individuals approaching the size of a dinner plate — and they are shaped like nothing so much as a *shovel*: a broad, flat, heavily armoured body that tapers to a point at the rear and flares into a wide, blade-like structure at the front.

The blade is their tool. It is a modified carapace segment, mineralised to the hardness of the young rock around them, shaped by evolution into a perfect instrument for moving through sediment. The Borrower presses forward, the blade slices through the fine powder, and the body follows — a smooth, continuous, submarine motion that displaces sediment the way a ship displaces water. They do not tunnel in the conventional sense. They do not dig and push and excavate. They *flow*, the sediment parting before them and closing behind them, leaving no trace of their passage except the faintest disturbance of the surface above.

They are filter-feeders. This is the key to their existence, the ecological niche that allows them to thrive in an environment that appears to contain nothing to eat. The sediment is not sterile. It is *rich* — rich with the accumulated organic debris of the Undershelf's relentless, efficient cycling. Dead Processional exoskeletons, shed and discarded, settle into the sediment and are broken down by microbial decomposers into fine organic particles. The waste products of every creature in the Undershelf — the faecal pellets of arthropods, the sloughed skin cells of Stillwaters, the fragmented remains of Ceilingskimmers — find their way, eventually, to the sediment basins, where they are incorporated into the fine powder and transformed, by chemistry and biology, into a diffuse, distributed food source.

The Borrowers extract this food. Their mouthparts — located on the underside of the blade, hidden from view — are a complex array of filtering structures: fine, feathery setae that strain organic particles from the sediment as the Borrower moves through it. They are, in essence, swimming through their food, continuously, endlessly, extracting nutrition from the medium they inhabit the way a whale extracts krill from seawater.

They are not fast. They are not agile. They are not, in any conventional sense, *interesting* to watch — they spend their entire lives beneath the surface, visible only as the occasional disturbance of the sediment above them. But they are *significant*. They are the Undershelf's recyclers, its processors, its engines of decomposition and redistribution. Without the Borrowers, the organic matter in the sediment would accumulate indefinitely, locking nutrients away in a form that nothing else could access. The Borrowers release these nutrients — through their waste products, through their own bodies when they die, through the simple mechanical action of their movement, which aerates and mixes the sediment and prevents it from compacting into an impermeable layer.

Beneath the Borrowers — smaller, simpler, more numerous — are the microfauna: a community of tiny, worm-like and mite-like and larva-like creatures that occupy the interstitial spaces between sediment particles. These are the true foundation of the sediment ecosystem, the base of a food chain that exists entirely beneath the visible surface. They feed on the microbial decomposers, on the finest organic particles, on each other. They are preyed upon by the Borrowers, by larger sediment-dwellers, by the occasional Processional that burrows into the surface layer in search of moisture.

And there are the things that live deeper still.

The sediment basins are not bottomless. They have a floor — the same young, fractured rock that constitutes the rest of the Undershelf's floor — but between the surface and that floor, the sediment can be deep. In the oldest basins, the ones that have been accumulating particles for the longest, the sediment may be a metre deep. Two metres. More. And in these deep sediments, in the dark, compressed, oxygen-poor layers far below the surface, there are creatures that have never been seen.

Their presence is inferred. By the Borrowers, which occasionally dive to depths that take them out of range of surface observation and return — or do not return — with damage that suggests predation. By the patterns of disturbance in the deep sediment, which sometimes show evidence of large-scale movement: a shifting, a churning, a slow, massive displacement of particles that cannot be attributed to any known sediment-dweller. By the simple, inescapable logic of ecology: where there is energy, there is life, and the deep sediment contains energy — the accumulated organic matter of centuries, compressed and concentrated and waiting to be exploited.

What these deep-dwellers are — what they look like, how they move, what they eat, how large they grow — is unknown. The Undershelf does not reveal its deepest inhabitants. It keeps them, as it keeps everything, in the grey-brown dimness, in the cool, in the silence beneath the sound.

## The Symbionts

Not everything that lives in the Undershelf is *separate*.

The pale growths — the crust-forms, the rosettes, the waxy, marginal organisms that occupy the boundary between fungus and plant — are not, strictly speaking, individual organisms. They are *communities*: associations of multiple species living in such intimate physical and chemical contact that the boundary between one organism and another becomes ambiguous, theoretical, a matter of definition rather than observation.

The crust-forms are the most obvious example. What appears to be a single, continuous biological film on the rock surface is, on closer inspection — much closer, the kind of inspection that requires magnification and patience and a willingness to accept that the categories of *individual* and *species* are less clear-cut than surface biology suggests — a consortium. A partnership. A merger of at least three distinct types of organism, each contributing something the others lack, each dependent on the others for survival.

The first component is a photosynthesiser — or would be, if there were enough light to photosynthesise. There is not. What there is, instead, is a chemosynthesiser: a microorganism that extracts energy from the chemical weathering of the young rock, from the oxidation of minerals released by the fracturing and dissolution of the limestone. This organism — a bacterium, or something analogous to a bacterium — is the primary producer of the crust-form community, the engine that converts inorganic chemistry into organic energy.

The second component is a structural organism: a filamentous, branching, fungus-like creature that provides the physical framework of the crust. It secretes a waxy, water-resistant matrix that binds the community to the rock surface, protects it from desiccation, and creates a microenvironment — a thin, moist, chemically stable zone between the rock and the air — in which the other components can function. This organism is the *body* of the crust-form, the thing you see when you look at a pale patch on the rock: the texture, the colour, the slight, slimy give when you touch it.

The third component is a recycler: a microorganism that breaks down the waste products of the other two and converts them back into usable forms. It is the decomposer of the crust-form community, the closer of the loop, the guarantor that nothing is wasted and nothing is lost. Without it, the crust-form would eventually choke on its own metabolic byproducts. With it, the community is — like the Undershelf itself — a closed system, self-sustaining, self-maintaining, capable of persisting indefinitely on the thinnest of energy inputs.

These three organisms are not merely associated. They are *integrated*. Their cells are intermingled at the microscopic level, their membranes in direct contact, their chemical processes so tightly coupled that the waste product of one is the raw material of another, transferred not through the environment but *directly*, cell to cell, membrane to membrane, in a continuous, intimate exchange that has been running for as long as the crust-form has existed.

Whether they constitute a single organism or three organisms in partnership is a question that the Undershelf does not answer and does not care about. The distinction is a human one, a surface-dweller's need to categorise and label and draw boundaries. The crust-form simply *is*: a living surface on a non-living surface, a skin of biology on a body of rock, persistent, patient, and utterly indifferent to the taxonomic anxieties of observers.

The rosettes — the branching, ivory-coloured growths in the sediment basins — are similarly composite, though their structure is more complex. They appear to incorporate not three but five or six distinct organisms, each occupying a specific zone within the rosette: the base, the stem, the fronds, the fruiting bodies. The integration is less complete than in the crust-forms — the individual components can, under certain conditions, survive independently — but the association is ancient, stable, and clearly beneficial to all parties.

The rosettes are the only organisms in the Undershelf that produce anything resembling a flower. The fruiting bodies — those small, hard, pale spheres the size of pinheads — are reproductive structures, containing the spores or seeds or propagules of the rosette's component organisms. They are produced infrequently — the energy cost is significant, and the Undershelf does not reward extravagance — but when they appear, they are *consumed*. The Processionals eat them with evident enthusiasm, clustering around a fruiting rosette in numbers that can temporarily strip the structure bare. This consumption is not destructive; it is dispersal. The Processionals consume the fruiting bodies, and the propagules pass through their digestive systems unharmed — protected by a hard, mineralised coating — and are deposited, in faecal pellets, at whatever location the Processional happens to be when digestion is complete. A new rosette may germinate from these pellets, establishing a new colony in a new location, extending the rosette's range across the Undershelf one Processional-meal at a time.

It is a neat system. Efficient. Characteristic of the Undershelf's relentless, closed-loop economy. The rosette feeds the Processional; the Processional plants the rosette. Neither is aware of the arrangement. Neither needs to be. The system works because it *works*, not because any participant understands it.

## The Decomposers

They are invisible.

Not metaphorically. Literally. The decomposers of the Undershelf — the bacteria, the fungi, the microscopic invertebrates that break down dead organic matter and return its constituent elements to the system — are too small to see with the naked eye. They exist at the boundary of perception, in the world of the very small, and their presence is known only by their effects.

But their effects are *everywhere*.

Every dead Processional — and Processionals die constantly, in vast numbers, their small, pale bodies littering the rock like fallen leaves — is colonised within minutes by decomposers. The soft tissues are the first to go: the muscles, the organs, the fat reserves, consumed by bacteria that multiply with astonishing speed in the cool, moist environment. The chitinous exoskeleton takes longer — chitin is a tough, resistant material, and breaking it down requires specialised enzymes that only certain decomposers possess. But it, too, is consumed, eventually, reduced to its component molecules and returned to the system.

The process is fast. In a surface ecosystem, a dead arthropod might persist for days or weeks, its body slowly desiccating or being consumed by scavengers. In the Undershelf, the timeline is compressed. The cool temperature slows the decomposers' metabolism, but the *density* of decomposers compensates: there are so many of them, packed into every crevice and film and grain of sediment, that the sheer weight of numbers overwhelms the resistance of even the toughest organic material. A dead Processional, left undisturbed on a rock surface, will be reduced to a faint, greasy stain within hours. Within a day, even the stain will be gone, absorbed into the crust-form colonies or washed into the sediment by condensation.

This speed is essential to the Undershelf's ecology. The energy inputs to the system are minimal — the chemosynthesis of the crust-forms, the slow weathering of the young rock — and the biomass of the system is large. The gap between input and standing crop is bridged by *recycling*: by the rapid, efficient, comprehensive breakdown and reuse of every molecule of organic matter. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The decomposers are the guarantors of this efficiency, the invisible workforce that keeps the loop closed and the system stable.

They are also, in a sense, the most *successful* organisms in the Undershelf. They are the most numerous — their populations dwarf even the Processionals, though their individual size makes them invisible. They are the most widespread — they exist on every surface, in every pool, in every grain of sediment, in the air itself, as spores and cysts and dormant cells waiting for the conditions that will allow them to activate. They are the most resilient — they can survive desiccation, starvation, temperature fluctuations (though the Undershelf provides none of these), and chemical stress that would kill any of the larger organisms.

They are, in the most fundamental sense, the *foundation* of the Undershelf. Not the rock. Not the water. Not the light. The decomposers. They are the base on which everything else rests, the invisible infrastructure that supports the visible ecosystem. Without them, the Undershelf would collapse — not dramatically, not with a crash of falling rock or a die-off of fauna, but slowly, gradually, as the nutrients locked in dead organic matter accumulated and the living system starved.

They do not announce themselves. They do not move, visibly, or make sounds, or produce structures that the eye can detect. They are simply *there*, in the rock, in the water, in the sediment, in the bodies of every living thing, doing the work that no one sees and everyone depends on. They are the Undershelf's silent majority, its unsung labour force, its most essential and least acknowledged inhabitants.

## The Rare Ones

Not everything in the Undershelf is common.

Amid the overwhelming numerical dominance of the Processionals, the patient ubiquity of the Stillwaters, the darting presence of the Ceilingskimmers, and the invisible omnipresence of the decomposers, there are creatures that are *rare*. Rare not in the sense of endangered — nothing in the Undershelf is endangered, because nothing changes, and endangerment requires change — but rare in the sense of *uncommon*. Present in small numbers. Seen infrequently. Existing at the margins of the ecosystem, in niches so narrow or so specialised that they can support only a handful of individuals.

There is a creature — seen perhaps a dozen times, if the observers' reports can be trusted, and they may not be, because observation in the Undershelf is a difficult, uncertain art — that appears to be a *predator of Stillwaters*. It is large: larger than a Stillwater, larger than anything else in the Undershelf, perhaps the length of a human leg. It is pale — of course it is pale; everything here is pale — and it is *flat*, even flatter than the Processionals, pressed so thin against the rock that it is nearly invisible when motionless. It has been seen only at the edges of pools, in the space between the water and the dry rock, and it has been seen only briefly: a pale, flat shape, a flash of movement, a disturbance in the water. Then gone.

What it is — how it hunts, what it eats, where it lives when it is not at the pool's edge — is unknown. It may be a specialised ambush predator that preys on Stillwaters when they come to the surface to feed. It may be a scavenger that claims the remains of Stillwaters killed by other means. It may be something else entirely: a migrant, a wanderer, a creature from the far reaches of the Undershelf that appears in the observed regions only occasionally, following its own inscrutable routes through the grey-brown dimness.

There is another creature — or type of creature, for the reports are inconsistent — that appears to live *in the ceiling*. Not on the ceiling, as the Ceilingskimmers roost on the ceiling, but *in* it: within the cracks and fissures and micro-cavities of the young, fractured rock. These creatures are small — the size of a fingertip, perhaps smaller — and they are never seen directly. Their presence is inferred from the sounds they produce: a faint, high-pitched *ticking*, irregular but persistent, that emanates from certain areas of the ceiling and stops when those areas are approached. The ticking may be a feeding sound — the scraping of mouthparts against the rock's interior surface, extracting some nutrient that exists only within the stone. It may be a communicative signal — a call, a warning, a mating display. It may be something else entirely. The ceiling-dwellers do not emerge. They do not show themselves. They tick, and they stop ticking, and they remain, invisible and unknown, within the rock that defines the Undershelf's upper boundary.

And there is — or may be — a *thing* in the far reaches.

The reports of this are the least reliable, the most fragmentary, the most easily dismissed as imagination or misperception or the kind of pareidolia that the dim light and constant ambient noise encourage. But they persist. Multiple observers — different observers, at different times, in different regions of the Undershelf — have reported a *presence* in the far distance: a large, slow-moving shape at the very edge of visibility, where the grey-brown light fades into grey-brown obscurity. It is not a rock formation — it moves. It is not a Stillwater — it is too large, and it is not in a pool. It is not a Ceilingskimmer — it does not fly.

It *walks*. That is the consistent element across all the reports: the thing in the far reaches walks. It walks slowly, with a gait that is described as deliberate, purposeful, unhurried. It walks on the floor, on the young, sharp rock, and it walks *away* — always away, always deeper into the dimness, always receding from the observer at a pace that is neither fast nor slow but simply *sufficient*. Sufficient to maintain distance. Sufficient to remain unseen, or nearly unseen, a shape at the edge of perception that resolves, on closer inspection, into nothing.

What it is — if it is — is the Undershelf's greatest mystery. It may be a large, undiscovered species, a creature of the far reaches that occasionally wanders into the observed regions and retreats when detected. It may be a group of smaller creatures, moving together in a way that creates the illusion of a single large body. It may be a trick of the light — the dim, grey-brown, unvarying light, playing on the tired eyes of observers who have spent too long in a world without visual landmarks.

Or it may be something that the Undershelf has not yet decided to reveal. A possibility. A potential. A creature that exists in the way that the deep-sediment dwellers exist: inferred, suspected, *implied* by the logic of the ecosystem but never confirmed, never seen clearly, never brought into the small circle of the known.

The Undershelf does not give up its secrets easily. It has had a long time to keep them.

## The Community

Together, these creatures — the Processionals and the Stillwaters and the Ceilingskimmers and the Undertow and the Symbionts and the Decomposers and the Rare Ones — form a community that is unique in the truest sense: there is nothing else like it, anywhere, because there is nowhere else like the Undershelf.

The community is not diverse. By the standards of a rainforest or a coral reef, it is *impoverished*: a handful of species, a limited set of interactions, a food web that could be diagrammed on a single page. But it is *dense*. Dense in a way that surface ecosystems rarely achieve. The number of individual organisms per unit area — per unit *volume*, since the community occupies the floor, the walls, the ceiling, the sediment, the water, and the air — is extraordinary. The Undershelf is not rich in species, but it is *overwhelmingly* rich in life.

This density creates a quality that is difficult to describe to someone who has not experienced it. It is not the lush, enveloping vitality of a jungle, where life is abundant but varied, where every surface hosts a different community and every glance reveals a new species. It is, instead, a *monochrome* vitality: a small number of species, repeated endlessly, filling every available space with the same pale, scuttling, fluttering, burrowing, clicking, rustling, dripping *presence*. It is like being inside a single organism — a vast, distributed, multi-bodied organism whose cells are individual creatures and whose tissues are the aggregations of those creatures on the rock, in the sediment, around the pools.

The community is stable. Not dynamically stable — not stable in the way that a surface ecosystem is stable, with populations cycling around a mean, with predator and prey engaged in an eternal, oscillating dance. The Undershelf's community is *statically* stable. The populations do not cycle. They do not oscillate. They are *constant*: the same number of Processionals, the same number of Stillwaters, the same number of Ceilingskimmers, day after undifferentiated day, in a world without days. Births balance deaths with an exactitude that would be uncanny anywhere else but here is simply the way the system works.

This stability is maintained by the closed-loop economy that the decomposers underpin. Every death feeds a birth. Every waste product feeds a producer. Every molecule of organic matter is cycled and recycled with an efficiency that approaches — but never quite reaches — perfection. The small inefficiencies, the tiny losses, the molecules that escape the loop and are locked into the sediment or dissolved in the water or incorporated into the rock, are compensated by the slow, steady input of new minerals from the weathering of the young stone. The system breathes, but it breathes so slowly that the breath is imperceptible.

The community has no predators from outside. No parasites, or none that have been identified. No diseases, or none that produce visible symptoms. The creatures of the Undershelf live in a world without external threats, a world where the only dangers are the ones they pose to each other — the Stillwater's strike, the Ceilingskimmer's snatch, the Borrower's slow, blind consumption — and these dangers are *known*. They are *constant*. They are part of the pattern, the steady, unchanging pattern that defines existence in the Undershelf.

Whether the creatures of the Undershelf *experience* this pattern — whether they have anything resembling consciousness, awareness, the subjective sense of being alive in a particular place at a particular time — is a question that cannot be answered. The Processionals process. The Stillwaters wait. The Ceilingskimmers hunt. The Borrowers flow through the sediment. The decomposers decompose. Each of them does what it does, perfectly, consistently, without deviation, and whether there is a *someone* inside those pale, eyeless bodies doing the doing is a mystery that the Undershelf guards as jealously as it guards everything else.

What can be said is this: they are *there*. They are alive. They are numerous beyond counting, packed into a space that should not be able to support them, sustained by an economy that should not be able to sustain them, existing in a world that should not be able to exist. They are the inhabitants of the Undershelf, and they are — in their pale, scuttling, fluttering, burrowing, clicking, rustling, unchanging, eternal *presence* — the place itself.

## The Sound They Make Together

Because it comes back to the sound. It always comes back to the sound.

The Processionals rustle. The Stillwaters click. The Ceilingskimmers chirp and flutter. The Borrowers shift the sediment. The ceiling-dwellers tick. The decomposers are silent, but their silence is a component of the whole, the pauses between notes that give the music — if it can be called music — its shape.

Together, they produce the sound of the Undershelf: that layered, continuous, textured loudness that fills the compressed airspace from floor to ceiling, from here to the grey-brown edge of visibility and beyond. It is not a sound that any single creature makes. It is a sound that the *community* makes, the community as a whole, the community as a single, distributed, multi-bodied organism that speaks with ten thousand voices and says, always, the same thing:

*We are here. We are alive. We are many. We are not stopping.*

The sound does not change. It does not swell or diminish. It does not respond to the time of day, because there is no time of day. It does not respond to the season, because there is no season. It is the sound of a system at equilibrium, of a world that has found its configuration and will maintain it, unchanged, for as long as the rock above holds and the water below seeps and the dim light holds its grey-brown constancy.

If you stand in the Undershelf — if you are there, somehow, in the cool, in the dimness, with the low ceiling above and the sharp rock below and the life, the overwhelming, inescapable *life* all around you — the sound is the thing you will remember. Not the sight of the Processionals, not the stillness of the Stillwaters, not the darting of the Ceilingskimmers. The *sound*. The combined, cumulative, total sound of all of them, together, being alive in a space that is too small and too dim and too enclosed for the amount of life it contains.

It is not beautiful. It is not ugly. It is not musical, not cacophonous. It is *full*. It is *present*. It is the sound of the Undershelf, and it does not stop, and it will not stop, and it has not stopped, and the distinction between these tenses is, like everything else here, a luxury of the surface world.

*Tap.* A drop of water falls from a young stalactite.

*Rustle.* A thousand Processionals cross a slab of young rock.

*Click.* A Stillwater lies at the bottom of its pool, patient as stone.

*Zip.* A Ceilingskimmer changes direction in mid-air, chasing something too small to see.

*Shift.* Something moves beneath the sediment, deep beneath the sediment, in the dark, compressed layers where the light has never reached.

*Tick.* Something in the ceiling, something in the rock, something that has never been seen and never will be, continues its small, persistent, unknowable labour.

And underneath it all, beneath every sound, beneath the rustling and the clicking and the fluttering and the dripping and the shifting and the ticking, there is a silence. Not the silence of absence, but the silence of *presence*: the silence of a world that does not need to speak because it is already saying everything it has to say, in a language made of legs and wings and water and rock and the slow, patient, unchanging persistence of life in a place where life should not be possible.

The Undershelf speaks. Its inhabitants are its voice. And what it says, what it has always said, what it will always say, is this:

*We are here.*

*We are here.*

*We are here.*
