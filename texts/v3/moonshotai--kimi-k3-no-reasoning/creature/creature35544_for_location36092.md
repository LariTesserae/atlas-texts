# What Lives in the Umber-Vast

## The Fen Itself

First and largest, the inhabitant that contains all others: the fen is alive, and it is the house.

This is not metaphor. The single organism—or fused parliament of organisms, the distinction long since dissolved—constitutes a resident with its own rhythms, preferences, and slow intentionality. It makes decisions. You can watch it make them, if you are patient enough: a channel narrowing over the course of a season, redirecting flow away from a chamber that has begun to rot; a wall of leaves thickening against the ingress of wind from the hills; the maintenance of those light-apertures, which are not accidents but choices, held open against the constant inward pressure of growth that would otherwise seal every gap and suffocate the interior in its own density.

The fen is not sentient in any way that would recognize the word. But it is *attentive*. It notices what touches it. The dimple-and-pushback of the ground underfoot is not mere elasticity—it is assessment, the way a large animal tenses a muscle beneath an insect before deciding whether the stimulus warrants response. Most of the time it decides you are not worth responding to. You are small, and warm, and brief, and the fen has absorbed warmer and briefer things than you without altering its course.

But it knows you are there.

Its life is hydraulic and electrical and chemical, a conversation among systems that have been running so long they have become load-bearing. The pumps—those cast-iron intrusions now half-swallowed by root-flesh—have been incorporated into the organism's circulatory logic. Whether the fen understands the pumps as organs or parasites or symbionts is unclear, perhaps undecided, but it has made room for them, grown around their housings with the deliberate care of tissue accommodating a splinter it cannot expel and has chosen, instead, to use. The pistons' arrhythmic thudding has become part of the fen's own heartbeat. It is possible the fen now depends on the water redistribution the pumps provide. It is equally possible the pumps depend on the fen for the structural stability that keeps their concrete footings from sinking into the peat.

The cables too. Copper threads through the body like a secondary nervous system, carrying current whose source and purpose are equally obscure. The fen has not rejected them. It has, if anything, threaded itself *along* them, new growth following their catenary curves as if the wires were trellises, as if the organism recognized in their humming something analogous to its own slow electrical signaling—roots speaking to shoots through ionic gradients, action potentials creeping through vascular tissue at speeds that make neural transmission look like light.

The fen lives here. The fen *is* here. These are the same statement.

## The Glass-Eels

They are the fen's most visible secret, which is to say they are barely visible at all.

A glass-eel in still water is effectively absent. Its transparency is not the partial, cloudy translucence of a cave fish or a larval salamander—it is near-total, a clarity so complete that the animal reads as a *distortion* rather than a body, a place where the water's relationship to light has been locally revised. You see them the way you see heat rising from pavement: not as a thing but as a warping, a shimmer that suggests something present without revealing what.

They live in the vascular channels, those thigh-thick pipes of moving water that run horizontally through the plant-mass. The channels are the fen's bloodstream, and the eels have made themselves at home in the current, riding the slow hydraulic pull from chamber to chamber, from the bright green pockets near the light-apertures to the dark waxy reaches where the plant-flesh goes mineral and still.

They grow long. Very long. The channels constrain their width—an eel thicker than the channel it inhabits would be trapped, and the fen does not make channels wider than it needs—but nothing constrains their length, and so they have become ribbon-like, serpentine, some of them stretching three or four meters from blunt head to tapering tail. They are rarely seen in full because the channels are rarely straight, and a glass-eel rounding a bend in a translucent plant-wall presents itself only as a series of segments: here a length of moving distortion, there another, the connection between them hidden behind opaque tissue. You assemble the animal in your mind from these glimpses and the result is always larger than you expect.

What they eat is uncertain. They have been observed—if observed is the word for something you infer from slight changes in water-clarity and the occasional, almost imperceptible constriction of a channel wall—clustering near the pump intakes, where the mechanical suction concentrates suspended organic matter into a thin, nutrient-rich gruel. They may filter-feed. They may absorb nutrients directly through their transparent skin, which is so thin that the boundary between the eel's body and the surrounding water seems more like a suggestion than a barrier.

They may also eat the spore-moths that fall into the water. The moths are clumsy near liquid surfaces, and sometimes, in their slow spiraling flight, they descend too far and touch the standing pools that collect in the fen's internal chambers. A moth on water creates ripples. A glass-eel creates none. The one announces itself; the other arrives without announcement. If feeding occurs, it is invisible—the moth simply stops being on the surface, and the water resumes its amber stillness, and if you were watching closely you might detect a brief, localized distortion in the fluid, a shape that was not there a moment ago and is already gone.

The eels do not leave the channels. They cannot. Their bodies are adapted to total immersion, to the specific chemistry of the fen's internal water—slightly alkaline, faintly creosote-flavored, carrying dissolved compounds from the plant-mass that would be toxic to any creature not born to them. A glass-eel removed from its channel collapses, its transparency becoming opacity as its tissues fail, and what remains is a thin, whitish thread that dissolves within hours.

They are, in this sense, less inhabitants than *expressions* of the fen—extensions of its water system that have acquired, over millennia, just enough independence to be called animals. Whether the fen tolerates them, cultivates them, or simply has not yet gotten around to absorbing them is a question the fen shows no urgency about answering.

Their breeding is unknown. No one has seen eggs, or young, or any behavior that suggests reproduction. The eels simply *are*: present in the channels, long and clear and patient, moving with the water or against it, visible only as a quality the water briefly possesses.

## The Spore-Moths

If the glass-eels are the fen's secret, the spore-moths are its weather.

They live in the canopy-rooms, those chambers where the light-apertures admit columns of tea-colored sun and the air is marginally drier, marginally warmer, marginally more hospitable to anything that needs to move. Here the fen produces its spores—not in any concentrated seasonal burst but continuously, a slow, ambient release of reproductive dust that drifts through the rooms like a permanent, low-grade fog. The spores are the fen's attempt at elsewhere: tiny packets of genetic possibility, designed to be carried by wind or water to some location where a new fen might take root.

Most never leave. The canopy-rooms are enclosed enough, the air still enough, that the majority of the spores simply settle onto the leaf-walls and are reabsorbed, their potential recycled into the mass that produced them. The fen is not wasteful. It treats its own reproductive effort the way it treats everything else—as material to be used, if not in one way then in another.

The moths are the intermediary. They feed on the spores, grazing the drifting dust with mouthparts so fine they function less as mandibles than as sieves, filtering the spores from the air in slow, deliberate passes. A single moth consumes very little. But the moths are *many*.

Their numbers are difficult to estimate because they are difficult to see. They are small—the largest no bigger than a thumbnail—and colorless, their wings a matte, desaturated gray-white that matches the tone of old parchment or unbleached linen. When they rest on the leaf-walls, which they do for long stretches, they are effectively invisible, their flat profiles and dust-colored wings merging with the plant-flesh until they are detectable only by the faintest texture, a slight roughness on an otherwise smooth surface.

When they fly, they become audible before they become visible. The sound of a single spore-moth is nothing—a whisper of wing on air, below the threshold of notice. But the moths congregate in densities that transform individual silence into collective sound. Hundreds of them, thousands, rising from a wall or descending through a light-column, their combined wingbeats producing that papery, shuffling rustle, the sound of pages being turned in a room you cannot see. It is the most animate sound in the fen, and it is made by creatures so insubstantial they barely exist.

Their flight is unhurried. This is not the desperate, zigzag evasion of a moth avoiding a bat; it is closer to drifting, to a controlled fall that happens to move sideways. They rise in spirals when disturbed—a hand passing too close, a shift in air pressure from a pump cycling on—and they settle again within seconds, unpanicked, as if the interruption were a minor note in a symphony they have been listening to their entire lives. They have no predators in the fen. The glass-eels take the ones that fall to water, but this is opportunism, not hunting, and it exerts no selective pressure toward speed or wariness. The moths have forgotten fear the way the fen has forgotten emptiness: gradually, completely, and without apparent loss.

What they contribute to the fen, beyond spore-consumption, is unclear. They may aid in pollination-equivalent processes, carrying viable spores on their bodies from one part of the mass to another, effecting a kind of internal cross-fertilization that the fen's static architecture would otherwise prevent. They may simply be a byproduct—the spores exist, therefore something evolved to eat them, and the fen tolerates the moths the way it tolerates the pumps: without enthusiasm, without objection, with the vast indifference of something that has already incorporated everything it cannot destroy.

They live briefly, by the fen's standards. A spore-moth's lifespan is measured in weeks, perhaps a month. They hatch from eggs laid in the crevices of the leaf-walls, emerge as pale, winged things already feeding, and die in the air or on the walls or on the water, their tiny bodies joining the slow rain of organic matter that the fen processes into itself. They are, in this way, the closest thing the fen has to transience—small lives that begin and end within a single organism's body, unremarked, unrecorded, recycled.

The rustling continues regardless. It is one of the fen's constants: the pump-thud, the water-pull, the cable-hum, and the paper-wing sound of moths in their thousands, feeding on the dust of a body that is trying, perpetually and without urgency, to reproduce.

## The Pump-Housings

Not alive in any biological sense. Not dead, either, in the way that a stone is dead. They occupy a third category, one the fen has created by absorbing them.

The functioning pumps—and roughly a third of them still function, their pistons rising and falling in those deep, arrhythmic cycles—are active participants in the fen's hydrology. They move water from one part of the mass to another, and while the original purpose of this movement is lost (if purpose is even the right word for something designed by whatever intelligence built them, an intelligence that left no other trace), the fen has integrated the flow into its own circulatory needs. A pump that draws water from a flooded chamber and deposits it into a dry one is performing a service the fen recognizes and has learned to rely on, the way a body relies on a pacemaker it did not ask for but has incorporated into its rhythm.

The dead pumps are something else. They sit in the plant-mass like organ without function, their housings corroded, their pistons seized, their concrete footings cracked and penetrated by roots that have grown *through* the metal in slow, vegetal explosions of pressure. The fen does not remove them. It grows around them, over them, through them, treating their rust as a nutrient source and their bulk as architecture—load-bearing, in some cases, the iron cylinders serving as columns around which the plant-flesh organizes itself.

If you press your ear to a functioning pump-housing, you feel the piston's stroke as a physical force, a pressure that travels through the iron and into the surrounding tissue and thence into your skull. The rhythm is irregular—not the steady metronome of a machine operating within parameters, but something more organic, more hesitant. The pumps pause. They resume. They cluster their strokes in groups of two or three and then fall silent for minutes, as if considering. Whether this irregularity is a product of corroded mechanisms or deliberate control—by what, by whom—is another question the fen does not answer.

The cables that connect them hum. This hum is the pumps' other voice, the electrical whisper beneath the mechanical percussion, and it is constant even when the pistons are still. Current flows through the system whether or not the pumps are active, suggesting a grid, a network, a distribution of power that serves purposes beyond the simple movement of water. The copper has oxidized to that vivid green, and the fen has responded to this color by producing new growth in adjacent tissue that is also green, also vivid, as if the organism were attempting camouflage or flattery or some vegetal form of solidarity.

## The Alcoves

The worn alcoves at the bases of the plant-walls, those smooth, curved depressions that suggest long habitation, are empty now. But their emptiness is articulate. Something lived here, once. Something that pressed its body against the plant-flesh for long enough—years, decades, generations—to wear the living tissue into a shape that accommodated it. The fen allowed this. It grew *around* the occupants, the way it grows around the pumps, but more intimately, more responsively, adjusting its surface to the contours of whatever sheltered there.

What were they? The alcoves are sized for something between a large dog and a small human. The wear patterns suggest bodies that returned to the same spot repeatedly, that settled into the same position night after night, that treated the alcoves as dens or nests or something analogous. The smoothness of the interior surfaces is profound—not the rough polish of occasional contact but the deep, glossy finish of sustained, habitual pressure. These places were *lived in*.

Nothing lives in them now. Whatever they were, they are gone, and the fen has not filled their absence. The alcoves persist, maintained by the same processes that created them—the fen's tissue holds their shape the way a mattress holds the impression of a body long after the body has risen. The plant-flesh could grow inward, could fill the voids, could reclaim the space, but it has not. Whether this is respect, or indifference, or simply the fen's vast inertia operating on yet another timescale too slow to witness, is unclear.

But the alcoves feel *occupied* in the way that a chair pushed back from a desk feels occupied—as if the resident has stepped away briefly and will return. They are the fen's most haunting feature: evidence of a relationship between the organism and something that lived within it, a relationship that was close enough to reshape the tissue, and that ended without apparent trauma, without any sign of conflict or expulsion. The occupants simply stopped being there, and the fen continued, and the alcoves remain, smooth and empty and waiting for something that will not come back.

## The Microbial Consortium

Beneath notice, beneath visibility, beneath even the glass-eels' ghostly presence: the fen's smallest inhabitants are its most numerous and perhaps its most essential.

The amber fluid that pools in the internal chambers is not sterile. It *teems*. Microscopic life—bacterial, archaeal, things that resist classification—fills every drop, processing the dissolved organic matter, cycling nutrients, breaking down the complex compounds that the plant-mass exudes and converting them into forms the fen can reabsorb. The creosote-and-fig smell of the pools is their doing, the olfactory signature of millions of tiny metabolisms working in concert.

They are the fen's gut flora, its microbiome, its internal ecology. Without them, the standing pools would stagnate and toxify; with them, the fluid remains chemically active, a living medium that supports the glass-eels and nourishes the plant-walls it contacts. The consortium is specific to the fen—each pool has its own community, slightly different in composition from its neighbors, and the differences reflect local conditions: light levels, water chemistry, proximity to functioning pumps, the presence or absence of eel activity.

Some of these microbes are bioluminescent. In the darkest reaches of the fen, where no light-apertures penetrate and the plant-flesh has gone black and waxy, the pools emit a faint, amber glow—so dim it is visible only to dark-adapted eyes, so steady it seems less like light than like a quality of the fluid itself, as if the liquid were remembering illumination it received long ago. This glow is the consortium's breath, the visible byproduct of metabolic processes that have been running continuously for longer than any of the fen's larger inhabitants have existed.

## The Whole

Taken together, what lives in the Umber-Vast is a system: the fen, the eels, the moths, the pumps, the microbes, all of them bound into a single, slow, self-sustaining process that has been running long enough to have forgotten its beginning and shows no interest in its end.

The fen is the body. The eels are its currents. The moths are its breath. The pumps are its heartbeat, borrowed and absorbed. The microbes are its digestion. The alcoves are its memory.

And you, if you enter, are its momentary awareness of something small and warm and passing through—registered by the ground beneath you, assessed, and set aside, as the fen returns to the vast, unhurried work of continuing to be what it is.
