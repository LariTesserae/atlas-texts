# The Warm Basin

The large thing that grazes the deep meadows has no name for itself either, and no need of one. It is old by the reckoning of this place—old enough to remember when the basin was smaller, when the ridges gleamed brighter with their newborn stone. Its body is broad and low-slung, built for pushing through the dense submerged vegetation, and its hide carries a patina of mineral accretion, a slow accumulation from years spent in water that wants to turn living things to stone. Not fast enough to harm it. Just enough to make it heavy, to make it sink a little lower each year, which suits it fine. It prefers the bottom.

It feeds by dragging its wide mouth through the meadows, taking in great slow mouthfuls of the layered mat—leaves, stems, the small silver fish that do not flee quickly enough, the drifting clouds of microscopic lives that thicken every swallow. It chews without hurry. Its teeth are flat and numerous, arranged in rows that replace themselves as they wear down against the mineralizing plants. When it surfaces, which it does perhaps twice a day, it does so because its lungs—simple, inefficient things—require it. The auxiliary breathing structures along its flanks, similar to those the smaller fish are developing, are not yet sufficient for full submersion. They may be, in time. The creature has time.

It is not alone, though it is the largest. Smaller versions of itself—its own offspring, or near-relations—move through the shallower parts of the basin, grazing the margins where the spear-plants give way to the flat, overlapping leaves. They are more numerous than they once were. The basin supports them well.

The fish, the small silver ones, school in the shallows where the light reaches and the mineralized plant-statues provide cover. They are quick when they need to be, but mostly they drift, filtering the suspended multitudes from the water with their fine, feathery gills. The auxiliary structures they are developing—small, pouch-like chambers behind their primary gills—are still rudimentary. They use them when the water grows too warm, too dense with life, when the oxygen thins. This happens sometimes, in the deepest part of the day, when the sun has been heating the basin for hours and the microscopic lives bloom so thickly the water turns nearly opaque.

In the deepest part of the basin, where the vegetation gives way and the bottom drops into darkness, other things live. Not large. Not complex. But present. The warmth that rises from below—the source of those occasional, slow bubbles—supports a different kind of ecosystem. Bacterial mats, perhaps, or something analogous. Things that do not photosynthesize but feed on the heat itself, on the chemical richness that wells up from the still-cooling stone. They form thin, glistening films across the submerged rock, and they glow faintly, though not enough to penetrate the darkness above them. Only if you descended into that depth, into that warmth, would you see them: a faint phosphorescence, a dim constellation of life clinging to the boundary between water and earth.

The spear-plants that ring the basin and nod in the humid air are not plants, exactly, though they photosynthesize. They are closer to enormous, communal algae—each spear a colony of millions of individual cells, cooperating to build those hollow, ribbed stalks. When they die, as they do in cycles, they fall into the water and begin the slow process of mineralization. The stone that forms around them is not quite stone. It remains slightly porous, slightly flexible, and it retains the pattern of the original plant so precisely that a casual observer might think the basin floor is littered with living specimens, frozen mid-sway.

The flat, scale-leaved plants that cover much of the water's surface are similarly cooperative. Each leaf is connected to its neighbors by a network of fine threads, and the whole mat can shift and migrate across the surface in response to light, to warmth, to the slow movement of the water beneath. At night, the leaves close slightly, overlapping more tightly, and the mat becomes almost solid, a floating skin that insulates the water below and keeps the warmth in.

The microscopic lives—the ones that make the water thick, that turn it amber and green and sometimes, at certain angles, crimson—are too varied to enumerate. Some are photosynthetic, tiny drifting algae. Some are predators, barely visible to the naked eye, that hunt the algae and each other. Some are decomposers, breaking down the constant rain of dying matter from the plants above, though they work slowly enough that the mineralization process often overtakes them. The water tastes alive because it is, every drop of it, a dense suspension of births and deaths and transformations.

The occasional splash that breaks the silence comes from a different kind of creature—something that has only recently arrived, or recently evolved. Amphibious, perhaps, or on its way to becoming so. It spends most of its time in the water, but it has begun to venture onto the thin skin of moss and rhizome that covers the raw stone ridges. It is small, about the size of a human hand, with a body that flattens when it rests and fins that are beginning to show the first hints of digits. It breathes air and water both, not yet committed to either. It moves awkwardly on land, but it persists, driven by some instinct or opportunity that the water alone cannot satisfy. There are insects up there, or things like insects—small, crawling, many-legged creatures that feed on the moss and on each other. The amphibious thing hunts them, clumsily, with more enthusiasm than skill.

The insects themselves are recent colonists. They arrived on the wind, perhaps, or were carried by other means. They are still adapting to the place, to the heat and humidity and the strange chemistry of the water. Some have begun to develop resistance to the mineralizing effect, their exoskeletons incorporating the stone-minerals rather than being replaced by them. They gleam, faintly, when the light catches them right.

The ridges themselves are not entirely lifeless. The moss that covers them is tough, pioneering, able to extract what little nourishment it needs directly from the stone. It grows slowly, but it grows. And where it grows, other things follow: the creeping rhizomes, which are not quite plants and not quite fungi but something between, and the small crawling things that feed on them both.

The whole basin, in fact, is a kind of conversation between the living and the not-yet-alive, the organic and the mineral. The water mediates. The warmth accelerates. And the result is a place where the boundary between life and stone is permeable, negotiable, constantly shifting.

At the center, where the warmth rises and the bubbles ascend through the darkness, there is something else. Not a creature. Not exactly. But a presence—a concentration of the same forces that animate the rest of the basin, intensified. The water there is thick with potential. Things form there that do not form elsewhere: small, temporary aggregations of cells that behave almost like organisms before dissolving back into the general murk. Experiments. Trials. The basin is testing possibilities, and that deep, warm center is where the tests are most ambitious.

The large grazing thing sometimes descends toward that center, drawn by the warmth or by something less explicable. It does not go all the way down. It has no need to. But it lingers at the edge of the drop-off, where the light begins to fail, and it rests there sometimes for hours, doing nothing, simply being present in that place where the water is warmest and the boundary between life and not-life is thinnest.

When the bubble rises from the depths and breaks the surface, releasing that rich, concentrated smell, the grazing thing lifts its head. It does not understand the smell, but it recognizes it. It is the smell of the basin itself, distilled. The smell of what this place is becoming.

Nothing here is finished. The basin is still filling, still warming, still accumulating lives and minerals and possibilities. The fish are still developing their auxiliary breathing structures. The amphibious thing is still learning to walk. The plants are still migrating upward, outward, colonizing new shallows as they form. The ridges are still acquiring their thin skin of green. The deep center is still warm, still rising, still releasing its slow bubbles into the darkness above.

The water is warm as breath, and it holds its breath, and it breathes.
