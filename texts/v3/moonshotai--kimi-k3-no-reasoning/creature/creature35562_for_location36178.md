# Inhabitants of the Hollow Verdure

## On the Thallus Itself

Before anything else with legs or wings or intentions, there is the Thallus, and the Thallus is inhabited.

Not in the way a forest is inhabited — creatures moving through it, atop it, beneath its canopy. The Thallus is inhabited the way a body is inhabited. It has residents *within* it, in the warm wet channels between its cell-walls, in the saturated pockets where water pools and nutrients concentrate, in the slow peristaltic rivers that carry moisture and chemistry from basin to ridge and back again. These residents are not visitors. They are tissue. They are function. They are the difference between a carpet of moss and a living world.

The most abundant of these internal inhabitants are the **glow-wrights** — organisms somewhere between bacteria and something more complex, colonial in structure, existing in dense mats within the Thallus's vascular channels. They are the source of the bioluminescence. Not the Thallus itself. The light comes from the glow-wrights, from their metabolic chemistry, from the slow oxidation of compounds they synthesize from the raw materials the Thallus provides. The Thallus is the house. The glow-wrights are the lamps.

But they are not passive lamps. The patterns of light — the propagation waves, the pulsing nodes, the color shifts around feeding Marrow-Drakes, the frantic chaos around kill-sites — these are glow-wright behaviors. They respond to chemical signals from the Thallus, to changes in pressure and temperature and pH, to the presence of foreign organisms in the vascular channels. The rippling waves of blue-green that cross the basins are glow-wright colonies activating in sequence, a cascade of chemical excitation passing from colony to colony like a rumor spreading through a crowd.

Whether the glow-wrights are symbionts or parasites or organs — whether they are part of the Thallus or merely resident within it — is a distinction that may not have meaning here. They have been together long enough that the boundary has blurred. The Thallus provides them with structure, nutrients, protection. They provide the Thallus with light, with the ability to signal across its own vast body, with something that looks very much like a nervous system.

If the Thallus thinks — and the Listening Rooms suggest that something, somewhere in this system, is doing something that resembles thinking — it thinks through the glow-wrights. They are its neurons. Its optic nerves. Its voice.

Deeper within the Thallus, in the oldest and warmest basins, live the **channel-worms**. These are not worms in any terrestrial sense — they are long, muscular, segmented organisms, some as thick as a man's arm and many meters in length, that move through the Thallus's vascular system, clearing blockages, consuming dead tissue, redistributing nutrients. They are the Thallus's circulatory maintenance crew. They are also, incidentally, a food source for the people, who fish for them by cutting small holes in the Thallus's surface and dangling lures of Marrow-Drake bone into the channels below. The channel-worms are attracted to the vibration of bone against the channel walls — it mimics the sound of a blockage, and they come to clear it.

Channel-worms glow faintly — not with their own light, but with the luminescence of the glow-wrights that coat their bodies in a thin, living film. When a person pulls a channel-worm from the Thallus, it emerges dripping with blue-green light, writhing and slick, and the light persists on the skin for hours afterward, marking the fisher's hands like a blessing or a contamination.

The people have a complicated relationship with the channel-worms. They eat them — the flesh is dense, rich, faintly sweet — but they also *listen* to them. A channel-worm's movement through the vascular system produces a distinctive sound, a wet sliding rhythm that experienced listeners can use to map the channels beneath their feet, to find water, to locate the deep warm pockets where the Heart-Drummer lives. The channel-worms are, in a sense, the Thallus's blood, and the people read its pulse.

## The Marrow-Drakes, Considered More Closely

The description of the Marrow-Drakes in the survey is accurate but incomplete. They are more than large herbivores. They are more than water-finders. They are, in the ecology of the Verdure, something closer to * gardeners* — though the people would never use that word, because they do not have gardens, and the concept of deliberately shaping the Thallus is foreign to them.

When a Marrow-Drake pierces the Thallus with its proboscis and drinks from a water pocket, it does more than extract moisture. It *inoculates*. The proboscis is coated with a microbial film — a complex community of bacteria and proto-fungi that live in the Drake's digestive tract and are deposited, with each feeding, into the Thallus's interior. These microbes colonize the wound site, accelerating healing, stimulating new growth, creating the small, sweet nodules that the people harvest as food.

The Marrow-Drakes are seeding the Thallus with the organisms that make it more nutritious, more resilient, more *alive*. The Thallus's violet color-shift around feeding Drakes is not a wound response. It is a welcome. The glow-wrights are signaling — to the Thallus, to the microbes, to the Drake itself — that this interaction is beneficial, that it should continue, that this particular Drake is known and accepted.

Old Marrow-Drakes — and they can live for a very long time, decades, perhaps longer — develop individual relationships with specific regions of the Thallus. They return to the same water pockets season after season, and the Thallus around those pockets grows differently: denser, richer, more elaborately patterned with bioluminescence. The people call these places **Drake-gardens** and treat them as prime harvesting grounds, because the nodules that form there are larger, sweeter, more abundant than anywhere else.

A Marrow-Drake herd — typically five to fifteen individuals, led by an elder whose flattened head is scarred and thickened from decades of pressing against the Thallus — moves slowly across the landscape, following routes that have been established over generations. These routes are visible from a high vantage point: trails of altered bioluminescence, subtle shifts in the Thallus's growth pattern, faint depressions in the cushioned surface where countless feet have pressed. The people follow these trails. They know them. They have names for them — names that describe not the destination but the *sound* of the trail, the particular rhythm and pitch of the creaking that the Thallus produces when walked upon at that location.

Marrow-Drakes communicate with each other through the Thallus. Not through the air — the thin atmosphere carries sound poorly, and the constant creaking would drown out most vocalizations. Instead, they *stamp*. A specific pattern of footfalls, transmitted through the living ground, can be detected by other Drakes miles away, their broad, vibration-sensitive heads picking up the signal like a radio receiver. The people have learned to read these stamp-patterns. A rapid triple-stamp means *water here*. A slow, heavy double-stamp means *danger, move*. A long, rolling sequence of alternating stamps means *follow me*.

The Drakes also stamp for the Thallus. Or at least, their stamping affects the Thallus in ways that look like communication. A Drake that stamps repeatedly in one location will, over time, stimulate the Thallus to grow thicker and warmer there, to form a new water pocket, to become a more attractive feeding site. Whether the Drake is deliberately farming the Thallus or whether the Thallus is training the Drake — rewarding certain behaviors with resources — is, again, a question without a clear answer. The system is too old, too intertwined, too mutually shaping for cause and effect to be cleanly separated.

## The Silence, Considered More Closely

The survey describes the Silence as a predator, and this is true as far as it goes. But the Silence is not simply an animal that hunts. It is an *absence* that hunts. It is defined not by what it is but by what it *removes*.

Where the Silence passes, the Thallus goes quiet. The creaking stops. The glowing dims. The wind seems to hold its breath. This is not a behavioral choice — the Thallus does not *decide* to be quiet. Something about the Silence's presence *suppresses* the Thallus's normal activity. The glow-wrights dim. The channel-worms still. The creaking of growth and stress and peristalsis ceases.

The people have theories. The most common is that the Silence produces some kind of field — acoustic, chemical, electrical — that inhibits the metabolic processes underlying the Thallus's sound and light production. This would make it a kind of living anaesthetic, a creature that numbs the world around it in order to move undetected.

Another theory, less popular but persistent, is that the Silence does not suppress the Thallus — it *absorbs* the sound. It takes the noise into itself, consumes it, uses it as energy or information or both. This would explain why the Silence's dead zones are not uniform — they have texture, structure, areas of deeper and shallower quiet, as if the Silence is not simply muting the world but *listening* to it with an intensity that drains the sound away.

What the Silence looks like is unknown. No one has seen it clearly. The few who claim to have glimpsed it describe contradictory things: something low and fast, something tall and slow, something that is not a shape at all but a *fold* in the darkness, a place where the starlight bends wrong. The most consistent detail is that it leaves tracks — not footprints, but *silence-prints*, places where the Thallus has been compressed without making a sound, as if the Silence's weight is real but its passage is not.

The Silence hunts Marrow-Drakes, but it does not hunt them the way a normal predator does. It does not chase. It does not ambush. It simply *appears* within a herd — a dead zone opening in the soundscape like an eye — and by the time the zone closes, by the time the Thallus resumes its creaking and glowing, a Drake is down. The kill is always clean. A single wound, precise and deep, usually at the base of the skull or the junction of the spine. The Drake does not struggle. The herd does not panic. They simply move on, and the Thallus around the carcass glows in those frantic, chaotic patterns for days afterward, as if the plant mass is trying to understand what happened.

The people do not hunt the Silence. They avoid it. Their entire sound-culture is oriented around detecting its approach — the moving dead zone, the hole in the noise. Children learn to listen for silence before they learn to listen for anything else. The sound-stone codes include an elaborate set of warnings for Silence activity, and these warnings are the only messages that are *always* relayed, always passed from group to group, across the entire plateau, no matter the distance or the difficulty.

There is a story — old, much retold, probably not true in any literal sense — that the Silence is the Thallus's own shadow. That the Thallus, in its vast slow thinking, has dark thoughts, and those dark thoughts have to go somewhere, and the Silence is where they go. That it is the part of the world-mind that does not glow, does not creak, does not sing. The part that hunts.

The people who tell this story do not tell it in the Listening Rooms.

## The Heart-Drummer, Considered More Closely

The Heart-Drummer is not one creature. It is several. Possibly many.

The rhythmic thumping that resonates through the Thallus for miles is produced by something large living in the deepest, warmest, most water-saturated basins — the same basins where the Listening Rooms form. The people have never seen it. They have only heard it, and felt it, and mapped its presence by the subtle changes it produces in the Thallus's behavior.

The thumping is not constant. It follows a slow, irregular rhythm that correlates with the post-storm stimulation periods — when the lightning has charged the Thallus and the bioluminescence is running bright and fast, the Heart-Drummer's tempo increases. During quiet periods, between storms, it slows to a barely perceptible pulse, a deep bass beat so low and slow that it might be mistaken for the plateau's own geological settling.

The channel-worms avoid the Heart-Drummer's basins. The people who fish for channel-worms have noticed this — the channels near the deep basins are empty, abandoned, the vascular system rerouted as if the worms have been warned away. The Marrow-Drakes also avoid these basins, stamping their *danger, move* pattern when they approach the edges.

But the Thallus itself grows *toward* the Heart-Drummer. The plant mass in the deep basins is thicker, richer, more elaborately structured than anywhere else. The Listening Rooms form here. The golden bioluminescence is brightest here. It is as if the Thallus is *attracted* to the Heart-Drummer, drawn to it, feeding it or being fed by it or both.

The people believe the Heart-Drummer is the Thallus's heart — a literal organ, a muscular pump that drives the peristaltic flow of water and nutrients through the vascular system. The thumping is its heartbeat. The Listening Rooms grow around it because it is the center, the source, the place where the Thallus's slow circulatory system converges.

If this is true, then there are multiple hearts — multiple Heart-Drummers, one in each deep basin, each maintaining its own rhythm, each serving as the center of a regional Thallus network. And the Listening Rooms, with their extraordinary acoustics, might be the places where the hearts *communicate* — where the rhythms of one basin are transmitted to another, where the Thallus synchronizes its own vast, distributed pulse.

The sound-stone responses that come from the Listening Rooms — the altered, elaborated, composed echoes — might be the Heart-Drummers talking to each other. Using the people as intermediaries. As messengers. As instruments.

The people do not investigate the Heart-Drummer directly. This is not fear, exactly. It is something closer to respect — the respect you have for a thing that is larger than you, older than you, and *necessary* to you, even if you do not understand it. You do not open a heart to see how it works. You listen to its beating and you are glad it beats.

## The People, Considered More Closely

The survey calls them listeners, and this is accurate, but it undersells the depth of what they do. They do not merely listen. They *perform* listening. Listening is not a passive act in the Verdure — it is a skill, a discipline, a way of being in the world that requires constant attention and constant practice.

A child in the Verdure learns to listen before they learn to walk. This is not a metaphor. The Thallus's surface is uneven, yielding, unpredictable. A child who cannot hear the difference between solid ground and a thin spot over a water pocket will fall through. A child who cannot hear the approach of the Silence will die. Listening is survival, and it begins at birth.

The people have developed an auditory acuity that would seem supernatural in another context. They can distinguish between dozens of creaking patterns, each indicating a different condition of the Thallus — growth, stress, water saturation, temperature change, the presence of burrowing organisms. They can track a Marrow-Drake herd by sound alone, following the stamp-patterns and the violet glow-trails for days. They can hear a storm coming hours before it arrives, detecting the change in wind pitch and the shift in the Thallus's creaking as the plant mass braces itself.

Their language is rich in sound-words. They have more words for different kinds of creaking than for any other category of experience. They have words for the sound the Thallus makes when it is growing fast, when it is growing slow, when it is stressed by wind, when it is saturated with water, when it is healing after a Drake feeding, when it is responding to lightning. They have words for the silence that precedes the Silence — a specific quality of quietness, a flatness in the soundscape, that an experienced listener can detect minutes before the dead zone arrives.

Their music — if it can be called music — is based on the sound-stones. But it is not music for entertainment. It is music for *communication* — with each other, across distances, through the Thallus itself. A sound-stone performance is a message, a story, a map, a warning, a poem, all encoded in rhythm and pitch and duration. The best sound-stone players are the most respected members of any group, not because they are artists but because they are *loudspeakers* — the voices that carry, the signals that propagate, the connections that hold the scattered population together.

They have a concept they call **the long ear** — the idea that the Thallus itself is listening to their sound-stone messages, that it hears the patterns and responds to them, and that the Listening Rooms are where this hearing is most acute. When a sound-stone pattern is answered — when the altered, elaborated echo comes back from somewhere far away — they say the long ear has spoken. They do not agree on what this means. But they all agree that it *matters*.

Their relationship with the Silence is the closest thing they have to religion. Not worship — they do not worship the Silence. But they *acknowledge* it. They have rituals for it — specific sound-stone patterns played when a Silence kill is discovered, specific gestures made when a dead zone passes nearby. These rituals are not appeasement. They are *recognition*. The Silence is part of the Verdure. It is part of the soundscape — the part that is defined by absence. To deny it, to ignore it, would be to deny the shape of the world.

The people do not have a creation myth. They do not believe the Verdure was made. They believe it is *making itself* — that the Thallus is still growing, still spreading, still becoming whatever it is going to become. The Listening Rooms are proof of this. They are new. They are changing. The world is not finished. The people are not finished. Everything is in process.

This gives them a particular relationship with time. They do not think of the past as a fixed thing, a foundation. They think of it as a *sound* — something that was made, that propagated, that is still echoing somewhere in the Thallus, still audible if you know how to listen. The Listening Rooms, with their extraordinary acoustics, can sometimes produce sounds that seem to be *old* — patterns that no living person played, rhythms that match no known code, melodies that feel like memories of a time before the people arrived.

Whether these are actual echoes — sounds stored in the Thallus's structure, replayed by some mechanism of resonance and recall — or whether they are the Thallus's own compositions, its improvisations on themes it has heard over the years, is unknown. The people listen to these old sounds with a particular kind of attention, a stillness that is almost religious, as if they are hearing the world's own voice, speaking in a language that predates them and will outlast them.

## The Smaller Lives

The survey mentions the chitter-moths and the rock-voles, but the Verdure is full of smaller lives that deserve attention.

The chitter-moths are not moths. They are something between an insect and a small bird — warm-blooded, winged, social, about the size of a clenched fist. Their wings are not scaled but *fibrous*, made of a material similar to the Thallus's own tissue, and they produce their characteristic rattling buzz not by beating but by *vibrating* — a rapid oscillation that is more felt than heard, a dry hum that rises and falls in density waves as the swarms move across the basins.

They feed on the glow-wrights. This is their ecological role: they are the mechanism by which the Thallus's bioluminescence is *regulated*. Without the chitter-moths, the glow-wrights would proliferate unchecked, and the Thallus's light patterns would become chaotic, undifferentiated, a uniform blaze rather than a structured signal. The moths crop the glow-wright colonies, keeping them at densities that allow for clear, patterned communication.

The moths are themselves bioluminescent — not from their own chemistry, but from the glow-wrights they consume. A moth that has fed well glows faintly from within, its fibrous wings translucent, its body a small, warm lantern. At night — which is always — a swarm of chitter-moths is a river of dim, moving lights, drifting across the dark basins like a reflection of the stars above.

The people eat chitter-moths. They catch them in nets of Thallus fiber, swung through the swarms in wide arcs. The meat is light, almost insubstantial, but the real prize is the glow-wright paste in the moths' digestive tracts — a concentrated, bioluminescent substance that the people use as a light source. A small container of glow-wright paste, kept warm and fed with Thallus sap, will produce light for days. It is the closest thing the people have to a lamp.

The rock-voles are more conventional — small, quick, furred, big-eyed creatures that dart across the ridge-lines, their claws clicking on the thin Thallus skin. They live in the cracks and fissures of the young, sharp rock, emerging to feed on Thallus fiber and the small, sweet nodules that form at healing sites. They are prey for larger things — the chitter-moths' predators, the occasional juvenile Silence (if the Silence has juveniles, which is another open question), and the people themselves, who hunt them with bone-tipped spears and snares of Thallus fiber.

But the rock-voles have a behavior that the people find deeply unsettling. They *gather*. Periodically — every few weeks, usually after a storm — the rock-voles of a given region will converge on a single location, a ridge-top or a rock formation, and sit together in silence. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Sitting motionless, facing the same direction, their big eyes reflecting the starlight. They do not feed. They do not mate. They do not interact. They simply sit, together, in silence, for hours.

And then they disperse, all at once, as if released by a signal.

The people call these gatherings **the vole-vigils** and they do not like them. They do not like them because the voles, during these gatherings, are *silent* — and silence, in the Verdure, is the province of the Silence. The vole-vigils create dead zones in the soundscape, areas where the usual clicking and scurrying is absent, and these dead zones can mask the approach of the actual Silence. More than one person has died because they mistook a vole-vigil for normal silence and failed to move.

What the voles are doing during these gatherings is unknown. They are facing the same direction — always toward the nearest Listening Room. Whether they are listening to it, or being called by it, or simply orienting themselves by some cue that the people cannot perceive, is another open question.

## The Things in the Water

The Thallus's water pockets are not empty. They are inhabited by a complex community of aquatic organisms that live in the warm, nutrient-rich fluid held within the plant mass.

The most visible of these are the **lantern-fish** — small, translucent, eel-like creatures that swim through the larger water pockets, their bodies glowing with the same blue-green bioluminescence as the glow-wrights. They are not fish. They are something between a fish and a worm and a larval stage of something that never matures — they have no adult form, no metamorphosis, no life beyond the water pockets. They are born, they swim, they eat, they die, all within the body of the Thallus.

The lantern-fish are the reason the water pockets glow. Not the glow-wrights — the glow-wrights live in the channels, not the open water. The pockets themselves are illuminated by the lantern-fish, thousands of them, swimming in slow, lazy circles, their combined light creating the golden luminescence that fills the Listening Rooms.

The people harvest lantern-fish by cutting into a water pocket and scooping them out with nets of Thallus fiber. The fish die quickly out of water, but their bioluminescence persists for hours afterward, and the people use them as temporary light sources — a handful of dying lantern-fish in a bone container will illuminate a shelter for an evening. The meat is edible but bland. The real value is the light.

Deeper in the water pockets, in the oldest and largest ones, live things that the people do not harvest and do not name. They are glimpsed occasionally — a movement in the golden light, a shape that is too large, too dark, too *wrong* to be a lantern-fish. The channel-worms avoid the deep pockets. The Marrow-Drakes will drink from the shallow edges but never from the center. The people follow the Drakes' example.

These deep-water inhabitants are connected, somehow, to the Heart-Drummer. The thumping is loudest near the deep pockets, and the water in these pockets *pulses* — a slow, rhythmic surge that matches the Heart-Drummer's beat. It is as if the Heart-Drummer is pumping the water, circulating it, keeping it fresh and oxygenated for the things that live in it.

Or *breeding* them. The deep pockets might be nurseries — places where the Heart-Drummer produces its young, or where the Thallus produces new glow-wright colonies, or where some other generative process occurs that requires warmth, water, darkness, and the constant, rhythmic thumping of a vast, unseen heart.

The people do not investigate. They have learned, over generations, that the deep pockets are not for them. They are for the Thallus, and for the Heart-Drummer, and for whatever lives in the golden light at the bottom of the world.

## The Listening Rooms, Revisited

The survey describes the Listening Rooms as structures — domes, arches, spiraling columns of Thallus tissue that form enclosed spaces with extraordinary acoustics. This is accurate. But it does not capture what it *feels like* to be inside one.

A Listening Room is warm. Warmer than the surrounding basin, warmer than any shelter the people can build. The air is humid, thick with moisture and the sweet, earthy smell of the Thallus's metabolism. The golden bioluminescence is bright — not bright by the standards of a sunlit world, but bright by the standards of the Verdure, bright enough to see by, bright enough to cast shadows, bright enough to make the interior surfaces of the Room visible in detail.

And the surfaces are *worth seeing*. The inner walls of a Listening Room are not smooth. They are textured — ridged, folded, patterned with intricate structures that look almost like carvings. Spirals. Lattices. Recursive, fractal forms that repeat at every scale, from the architecture of the Room itself down to the finest detail of the wall surface. These textures are not decorative. They are *acoustic*. They shape the sound within the Room, focusing it, amplifying it, creating resonances and harmonics that give the space its extraordinary sonic properties.

A whisper in a Listening Room does not simply fill the space. It *blooms*. The walls catch the sound and return it, amplified and clarified, from every direction simultaneously. A sound-stone struck gently produces a chord that builds and builds, layering upon itself, until the air itself seems to vibrate with a single, sustained, impossibly rich tone.

And sometimes — the survey says sometimes, and it is right to be tentative — the Thallus responds. The response is not immediate. It comes minutes or hours later, from somewhere else on the plateau, transmitted through the Thallus's vascular system, through the glow-wright network, through the slow peristaltic channels that connect the deep basins. The response is the same pattern, altered. Shifted in pitch. Extended. Elaborated.

The people have catalogued these responses. They have identified patterns — recurring motifs, structural elements that appear again and again across different Rooms, different responses, different years. These motifs are not random. They have *grammar*. They have syntax. They have something that looks very much like *meaning*.

The people do not know what the motifs mean. But they have begun to *use* them — to incorporate them into their own sound-stone codes, to play them in the Listening Rooms and see what comes back. The results are inconsistent. Sometimes a motif produces a strong, clear response. Sometimes it produces nothing. Sometimes it produces a response so complex, so elaborated, so *overwhelming* in its sonic richness that the listeners are left shaken, weeping, unable to speak for hours.

One motif — the people call it **the long question** — produces a response every time it is played. Without exception. From every Listening Room. The response is always the same: a single, sustained tone, impossibly low, felt more in the bones than in the ears, that lasts for exactly as long as the original motif. It is not an answer. It is an *acknowledgment*. It is the Thallus saying: *I hear you. I am here. I am listening.*

The people play the long question on special occasions. At births. At deaths. At the start of long journeys. When the Silence has been particularly active. When the storms have been particularly violent. When the world feels uncertain and the darkness feels too large.

The response — that single, sustained, bone-deep tone — is the closest thing they have to comfort.

## What Lives Here, Finally

What lives in the Hollow Verdure is not a collection of separate organisms. It is a *system* — a single, vast, interconnected web of life that spans the plateau from ridge to basin, from the Thallus's surface to the deepest water pockets, from the smallest glow-wright to the largest Marrow-Drake, from the channel-worms in the vascular system to the Heart-Drummer in the deep basins, from the chitter-moths in their glowing swarms to the rock-voles in their unsettling vigils, from the people with their sound-stones to the Silence with its dead zones.

Everything here is connected. Everything here is part of the same breathing, creaking, glowing, listening whole. The Thallus is the substrate, the medium, the world-body. The glow-wrights are its nerves. The channel-worms are its blood. The Marrow-Drakes are its gardeners. The chitter-moths are its regulators. The lantern-fish are its lanterns. The Heart-Drummer is its heart. The Listening Rooms are its ears and its voice.

The people are its *listeners* — the part of the system that is aware of the system, that can hear the whole and respond to it, that can play the long question and receive the long answer.

And the Silence is its shadow. The part that does not glow. The part that does not creak. The part that hunts in the spaces between sounds, in the gaps in the world's own noise.

Everything in the Verdure is alive. Everything is listening. Everything is being listened to.

The darkness breathes, and everything in it breathes together.
