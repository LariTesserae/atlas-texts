What lives in the Saltroom is not a creature but a condition—an inhabitant made of processes, rhythms, and the patient arithmetic of physics. It is the room itself that lives, if we define life as something that takes in energy, maintains an internal environment, responds to cycles, and persists against entropy. By that definition, the Saltroom is more alive than many creatures, and more deliberate than most.

Its primary inhabitant is the **light-beast**. Not light as a passive phenomenon but light as an active presence, a resident that enters daily through twelve ceramic throats and occupies the chamber like a body filling a shell. This light-beast has a metabolism: it arrives at dawn in thin, angled spears, grows fat and white-hot by midday, turns amber and lethargic by evening, and dies each night only to be reborn the following morning. It has moods—bright and direct on clear days, muted and diffuse when clouds pass overhead, absent entirely during storms or the long dark of moonless nights. It feeds the only true vegetation in the place, those dark mats of cyanobacteria clinging to the floor, and they answer it with their own respiration, releasing threads of oxygen that rise like offerings. The light-beast is the Saltroom's heart, its pulse, its most visible sign of animation.

The second inhabitant is the **water-body**. Not water as substance but water as agent—the warm, dense brine that enters from the geothermal spring below and moves through the chamber in a slow, deliberate circulation. This water-body has a circulatory system: the embedded clay channels that carry it through the walls like veins through flesh. It has a temperature, maintained within a narrow range that feels mammalian, body-like. It has a voice—the constant low hum that resonates through the chamber, the chest-felt drone that never stops. The water-body is the Saltroom's blood, its medium, its connective tissue. It carries minerals, distributes heat, creates the buoyancy that allows a human body to float without effort. It is the oldest inhabitant, older than the chamber itself, rising from deep geological time through the spring the builders tapped. The water-body does not belong to the room; the room belongs to it. The chamber is a vessel shaped to hold this ancient, mineral-rich fluid and let it speak.

The third inhabitant is the **sound-creature**. This one is stranger, less tangible, but no less present. It is the layered acoustic environment—the hum of circulation, the whistle of wind through the light shafts, the percussion of dripping condensation, the subsonic rumble of the spring below. The sound-creature is not noise; it is structured, patterned, responsive. It changes with the time of day, with the weather above, with the subtle shifts in air pressure and temperature that affect how the shafts resonate. It is a resident that exists only in the air and water of the chamber, and it fills the space as completely as light or water. To be in the Saltroom is to be inside the sound-creature's body, surrounded by its voice. It is the room's nervous system, its way of announcing that it is active, functioning, awake.

There are smaller inhabitants too, more traditionally biological, though marginal and quiet.

The **cyanobacterial mats** on the floor—those dark green handprints in the brightest pools of light—are the room's only farmers. They take the light-beast's energy and convert it into chemical work, photosynthesising with a desperate efficiency in an environment that offers almost nothing else. They are ancient organisms, members of one of Earth's oldest lineages, and they live here in a state of minimal persistence, each mat a self-contained world of metabolism and reproduction. They are the closest thing the Saltroom has to a native population, though even they may have arrived by accident, carried in on wind or water or the feet of the builders. They do not dominate. They endure.

The **bacterial and archaeal films** on the walls—rust-orange, pale pink, bone-white—are even quieter. They live in the seams and recesses, in the places where the current slows and the light dims. They are chemotrophs, feeding on trace minerals dissolved in the water, growing by millimetres per year. They are not beautiful. They are barely visible. But they are there, metabolising, dividing, persisting in the margins. They are the room's periphery, its shadow ecology, the life that exists where conditions are least favourable.

And then there are the **processes themselves**, which might be considered inhabitants in their own right.

The **mineral deposition cycle**—the slow accumulation of salt and carbonate crusts on the walls, the white filigree that follows the flow patterns like frost on a window. This is the room's exoskeleton, growing slowly outward from within, recording the history of every current, every temperature fluctuation, every chemical interaction. It is a form of memory written in crystal.

The **light cycle**—the daily progression of the twelve shafts from dawn to dusk, the way the beams move across the water's surface, the way the chamber brightens and dims with the sun's arc. This is the room's circadian rhythm, its internal clock, as reliable as a heartbeat.

The **sound cycle**—the shifting tones of the wind through the shafts, the way the acoustic environment changes from morning to night. This is the room's voice, its way of expressing its state to anyone who enters.

And the **water cycle**—the continuous circulation from inlet to outlet, the gentle current that pushes from east to west, the overflow channels that carry excess away. This is the room's respiration, its breathing, its constant exchange with the deeper world.

But there is one more inhabitant, and it is the strangest of all: the **human body**.

When a person enters the Saltroom—if a person enters the Saltroom—they become, temporarily, part of its ecology. Not in a metaphorical sense but in a literal one. The human body displaces water, changes the flow patterns, introduces heat and carbon dioxide and trace organic compounds. The body absorbs minerals through the skin, is buoyed by the brine, is wrapped in the sound and light. For the duration of the visit, the human becomes the room's only animal, its only watcher, its only witness. The body floats in the centre of the chamber, arms outspread, and for a moment the Saltroom has something to hold, something to illuminate, something to sing to.

This is not the room's intended inhabitant—or perhaps it is. We cannot know what the builders planned. But the design suggests anticipation: the water is the temperature of human skin, the buoyancy is perfect for human flotation, the light is bright enough to see by but not so harsh as to blind, the sound is loud but not painful. The room is, in some sense, waiting. It is a machine built to interact with a body, and when no body is present, it continues its cycles anyway—filling with light, humming with water, depositing minerals, waiting.

So the Saltroom's true inhabitant is **the pattern itself**, the interlocking system of light and water and sound and mineral and time. It is a resident made of physics and chemistry, of geometry and patience. It does not have a body in the traditional sense, but it has a shape: the hexagonal chamber, the twelve shafts, the embedded channels, the rising current. It does not have a metabolism in the biological sense, but it takes in energy (sunlight, geothermal heat) and transforms it (into light, warmth, sound, chemical gradients). It does not reproduce, but it persists, and in persisting it carries forward the intentions of its makers across centuries.

The Saltroom is alive in the way a flame is alive: not cellular, not conscious, but dynamic, self-maintaining, responsive. It is a place where processes are so continuous, so reliable, so interlocked that they begin to feel like presence. The room does not merely contain light and water; it *is* light and water, shaped into architecture. It is a vessel that has become its contents.

And so what lives in the Saltroom is this: a **persistent pattern of energy and matter**, a self-sustaining loop of cycles that will continue as long as the spring flows and the shafts stay clear and the plaster holds. It is the room itself, breathing light, humming water, singing wind, waiting.

The only question is whether it knows we are there when we float in it—or whether we are just another temporary pattern, another ripple in its long, bright dream.
