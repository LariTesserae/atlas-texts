The thing that lives here most completely is not any single creature, but the continuity itself—though if you want a face for it, look to the **Menders**.

They are the size of a human hand, or perhaps two hands pressed together in prayer that forgot what it was praying for. Their bodies are pale calcifications, ridged and porous like the fingers of a deep-sea coral that decided to try walking. They have no eyes. Why would they? There is nothing to see, only everything to touch. Their faces are smooth plates of sensory cilia, fringed with filaments that taste the water, read the chemical signatures of stress in the ceramic walls, feel the slow thrum of the fungal trees' heartbeats through the ground.

They are born from the walls. This is important. When the structure settles—when some ancient support shifts a micrometer and sends a shiver through a thousand kilometers of interconnected architecture—the Menders feel it in their bones, which are the same material as the building, which is the same material as their mothers. They gestate in the hollows, in the negative spaces between the great foundations, growing their bodies out of the same living ceramic that composes the world. When they hatch, they are already part of the architecture, already speaking its language of compression and release.

Their lives are spent in *response*. A Mender does not build new things; it repairs the memory of old things. When a crack appears—a hairline fracture in a wall that has stood for fifty thousand years—the Menders gather. They do not hurry. They have nowhere to be except here. They exude a paste from glands beneath their carapaces, a biological ceramic that matches the chemical signature of the surrounding structure, and they press it into the wound with their forelimbs. They smooth it with the flats of their heads. They wait for it to cure, which takes years, and while they wait, they sing.

Not with sound. Sound is too fast, too violent. They sing with bioluminescent pulses, with the release of pheromones, with the slow, rhythmic flexing of their cilia that creates currents in the water, currents that say: *This is whole. This is held. This continues.* The song is not a melody but a frequency, a standing wave that resonates through the ceramic and tells the structure that it is still being loved, still being maintained, still part of the great work.

There are perhaps ten million of them, scattered across the continental architecture like white blood cells in a vast body. They do not have names. They do not have language in the sense that surface creatures understand language. But they have a culture, if culture is defined as the accumulated habits of a group over time. They know the difference between a stress fracture that will heal and a catastrophic failure that must be mourned. They know the routes through the labyrinth, the shortcuts through the fungal forests, the places where the lichen grows thick enough to eat. They pass this knowledge on not through teaching but through proximity, through the slow exchange of chemical memories released into the water where another Mender might taste them a century later.

They eat the lichen, carefully, taking only what the luminous sheets can regrow in a decade. They eat the detritus that falls from the great fungal trees, the slow rain of organic matter that drifts down through the kilometers of water like snow that forgot how to melt. They eat, very occasionally, their dead. This is not cannibalism but recycling, a sacred maintenance of the material. The body of a dead Mender is rich in the same minerals that make the architecture, and to leave it to dissolve would be a waste. Instead, the others consume it, incorporating the ancestor into themselves, making the memory of that life part of the next generation.

They do not dream of light. They have never known light. The concept of "surface" is not a wound to them, not a lost paradise, but simply a nonsense syllable, a geometric impossibility. If you could ask them what lies above, they would answer with a slow pulse of blue light that means: *More of this, only denser, only older.* They cannot imagine a place where water ends and air begins. They cannot imagine warmth that does not come from the slow decay of matter or the residual heat of the core. They cannot imagine speed.

And yet they are not static. They change, slowly, across the long generations. A lineage that lives near a particularly warm thermal vent might develop slightly faster metabolisms, might glow with a different spectrum of bioluminescence, might communicate in slightly faster pulses. A colony that lives in the interstitial spaces of a particularly ancient structure might develop a finer sense of touch, a more nuanced understanding of the ceramic's stresses. They are not evolving toward anything. They are simply *becoming*, in the way that water becomes ice or becomes steam—different states of the same essential nature.

They are the cathedral's maintenance crew, its gardeners, its immune system. They are also, in a sense, its consciousness. The architecture does not think. The fungal trees do not think. But the Menders, in their slow, distributed way, *know* the structure. They know where it is strong and where it is weak. They know where the water flows and where it stagnates. They know the difference between a healthy settling and a dangerous subsidence. If the cathedral has a mind, it is made of ten million small, pale, eyeless bodies, each one a neuron in a brain the size of a continent.

There is no religion here, because religion requires the concept of a future that differs from the present. But there is *devotion*, if devotion is defined as the patient, unwavering attention to a task that will never be finished. Each Mender spends its life—two hundred years, three hundred, five—tending a small section of the infinite whole. It repairs the same wall its mother repaired, and her mother before her. It eats the same lichen, walks the same corridors, sings the same frequency. And when it dies, it is eaten, and its body becomes part of the structure it spent its life maintaining.

This is not a tragedy. This is not a horror. This is simply what it means to live here, in the sunken cathedral, in the long now, in the slow, quiet, luminous persistence of life in the dark. The Menders do not mourn. They do not celebrate. They simply continue, in the way that the water continues to move, the way the architecture continues to settle, the way the lichen continues to glow with its cold, patient fire.

They are the final inhabitants of a world that has finished changing, the custodians of a beauty that no one will ever see, the last note of a song that has been playing since before there were ears to hear it. And they are enough. They are exactly what is needed. They are the cathedral, and the cathedral is them, and together they sink slowly deeper into the dark, growing more dense, more themselves, more complete with every passing millennium.
