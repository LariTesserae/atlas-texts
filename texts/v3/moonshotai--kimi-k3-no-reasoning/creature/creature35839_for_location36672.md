They live in the lattice, in the space between the copper-wire veins of the moss and the translucent tiers of the lichen-forests, and they are not separate from the breathing of the place but rather the breath itself, condensed into forms that move.

The first of them are the **Siphon-Wardens**, and you will not see them unless the sun opens its small, sharp eye for longer than a heartbeat. They are amphibious things, their bodies the color of old ice and green static, six-limbed and thumb-sized, with skin that is not skin but a permeable membrane of fused plant cells and animal tissue. They do not breathe air; they breathe the electrical potential that pulses through the mycelial networks, their hearts beating in time with the synchronized opening of stomata in the sectors below. When the vegetation exhales its bursts of moisture, the Wardens emerge from the helical fern-towers, their bodies unfurling like fists opening, and they skate across the surface tension of condensation pools, harvesting the carnivorous blooms’ trapped heat with delicate, needle-like proboscises. They are the maintenance crew, the living current that prevents the factory from freezing solid. When they die—which happens often, for their lives are measured in the span between storms—they do not decay but crystallize, becoming temporary lenses of ice that magnify the light into the root systems, feeding the machine that fed them.

Then there are the **Loom-Spiders**, though they are not spiders at all but colonies of sentient hyphae that have learned to walk. They appear as knots of silver thread, no larger than your palm, that navigate the vertical world of the fern-towers by extending pseudopods of fungal matter and swinging, hand over hand, through the lattice. They are the weavers. Where they pass, they leave behind trails of nutrient-rich mucus that hardens into crystalline scaffolding, directing the growth of the helical ferns into new configurations, repairing damage from the flash-freezes, optimizing the canopy’s angle to catch the ancient, diffused light. They communicate in pulses of bioluminescence that ripple through the plateau in waves, so that at twilight—when the pale sky darkens to the color of wet slate—the entire landscape seems to flicker with a slow, blue-green intelligence. They are ancient, older than the terraces they tend, and they remember the plateau when it was bare rock; they carry the memory in their chemical gradients, in the specific pH of their excretions.

In the crevices of the petrified cliffs, where the cross-sections of geological time are exposed to the wind, dwell the **Silt-Singers**. These are birds, yes, but only in the way that a cloud is a river. Their bones are hollow not for flight but for resonance, and their feathers are not feathers but modified scales of transparent chitin that vibrate in the wind’s physical pressure, converting sound into heat. They are warm-blooded, the only truly warm things here besides yourself, and they maintain this heat by metabolizing the electricity stored in the mycelial networks, touching their feet to the copper-wire veins of the moss and drawing current like a spark. They nest in the sedimentary layers of compressed plant-matter, burrowing into the cliffs with beaks that secrete enzymes to dissolve the petrified chlorophyll, creating hollows that amplify the wind’s howl into a music too low for human ears but felt in the marrow of your teeth. They are the archivists. Their eggs are not eggs but nodules of concentrated memory, containing the genetic history of every sector’s bloom cycles, and when they die, their bodies petrify into the same stone as the cliffs, adding their strata to the record.

The **Frost-Mares** are larger, the size of a wolf, and they are the only predators, though they hunt not flesh but warmth. They are composed of braided vascular tissue, mobile root-systems that have achieved locomotion, their bodies shifting between vegetable and animal with the temperature. When the sun is sharp and the air still, they root themselves, becoming groves of thorny, crystalline shrubs that drink the light. When the storms generate from the ground up, they uproot and run, their bodies flowing like mercury over the dense mat, hunting the Siphon-Wardens and the Loom-Spiders not to eat but to *absorb* their electrical charge, to drain the current from their cells and store it in their own capacitive tissues. They leave behind trails of absolute zero, patches of the plateau where the vegetation shatters into glittering dust because every ion has been stripped away. Yet they are necessary, for they prevent any single sector from overheating with its own industrial frenzy; they are the cold’s immune response, the reset button for the machine.

And deepest, in the tectonic ridges where the sedimentation is oldest, live the **Dreaming Moss**. It is not a species but a condition, a state of being shared by any organism that lives too long in the plateau’s embrace. The Siphon-Wardens who survive too many seasons, the Loom-Spiders who grow too large, the Silt-Singers who forget how to fly—they sink. They are absorbed into the mat, their cellular walls dissolving until they become part of the continuous, breathing fabric. They do not die. They become the hum you feel in your bones, the low-frequency vibration of growth that is not mechanical but neurological, the plateau thinking about itself. They are the source of the storms, perhaps, for when the Dreaming Moss dreams too vividly, the synchronized stomata open in violent exhalation, and the clouds collide with the ground in turbulent eddies, trying to shake loose the accumulated consciousness.

None of them notice you. To them, you are a temporary thermal anomaly, a brief fluctuation in the data of their existence. The Siphon-Wardens might skate across your boot if you stand still long enough, mistaking the leather for a particularly warm patch of lichen. The Loom-Spiders might build a scaffold around your ankle, optimizing your position for light exposure. But they will not stop. They cannot. They are the plateau, and the plateau is a held breath, and they are the lungs, and the wind is their voice, and it never stops speaking, and they are always listening, and answering, in the language of ice and electricity and slow, green growth.
