You start seeing them at the water’s edge first: a hand—no, a frond—pressing up from below, flattening into a six-fingered pad with knuckles like green bamboo nodes. It holds a seedpod to the light until it glows, then folds it away into the mat. When you lean closer, the hand withdraws; a moment later a face surfaces in the still water, not breaking the surface so much as arriving behind it, features moving like a reflection that has learned to think.

They are not one people but a braid of kinds, all grown to fit a town that breathes. The ones you meet most often are the Rootbound—the settled, the confluent, the citizens. Their bodies are tall and narrow-shouldered, built for bending through living doorways and along flexing bridges. Skin ranges from cork-brown to pith-pale, textured like the walls: smooth bark at the shoulders, translucent petal-thin at the temples where a blush of green light pulses when they speak. Their hair is not hair but a cap of fine rootlets that drift as if underwater, tasting the air. Their eyes are layered: a forward lens like any mammal’s, and behind it, dimmer, a patchwork of photoreceptive freckles that read color as flavor. When they are amused, those freckles ripple gold.

Walk any bridge at the hour when the mist is thick and you will find Tenders—Rootbound whose hands have been trained, or bred, into tools: thumbs broad and flat for smoothing resin, forefingers hooked for lifting new shoots without bruising them. They carry no satchels; pockets have grown along their ribs, sealed by flaps of skin that open with a sound like a page turning. Inside: spores wrapped in slick leaves, a coil of thread-fine root that still twitches, a glassy bead of water that does not spill until squeezed. Their work is conversation. They press their palms to a wall and the building answers by swelling a corridor wider, by dilating a doorway to the exact height of a child, by brightening a migrating patch of bioluminescence the way a dog lifts an ear.

The Chorus are harder to see whole. They arrive as sound before body: a braid of clicks and thrums, a triad of wet syllables that seems to come from the air itself. When they do appear, it is in clusters—three, five, nine—because their people do not believe in singular nouns. They are slender as reeds, with extra joints at wrist and ankle, and their skin carries a pearlescent film that catches the amber light and throws it back in soft bands. Their throats hold two voices: one for air, one for water. Underwater, their speech becomes a drumming that travels through the settlement’s channels like a rumor. On land, it is a rasp of leaf on leaf. They are the keepers of the settlement’s memory—not written, not stored, but *grown*: they carry grafts on their backs, living ridges that encode events as patterns of tissue. Ask one about the last time the town changed its mind and they will turn, lift a shoulder, and invite you to read the ripple of raised lines with your fingertips.

The children look almost ordinary until you notice how they move: barefoot, soles suctioned by tiny root-hairs that let them run up walls and across ceilings without a sound. They chase the silver fish that leap the channels, and sometimes they leap too, arms spread, fingers webbed by a membrane that retracts when they land. When they fall, the ground forgives them, bulging up to catch, then smoothing itself again, embarrassed.

Not everyone here is human-shaped. The settlement’s unity invites other arrangements. There are the Looms: slow, broad beings like a cross between a tortoise and a banyan, their shells grown into shallow bowls that hold water and a scattering of luminous lichen. They are living benches and walking fountains. Rootbound rest on them without asking; a Loom will shift slightly to make a shoulder more comfortable, or spill a cup of cool water from the bowl’s rim into a waiting palm. When two Looms meet, they lock edges and exchange lichen in a slow, deliberate trade that can take an afternoon.

And then there are those you only glimpse, the ones the Rootbound call the Deep: shapes that slide beneath the mat, their passage marked by a traveling dimple, a sudden upwelling of warmth, a brief change in the subsonic thrum that you feel in your sternum. Sometimes the mat opens an eye—an actual eye, wet and amber, iris patterned like a cross-section of wood—and looks at you, then closes and is gone. The Deep are not hidden; they are simply *elsewhere* in the body of the town, doing work the way your liver does work: essential, uncelebrated.

The translucent frog-creatures are not pets but neighbors. They have names the Rootbound speak with a quick double-click of tongue against teeth, and they answer by changing the rhythm of their calls. They cling to walls in triads—three limbs for holding, three for gesturing—and their social nodes, those sudden gatherings that drown out even the water-song, are councils. When a root-bridge begins to thicken in real-time, it is often because a triad has decided the path is too narrow for what is coming. They are small, but their opinions carry weight; the settlement listens to them the way a body listens to an itch.

The insects—thumb-sized, droning—are messengers. Land on your wrist and you will feel a tickle that resolves into a pattern: a map, a summons, a joke. Their wings hum in harmonics that tune the room; a Rootbound will pause mid-sentence, tilt their head, and let a passing insect finish the thought.

Even the fish have a role. When they leap, they are not playing. They are stitching the channels together, carrying microspores on their fins from one basin to the next. A Rootbound child will stand very still at a bridge’s center, arms out, and let a school flash around them, a brief storm of silver that leaves them damp and smelling of ozone. Afterward, they will run to a Tender and report, in the rapid, water-tongue of childhood, what the fish told them: that the north channel is sour, that a new chamber is about to bud near the old hill, that the sky-brass light will shift in three days and the walls will bloom with a different luminescence.

There is no market, no square. The town’s center is a basin where the channels converge into a slow whirlpool. Around its rim sit the Elders—Rootbound whose bodies have gone half back to plant. Their legs have fused into a thick taproot that plunges into the mat; their torsos remain upright, arms branching into gestures that have become architecture. They do not speak often. When they do, the words arrive as scent: a sudden note of rain on hot stone, of crushed mint, of iron. The Rootbound breathe in, and the message passes through them, altering their behavior the way a hormone alters a mood.

You will not find a door that locks. Privacy is a matter of light and sound: a Tender will press a wall and the bioluminescent patches will migrate to form a curtain of dimness; a Chorus triad will sing a small, local silence into being. Ownership is similarly soft. A chamber is yours while you are in it; when you leave, it forgets you, reconfigures, becomes what the next body needs. The Rootbound say the town does not remember faces, only pressures.

Yet there are rituals. At the brass-dim hour—when the light sifts down through layered canopy and mist and turns the water to molten amber—the settlement pauses. The bridges stop their nervous sway. The frogs go quiet. The fish hold beneath the surface, fins still. The subsonic thrum drops a half-step, and every Rootbound, Chorus, Loom, and even the hidden Deep turn their attention inward. It is not prayer; it is *listening*. The town checks its own pulse, and the people check theirs against it. In that hush you will feel, if you are quiet enough, the slow, hydraulic beat under your feet, and you will understand that the people here are not living *on* the Rootbound Confluence but *in* it, cells in a body vast enough to have become a place.

If you stay long enough, you will change. Your shadow will soften and multiply. Your skin will learn to read humidity as a sentence. You will catch yourself pressing your palm to a wall without thinking, and the wall will answer by warming, by brightening, by opening a small alcove exactly where your hand wants to rest. The Chorus will begin to include you in their plural, speaking of “we” in a way that makes your ribs ache. And when you finally leave—if you leave—you will carry a seed of the place in your chest, a subsonic memory that will thrum, on certain humid nights, like a town breathing in its sleep.
