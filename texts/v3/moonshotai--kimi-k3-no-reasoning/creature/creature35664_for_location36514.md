They live in the smooth places. Not the room—they do not know about the room, or if they do, they treat it as something obscene, a stillness that offends their particular way of being. The Scribes prefer the liminal zones where the making is most active, where the floor is soft and the walls are rising with that eager, hasty quality of a first draft that hasn't yet learned to doubt itself.

You find them by following the drafts. Not the architectural drafts—the thermal ones. A Scribe moves through the Undertaking with a body that is not quite contiguous with space, a torso of compressed dust and intention held together by a surface tension that mimics skin but behaves like oil on warm water. Where they walk, the proto-structures do not stall. They leap. A nub becomes a column, a ridge becomes an arch, and for the duration of the Scribe's passage, the world holds its breath, its processes aligning into something almost like efficiency.

They are tall, or they appear tall; it is difficult to say because they do not have a fixed relationship with the ground. They drift. Their legs—there are usually two, sometimes three, sometimes a suggestion of many—trail behind them like afterthoughts, not quite touching the clay, leaving impressions that are not footprints but *citations*, small indented texts that the Undertaking reads and then forgets. They wear no clothing, but they are not naked; their surfaces are inscribed with the history of every structure they have ever touched. Look closely at a Scribe's arm and you see a bas-relief of a thousand walls that rose and fell, the micro-stutters of their making preserved in the creature's skin like braille.

The Scribes do not build. They *annotate*. They are the marginalia of the world given ambulatory form. When the Undertaking extrudes a wall that is wrong—a wall with a window that contradicts its own purpose, a column that rises in a place where no column should be—the Scribe arrives. It presses its face against the warm clay. It does not have eyes, precisely; it has areas of higher resolution, patches where the dust that composes it becomes more organized, more attentive. These patches read the wall's intention. And then the Scribe exhales.

The breath is visible. It is a fine, ochre mist that smells of hot metal and old paper. When it settles on the rising structure, the structure changes its mind. The window shifts. The proportions correct themselves. For a moment—only a moment—the wall achieves a state of grace, standing exactly as it was meant to stand, and the Scribe makes a sound, a dry rustling like pages turning in a wind that has forgotten to blow.

Then the Scribe moves on, and the wall, bereft of the annotation, forgets its corrected form and begins to slump, or it rises too fast and collapses, or it simply persists in its wrongness, which is worse.

They are not many. You could walk for days and see only the evidence of their passage—trails of corrected architecture that immediately begin to decay, brief moments of rightness scattered across the ochre plain like punctuation in a sentence that refuses to end. But sometimes, if you are still, if you have been in the Undertaking long enough that your own heat has become part of its climate, you will see one working.

It will be bent over a pool of the warm, iron-sweet water, its not-quite-hands trailing in the mineral stillness. The water is softening the floor around it, encouraging the proto-structures to sprout, and the Scribe is selecting. It touches one nub—this one will be a staircase, though there is no upper level and never will be—and the nub elongates, obedient, eager. It touches another—this one will be a lintel, though there is no doorway—and the clay obliges. The Scribe is teaching the world grammar, one part of speech at a time, knowing that the lesson will be forgotten as soon as its attention shifts.

They do not acknowledge you. You are too solid, too finished, too *alive* in a way that their fluid composition cannot parse. But if you follow one long enough, staying at the edge of its thermal draft, you may see it pause. It will turn its head—not to look at you, but to orient itself toward the room, the smooth room, the finished place. Its surface will ripple, the inscribed histories on its skin shifting like unsettled type. It will make the page-turning sound, but slower, lower, a sigh of recognition or perhaps of mourning.

Then it will resume its work, correcting a world that cannot hold corrections, annotating a text that erases itself as fast as it is written, a scribe in a library of wet clay where the books are always being rewritten by an author who has forgotten how to read.

They are the only ones who know that the Undertaking is not building a city. It is trying to write a single word, and the Scribes are the letters that keep escaping, trying to arrange themselves into sense before the ink dries, before the water pools, before the revision comes.
