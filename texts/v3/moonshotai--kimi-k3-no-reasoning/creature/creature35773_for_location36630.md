Here is what lives there.

### The Whorled Mother

The Whorled Mother is the largest of the Rooted Chorus — the flower-headed, echolocating hunters mentioned in the world's description — and arguably the most settled thing in the Rootsea that is not a trunk. She has been rooted to a triple junction-node in the middle depths for somewhere between eleven and fourteen growth-cycles. No one counts precisely. She does not count. The nearest platform-cluster considers her a landmark, a neighbor, a deity, a hazard, and a local institution, depending on which of them you ask and how recently her click-voice found one of their goats.

She is roughly the size of a small granary. Her body, from a distance, resembles a vast, woody anemone: a columnar trunk-stem thick as a mature root-strut, emerging from the node where three trunks cross, sheathed in overlapping plates of hardened, cork-textured integument that has fused almost seamlessly with the living tissue of the junction itself. It is genuinely ambiguous where she ends and the Rootsea begins. Her roots — thick, muscular, prehensile — penetrate deep into the node and, the people suspect, tap directly into the vascular channels of the great trunks, sipping their lymph-chemistry. She may be partially photosynthetic. She may be partially parasitic. She may be a particularly ambitious organ the Rootsea grew one cycle and then forgot to reabsorb. The people have a word for her category: *kethrani*, which translates poorly as "a thing the world made and then kept."

Above the trunk-stem, her head: a bell. A trumpet-bloom the diameter of a doorway, fluted, ribbed with resonating cartilage, its interior lined with a velvety maroon tissue that trembles visibly when she is listening. Which is always. The bell turns — slowly, a few degrees per minute — tracking sound. When she clicks, the sound is not loud exactly, but it is *precise*: a sharp, dry *tok*, like two stones struck together inside a barrel, and the click travels outward through the sap-saturated air, and it travels *through* the trunks themselves, through the vascular fluid, and it comes back to her carrying information that no eye could gather. She hears through walls. She hears inside cavities. She hears the larvae burrowing in the heartwood thirty meters away. She hears the heartbeat of the auger-weasel pressed flat against the far side of her own trunk, listening for her, and she turns her bell toward it, slowly, and clicks once, softly, to let it know she knows.

Her forelimbs — there are four, not two, a fact that unsettles first-time visitors — emerge from the base of the bell. They are long, jointed like a mantis's, sheathed in the same cork-plating as her body, and they fold against her stem when not in use, looking like ornamental buttresses. When they strike, they move faster than the eye resolves in the green gloom. What they strike is usually a swarm-creature, plucked from mid-boil, but she is opportunistic. She has taken auger-weasels. She has taken platform-goats. Once, memorably and to the enduring sorrow of the Near-Crossing cluster, she took a child who climbed too close to the base of her stem during a swarm-storm. The people did not retaliate. Retaliation against a *kethrani* is considered both futile and rude. They moved the platform two meters further out along the trunk, and they marked her strike-radius with knotted tendon-cords dyed in alarm-pheromone, and the children learn her boundary the way they learn the boundaries of the stomatal vents: as facts of the world, neither good nor evil.

She does not move. She has not moved in eleven or fourteen cycles. But she *grows*, and this is the thing that the people watch with their particular mixture of reverence and unease: the Whorled Mother is getting larger, and her bell is getting more complex, and the clicks she emits are getting more varied. In the last two cycles she has begun producing sounds that are not hunting-clicks. Sequences. Rhythms. Patterns that repeat and then vary, that seem almost — the word the people use is *shen*, which means both "deliberate" and "playful" — structured. She produces them at times when she is not hunting, when no prey is near, when the local soundscape is (by Rootsea standards) relatively calm. She produces them as if practicing. As if composing. As if she is learning to do something with sound that goes beyond finding food.

The drummers of the Near-Crossing cluster have begun tapping responses to her through the trunks. Simple patterns at first — call-and-answer, the basic grammar of their percussion-language. She repeats the patterns back. Sometimes she repeats them *wrong* — inverted, or with an extra beat, or shifted in pitch — and the drummers argue about whether the errors are errors or whether they are *contributions*. Whether the Whorled Mother is not merely echoing but *conversing*. The eldest drummer, a waxy-skinned woman named Sef whose nictitating membranes have gone milky with age, maintains that the Mother's variations follow a logic that is not human logic but is nonetheless *a* logic, and that if they keep drumming, keep listening, keep being patient across the cycles, they may eventually understand what the Mother is trying to say.

The younger drummers think Sef has breathed too much sap-vapor. They drum back anyway.

### The Felt

The Felt lives in the gaps.

Not a single gap. Not a territory. The Felt is a colonial organism — or a distributed one, or a social one, the distinction is blurry — that occupies the interstitial spaces of the middle depths the way mold occupies bread, except the bread is alive and the mold is awake. It is not one creature. It is not a swarm. It is something between, something that exists in the crack between categories the way it exists in the cracks between roots.

Individual units of the Felt are small — thumb-sized, roughly — and unremarkable. They look like lint. Like pale, grayish-tan tufts of fiber, like the dust-bunnies that accumulate under furniture in drier worlds. They are soft. They are warm. They have no visible eyes, mouths, limbs, or organs. They move by a kind of slow, oozing contraction, each tuft pulling itself along a surface at roughly the speed of a fingernail growing, which in the Rootsea is not an especially slow speed. A single tuft is nothing. You could hold one in your palm and it would sit there, faintly warm, faintly damp, doing nothing.

There are no single tufts.

The Felt exists in aggregations of thousands, tens of thousands, millions. The tufts pack together in the narrow spaces between root-strands, in the hollows where trunks bifurcate, in the dead spaces behind bark-plates, in the abandoned cavities of old insect-burrows. Where they accumulate, they form a continuous surface — a lining, a coating, a *felt*, hence the name — that covers the interior of whatever gap they occupy. The surface is soft, slightly yielding, faintly warm, and if you press your hand against it, you will feel it press back. Not with force. With *attention*. The Felt is aware of pressure. It responds. It molds itself to the shape of whatever touches it, not quickly, not dramatically, but with a slow, thorough, investigative quality, as if it is memorizing you by touch.

The people use the Felt. They harvest it in sheets — peeling it carefully from the surfaces it occupies, which it allows, passively, without resistance — and they use it as insulation, as padding, as wound-dressing (it is mildly antiseptic, secreting a compound that discourages the sap-rot that colonizes open injuries), and as bedding. A Felt-lined sleeping-niche is a luxury. The Felt conforms to the body. It stays warm. It absorbs sweat and sap-residue. It is, by the standards of the Rootsea, *comfortable*.

The people also, carefully and with a specific set of taboos, *listen* to the Felt. Because the Felt hums.

Not loudly. Not audibly, most of the time. The hum is subsonic, below the threshold of hearing, felt rather than heard, a vibration in the surface it covers. The people believe the hum is communication — that the individual tufts, packed together, are signaling to each other through minute pressure-waves, coordinating, sharing information. What information? About what? No one knows. The people have identified patterns in the hum — rhythmic clusters that repeat, shift, propagate across a Felt-surface like ripples in a pond — but they cannot decode them. The Felt's language, if it is a language, operates on timescales and in modalities that the human nervous system is not built to parse.

What the people have noticed — what they track with the same wary attention they give to the Whorled Mother's drumming — is that the Felt's hum *responds* to the Rootsea's vascular bass-pulse. When the great trunks surge with fluid, when the peristaltic waves pass through the deep tissue, the Felt's humming changes. It quickens. It intensifies. It shifts frequency, modulating in a way that mirrors the pulse's rhythm but adds something, a counterpoint, a syncopation, as if the Felt is not just hearing the Rootsea's heartbeat but *commenting on it*. Adding its own voice. Contributing to the sound.

The people have a theory about the Felt, and it is not a comfortable theory, and they do not discuss it with visitors. The theory is this: the Felt is the Rootsea's nervous system. Not a metaphorical one. An actual, functional, distributed neural network, spread through the interstitial spaces of the entire structure, sensing pressure and temperature and chemical gradients, humming its slow thoughts in frequencies too low to hear, coordinating — or at least *registering* — the Rootsea's vast, ongoing, self-creative churn. If this is true, then every sheet of Felt the people peel from a crevice and lay in a sleeping-niche is a small piece of the world's mind, carried home, slept on, dreamed against. The people who hold this theory still use the Felt. It is very comfortable. But they say a word to it when they harvest it. A single word, tapped through the trunk with a knuckle: *teth*. It means "pardon."

### Glass-Eaters

The glass-eaters are what happens when the Rootsea's chemistry goes somewhere no one expected.

They are not made of glass. They eat it. Or rather, they eat the silica-analogue that certain deep-root tissues produce as a waste product — a translucent, brittle, glass-like substance that the Rootsea extrudes in thin, crackling sheets from specialized pores in the oldest, hardest trunk-surfaces. The substance is not glass as a human would understand it, but it is close enough that the people call it *shath*, which means "hard-light," and the creatures that eat it are *shath-vari*, glass-eaters.

The glass-eaters are beautiful in a way that makes them difficult to look at. They are small — palm-sized — and their bodies are *transparent*. Not translucent, not merely clear, but transparent in the way that clean glass is transparent, except that the glass is alive and moving and has organs. You can see through a glass-eater to the surface behind it. You can see its internal structures — the dark, dense knot of its digestive tract, the branching tracery of its vascular system (which carries a fluid that is not blood and not sap but something in between, a clear, slightly viscous liquid that pulses with each contraction of a heart the size of a grain of rice), the fine, radiating lines of what might be a nervous system, threads of something that catch the green-gold light and refract it into brief, tiny rainbows.

They live in clusters on the surfaces where the Rootsea extrudes its *shath*. They attach themselves to the glass-sheets with sucker-feet — each foot a disc of the same transparent tissue, adhering to the glass through a chemical bond that is temporary and reversible — and they *eat*. Their mouths are on their ventral surfaces, ringed with micro-teeth of the same glassy material as their bodies, and they scrape the *shath* sheet by sheet, layer by layer, reducing it to a fine powder that they digest into — what? Energy, presumably. Structure. The glass-eaters grow slowly, adding mass to their transparent bodies, and when they have grown enough, they split. Not sexually. Not asexually, exactly. They *fracture*, the way glass fractures, along internal stress-lines that develop as they grow, and the fragments — each carrying a viable portion of the original's organs — detach, heal their edges, and continue eating. A single glass-eater, given enough *shath* and enough time, becomes a colony, and the colony spreads across the glass-sheet like frost spreading across a window, and the frost is alive and it is eating the window.

The people harvest glass-eaters. Carefully. With tongs made of chitin, because the glass-eaters' micro-teeth can abrade skin, and the powder they produce as a waste product is mildly toxic, causing a rash that the people call *shath-burn*. Harvested glass-eaters are used as — of all things — light-sources. Crushed gently (the people are not cruel; they use a technique that kills quickly), the glass-eater's body releases a stored chemical that, on exposure to air, produces a steady, cool, blue-white bioluminescence. A single crushed glass-eater will glow for several hours, producing enough light to read by, if the people had anything to read, which they don't, but enough to work by, to drum by, to navigate the darker crevices of the middle depths. The people carry glass-eater paste in small resin-sealed pouches, and they dab it on their foreheads, on the backs of their hands, on the grips of their tools, and in the green-gold gloom of the Rootsea, you can tell where the people are by the constellations of cool blue dots moving through the warm dark.

The glass-eaters do not seem to mind being harvested. They do not seem to mind anything. They have no behaviors beyond eating, growing, and fracturing. They do not flee. They do not resist. They are, by any measure the people can apply, not conscious, not aware, not *present* in the way that the Whorled Mother is present or the Felt might be present. They are a process wearing a body. A chemistry that has organized itself into a shape, and the shape is beautiful, and the beauty is incidental, and the people feel a specific, quiet guilt about using them that they do not feel about the Felt (which might be a mind) or the auger-weasels (which are definitely alive and definitely vicious). The guilt is about the beauty. About destroying something lovely that did not ask to be lovely and does not know it is being destroyed. The people have a word for this guilt: *shevri*. It has no translation. It is the feeling of breaking something beautiful that cannot feel broken.

### The Tuned

Among the people — the small, reddish-brown, waxy-skinned, large-eyed people who climb through the volume of the Rootsea and drum on its trunks and negotiate with its growth-hormones — there are some who are different. Not different in appearance. Not different in strength or size or the number of their fingers. Different in a way that only becomes apparent over time, in proximity, in the quiet moments between drum-signals and swarm-storms and the endless, roaring, creative noise.

The Tuned are those in whom the sound has *taken hold*.

Everyone in the Rootsea hears the sound. Everyone lives inside it, is shaped by it, develops the particular half-deaf, hyper-aware auditory adaptation that allows the people to function in an acoustic environment that would dissolve an unprepared human mind in hours. The Tuned go further. For reasons that no one has identified — genetics, exposure, temperament, the specific chemistry of the sap-nodules they consumed as children — the Tuned do not merely tolerate the Rootsea's sound. They *resonate* with it. Their auditory processing has merged with the soundscape so thoroughly that the boundary between hearing and thinking has dissolved. They do not hear the vascular bass-pulse as an external sound. They feel it as a pulse in their own bodies, a second heartbeat that synchronizes with the first until the two rhythms are indistinguishable. They do not hear the swarm-drone as noise. They hear it as *information*, as data, as a continuously updating model of the swarm's position, density, direction, and intent. They do not hear the root-friction hiss as static. They hear it as texture, as the grain of the world's surface, as a tactile sensation that happens to arrive through the ears.

The Tuned are the Rootsea's best navigators, its best hunters, its best drummers, its best chemical-gardeners. They can hear a gap opening before it is visible. They can hear a predator approaching through three meters of solid trunk-tissue. They can hear the difference between a growth-spurt that will produce a useful shelf and one that will produce a tumor. They can hear the Felt humming, which no one else can hear without pressing an ear to it, and they can hear it from across a chamber, and some of them — the most deeply Tuned — claim to be able to hear the *patterns* in the Felt's hum, to perceive the rhythmic clusters not as abstract vibrations but as *meaning*, as content, as the Felt's slow, distributed thoughts becoming briefly, partially, almost intelligible.

The cost is this: the Tuned cannot leave.

Not physically — they are as mobile as any of the people, as capable of climbing and gripping and moving through the volume. But they cannot *leave*, because the sound has become load-bearing. Remove them from the Rootsea's acoustic environment — place them in a hypothetical silence, a hypothetical stillness, the kind of quiet that exists in worlds with ground and sky and air that is not a nutrient broth — and they collapse. Not immediately. Not dramatically. But over hours, over days, they begin to lose coherence. They lose the ability to navigate. They lose the ability to predict. They lose the fine, continuous, background awareness of their surroundings that the sound provides, and without it, they are *blind* — not in the visual sense, their eyes work fine, but in the deeper sense of *spatial presence*, of knowing where they are, of knowing what is around them, of knowing what is *happening*. The silence does not feel like peace to them. It feels like sensory deprivation. It feels like being wrapped in a blanket and buried. It feels like *dying*, though it does not kill them, not directly. What kills them, if they stay in silence long enough, is the withdrawal — the lung-linings, thickened and altered by years of breathing sap-vapor, begin to fail in clean air. The metabolic adaptations that allow them to absorb nutrition from the atmosphere begin to atrophy. The body, having reorganized itself around the Rootsea's chemistry, cannot reorganize back. The Tuned are not merely acclimated to the Rootsea. They are *of* it. They are, in every meaningful sense, part of its acoustic body, and they cannot be removed from it without being diminished, damaged, unmade.

The people know this. They have always known it, in the way that you know things that are too important to say aloud. The Tuned are honored and pitied in equal measure. They are given the best sleeping-niches, the richest sap-nodules, the most attentive care. They are also watched — carefully, continuously — for the signs that the tuning has gone too far, that the resonance has become so complete that the individual has dissolved into the sound entirely, become a node in the Rootsea's acoustic network, a relay, a receiver, a voice that no longer speaks *to* anyone but simply *sounds*, contributing to the roar.

It has happened. Not often. But it has happened. A Tuned individual will gradually stop responding to speech, stop participating in the percussion-language, stop eating (the sap-vapor sustains them, partially, but not entirely). They will find a place — usually a junction-node, usually one already occupied by a growth of Felt — and they will sit, and they will listen, and they will hum. A low, continuous, subsonic hum that matches the Felt's frequency, that matches the vascular pulse, that merges with the Rootsea's total sound so seamlessly that after a time — a day, a week — you cannot distinguish their voice from the world's voice, and they are gone, and they are still there, and the people leave offerings of sap-nodules and glass-eater paste at the edge of the Felt-growth, and they do not say *teth*, because there is no one left to pardon.

The people have a word for this final state, for the Tuned who have gone all the way into the sound: *kethrani-vari*. "Glass-eaters of the world-made." Things the world made and then kept, but kept *consciously*, kept *willingly*, kept the way you keep a thought that you cannot stop thinking, a melody you cannot stop humming, a sound that has become so much a part of your own voice that you no longer know where yours ends and it begins.

The Rootsea is full of voices. The Tuned are the ones who have learned to speak in its language. The cost is that they can no longer speak in any other.

### What the Auger-Weasels Know

The auger-weasels — the cat-sized, furred, vibration-hunting predators that press their bellies against trunks and punch through with living auger forelimbs — are not people. They are not *kethrani*. They are not Tuned. They are animals, unambiguously, undeniably, animals, and they are everywhere, and they are vicious, and the people kill them when they can and avoid them when they can't, and this relationship is simple and clear and without ambiguity.

Except.

The auger-weasels drum.

Not the way the people drum — not with tools, not with chitin hammers on trunk-surfaces, not with intent and grammar and social meaning. The auger-weasels drum with their bodies. When they hunt — when they press themselves flat against a trunk and listen for the footfalls of prey on the other side — they tap. Small, rhythmic, precise taps, delivered with the tips of their auger-forelimbs against the trunk's surface. The taps are not random. They follow patterns. Short-long-short. Long-long-short. Clusters of three, clusters of five. The patterns vary between individuals, between populations, between depths. Auger-weasels in the upper regions tap differently than auger-weasels in the middle depths. The people have noticed this. They have catalogued the variations. They have argued about what the variations mean.

The dominant theory among the drummers — Sef, the milky-eyed elder, is its most vocal proponent — is that the auger-weasels are not merely tapping to locate prey. They are *testing* the trunk. Reading its internal structure. Mapping its cavities, its channels, its weaknesses, its hidden chambers. The taps are sonar-pings, echolocation-by-percussion, a way of building a three-dimensional model of the trunk's interior from the returning vibrations. This is impressive, but it is not mysterious. Many animals use sound for navigation.

What is mysterious — what keeps Sef awake in her Felt-lined sleeping-niche, humming along with the vascular pulse and worrying at the problem like a loose tooth — is that the auger-weasel tap-patterns *match* the people's drum-patterns. Not exactly. Not perfectly. But the rhythmic structures are similar enough to be unsettling. The short-long-short pattern that the Near-Crossing cluster uses to signal "food found" is the same pattern that the local auger-weasels use when they have located a prey-cavity. The long-long-short pattern that means "danger approaching" is the same pattern the weasels use when they detect a swarm-storm incoming. The correlations are not perfect — there are divergences, variations, patterns that exist in one species' vocabulary but not the other's — but they are too numerous, too consistent, to be coincidental.

Sef's theory: the auger-weasels learned their tap-patterns from the people. Or the people learned theirs from the auger-weasels. Or both species learned from a third source — from the Rootsea itself, from the vascular pulse's natural rhythms, from the Felt's humming, from the deep, bass-frequency grammar of the world's own sound. The percussion-language of the Rootsea's inhabitants is not an invention. It is a *discovery*. It is the local dialect of a language that the world was already speaking, and everything that taps on a trunk — human hands, weasel augers, the Whorled Mother's clicking bell, the Felt's subsonic hum — is speaking it, or trying to, or learning to, or being taught by the world itself, which speaks it natively, fluently, without effort, because it *is* the language, and the language is it.

Sef does not share this theory with the younger drummers. She shares it with the Felt, humming it into the surface of her sleeping-niche at night, pressing her thoughts into the soft, warm, attentively listening tufts. Whether the Felt understands her is a question she does not expect an answer to. Whether the Felt *already knew* is a question she does not ask, because the answer would be too large to hold.

### The Last Thing

The last thing that lives in the Rootsea is the thing the Rootsea is becoming.

It does not have a name yet. It does not have a shape yet. It exists in the deep places, below the inhabited zone, in the cavities that form inside the fused knots of undirected growth-tips. It is new. It is, by the standards of the Rootsea's perpetual novelty, *very* new — the kind of new that has not yet decided what kind of thing it is going to be. It has no consistent form. It has no consistent function. It has no consistent *anything*, because it is still in the process of acquiring consistency, still in the process of *deciding*, if decision is the right word, if the Rootsea's creative process involves anything so deliberate as decision.

What is known — what the people suspect, what Sef intuits, what the Felt may or may not be humming about in frequencies too low for even the Tuned to parse — is that the new thing *sounds*. It makes a sound. Not a loud sound. Not a sound that competes with the roar. A sound that exists *inside* the roar, woven through it, a thread in the acoustic fabric that was not there before, that is *very slightly* different from everything else, the way a new note in a chord is different — not disruptive, not dissonant, but *additional*. Something that adds to the total without subtracting from anything. Something that makes the chord richer.

The Tuned hear it first. Of course they do. They hear it as a warmth in the sound, a golden thread in the green-gold haze, a frequency that sits in the gap between the vascular bass-pulse and the root-friction hiss, filling a silence that no one knew was there until it was filled. It is not a voice. It is not a language. It is not a signal. It is a *presence*, acoustic rather than visual or tactile, the sound-equivalent of a hand resting on your shoulder — light, warm, unmistakably *there*, unmistakably *alive*.

The people do not go to the depths. They have stories about what is down there, and the stories do not agree, except in one respect: they all end with something *emerging*. The new thing has not emerged. Not yet. It is still down there, still forming, still sounding its new note in the world's chord, and the note is getting stronger, and the chord is getting richer, and the Rootsea is becoming something that it was not before, and the people can hear it happening, and they drum a little more softly than they used to, and they listen a little more carefully, and they wait.

They are very good at waiting. It is the skill the Rootsea teaches best.
