# The Inhabitants of the Breathing Hollows

---

## The Root-Swimmer

The gray-white creature that tunnels beneath the moss does not have a name. No one has been here long enough to name it, and it does not name itself. But it is here, and it is more present than its rarity suggests — for every bulge you glimpse moving through the root mat, there are a dozen more moving too slowly, too deep, or too carefully to be noticed.

It is not a rodent. That was a first guess, and a lazy one. Look closer — if you ever get the chance to look closely, which you probably will not — and the affinities blur. Its body is low and broad, yes, but flattened in a way no rodent is flattened, shaped like a river stone, like something designed to pass through narrow spaces without disturbing them. The dense fur is not fur exactly; each hair is hollow, filled with trapped air for insulation, and the undercoat is so tightly packed that water cannot penetrate it. The animal is functionally dry at all times, even when swimming through half-frozen slush or pressing its body against the wet undersides of leaves.

Its face is its most striking feature, if you ever see its face, which requires luck and stillness and proximity to a pool at the right moment. The snout is broad and blunt, whiskered with thick, stiff vibrissae that are not sensory organs in the mammalian sense but something more specialized — they are hydrophilic, drawing moisture from the air and from surfaces they touch, channeling it along microscopic grooves to the corners of the mouth. The root-swimmer drinks through its whiskers. It can sustain itself for days on condensation alone, pressing its face against cold metal machine housings and letting the thin film of moisture flow inward. This is why it visits the pools so briefly, so furtively — it does not need much open water. The pools are a supplement, not a necessity.

Its eyes are small and dark and set wide on its skull, giving it a field of vision that extends nearly to the sides of its body. It is not a visual animal. The eyes detect changes in light — the dimming that passes for night, the brightening that passes for day — and little else. What it sees of the world, it sees through touch and vibration. Its paws are broad, clawed with short, thick nails that it uses not for digging (the root mat does not need to be dug; it can be parted, compressed, slipped through) but for gripping the dense rhizome networks that form the structural skeleton of its world. It moves through the mat the way a fish moves through kelp — pushing, sliding, parting, closing. The mat closes behind it. It leaves no tunnel, no permanent trace.

It is warm-blooded, but barely. Its core temperature runs several degrees below what a mammal of its size should maintain, a metabolic compromise that allows it to survive on very little food. And food is scarce. It eats the growing tips of the rhizomes it lives among — the pale, tender meristems where the root mat is actively extending. It does not eat much. A few grams a day. It is a grazer, not a browser, not a hunter, not a scavenger. It takes what the plants give freely, which is their own growth, and it takes it gently.

The root-swimmer is solitary, but not by preference. It is solitary because there are very few of them — perhaps a few hundred across the entire valley — and the distances between individuals are large. They communicate through the root mat itself, pressing their bodies against the dense rhizome networks and producing low-frequency vibrations, too low for human ears, that propagate through the plant tissue like signals through a cable. These vibrations carry simple information: location, reproductive status, alarm. The root-swimmer can detect these signals through the soles of its paws and the sensitive skin of its belly, which is in constant contact with the mat.

When two root-swimmers meet — a rare event, happening perhaps a handful of times in a year for any given individual — the encounter is cautious, deliberate. They approach each other through the mat, feeling each other's vibrations, assessing. If both are receptive, they mate quickly, without ceremony, and separate. Gestation is long — four months, five, slowed by the cold. The female gives birth to one or two young, hairless and blind, in a shallow nest she constructs by compressing a pocket in the mat and lining it with shed fur and fragments of dry leaf. She nurses them for six weeks. Then she leaves, and the young disperse, each going its own direction through the root mat, alone from the first day of independence.

The root-swimmer lives a long time. Twelve years, fifteen, perhaps more. It does not age visibly. Its fur stays gray-white throughout its life. It does not scar easily — the mat cushions it, protects it. It has no predators here. Its only enemy is the cold, and the cold is not an enemy so much as a condition, a fact, the medium in which it lives.

It is aware of the machines. Not intellectually — it has no concept of what they are or what they do — but viscerally, through the vibrations they send through the root mat. Each machine has a signature, a specific frequency and rhythm that the root-swimmer learns and recognizes. The big generator near the center of the valley produces a steady, deep throb that the root-swimmer finds soothing, or at least not alarming. The pump stations produce a higher, more erratic vibration that it avoids when possible. The ventilation fans produce almost nothing — their vibrations are too high-frequency to propagate through the mat.

It does not fear the machines. They are part of the landscape, as immutable and incomprehensible as the rock. What it fears — if fear is the right word for an emotion so muted, so controlled by the cold — is disruption. A change in a machine's rhythm. A new vibration in the mat. The approach of something large and warm and moving on the surface, something that does not belong to the world of the root mat. When a human walks through the Breathing Hollows, every root-swimmer within a hundred meters goes still, presses itself flat against the ground, and waits. They are very good at waiting.

## The Pale Glider

The thing that flies — or glides — in the upper reaches of the valley is harder to describe, not because it is complex but because it is barely there. It is the most tenuous inhabitant of the Breathing Hollows, the one most likely to be a trick of the light, a misreading of a shadow, a wish.

But it is real. It is real in the way that a breath on cold glass is real: briefly visible, quickly gone, leaving only a faint trace.

Its wingspan is closer to a meter and a half than a meter. The wings are broad, membranous, pale — not white but the color of old ice, slightly translucent, catching the dim light and scattering it. They are not feathered. They are not scaled. They are skin, thin and taut over a framework of slender bones, with a surface texture that resembles frost patterns — branching, dendritic, fractal. In certain lights, the wings seem to disappear entirely, and what remains is a small, dark body suspended in the air, held up by nothing.

The body is light — hollow-boned, barely fleshed. It weighs less than it looks like it should, and it looks like it should weigh almost nothing. The head is small, smooth, with large eyes that occupy most of the front of the skull. These eyes are adapted to the dim light: wide pupils, a reflective layer behind the retina that doubles the light-gathering capacity, a density of rod cells that borders on the absurd. The pale glider sees well in the twilight of the Hollows. It sees the pools from above, dark mirrors in the green-brown expanse of the root mat. It sees the warm halos around the machines, islands of amber in the blue-gray dim. It sees the slow, sinuous movement of vines and the microsecond flicker of frost forming on a leaf.

It does not flap its wings often. It is a glider, not a powered flier. It launches from high points — ledges on the far walls, the tops of the tallest fern clumps, the upper surfaces of machine housings — and rides the slow, cold air currents that circulate through the valley. The air in the Breathing Hollows barely moves, but it moves enough. The heat from the machines creates microcurrents, faint updrafts and downdrafts that the pale glider reads with exquisite sensitivity, adjusting its wing surface by fractions of a degree, holding itself aloft for twenty minutes, thirty, an hour, before it must land and rest.

What does it eat? This is the question that has no satisfactory answer. The insect-analogues that cluster around the machines are the obvious prey, but the pale glider has never been observed feeding on them. It does not dive, does not stoop, does not perform any of the aerial maneuvers associated with hunting. It simply glides, high above the root mat, and then it is gone.

The answer — if there is an answer — may lie in the upper reaches of the valley, in the space between the root mat and the distant ceiling, where the mist hangs thickest and the light is most diffuse. Something lives up there, or grows up there, or exists up there in some form that is neither living nor growing in the conventional sense. Airborne microorganisms, perhaps — cold-adapted algae or bacterial films that drift in the mist, too small to see, too sparse to detect. The pale glider may filter-feed, its mouth open as it glides, straining the thin air for sustenance the way a baleen whale strains the ocean. This would explain its silence, its unhurried flight, its apparent indifference to the world below. It is not ignoring the root mat and the machines and the pools. It is simply eating something they cannot see.

It is alone. As far as anyone can tell — and no one can tell very far, given the rarity of sightings — there is only one. One pale glider in the entire valley, riding the cold air in slow, patient loops. Whether it is the only one of its kind, whether there are others in other valleys like this one, whether it has ever had a mate or offspring, whether it is young or old — none of this is known. It is the most solitary creature in a solitary place.

It does not call. It does not sing. It makes no sound at all. The noise of the machines fills the air it flies through, and it moves through that noise as though it were weather — a condition to be endured, navigated, but not engaged with. Its silence is not the silence of fear or caution. It is the silence of something that has nothing to say.

When it lands — and it must land, to rest, to sleep, to do whatever it does when it is not gliding — it chooses high, inaccessible places. Ledges on the far walls, where the bare rock offers no purchase for the vegetation below. The tops of machine housings, where the metal is faintly warm and the vibration is a steady, tolerable hum. Once, it was seen perched on a junction box, its wings folded tight against its body, its head turned toward the valley, motionless, for over an hour. The observer — a human, rare as the glider itself — watched from a distance, afraid to move. When the human finally shifted position, the glider did not react. It was asleep. Or it was doing something that looked like sleep and was not. When the human looked away for a moment and looked back, the glider was gone.

## The Condensation Beetles

The insect-analogues that cluster around the machines are the most numerous animals in the Breathing Hollows, which is to say they are still very rare by the standards of any other ecosystem. Around a single generator housing, you might find thirty or forty of them. Around a pump station, a dozen. Around a junction box, two or three. In the vast spaces between machines, none.

They are small — the largest are a centimeter long, most are half that. Their bodies are oval, flattened, armored with overlapping plates of dark chitin that give them a segmented, armadillo-like appearance. The plates are slightly iridescent, catching the amber light of the machine housings and returning it as faint, shifting bands of color: green, purple, a deep oily blue. They are beautiful, if you get close enough to see them, which requires kneeling on the root mat and holding still and bringing your face to within a few inches of the machine surface.

They move slowly. Everything in the Breathing Hollows moves slowly, but the beetles are slow even by local standards. A beetle crossing a generator housing — a distance of perhaps half a meter — might take ten minutes. They are not built for speed. They are built for patience, for endurance, for the long, cold work of extracting moisture and nutrients from surfaces that offer very little of either.

Their mouthparts are their most remarkable feature. Each beetle possesses a pair of flat, blade-like mandibles that it uses to scrape condensation and the thin biological film that grows in condensation — algae, bacteria, fungal hyphae — from metal surfaces. The mandibles are edged with microscopic serrations that function like a squeegee, channeling liquid into the mouth with remarkable efficiency. A beetle can strip a square centimeter of metal surface in a single pass, leaving it clean and dry and slightly polished. Over time, the areas of machine housings most frequented by the beetles develop a faint sheen, a mirror-like quality that distinguishes them from the frost-dulled metal around them.

The beetles are not social, but they are not antisocial either. They tolerate each other's presence without interaction, without conflict. Two beetles meeting on a machine housing will pause, assess each other with a brief waving of antennae, and then continue on their separate paths. There is no hierarchy, no territory, no cooperation. They are simply co-present, sharing a resource that is, for now, sufficient for all of them.

They reproduce slowly. A female beetle lays a single egg every few weeks, depositing it in a crevice of the machine housing — a bolt hole, a seam between plates, a gap in the insulation wrapping. The egg is tiny, pale, almost invisible. It takes a month to hatch. The larva that emerges is a miniature version of the adult, already armored, already equipped with mandibles, already ready to begin the slow work of scraping and feeding. There is no metamorphosis, no pupation. The beetle's life is a straight line from egg to adult, an incremental scaling-up without transformation.

Their relationship with the machines is symbiotic, or at least commensal. The beetles benefit from the warmth, the condensation, the biological film that grows in the microclimate around each machine. The machines — if machines can be said to benefit — are kept slightly cleaner, their surfaces slightly freer of biological accumulation. Whether this matters to the machines' function is unclear. Probably not. The machines are indifferent. The beetles are indifferent to the machines' indifference. It is a relationship without reciprocity, without acknowledgment, without meaning — and it works.

The beetles are prey for nothing. The root-swimmer does not eat them — it is a grazer, not an insectivore, and the beetles are too small and too dispersed to be worth pursuing. The pale glider does not eat them — if it feeds at all, it feeds on something else, something airborne. The beetles exist in a niche without predation, without competition, without pressure. Their only challenge is the cold, and they have solved the cold by living next to warm machines. They are, in their small, armored, patient way, the most successful animals in the Breathing Hollows.

## The Things in the Pools

The pools are not empty.

This is not obvious. The water is black, still, and very cold. It reflects the dim light from above without revealing anything below the surface. It looks like a hole in the world, an absence, a place where the landscape simply stops. You could stand at the edge of a pool for an hour and see nothing move.

But things live in there.

They are small — smaller than the beetles, most of them. Microscopic, some of them. The pools harbor a thin, cold-adapted ecosystem of single-celled organisms, tiny crustacean-analogues, and filamentous algae that grow along the bottom in slow, dark mats. The water is not sterile. It is not even clean. It is a soup, dilute but real, of biological activity operating at the edge of freezing, at the edge of energy availability, at the edge of what life can sustain.

The crustacean-analogues are the largest of the pool-dwellers — a few millimeters long, translucent, their internal organs visible through their carapaces as faint, darker shapes. They swim with slow, rhythmic pulses of their appendages, filtering the water for bacteria and organic particles. They are the color of the water they live in: black, or nearly so, a pigmentation that absorbs what little light penetrates the surface and converts it to a trace of metabolic warmth. They are, in effect, solar-powered, though the sun they draw on is a dim, sourceless glow that barely qualifies as light.

The algae grow in thin, rubbery films on the bottom and sides of the pools, anchored to the rock or the compacted root material that forms the pool's substrate. They are dark green, almost black, and they grow so slowly that a film a centimeter thick might represent a decade of accumulation. They are the primary producers of the pool ecosystem, converting the dim light into chemical energy with an efficiency born of desperation. Everything else in the pool feeds on them, directly or indirectly.

There is something else in the pools. Something larger. Not much larger — a few centimeters, perhaps — but larger than the crustaceans, larger than the algae. It does not swim. It does not filter-feed. It sits on the bottom of the pool, motionless, anchored to the substrate by a broad, flat foot, and it waits.

It is not a mollusk, though it shares a mollusk's stillness. It is not an arthropod, though it has a hard outer shell. It is something that does not fit neatly into any terrestrial category, something that evolved here — if "evolved" is even the right word for a creature in an ecosystem this young — in response to conditions that have no parallel on the surface.

It has no eyes. It does not need them; the water is dark, and there is nothing to see. It has no mouth in the conventional sense. Instead, the upper surface of its shell is covered with a dense field of microscopic pores, each one connected to a network of internal channels that filter the water, extracting dissolved organic matter and microorganisms. It is a living filter, a passive processor, a creature that has outsourced the work of feeding to the simple physics of diffusion and osmosis. It sits. It filters. It grows, slowly. It does not move.

It has no name. It has never been seen clearly — only detected as a slight irregularity on the bottom of a pool, a shape that is not quite a rock, not quite a shadow. It might be common. It might be in every pool. It might be the most abundant animal in the Breathing Hollows, hidden in plain sight, beneath the surface of the black water, doing nothing, going nowhere, alive.

## The Colonizers on the Scree

At the base of the far walls, where the scree slopes of broken rock meet the advancing edge of the root mat, a different kind of life is taking hold.

The vegetation here is different from the valley floor — sparser, hardier, more specialized. The first plants to colonize the bare rock are not the broad-leafed ground covers or the thick vines of the valley floor but small, tenacious things: crustose lichens that etch themselves into the rock surface, moss species that can survive on minimal moisture, and a peculiar, cushion-forming plant that looks like a green-gray brain coral, dense and hard, clinging to near-vertical surfaces.

Among these pioneer plants, a creature lives that is found nowhere else in the Breathing Hollows.

It is a mite — or a mite-analogue. Tiny, eight-legged, armored, dark. It lives within the cushion plants, burrowing into the dense tissue and feeding on the plant's interior cells. It is small enough that a single cushion plant can support a population of hundreds. It is slow-moving, like everything here, but within the microhabitat of the cushion plant, it is relatively active — the plant's tissue provides insulation and moisture, creating a microclimate that is a degree or two warmer than the surrounding air.

The mite is unremarkable in every way except one: it is the only animal in the Breathing Hollows that is actively expanding its range. As the vegetation colonizes the scree slopes, the mite rides along, carried by the growing cushion plants, spreading outward from the base of the walls in a slow, inexorable advance. It is the vanguard of the animal kingdom in this new world, the first mobile creature to push into territory that was bare rock only months ago.

It does not know this. It has no concept of expansion, of colonization, of being first. It simply lives where the cushion plants live, and the cushion plants are moving, and so it moves with them. It is a passenger on a biological wave, a tiny rider on the leading edge of life's advance into a new and empty place.

## What Lives Here, Taken Together

The inhabitants of the Breathing Hollows are few, small, cold, and quiet. They do not dominate the landscape — the landscape dominates them, vast and loud and indifferent. They are marginal to the system that surrounds them, peripheral to the great processes of plant growth and machine operation that define this place.

But they are here. They are real. The root-swimmer pressing through the mat, feeling the vibrations of distant machines through its paws. The pale glider riding the thin, cold air, alone in the dimness. The beetles scraping condensation from metal, patient as stone. The filter-feeder sitting motionless at the bottom of a black pool, doing nothing, alive. The mite riding the cushion plants into new territory, tiny and blind and unstoppable.

They are the beginning of something. Like the plants, like the machines, like the sharp rock slowly softening under frost and time. They are the first wave, the pioneers, the ones who are here before here is ready for them. They are making a world by living in it, one slow, cold, patient day at a time.

The roar continues. The frost forms and breaks and forms again. The light dims and brightens and dims. And in the root mat, in the air, on the machines, in the pools, on the scree slopes, life persists — sparse, strange, and absolutely unwilling to stop.
