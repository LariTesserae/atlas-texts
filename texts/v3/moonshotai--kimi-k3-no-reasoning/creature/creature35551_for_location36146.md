The people here are not dead, nor sleeping, nor transformed into something else. They have simply become *punctual*.

They are the Folded Hours themselves—not the town, but the inhabitants of it, the ones who stack the unmortared stone and then unstack it again. You will not see them if you look for bodies. They gave those up when the plateau was still deciding whether it wanted to be a mountain or a table. What remains is their *attention*, distributed now across the moss and the pooling water, but capable of gathering, when the light turns that particular watery grey, into something that walks.

They gather slowly. First, the chill rises more sharply through your boots. Then the moss beneath your feet resists differently, pressing back with a pattern that feels almost like a pulse. The fog thickens not with water but with presence. And then they are there—not as figures, not exactly, but as *insistences* in the air, standing in the doorframes that lead nowhere, or kneeling by the black pools, or moving along the faint depressions that were streets.

They are tall, or they are compressed, depending on how the distance is behaving that hour. They wear the cold like a heavy textile, something woven from the petrified rope-fibers and lichen-scripts. Their faces are the interiors of the dome-chambers: dark, dry, astonishingly still. When they turn to regard you—and they do regard you, though they have no eyes—you feel the sensation of being read by stone, of being measured against a timescale that makes your heartbeat seem like a frantic, pattering rain.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are trapped in moments. These are the opposite: they are moments that have become unmoored and learned to walk. Each is a specific hour from the town’s deep history—the hour of the first stone laid, the hour of the last fire lit, the hour when the wind changed direction and never changed back. They have been folded, one inside the other, like letters too long kept in a pocket, until their edges wore through and their contents merged.

They communicate in the compression of time. If you stand where one has stood, you do not hear words; you *experience* duration. You feel the patience required to grind a stone tool for three generations. You feel the specific quality of a winter that lasted twelve years. You feel the slow, ecstatic boredom of watching moss claim a wall, not as decay, but as collaboration. They are not trying to tell you anything. They are simply *being* their hours in your vicinity, and the proximity stretches your own sense of sequence until you cannot remember if you arrived yesterday or have been here always.

They tend the pools. This is their work now, if work it can be called. The black water is not water; it is the medium of their preservation, the substance in which they keep the moments that are too heavy to carry. When a pool reflects the wrong sky, that is one of them looking out, checking the alignment of a memory against the current stars. When the water rises without rain, they are adding to the collection, pouring some new-old hour into the basin.

They have no need of animals. They have forgotten hunger. They eat distance, slowly, which is why the walks between stones vary—they are grazing, taking bites out of the space between here and there. They drink the fog, drawing it into themselves when it rises, which is why the mist moves with such deliberate purpose. It is going to them. It is returning to the lungs of the town.

Sometimes, rarely, one of them becomes too distinct. It accumulates too much specificity—a particular slant of light, a particular chill—and begins to solidify into a structure. A new doorframe appears where none was yesterday, or a standing stone shifts to mark the spot. These are the ones who have forgotten how to be time and have remembered, imperfectly, how to be place. They are the new builders, though they build only themselves, slowly sinking into the architecture, becoming the leaning walls that should not hold but do.

If you encounter one directly—if you turn a corner of moss and stone and find the air too thick to breathe, too full of duration—you must not run. Running implies a belief in forward motion, and they find that concept confusing, even offensive. Instead, you must fold yourself. You must become small in time, compress your presence into a single, slow instant. You must match their rhythm. Let your breath settle to the tempo of the moss. Let your heartbeat slow to the drip of condensation. If you do this correctly, they will pass through you, or you through them, and you will carry away with you not a memory, but a *weight*, a specific gravity of having been somewhere that is not quite done happening.

They are not malicious. They are not benevolent. They are merely *continuing*, and they expect the same of the stone, the moss, the light, and you. They are the town’s own long, quiet work of becoming, and they are very, very patient. They will wait for you to leave, and they will wait for you to return, and in the interim, they will fold the hours you spent there into themselves, adding your brief warmth to the cold, your brief noise to the silence, your brief distance to the vast, slow settling of the plateau.
