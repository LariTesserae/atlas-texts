They live in the water and on the roofs and in the walls, and the distinction between these states is thinner than a visitor would expect.

The wade-folk are broad and low, as described, but that shape conceals a more fundamental fact: they are not individuals in the way that a visitor understands individuals. Each body—dark, smooth, many-limbed, slick with condensate or pool water or both—is less a single organism and more a node in a distributed body, a locus where the population's total mass happens, for a time, to be concentrated. They bud. They fuse. They split apart again. A wade-folk who stands in a pool this season may, two seasons hence, be the plates in a new vault and the wet bulk in a different pool three roofs over. The chorus continues because the voices are not tied to bodies the way human voices are. The song persists because the singers are, functionally, the same singer, wearing different shapes across time.

This is not a metaphor. It is how they reproduce, how they build, how they know what they know. When a wade-folk lies down in a warm seam in the rock and does not get up again, it does not die the way a human dies. It softens. It delaminates. Its shell-plates loosen and its limbs retract and its mass begins to settle into the stone, or into the sediment at the bottom of a pool, and over weeks or months it becomes material again—building stock, nutrient, the substrate from which new wade-folk will emerge. The city is literally made of them. The bones of the basin are their bones, the plaster is their secretions, the cord is their extruded proteins, the translucent sheets on the drying racks are their shed skins, cured and repurposed as filters or wrappings or membranes for the vaults. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be wasted. The equation is closed.

And because they are made of each other, because the boundary between self and city and neighbor is permeable in a way that human boundaries are not, their communication is not merely sound. The booming, vowel-heavy speech that carries across the roofs is only the topmost layer, the part that organizes work and position and the daily logistics of a dense population. Beneath it, in the pools, in the seams, in the pressed-together bodies on the wet stone, there is a chemical and tactile exchange that runs constantly: pheromones in the water, pressure signals through shell-plate, the slow osmotic transfer of proteins and information-rich fluids from one body to another through shared pools and shared surfaces. A wade-folk standing in a cistern is drinking its neighbors, in a sense—absorbing traces of their state, their health, their recent activity, their readiness to split or fuse or lay plate. The chorus is sound. The conversation is total.

This means that the wade-folk do not learn the way humans learn, because they do not need to. A new bud swelling up from the sediment does not begin as a blank mind. It begins with the chemical memory of the pool it emerged from, the seismic memory of the rock it was laid down in, the immune memory of the population's last crisis. It knows the song because the song is in the water. It knows the shape of the vaults because the shape is in the shell-plates it will eventually lay. It knows where the water rises and where it drains because the hydrology of the basin is written into its developing nervous system, encoded in the mineral balance of its forming shell. The city does not have blueprints because the city is the blueprint, and the wade-folk are the city, and so the knowledge is hereditary, architectural, somatic. They build what they have always built because they are what they have always been, and the form is not a choice but an expression of their material continuity with the place.

This is why nothing changes. Not because they are stagnant, not because they are trapped, but because change would require the equation to shift, and the equation is their body, their mind, their city, their water, their everything. A wade-folk who laid a plate differently, who built a vault higher, who diverted a channel, would be introducing a perturbation into a closed system, and the system would correct. Not violently—there is no violence in the basin, no predation, no competition, because there is nothing to compete over and no one to compete against—but smoothly, the way a body corrects a fever. The new plate would be absorbed. The new vault would slump. The channel would silt. And the wade-folk who made the change would, in time, soften into the rock and be replaced by buds who laid the plates the old way, not out of conservatism but out of physics.

They do not have a concept of innovation. They do not have a concept of tradition. They have only the practice, the laying of plates, the twisting of cord, the sieving of pools, the endless soaking and curing and stacking and adjusting, and the practice is not a culture but a metabolism. They are the organ that maintains the basin, and the basin is the body that sustains them, and the two are so tightly coupled that asking which came first is like asking whether the heart or the blood came first. The question dissolves.

What they feel, if they feel anything a visitor would recognize as feeling, is a kind of distributed contentment, a low-grade somatic hum that comes from being in the correct relationship to water and warmth and stone. There is no ambition because there is no lack. There is no grief because there is no absence—only transformation, only the shift from one configuration to another, and the chorus holds the space open while the shift completes. A wade-folk who has lain down to soften is not mourned; it is used, and that use is the continuation, and the continuation is the point. The song does not pause for individuals because the song is the individuals, and so long as the volume holds, the population holds, and the basin is whole.

They do not name things the way humans name things, because naming implies a separation between the namer and the named, and the wade-folk do not experience that separation. They have sound-signs, yes—calls that mean *water rising here* or *plate ready* or *come press against me so we can exchange what needs exchanging*—but these are not words in the human sense. They are more like hormonal signals rendered audible, more like the low-frequency vocalizations of whales or the rumble of elephants, communication that is also communion, information that is also tissue. A visitor with recording equipment would capture hours of booming, moaning, layered sound and find, on analysis, that the same phrases repeat at the same intervals in the same contexts, and might conclude that the wade-folk have a small vocabulary, a simple language. But the visitor would be missing the chemical substrate, the tactile exchange, the way a single sustained note from a cluster of bodies in a pool carries more meaning than the sound alone can encode. The language is not simple. It is total, and it is embodied, and it does not translate.

They do not have gods, because they do not have transcendence. The sacred, if it exists for them, is the water itself—not as symbol, but as substance, the medium that holds them, the solvent that carries their chemical speech, the thing that rises from the rock and drains back into it and makes the whole cycle possible. They do not worship the water. They are *in* the water, and the water is in them, and worship would require a distance that does not exist. If they have rituals, they are the daily acts of maintenance: the laying of plates, the clearing of channels, the periodic sieving of pools to remove excess sediment, the collective pressing-together in the cisterns when a new bud is about to emerge and the population gathers to ensure the chemical environment is correct. These are not ceremonies. They are homeostasis. They are what a body does to stay a body.

And so the wade-folk persist, not as a species in the human sense—because species implies lineage, and lineage implies discrete generations, and the wade-folk are more continuous than that—but as a process, a pattern that sustains itself by remaining itself. The basin is warm and dim and wet and loud because they are there, and they are there because the basin is warm and dim and wet and loud, and the loop is closed, and the loop has always been closed, and it will remain closed as long as the water rises and the stone weeps and the voices layer on voices in the heavy air.

They are not waiting for anything. They are not building toward anything. They are the answer to a question the basin asked once, long ago, or perhaps is always asking, and the answer is: *stay.* And so they stay.
