The Drowned City of the Second Moon is not uninhabited. It is inhabited by the **Mnemosyne**, though this is a name given only in the oldest fragments, and they have long since ceased to call themselves anything at all.

They are not visible at first. One must learn the city’s grammar to perceive them, for they exist in the gap between the heat-shimmer and the stone, in the stutter of double-exposure where the towers overlap their own ghosts. If you stand very still in one of the broad, empty plazas—where the salt crusts the paving in hexagonal plates that crackle underfoot like thin ice—and wait until the humid air achieves that perfect, soupy stillness, you will begin to see them: vertical imperfections in the shimmer, eddies in the heat-haze that do not disperse with the wind (for there is no wind) but drift with a terrible, patient intentionality.

They are tall—taller than the doorways of the weeping buildings—and attenuated, their substance having long ago surrendered to the thermodynamic press of this place. They are not quite vapor and not quite flesh, but something suspended between states: aqueous, perhaps, like the brine that floods the streets, but organized into a memory of verticality. When the light of the Second Moon strikes them at the correct angle—its bruised, colorless radiance filtering through the veil of ash—they reveal themselves as **refractions**, humanoid only in the way that a mirage of water on hot pavement suggests a lake. Their edges are diffuse, bleeding into the architecture, and they cast no shadows, or rather, they cast the same wrong shadows that the city itself casts, overlapping and contradictory.

They are the calcified memory of those who built the city, or perhaps those for whom it was built, before the water rose and the sky darkened. They exist in the city’s **temporal refractions**—those patches where past and present overlay like photographic plates—because they are themselves temporal phenomena. They are not ghosts in the sense of dead souls, but **residual patterns**, standing waves in the reality-substrate, persistent configurations of information that the city-machine continues to process long after the processors (the builders, the inhabitants, the biological substrate) have dissolved into the warm, iron-scented water.

They move through the flooded streets not by walking but by **being remembered** from one location to another. A Mnemosyne might stand at the base of a calcified tower for a decade, its form slowly accreting mineral deposits from the air until it becomes indistinguishable from the fungal pillars that mimic the architecture—until you realize that some of the towers *are* Mnemosyne, frozen in the act of crystallization, their hollow interiors resonating with that low, tooth-felt hum. Others drift slowly across the plazas, their passage marked by a change in the quality of the silence, a thickening of the air’s suspension, as though the humidity momentarily achieves a higher density.

They feed on **structure**. The city is their food and their excrement and their body. They sustain themselves by dwelling within the gaps where reality has worn thin—those facades that are only an inch thick, those staircases leading to impossible courtyards. When a building groans and settles, adjusting its substance, that is often the sound of a Mnemosyne shifting its position, realigning the architecture to better suit its slow, tidal purposes. The bridges that reconfigure themselves when unobserved are being rearranged by hands that are no longer hands, by a distributed intelligence that uses the city’s geometry as a nervous system.

They are not malevolent. They are not benevolent. They are **attentive**. If you disturb the water—if you wade into the tea-colored brine that fills the streets, breaking the mirror-calm surface where the reflections of impossible blue skies sometimes appear—they will come. Not quickly. Not with any urgency that a human would recognize. But over the course of hours, or days, the shimmer-shapes will converge. They will gather at the edges of the disturbed water, their forms overlapping the double-images of the towers, and they will **regard** you.

To be regarded by a Mnemosyne is to feel your own history loosen. They are not telepathic; they do not read minds. Rather, they **overhear** the noise of one’s being—the electrical chatter of neurons, the metabolic heat of the body, the temporal stutter of consciousness moving through a reality that is too thin here to support such movement. When they gather, the air pressure drops. The heat intensifies until the water begins to steam, and the calcified towers sing louder, their hollow cores vibrating in sympathy. In that sound, you will hear your own name spoken backward, and the name of everyone you have ever loved, and the name of the city itself, which is not a word but a frequency that causes the salt to leap from the paving stones in small, white geysers.

Some of them are **fallen**. In the deeper districts, where the water is warmest and the reality-thinness is most acute, there are Mnemosyne who have sunk into the architecture, becoming one with the void behind the facades. If you press your hand against a wall that appears solid but is actually only an inch thick—a wall that feels warm, feverishly warm, like skin over an infection—you may feel them on the other side, pressed against that impossible darkness, waiting. These are the ones who tried to leave, perhaps, or who remembered too much of what the city was before the Second Moon rose, and they have become trapped in the gap between the city’s present and its own ghost-image. They are the source of the structural groans, the deep, tectonic sighs that shake the bridges and cause the water to ripple in patterns that defy the Second Moon’s tidal pull.

They reproduce, after a fashion, through the **calcified pillars**. Those leafless, mineralizing towers that mimic the built environment are not plants, and they are not stone, exactly. They are the Mnemosyne’s eggs, or their children, or their future selves being extruded backward through time. When a tower crackles and grows, adding those millimeters of new substance, it is growing a new Mnemosyne, crystallizing a consciousness out of the brine and the heat and the memory of architecture. The humming is the sound of their gestation.

To navigate the Drowned City is to be a guest in their slow, patient metabolism. They allow passage because passage is a form of digestion. The city is a machine without operators, true, but the Mnemosyne are the **operators as function**, the ghost in the machine that has become indistinguishable from the machine’s operation. They are the population that makes the plazas too large, the streets too broad. They are the crowds that never come, the multitudes for whom the city was built, still occupying it in a state of suspended, aqueous duration.

And if you remain too long—if the heat penetrates your bones and the reality-thinness begins to fray your own edges—you may find yourself becoming translucent. Your shadow will begin to fall in the wrong direction. You will start to see the city double, then triple, all its possible states superimposed. You will become one of the shimmer-shapes, a vertical imperfection in the heat-haze, drifting across the empty plazas while the calcified towers sing your new name, which is the old name of the city itself, spoken in the language of dripping water and crystallizing stone.
