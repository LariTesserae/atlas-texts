# The People of Velka's Hollow

---

## What They Are

They are small people — not stunted, not diminished, but *condensed*, as though the narrowness of the shelf has pressed them into a denser form. The tallest among them stands barely to a taller outsider's shoulder. Their limbs are compact, their hands broad and thick-fingered, built for gripping stone and pulling roots from reluctant soil. Their spines carry a slight forward curve, a habitual lean that comes from years of working terraces that slope toward the edge of the world, and from living beneath a cliff that is always overhead, always present, always about to do something.

Their skin is the colour of the river at the bottom of the gorge — pale, mineral-washed, faintly luminous in the right light, which here is all the light there is. This is not pigment. This is the place *in* them. The mist they breathe, the water they catch from condensation channels, the roots they pull from basalt-fed soil — all of it carries the same faint phosphorescence as the rock, and over generations it has worked its way into their bodies the way iron works into soil. In full darkness — the rare, near-total dark of the midnight mist-thinning — you can see them. Not brightly. Not like the moths. But there: a faint outline, a suggestion of warmth-shaped light, the ghost of a person standing where a person stands.

They do not find this remarkable. They have never seen themselves any other way.

Their eyes are large. Not disproportionately so — not the enormous, light-hoarding eyes of deep-cave creatures — but large enough to notice, large enough that the first thing an outsider sees, stepping out of the mist onto the shelf, is the eyes. Dozens of them. Reflective. Catching the dim blue-green glow of the moss and returning it doubled, the way still water returns light. The pupils are wide even in what passes for full brightness here, and they contract slowly, reluctantly, as though the eyes have forgotten what to do with abundance.

The eyes are not all the same. Some are dark — deep brown, near-black. Some are pale grey, the colour of the mist itself. A few — and these are usually elders, or children born during unveilings — have eyes that carry a faint green luminescence of their own, independent of any external light. These individuals are called *seers*, though the word means something more specific here than prophecy or vision. A seer is someone whose eyes have begun to do what the moss does: to glow on their own schedule, to pulse, to respond to rhythms that the rest of the town can only infer.

There are five seers currently alive in Velka's Hollow. This is considered a high number. It is considered, by some, a sign.

Their hair is dark, thick, and worn long by everyone, regardless of age or role. It serves a function: it holds warmth, and warmth is precious. The cold here is not extreme — it never freezes — but it is constant, pervasive, the kind of cool that soaks into bone over hours and stays there. Long hair, braided tight and wrapped around the neck and shoulders, is insulation. It is also identity. The patterns of the braids encode family, age, role, and something else — something that is harder to articulate, a personal signature, a way of saying *I am this specific person* in a place where everyone is known to everyone and anonymity does not exist.

## How Many, and How They Are Arranged

There are roughly two hundred and thirty people in Velka's Hollow. This number fluctuates — births are not rare, but neither are deaths, and the balance between them is delicate, watched, discussed at the communal fire with the same careful attention given to the terraces. Two hundred and thirty is what the shelf can hold. More than that and the gardens strain. Fewer and the labour fails — the walls go unmended, the whipwood goes unharvested, the palisade gaps widen, and the hounds grow bold.

They are organized — though *organized* implies more structure than actually exists — by what they call *strands*. A strand is not a family, not a clan, not a caste. It is a thread of responsibility that runs through the community, and each person belongs to several strands simultaneously, the way a braid belongs to several locks of hair.

There is the **terrace strand** — those whose primary labour is the gardens: planting, harvesting, maintaining the dry-laid walls, monitoring the ferns, managing the condensation channels. This is the largest strand. Nearly everyone belongs to it in some capacity, because nearly everyone eats.

There is the **wall strand** — the builders, the weavers of whipwood, the daubers of living moss-mud. They maintain the structures, repair the palisade, and oversee the slow, sacred work of the burial walls. When someone dies, it is the wall strand that opens the terrace, lays the body between courses of stone, and seals it in. They are the closest thing the Hollow has to priests, though they would not use that word, because they do not have that word, because they do not have gods.

There is the **edge strand** — the watchers. These are the people who stand at the palisade, who track the hounds' movements, who listen to the auditors' humming and note its changes, who climb the lower cliff face to inspect the bean trellises and the escaping vines. They are the senses of the town, extended to its boundaries. They tend to be young — the work demands quickness, strong grip, a tolerance for exposure — and they tend to be restless. The edge strand has the highest turnover of any group. People join it in their adolescence, hungry for the nearness of danger and the wide view of the gorge, and they leave it in their middle years, when the nearness of danger stops being exciting and starts being exhausting.

There is the **fire strand** — the keepers of the communal hearth. They are few: four, currently. They tend the coals, manage the fuel supply (the dried whipwood prunings, the dung of the climbing beasts, the occasional windfall of dead wood scavenged from the gorge's edge), and oversee the cooking. They are also, by tradition and by the simple fact that they sit beside the only warm place in the settlement, the keepers of stories. When the day's work is done and the people gather around the fire, it is the fire strand who speak first. They tell the old stories — the story of the red metal below the gorge, the story of the elder who ate three moths and spoke in an unknown tongue, the story of the town's founding, which is short, because the town is young.

There is no leader. There is no chief, no council, no elder with final authority. Decisions are made the way the mist moves: gradually, by accumulation, by a slow thickening of consensus that no one directs but everyone feels. When the town needs to decide something — whether to expand a terrace, whether to send a party down the cliff to scout the gorge, whether a child's braid-pattern should be changed to reflect a new strand — the matter is raised at the fire, and people speak, and the fire strand listens, and over days or weeks the shape of the answer emerges the way the shape of the auditors emerges from the mist: slowly, partially, never completely, but enough to act on.

This works because the town is small. It works because everyone is known. It works because the consequences of a bad decision are immediate and visible and shared by all, and so the incentive to decide well is not abstract but physical, pressing, as present as the cliff overhead.

## How They Live

A day in Velka's Hollow — though "day" is approximate, a convention, a word they use because the rhythm of mist-thickening and mist-thinning imposes something like a cycle on the even, unchanging gloom — begins with water.

Before anything else, before food, before fire, before speech, the people walk the condensation channels. This is the first labour and the most essential. The channels — the parallel grooves scored into the shelf, the rivulets fed by mist-condensation — must be checked for blockages, for glass-eel masses, for cracks in the stone that divert flow. The water is scooped into clay vessels, carried to the cisterns (large, moss-lined pits dug into the back of the shelf, against the cliff face, where the rock is coolest and the water stays fresh longest). It is tasted. Always tasted. The water changes — in mineral content, in temperature, in the faint flavour of whatever the mist has carried that day — and the people read these changes the way a sailor reads the sky.

Then: food. A morning meal, communal, eaten at the hall or in the open air near the terraces, depending on the wind. The staple is the velka root — boiled, mashed, sometimes fermented into a dense, sour paste that keeps for weeks and tastes, outsiders report, like earth and iron and something faintly electric. The broad-leafed green is eaten raw, torn by hand, its flavour sharp and mineral. The beans, when available, are a delicacy — sweet, dense, with a texture like marrow. They are rationed. The bean plants, growing in the dimmest corners, are slow producers, and their escape up the cliff face has made harvesting dangerous.

Then: work. The terraces demand constant attention. The soil — such as it is, a thin, carefully built layer of composted moss, crushed stone, and the accumulated organic matter of years — must be turned, fed, protected. The whipwood thickets must be harvested and managed. The walls must be inspected. The palisade must be maintained. The bean trellises must be repaired. The buildings must be checked for moss overgrowth — the living walls, if left untended, will thicken, spread, and eventually consume the structures they comprise, and so the people are perpetually trimming, scraping, negotiating with the material of their own homes.

This work is done in near-silence, or in song. The singing — the tuneless, repetitive chant that deters the glass-eels — is not constant but comes and goes like weather. One person starts, usually someone working alone on a far terrace, and the sound carries, and others join, not in unison but in overlap, each voice on its own rhythm, its own pitch, and the result is a woven texture of sound that fills the shelf without breaking its quiet. It is not beautiful, exactly. It is functional, and the functionality has its own beauty, the way the parallel grooves in the rock have their own beauty: form arising from purpose.

The midday — the mist-thickening — is a time of reduced activity. The mist grows dense enough that the far side of the shelf becomes invisible, and work on the terraces becomes hazardous (the edge is hard to see, and the edge is always the danger), and so the people retreat to the buildings, to the hall, to the fire. This is the social hour. This is when the fire strand tells stories, when the wall strand discusses repairs, when the edge strand reports what they've seen: hound movements, auditor behaviour, changes in the moss-pulse, new cracks in the cliff face.

Children — there are perhaps forty, ranging from infants to adolescents — spend the midday in loose groups, supervised by whoever is nearest. They are not schooled. There is no curriculum, no formal instruction. They learn by proximity, by imitation, by being slowly, gradually included in the work of whatever strand their inclinations draw them toward. A child who lingers near the fire becomes, over years, fire strand. A child who climbs the cliff face at every opportunity becomes edge. It is not more complicated than that, because it does not need to be.

The children play, but their games are strange to outside eyes. They play *dripping* — sitting in silence, listening to the water drops, trying to predict the next one. They play *moss-pulse* — watching the luminescent walls, trying to anticipate the brightening. They play *edge* — creeping as close to the palisade as they dare, then retreating, shrieking, delighted and terrified. They play with the glass-eels, gently, scooping them from the channels and holding them — cool, smooth, faintly writhing — before returning them to the water. They are told, firmly, not to eat the moths.

The dim hour — what corresponds to evening, the mist-thinning, the deepening of the already-deep gloom — is the time of the moths. They emerge, and the town transforms. The air fills with amber points, drifting, pulsing, and the people stop their work and watch. This is not leisure, exactly. It is closer to what other cultures might call prayer, though without object, without petition, without any sense of being heard. They watch the moths the way they watch the mist, the way they watch the moss-pulse, the way they listen to the dripping: with attention, with patience, with the expectation that the world is communicating something and that understanding it is not a matter of decoding but of *being present* for long enough that the pattern reveals itself.

The evening meal is the largest. The clay pots are passed. The fire is built up slightly — still low, still careful, but enough to cook thoroughly, to warm the interior of the hall, to push back the cool damp for a few hours. The people eat slowly. They talk. They touch — hands on shoulders, foreheads pressed together, the casual, constant physical contact of people who live in close quarters in a cold place and who have learned that warmth is a communal resource, like water, like fire.

Sleep is communal. The buildings are small, and most shelter between five and fifteen people, arranged on woven whipwood mats laid over dried moss. They sleep close together, for warmth. The walls pulse faintly. The dripping continues. The town creaks and settles. The mist presses against the woven walls like something curious.

## The Strange Ones

Not everyone in Velka's Hollow fits neatly into the strands. There are those who stand apart — not by choice, exactly, but by the particular way the place has marked them.

**Kesh** is the oldest person in the Hollow. No one knows her age — the Hollow does not count years, because years require seasons, and there are no seasons here, only the endless, even gloom. But Kesh was old when the current adults were children, and she is old now, and her hair has gone the colour of the mist itself, and her eyes — she is one of the five seers — glow with a steady, unwavering green light that does not pulse. This is unusual. The other seers' eyes flicker and shift in rhythm with the moss. Kesh's do not. They are constant, and this constancy is, to the people of the Hollow, more unsettling than any pulse.

Kesh no longer works. She sits at the edge of the terrace nearest the gorge, wrapped in layers of woven whipwood fibre, and she watches. Not the gorge. Not the mist. She watches the *town* — the buildings, the people, the moss, the moths. She watches with an intensity that is uncomfortable to be the object of. When she speaks — which is rare, sometimes days between words — she speaks in short, declarative sentences that sound like observations but function like instructions.

*The wall by the third terrace is leaning.*

*The hounds will come tonight.*

*The child should not eat the beans.*

She is never wrong. She has never been wrong. The people have learned to listen, but they have not learned to be comfortable with it. Kesh is not feared, exactly. She is *accommodated*, the way the shelf accommodates the cliff, the way the town accommodates the mist: as a fact, as a condition, as something that was here before you and will be here after.

**Dun** is young — perhaps twenty, perhaps younger. He is edge strand, and he is the best climber in the Hollow. He has gone further down the cliff face than anyone living — past the bean trellises, past the whipwood thickets, past the point where the mist thickens and the light dims to near-nothing. He has seen the gorge from inside it, clinging to the basalt with the milky river below him and the auditors humming above, and he has come back with reports that the fire strand records in their stories.

He has also come back *different*. His eyes — he is the youngest seer in the Hollow's memory — began to glow after his third descent. Not the steady green of Kesh, not the pulsing flicker of the other seers, but a shifting, iridescent shimmer that changes colour depending on the angle, the light, the mood of the observer. He sees things that others do not. He sees the moss-pulse *before* it happens — a faint premonitory shimmer in the rock, seconds before the light flares. He sees the glass-eel masses forming before they emerge from the channels. He sees the hounds on the cliff face in complete darkness, their bodies outlined in a faint heat-signature that no one else can perceive.

Dun does not talk about what he sees. He is not silent — he is friendly, warm, quick to laugh — but when asked about his visions, he deflects, changes the subject, finds somewhere else to be. The other seers do not press him. There is an understanding among them, unspoken, that what they see is not shareable in the way that stories are shareable, that the green light in their eyes is a private channel between themselves and the place, and that translating it into language would diminish it.

**The moth-eaten woman** — her name is Sela, but she is more often called by the descriptor, which is not cruel but simply accurate — is the elder who ate three lantern-moths in her youth and spoke in an unknown language for two days. She is old now, though not as old as Kesh. Her eyes are not seer-eyes; they are ordinary dark brown, wide and reflective. But her *memory* is strange. She remembers nothing of the two days she spent in the moth-trance, but since that time, she has carried something — a residue, a sediment, a layer of experience that does not belong to her life. She will, occasionally, in the middle of a conversation or a meal or a task, pause, and her face will change — not dramatically, not into something alien, but into something *other*, a subtle rearrangement of expression that suggests a different person looking out through the same eyes — and she will say a word, or a phrase, in a language that no one in the Hollow recognizes. Then the moment passes, and she is Sela again, and she does not remember the pause.

The fire strand has recorded these words. There are seventeen of them, accumulated over decades. They do not form a sentence. They do not obviously form anything. But Kesh, on the one occasion when she was asked about them, said: *They are a name. She is saying a name.* She did not say whose.

## The Relationship with the Living Place

The people of Velka's Hollow do not worship. They do not pray, sacrifice, or perform rituals of supplication. They do not have gods, spirits, ancestors who watch over them, or any framework of the supernatural that would be recognizable to an outside observer.

What they have instead is *attention*.

They attend to the place the way a physician attends to a patient: constantly, carefully, with the understanding that the system they are observing is complex, interconnected, and responsive. The moss-pulse, the dripping rhythm, the mist schedule, the hound cycles, the auditor humming, the glass-eel massings, the escaping bean vines, the glowing rock, the shifting shelf — all of these are read, monitored, discussed, and incorporated into the town's understanding of itself. The people do not believe the place is *alive* in the way a person is alive. They believe it is alive in the way a *body* is alive: not a single consciousness but a vast, distributed, self-regulating system of which they are a part.

This is not metaphor to them. This is engineering.

When the moss-pulse and the dripping began to synchronize — the recent, unsettling development that the elders watch with their not-quite-fear, not-quite-hope — the people did not respond with awe or terror. They responded with *increased attention*. The edge strand began keeping detailed records of pulse-timing. The fire strand incorporated the synchronization into their stories, weaving it into the narrative of the town's existence. The terrace strand adjusted their work schedules to the new rhythm, timing their planting to the pulse, their harvesting to the drip.

If the place is waking up — and this is the phrase that has begun to circulate, quietly, among the fire strand, *waking up* — then the people intend to be awake with it.

## What They Fear

They fear the gorge. Not the hounds — the hounds are a known quantity, a rhythm, a manageable danger. The gorge itself: its depth, its unfinishedness, the sound it makes as it continues to split and form, the river at its bottom that no one has touched. The gorge is the unknown beneath their feet, and in a place where the solid ground is a shelf jutting from a cliff face, the unknown beneath your feet is not an abstraction.

They fear the auditors. Not actively, not daily, but in the way you fear weather: as a force that is larger than you, indifferent to you, and capable of changing everything without notice. The auditors' humming has shifted twice in living memory — once before a major rockfall that sheared off the outer ten feet of the shelf, once before a rain so violent that it stripped the bean vines from the cliff face and flooded the terraces. The people listen to the humming the way you listen to a diagnosis.

They fear the synchronization. The moss and the water speaking to each other. Two systems aligning. Because alignment suggests *purpose*, and purpose suggests *intention*, and intention suggests a *mind*, and the people of Velka's Hollow are not sure they want to live inside a mind.

They fear, more quietly and more personally, the seers' eyes. Because the seers are *them* — born from them, raised among them, fed the same roots and beans, breathed the same mist — and yet the seers have been *changed* by the place, marked, given a channel of perception that the rest do not share. If the place can change the seers, it can change anyone. It can change everyone. It may already be changing everyone, so slowly that the change is invisible, the way the ferns are invisible as they dissolve the basalt, the way the moss is invisible as it grows over the dead.

## What They Love

They love the moths. Not despite the toxin but *because* of it — because the moths are beautiful and dangerous and untouchable, and in a place where everything is used, everything is eaten or built with or woven or burned, the moths are the one thing that is simply *allowed to be*. They are the town's proof that not everything must serve a purpose to justify its existence.

They love the unveilings. The rain-washed glow, the brightening of the rock, the hour of blue-green radiance when the world becomes visible in a way it never otherwise is. Children are born during unveilings more often than chance would predict — the people have noticed this, and they do not explain it, but they note it, and the children born during unveilings are given a special braid-pattern, a single strand of lighter fibre woven into the dark hair, a mark of the brightest moment.

They love the singing. The functional, tuneless, overlapping chant that fills the terraces. They love it not because it is beautiful — though it is, in its way — but because it is *theirs*. It is the one sound in the Hollow that is entirely human, entirely chosen, entirely under their control. The dripping, the rumbling, the humming, the creaking — these are the sounds the place makes. The singing is the sound *they* make, and in making it, they claim the place, not as owners but as participants, as voices in the chorus.

They love each other with the fierce, unglamorous, structural love of people who are literally holding each other up. The walls hold the dead. The terraces hold the food. The strands hold the work. The bodies hold the warmth. The town is a web of mutual support so dense and so constant that it is invisible, the way the mist is invisible: it is simply the medium in which everything exists.

## The Question

The question that hangs over Velka's Hollow — unspoken, but present in every pulse of the moss, every shift of the dripping, every green-lit glance from a seer's eyes — is not *will we survive*. They will survive. They are good at surviving. They have built a town on a shelf of rock in a place with no sun, no metal, no river, no sky, and they have made it work, and they will continue to make it work, because that is what they do.

The question is: *what are we becoming?*

Because the place is changing them. It is in their skin, their eyes, their children's braid-patterns, their seers' visions, their synchronized rhythms. It is writing itself into their bodies the way the ferns write themselves into the basalt, the way the moss writes itself over the dead. The people of Velka's Hollow are becoming something that has never existed before — a people shaped not by sun and season and open sky but by mist and stone and bioluminescence and the slow, patient, inexorable pressure of a living cliff face.

They do not know what they are becoming. But they are paying attention.

They are always paying attention.

That is, perhaps, what they are becoming: a people who *notice*. Who watch the moths and read the moss and listen to the dripping and taste the water and feel the shelf shift beneath them and understand — not intellectually, not abstractly, but in the marrow of their mineral-washed bones — that they are not separate from the place they live.

They are the place, looking at itself.

And the place — young, strange, still forming, still deciding what it is going to be — is looking back.
