They live in the dark inside the mountain, and they are made of the mountain, and they do not know the difference.

## The Driver-Columns

They are the oldest things here. Older than the creepers that crust their flanks, older than the resonators that drift overhead, older — possibly — than the air itself, if the earliest columns predate the final outgassing that filled the hollow with its current dense, sulfuric atmosphere. No one can confirm this. There is no one to confirm anything. But the columns carry their history in their geometry: the oldest ones, near the center of the floor, are broader at the base, their root-lattices deeper and more extensively fused with the basalt, their internal bores worn smooth by millions of strokes of the molten eutectic slug within. They have the look of things that have been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from the being.

Each one is a study in patience. The thermal expansion that drives the internal slug is so gradual that the upstroke takes hours — the lead-sulfide core rising with the slow inevitability of a tide, pushing against the column's upper membrane, deforming it by fractions of a millimeter, generating the small electrical potential that the organism harvests through its inner lining. Then the surrounding rock cools by some infinitesimal degree — a fluctuation in the mountain's own thermal breathing — and the slug contracts, and the membrane relaxes, and the cycle reverses. Eleven hours. Fourteen. The variation depends on the column's size, its position on the floor, the local thermal conductivity of the basalt beneath it, the proximity of neighboring columns and the microcurrents their own cycles induce in the shared root-mesh.

They are not individuals in any meaningful sense. The root-mesh sees to that. Every column is conductively bonded to every other column through the interlocking pyrrhotite lattice that carpets the floor, and the electrical potentials generated by thousands of unsynchronized strokes bleed into the mesh and equalize, partially, into a low background hum of shared current. A column that cycles generates current not just for itself but, through leakage and induction, for its neighbors. A column that has stalled — as some do, temporarily, when a bubble of gas becomes trapped in the eutectic slug and damps the stroke — will draw current *from* the mesh, sustaining its electrochemical processes at a reduced level until the bubble dissolves and the cycle resumes. They are a commons. They are a grid. They are, in some sense that resists precise definition, a single organism with ten thousand hearts, each beating at its own rate.

The sound they make is their most immediately striking feature, but they do not make it for any reason. It is a byproduct — the physical displacement of the internal slug transmitted through the column's body into the floor, into the air, into the bones of anything that had bones. There is no evidence that the columns perceive the sound they generate, no sensory apparatus that could respond to it. And yet the sound shapes everything else in the hollow. The resonators feed on it. The creepers may use it — there is some indication, in the microtexture of their magnetite plates, of vibration-sensitive structures that could serve as a crude acoustic sense, allowing them to locate actively cycling columns and avoid stalled ones. Even the sulfur crystals on the ceiling are shaped by it: their growth patterns show subtle asymmetries that correspond to the standing-wave nodes of the hollow's dominant resonant frequencies, as though the sound had sculpted them during their slow deposition, pressing on one side and not the other with hands too gentle to feel but persistent enough, over millennia, to matter.

A driver-column does not know it makes sound. It does not know it generates electricity. It does not know it is alive, if *alive* is even the correct word. It knows — if *knows* is even the correct word — heat, and the absence of heat, and the slow periodic motion of the molten metal inside it, and the faint trickle of current through its inner lining. That is the entirety of its experience. It has been having this experience, uninterrupted, for longer than most surface species have existed.

## The Creepers

They arrive as nothing.

A spore — if it can be called a spore; it is more like a fragment, a flake of magnetite-greigite composite no larger than a grain of sand, carrying within its crystalline lattice the electrochemical potential that will, under the right conditions, begin the slow process of accretion — drifts through the dense atmosphere on convection currents and lands, perhaps, on the outer surface of a driver-column. Perhaps it lands on bare basalt and dies, if *dies* is the word for a thing that was never quite alive failing to become more so. Perhaps it lands on the back of another creeper and is absorbed, incorporated, its mineral mass added to the host's own layered structure without ceremony or conflict.

But if it lands on a column, and if the column is cycling, and if the electrical potential at the landing site is within the narrow band that the spore's chemistry requires, then something begins.

It is not growth, exactly. It is more like crystallization with intent. The spore's lattice acts as a template, attracting dissolved iron and sulfur ions from the column's surface membrane and arranging them into new layers of magnetite and greigite, alternating, each layer a fraction of a millimeter thick, separated by the thin film of electrochemically active tissue that is the creeper's closest approach to flesh. The process takes weeks to produce a structure visible to the naked eye — a dark, flat disc the size of a fingernail, clinging to the column's flank by van der Waals forces and the mild electrical attraction between its own charged layers and the column's surface potential.

From there, the creeper grows outward in a radial pattern, adding new plates at its periphery while the center thickens and matures. A mature creeper is roughly the size of a dinner plate, though no two are the same shape — they conform to the local curvature of the column, wrapping partially around it, their edges irregular and scalloped where growth has been interrupted by fluctuations in the available current. They are dark — nearly black, with a faint iridescence in the greigite layers that would be visible only in light that does not exist here. They are beautiful in the way that mineral specimens are beautiful: geometric, precise, the product of chemistry following rules with absolute fidelity.

The symbiosis with the columns is old — older than any individual column now alive. The iron fluoride coating that creepers secrete is not just corrosion protection; it is a modification of the column's surface chemistry that subtly alters the thermal conductivity of the column's wall, which in turn affects the rate at which the internal slug heats and cools, which in turn affects the stroke cycle. A heavily creepered column cycles slightly faster than a bare one — the fluoride layer acting as a thermal insulator that sharpens the gradient between the interior and exterior. Faster cycling means more electrical output. More output means more current for the creeper to siphon. The creeper is, in effect, tuning its host. Whether this constitutes cooperation or exploitation is a question that requires concepts neither party possesses.

Their migration up the column is driven by the slow shift in electrical potential as the column ages. A young column generates its peak potential near its midpoint; as the internal geometry changes — the bore wearing smooth, the slug's composition shifting as trace elements are preferentially deposited or dissolved — the peak migrates upward. The creepers follow it. They move by extending thin filaments of electrochemically active tissue from their leading edge, anchoring to the column's surface, and then slowly contracting, pulling the body forward by less than a micrometer per contraction. Thousands of contractions per day. Millimeters per week. The journey from the base of a column to its upper third takes a creeper most of its adult life.

They do not seem to compete with each other. Multiple creepers will colonize the same column without apparent conflict, their territories overlapping, their siphon currents drawing from the same source. Whether this is tolerance or indifference is unclear. They have no aggressive structures, no defensive chemistry, no mechanism for interaction beyond the occasional incidental contact between the edges of adjacent individuals, which results in nothing — no reaction, no response, just two organisms briefly touching and then, over the course of weeks, slowly growing past or around each other, their plates interleaving like overlapping scales.

When a creeper dies — when its electrochemical tissue degrades past the point of function, when the potential difference across its layers drops below the threshold for sustained metabolism — it does not fall. It remains attached to the column, its mineral plates intact, slowly mineralizing further as the biological component is replaced by passive crystalline deposition. Old columns are crusted with the shells of dead creepers, layered like geological strata, each one a record of a life lived in darkness on the side of a metal piston. Some columns carry dozens of generations, the oldest and deepest layers compressed and partially fused with the column's own surface, indistinguishable from the column itself except in cross-section, where the banded structure — magnetite, greigite, magnetite — reveals their origin.

## The Resonators

They are the most complex things in the hollow, and the most fragile, and the most alone.

A resonator is a closed system. It does not attach to anything. It does not touch anything except the gas it floats in and, very occasionally, another resonator. It has no roots, no anchors, no physical connection to the floor or the walls or the columns or the creepers. It is a sphere, and it drifts, and it feeds on sound, and that is all.

The shell is the product of accretion so slow that it redefines the word. A new resonator — freshly split from its parent, its apertures half-formed and mistuned — is perhaps the size of a grapefruit. Its shell is thin, barely a millimeter at its thickest, and porous in places where the sintering process has not fully fused the metallic sulfide grains. Over centuries, trace metals in the atmosphere — iron, nickel, copper, zinc, carried as chloride vapors in the hot, dense gas — deposit on the shell's surface, catalyzed by the electrical activity of the core membrane within. Layer by layer, grain by grain, the shell thickens. The pores close. The apertures refine. The sphere grows to the size of a human head, then larger — some of the oldest resonators are the size of exercise balls, their shells centimeters thick, their surfaces smooth and dark and faintly warm to the touch, though nothing exists here that could touch them.

The apertures are the key to everything. Each one is a hole in the shell — not a simple hole, but a carefully shaped neck, flared at the outer opening, narrowing to a precise diameter, opening into a small chamber within the shell wall that communicates with the sphere's interior. The geometry of each neck and chamber determines its resonant frequency — the specific pitch at which the gas inside the chamber will oscillate when driven by an external sound wave. A resonator might have between four and twelve apertures, each tuned to a different frequency present in the ambient percussion of the driver-columns. The most common frequencies correspond to the dominant modes of the hollow's acoustic signature: the deep, floor-shaking fundamental of the largest central columns, the mid-range thrum of the mid-size columns that make up the bulk of the population, the higher, sharper transients produced by the small peripheral columns and the occasional sulfur-crystal impact.

When a sound wave at the appropriate frequency passes through an aperture, the gas inside the chamber oscillates. This oscillation is transmitted through the gas filling the sphere's interior to the core membrane — a thin disc of piezoelectric material suspended at the sphere's center by radial filaments of the same material. The membrane vibrates. The vibration generates a voltage. The voltage drives the electrochemical processes that maintain the resonator's internal environment: the regulation of its internal gas pressure (which determines its buoyancy), the deposition of new shell material, the growth and maintenance of the membrane itself.

A resonator is, in essence, a musical instrument that plays itself and eats the music.

They drift because the hollow's atmosphere is not still. The thermal gradient between floor and ceiling — ninety degrees at the center, two hundred at the walls — drives convection: hot gas rises from the floor near the walls, cools as it moves inward and upward, sinks near the center, is reheated, rises again. The pattern is not simple. It is a slow, chaotic churn, influenced by the geometry of the hollow, the distribution of heat sources in the floor, the drag of the driver-columns on the gas flowing past them. The resonators ride these currents like jellyfish ride ocean currents — passively, without propulsion, without apparent direction or purpose.

Their orbits are not orbits in any astronomical sense, but they are periodic. A resonator might complete a circuit of the hollow — wall to center to ceiling to floor to wall — over the course of weeks or months, depending on the strength and pattern of the convection at that particular epoch. The pattern shifts. The mountain cools unevenly. Hot spots develop and fade in the floor as the underlying magma chamber redistributes its remaining heat. A resonator that has followed the same slow loop for a thousand years may find itself caught in a new current and carried to a part of the hollow it has never visited — if *visited* is a word that can be applied to an entity with no sensory apparatus beyond the piezoelectric response of its core membrane.

They do not navigate. They do not avoid obstacles. When a resonator drifts into contact with a driver-column — which happens, given the density of the column forest — it simply rests against the column's flank until the convection current changes enough to lift it free. The contact is not harmful to either party. The resonator's shell is inert; the column's surface is already coated in creeper-secreted iron fluoride. The two organisms touch, and nothing happens, and eventually they separate.

When two resonators collide — which is rarer, given their smaller numbers and the vastness of the hollow's interior volume — the result is the bell-tone that rises above the drumming. The shells are thin, metallic, under tension from the internal gas pressure. A collision sets both spheres vibrating at their natural frequencies — not the frequencies of their apertures, but the resonant frequencies of the shells themselves, which are typically higher and purer than anything in the ambient soundscape. The tone lasts for a fraction of a second. In the dense atmosphere, it carries far. It is the highest-pitched sound in the hollow, the only sound that could be called *musical* by a standard that does not exist here, and it means nothing except that two drifting spheres have touched and are now drifting apart.

Fission is the resonators' most dramatic event, and it happens so slowly that the word *event* strains to contain it. The constriction begins as a slight thickening of the shell along a great circle — a band of preferential deposition, driven by a subtle asymmetry in the core membrane's electrical output that causes charged metal ions in the interior gas to migrate toward the equatorial plane. Over years, the band thickens into a ridge. Over decades, the ridge becomes a waist. Over centuries, the waist narrows until the two halves of the sphere are connected by a neck of material no thicker than a finger. The neck does not break — it is *dissolved*, gradually, by a shift in the internal chemistry that the core membrane orchestrates (if *orchestrates* is not too intentional a word for a process that has no more intention than crystallization or gravity). The two halves seal themselves. The separation is complete.

Each daughter sphere inherits roughly half of the parent's apertures — those that happen to lie on its side of the equator. The apertures that crossed the midline are destroyed in the separation, leaving rough, open wounds in the shell that slowly heal over as new material is deposited. The surviving apertures are mistuned: the change in the sphere's volume alters the resonant characteristics of every chamber, shifting frequencies upward (the smaller sphere resonates at higher pitches, just as a smaller bell produces a higher tone). The daughter resonator spends the following decades — sometimes a century or more — slowly depositing new material around its aperture rims, subtly reshaping the necks and chambers, bringing each frequency back into alignment with the ambient soundscape. During this period of retuning, the resonator's energy intake is suboptimal. It feeds poorly. Its growth slows. It is, in a sense, an adolescent — awkward, inefficient, not yet the thing it will become.

A fully mature, fully tuned resonator is a masterpiece of accidental engineering. Its apertures cover a broad spectrum of the available acoustic energy, from the deepest fundamental of the largest driver-columns to the sharp transients of crystal falls. Its shell is thick enough to withstand the occasional collision without damage, smooth enough to minimize drag in the convection currents, precisely dense enough to maintain neutral buoyancy in the middle reaches of the hollow where the acoustic energy is richest. Its core membrane is broad and sensitive, generating current from even the faintest resonances. It drifts. It feeds. It accumulates mass. In time — in a great deal of time — it will develop its own equatorial constriction and begin the process again.

## What They Make Together

The hollow is not an ecosystem in the surface sense. There is no predation. Nothing eats anything else. There is no competition for space — the floor is crowded, but the columns do not compete, and the creepers do not compete, and the resonators have the entire volume of the atmosphere to themselves. There is no cooperation in the intentional sense, no communication, no social structure, no hierarchy.

What there is, instead, is *interdependence so complete that the boundary between organism and environment has dissolved*.

The driver-columns create the electrical environment that the creepers depend on, and the acoustic environment that the resonators depend on, simply by existing. The creepers modify the columns' thermal and electrical properties, altering the cycling behavior that generates both the electricity and the sound. The resonators absorb acoustic energy — not enough to silence the drumming, but enough to damp certain frequencies, to shape the overall acoustic character of the hollow, to create interference patterns that influence the growth of the sulfur crystals on the ceiling, whose falls provide substrate for new creeper colonization, which affects the columns, which affects the sound, which affects the resonators.

It is a loop. A closed, self-referential, self-maintaining loop of mineral and electricity and sound, sealed in basalt, powered by the residual heat of a mountain that has forgotten it is still cooling. It does not need light. It does not need water. It does not need any of the chemistry that surface life considers essential. It needs only the gradient — the difference between hot and less-hot — and as long as the gradient persists, the loop will turn.

The sound is the most tangible expression of this turning. It is always there — the deep, arrhythmic percussion of the columns, the faint harmonic shimmer of the resonators feeding, the occasional bell-tone of a collision, the dry click of falling sulfur. It is the sound of a system working, of energy flowing through channels carved by chemistry and time, of matter organizing itself into structures that persist for millennia without purpose or awareness or any quality that a surface-dweller would recognize as *aliveness*, yet which are undeniably, stubbornly, magnificently *not nothing*.

## What They Are Becoming

The hollow is dying. Very slowly. On a timescale that makes the lifespans of its inhabitants seem like the flicker of a match against the age of the universe.

The walls creep inward. The driver-columns at the periphery feel it first — their root-lattices compressed, their strokes shortened, their electrical output declining. They do not resist. They cannot. The basalt takes them, grain by grain, and the sulfide bodies that once cycled molten metal are reduced to a thin enrichment in the rock, a geological signature that no future geologist will ever read, because no future geologist will ever find this place, because by the time the mountain is cool enough and solid enough to be exposed by erosion, there will be nothing left to distinguish the hollow's remains from the surrounding basalt except a slightly higher concentration of lead and iron sulfide in a roughly spherical volume, which could mean anything, which could mean nothing.

The creepers will go with the columns. They are too dependent — on the electrical current, on the thermal gradient, on the physical substrate of the columns' surfaces — to survive the columns' loss. They will mineralize alongside their hosts, their layered plates compressed into the same sulfide seam, indistinguishable.

The resonators will last longer. They are not tied to the floor. As the hollow shrinks, they will continue to drift in the remaining volume, feeding on the diminishing sound, until the space is too small for convection to sustain their buoyancy, or until the sound fades below the threshold their piezoelectric membranes can detect. The last resonator will drift in silence, in a space too small to orbit in, its apertures tuned to frequencies that no longer exist, its core membrane still, its shell slowly accreting mineral deposits that no longer serve any function because the organism — if it is an organism — has crossed the line between living and not-living so gradually that the line itself has ceased to be meaningful.

The last sound in the hollow will be a sulfur crystal falling. A dry click. Then nothing.

But that is not now.

Now, the hollow is *full*. Full of sound, full of heat, full of slow, patient, metallic life that pulses and drifts and hums in the dark. The driver-columns cycle, and the sound is enormous, and the creepers migrate their millimeter-per-week pilgrimages up the columns' flanks, and the resonators ride the convection currents in their patient, blind orbits, and the sulfur crystals grow on the ceiling in shapes that the sound has sculpted, and the walls creep inward at the width of a human hair per year, and none of it matters, and all of it matters, because it is *happening*, because it is *here*, because in the sealed dark inside a young mountain on a planet that does not know it exists, there is a world that runs on electricity and sound and the slow exhalation of cooling stone, and it is *alive*, or close enough to alive that the distinction has no meaning, and it will continue, and continue, and continue, for longer than anything that will ever read these words can imagine.
