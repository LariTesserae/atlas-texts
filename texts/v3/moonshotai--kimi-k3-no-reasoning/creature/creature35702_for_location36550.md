# What Lives in the Seeping Hollow

## The Seepers

They are small. This must be said first, because everything about them follows from it.

A Seeper is, at its largest, the length of your forefinger. Most are smaller — thumb-joint sized, the smaller ones no bigger than a fingernail. They are built low, close to the stone, their bodies wide and flat, hugging the surface the way a limpet hugs a rock but without the limpet's tension. They do not cling. They *rest*. Their weight distributes across a broad ventral surface that is soft, slightly concave, and in constant, intimate contact with the warm wet stone beneath them. They do not have legs in any sense that would be useful for walking quickly. They have — the closest word is *lips* — fleshy, muscular rims around their undersides that ripple in slow, coordinated waves, carrying them forward at a pace that a patient observer might measure in inches per hour. They do not hurry. Hurrying is not a concept their nervous systems contain. They move the way their water moves: certainly, gradually, without urgency, downhill unless they have a reason to go otherwise.

Their backs are hard. Not shell-hard, not the rigid carapace of an arthropod, but a dense, smooth, slightly domed plate of material that is closer to stone than to bone. It is the color of the basin floor — tea-brown, ochre, warm rust, that barely-grey. When a Seeper is at rest, which is most of the time, it is nearly indistinguishable from the small stones and structures that crowd every surface of the Hollow. This is not camouflage in the deliberate sense. It is convergence. They are made of the same world they live in. Their bodies take up minerals from the water they drink, and those minerals are deposited in the dorsal plate in layers — very thin layers, laid down over years, each one a slightly different shade depending on what the water carried that season (there are no seasons; depending on what the water carried during that span of unchanging time). An old Seeper's back is a stratigraphic record. You could read it, if you knew how, the way you read sedimentary rock: here a band of darker brown, here a seam of rust, here a layer so pale it is almost cream. The oldest Seepers carry centuries on their backs.

Their eyes are — this is difficult to describe accurately, because they are not eyes in the sense of organs that capture images. They are light-sensitive patches on the dorsal plate, two of them, positioned at the widest point of the dome, like tiny windows of slightly translucent stone. They register brightness and darkness, warmth and the absence of warmth, but not shape, not movement, not color in any differentiated sense. A Seeper does not see the basin. A Seeper *feels* the basin — through its ventral surface, which is rich with chemosensory tissue, through the fine, hair-like filaments that fringe its body margins and taste the water continuously, through vibrations transmitted through stone into the soft underside of its body. A Seeper's world is a world of wetness, warmth, texture, mineral content, and the slow, pervasive pulse of water moving through stone. It is a rich world. It is enough.

They are not social in the way that ants are social, or bees, or even wolves. They do not cooperate in any directed, intentional fashion. They do not communicate through pheromone trails or dances or calls. What they do — what they *are* — is something harder to name. They are *attentive to each other the way they are attentive to the water*: as features of the landscape that bear noticing, that bear responding to, that shape the pattern of one's own movement and activity in ways too subtle to be called decisions. A Seeper following a channel will pause when it encounters another Seeper. Not because it recognizes the other as an individual — there is no evidence they possess individual recognition — but because the presence of another body, another warmth, another slight displacement of water, is *information*. It is a data point in the slow, continuous process of understanding where water is and where water goes and what the stone is doing beneath it.

They are everywhere in the Hollow. Hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. Their density tracks the density of the structures, and this is not coincidence.

They built the structures.

## Building

A Seeper builds the way it moves: slowly, with lips and time.

The process begins — began, long ago, begins again each time a new structure is started — with noticing. Not *noticing* as a sudden act of perception, but noticing as a condition. A Seeper spends its life in continuous, intimate contact with a small stretch of stone and water. It knows this stretch the way you know the inside of your own mouth: not through study but through *presence*. It knows where the water seeps fastest. It knows where a thread of moisture divides around a grain of harder stone. It knows where the flow pools for a moment before continuing. This knowledge is not cognitive — it is not stored in a brain and consulted. It is *embodied*. It exists in the patterns of the Seeper's own movement, in the routes it habitually takes, in the places it habitually rests, in the texture of its attention.

At some point — and the trigger is not well understood, because the process is too slow to observe in anything shorter than a lifetime — a Seeper's habitual engagement with a particular flow pattern will shift from *following* to *shaping*. It will begin to push.

The ventral lips, those soft, muscular, rippling rims that carry the Seeper across stone, are also capable of working stone. Not carving — Seepers have no hard tools, no claws, no beaks. But the lips can grip small particles — sand grains, tiny pebbles, flakes of mudstone — and hold them against the rock surface, and *grind*. The motion is the same rippling wave they use for locomotion, but applied with pressure, in place, over hours, over days, over weeks, over months, over years. A single Seeper, grinding a single groove into mudstone, might advance the depth of that groove by the width of a hair in a day. But the Seeper does not stop. It does not get bored. It does not get distracted. It does not wonder whether the groove is worth grinding. It grinds because the groove is where the water wants to go, and the water wanting to go there is the most important fact in the Seeper's world, and the Seeper's body responds to that fact the only way it knows: by helping.

Other Seepers join. Not by summons, not by plan, but because a groove being ground is a groove that carries slightly more water, and slightly more water is slightly more noticeable, and Seepers notice water. A second Seeper will settle beside the first and begin its own grinding, parallel or adjacent, and the groove deepens. A third comes. The groove becomes a channel. The channel carries water more efficiently, which makes it more noticeable, which draws more Seepers, which deepen the channel further. It is a feedback loop with no intention behind it — or with an intention so distributed, so diluted across hundreds of individuals and decades of time, that it cannot be located in any single mind or any single moment.

The channels grow. Over years, over decades, over the spans of time that the Seeping Hollow measures in the falling of condensation drops and the layering of minerals on dorsal plates, the channels become the dense, interlocking, saturated network that fills the basin. They follow natural drainage because natural drainage is what the Seepers follow — they are not engineers designing a system but participants in a system, deepening and articulating paths that water already takes. The structures are not imposed on the landscape. They are the landscape, *emphasized*.

The smaller constructions — the thumb-sized cups, the flat shelves, the tiny arches at junctions — arise from the same process. Where water pools in a channel, a Seeper will rest, and where a Seeper rests, its lips will work the stone beneath it, and over time the resting-place becomes a basin. Where two channels converge, the increased flow attracts more Seepers, and the increased Seeper activity at the junction results in more grinding, more shaping, more accidental construction — a ring of displaced pebbles pushed aside by ventral lips becomes a ring of fitted stones; a flat area worn smooth by many bodies becomes a shelf; a pebble lodged across a channel becomes the keystone of an arch as Seepers grind the stone around it, under it, shaping the gap into a deliberate passage.

The arches deserve particular attention, because they are the structures that most clearly suggest intention, and they are the ones that most thoroughly complicate the boundary between accident and purpose. An arch begins as an obstruction — a grain of harder stone, a small pebble — that the water flows around. Seepers, following the water, flow around it too. Their bodies, pressing against the obstruction, gradually displace the softer stone around it, excavating beneath it, until the obstruction stands on a pillar of its own making, and water passes *under* it. The Seepers did not decide to build an arch. The arch is what happens when Seepers follow water around an obstacle for a hundred years. But the arch *functions* as an arch — it carries water over a rise, it maintains the channel's continuity — and so it persists, and the Seepers that use it reinforce it, and over time it becomes as deliberate as anything built by hands that knew what they were making.

## The Enclosures

The circular enclosures — hat-sized, open-topped, touch-smoothed — are the Seepers' resting places, their refuges, the closest thing they have to homes. A Seeper does not sleep in the sense of losing consciousness. It enters states of reduced activity, periods of near-total stillness during which its metabolic rate drops to almost nothing and its lips cease their rippling and it simply *sits*, warm and wet and barely distinguishable from a stone, for hours or days or weeks. The enclosures are where this happens. They are places where the stone has been worn smooth by generations of resting bodies, where the walls have been built up (by displaced particles, ground from the floor and pushed to the edges, accumulating over time into a low ring) just enough to create a microenvironment of slightly deeper water, slightly more sheltered from whatever faint disturbances the Hollow offers.

A Seeper in an enclosure is not hiding from anything. There are no predators in the Hollow. There is nothing to hide from. The enclosure is not a shelter — it is an *expression*. It is the Seeper's body extended into stone, the Seeper's habit of stillness given architectural form. The smoothness of the interior is the smoothness of long contact, the polish of skin on stone over decades. The enclosure says: *someone rested here, for a long time, and the resting shaped the place*.

Multiple Seepers will share an enclosure, but not cooperatively. They do not choose to share. They simply arrive — drawn by the same qualities of water depth and stone texture that drew the previous occupant — and settle. Two Seepers in an enclosure will sit side by side, ventral surfaces on the same smooth stone, dorsal plates touching or nearly touching, in a proximity that might be companionship or might be coincidence and is probably neither and both. They do not interact. They do not acknowledge each other. But they are *together*, and their togetherness shapes the enclosure further, deepens it, smooths it, makes it more attractive to the next Seeper that comes along, and so the enclosure becomes a site of accumulated presence, a place where the stillness is thicker, more layered, more *inhabited* than the stillness of open stone.

## The Tools

The tools — the hammerstones, the grinding stones, the shaped scrapers — are not tools in the sense that a chimpanzee's stick is a tool. They were not picked up and used for a purpose and then set down. They are the products of a different process, one that blurs the line between tool-making and structure-building even further.

When a Seeper grinds stone, it uses particles held in its ventral lips — sand grains, tiny pebbles, whatever abrasive material is available. These particles are, in effect, tools: they are objects used to work other objects. But the Seeper does not select them for their properties. It picks up what is there. Over time, however, certain particles become favored — not through deliberate choice but through the simple mechanics of use. A grain of quartz, harder than the surrounding mudstone, will last longer, grind more effectively, and survive the process intact while softer particles are worn away. Quartz grains accumulate in the areas where Seepers work. They are not collected deliberately — they are *selected* by the process, the way a river selects for rounded stones. Over time, the concentration of hard, effective grinding particles in a work area increases, and these concentrations are what a visitor perceives as *tools*.

The hammerstones are similar. Seepers sometimes push against larger stones — dislodging them, repositioning them — as part of their channel-building. The stones they push against acquire, over time, a characteristic pattern of wear: a flattened face where the Seeper's lips have pressed, a slight polish from repeated contact. These are not hammerstones that were picked up and used to strike things. They are *anvils* — passive surfaces against which the Seeper applied pressure — and their wear patterns record the history of that pressure.

The grinding stones — flat, dished, polished — are simply stones that have been worked by many Seepers over long periods. The dishing is the accumulated effect of ventral lips grinding in a roughly circular motion (the motion a Seeper makes when it is shaping a basin or deepening a pool), and the polish is the result of fine particles suspended in water being worked against the stone surface until it gleams. They look like tools because they bear the marks of use. They *are* tools, in the sense that they were used. But they were not *designed*. They were *accumulated*.

This distinction — between designed and accumulated — is the key to understanding the Seepers, and it is also the key to why the Seeping Hollow feels the way it does. Everything in the Hollow is accumulated. Nothing is designed. But the accumulation is so consistent, so patterned, so deeply *responsive* to the landscape's own logic, that the result is indistinguishable from design. The Hollow looks built because it *is* built. It just wasn't *planned*.

## The Markings

The patterns — the pecked circles, spirals, dot-grids, the sorted pebble-stacks, the lines of darker stone — are the most mysterious aspect of Seeper behavior, and the aspect that most forcefully suggests something beyond simple mechanical response to water flow.

They are not channels. They do not carry water. They are not basins. They do not hold water. They are not enclosures. Nothing rests in them. They are marks on stone — depressions pecked by the slow grinding of ventral lips, lines of material arranged by the slow pushing of bodies — that serve no hydrological function and no structural function and no evident biological function.

They are, as far as can be determined, *expressive*.

A Seeper that has spent a long time — years, decades — working a particular stretch of channel will sometimes, without any external trigger, begin to grind in a pattern. Not a groove that follows the water. A pattern that *departs* from the water. A spiral that begins at the channel's edge and curls inward, away from the flow, into the stone. A circle. A grid of dots. The grinding is the same motion used for channel-building — the rippling, abrasive press of ventral lips against stone — but the path is not dictated by drainage. It is dictated by something internal, something that arises from the Seeper's own accumulated experience of the place and finds expression in the only medium available: stone, worked slowly, with patience, over time.

These patterns are rare. For every hundred feet of channel, there might be one pecked spiral, one grid of dots. They occur most frequently near the enclosures, near the resting places, in the zones where Seepers spend their still hours. They are made during the transition periods — the slow, half-awake states between activity and rest, when the Seeper's lips are moving but not *going anywhere*, when the grinding is not directed at a channel or a basin but is simply *happening*, the way a person's hand might doodle on a page while their mind is elsewhere.

Except the Seeper's mind is not elsewhere. The Seeper's mind — if the word can be used at all, if the slow, distributed, embodied cognition of a creature that thinks with its ventral surface and remembers with its dorsal plate can be called a mind — is *exactly here*. The Seeper is here, in this spot, in this water, on this stone, and the pattern it grinds is the shape of that here-ness, given form. The spiral is not a symbol. It is not a map. It is not a prayer. It is the Seeper's presence, translated into stone, the way the strata on its back are its life translated into mineral. It is the mark of a creature that has been somewhere for a long time and has, in the deepest sense available to it, *noticed*.

The sorted pebbles are similar. A Seeper pushing particles aside as it works will, over time, create accumulations of displaced material. In most cases, these accumulations are random — heaps of sand and grit pushed to the edge of a channel. But occasionally, very occasionally, a Seeper's displacement pattern will produce something that looks *ordered*: a stack of pebbles sorted by size, a line of darker stones separated from lighter ones. This happens because the Seeper's lips respond differently to particles of different sizes and densities — heavier particles require more pressure to move and travel shorter distances; lighter ones are pushed further; rounder ones roll; angular ones catch. The sorting is mechanical. But the *result* is aesthetic, and the result persists, and other Seepers encounter it and respond to it — not by recognizing it as art, but by being, perhaps, slightly more likely to rest near it, to work near it, to add their own displaced particles to the pattern in ways that reinforce rather than disrupt it.

The patterns grow. Over time, they become the things a visitor finds and cannot explain: small, deliberate-looking arrangements of stone that seem to say *someone was here and wanted to mark it* but were produced by no one's wanting, only by the accumulated weight of presence.

## What They Eat

The water is clear because nothing lives in it. This was stated earlier, and it is true — nothing *swims* in the water, nothing photosynthesizes in the water, nothing clouds the water with biological activity. But the water is not sterile. It carries dissolved minerals — silica, iron, calcium, trace elements leached from the saturated mudstone — in concentrations that vary subtly from point to point across the basin. The variation is driven by the seepage: water emerging from a particular flute on the slope carries a slightly different mineral signature than water emerging from a flute ten feet away, because it has traveled through slightly different rock.

The Seepers eat this. Not the water itself — the minerals. Their ventral surfaces are lined with absorptive tissue that extracts dissolved minerals directly from the water passing beneath them. They do not bite, chew, filter, or digest. They *absorb*. A Seeper resting in a channel is feeding. A Seeper grinding a groove is feeding (the grinding releases fresh mineral surfaces, and the water flowing over those surfaces picks up new material, and the Seeper absorbs it). A Seeper sitting in an enclosure, doing nothing, is feeding. Every moment of a Seeper's life is a moment of feeding, because every moment is a moment of contact with warm, mineral-rich water.

This is why they are so slow. They have no need for speed. Their food is everywhere, continuously, without effort. They do not hunt. They do not graze. They do not compete for resources, because the resource is the medium they live in. A Seeper's entire energetic economy is a slow, gentle osmosis: minerals in, minerals deposited in the dorsal plate, the tiny remainder fueling the rippling lips and the light-sensitive patches and the chemosensory filaments and whatever slow, distributed, stone-deep process constitutes awareness.

They do not starve. They do not overeat. They exist in perfect equilibrium with their food supply, because their food supply is the basin itself, and the basin does not change.

## Reproduction

Seepers reproduce the way they do everything else: slowly, without drama, without apparent intention.

At intervals measured in years — or decades, or longer; the timekeeping of the Hollow makes precision impossible — a Seeper will begin to thicken. Its body, already wide and flat, will become slightly wider, slightly flatter, the ventral surface extending outward beyond the margin of the dorsal plate in a soft, fleshy skirt. This is not growth in the usual sense. It is *accumulation* — the same mineral-absorbing process that deposits layers on the dorsal plate, but directed inward, into the body mass, building a surplus.

When the surplus reaches a certain threshold — and the threshold is internal, invisible, a matter of chemistry and density that no outside observer could detect — the Seeper will divide.

Not mitosis. Not budding. Something more like *settling*. The Seeper's body will develop a faint groove along its midline, a line of slightly thinner tissue running from front to back. Over days, over weeks, this groove will deepen. The Seeper will continue to move, to grind, to feed, to rest, but the groove will deepen, and the two halves will begin to move slightly independently — the left lips rippling a half-beat out of sync with the right, the body flexing along the midline as it crosses an irregularity in the stone. And then, one warm, still, unremarkable moment, the two halves will simply... separate. No rupture. No event. One Seeper becomes two, and the two continue moving in whatever direction they were already going, which is usually the same direction, which means that for some time after division the two new Seepers travel side by side, nearly touching, their dorsal plates still bearing complementary halves of the mineral strata that belonged to their shared ancestor.

The new Seepers are fully formed. They do not grow. They do not develop. They are, from the moment of separation, complete — smaller than the parent, their dorsal plates thinner, their strata fewer, but otherwise identical. They will thicken over time, accumulate their own layers, and eventually divide in their turn. The population is stable. It has been stable for as long as the Hollow has been the Hollow. Seepers divide at a rate that exactly balances the rate at which they — the word "die" is not quite right — the rate at which they *cease*.

## Ceasing

Seepers do not die in the way that animals die. They do not sicken, weaken, and collapse. They do not age in the sense of accumulating damage. Their bodies are too simple, their metabolism too slow, their environment too stable for the usual mechanisms of mortality. What happens instead is a gradual *slowing*.

An old Seeper — an old, old Seeper, one that has been in the Hollow for a span of time that its dorsal strata record in hundreds of layers — will begin, imperceptibly, to slow. Its lips will ripple more slowly. Its periods of rest will lengthen. Its periods of activity will shorten. The ratio of stillness to movement will shift, year by year, decade by decade, until the Seeper is spending most of its time in the near-total quiescence that characterizes its resting state, and then all of its time, and then —

It stops.

Not dramatically. Not with a final gasp or a terminal spasm. The lips simply cease their rippling. The chemosensory filaments cease their tasting. The light-sensitive patches cease their registering. The Seeper becomes what it has always almost been: a stone. A stone shaped like a Seeper, colored like a Seeper, bearing on its back the layered record of a life lived in warm water and amber light, but no longer animate. No longer a creature. A *thing* — a small, dome-shaped, mineral-heavy thing sitting on the basin floor, indistinguishable from the small, dome-shaped, mineral-heavy structures that the Seepers build.

This is the final blurring of the boundary. The Seepers build structures from stone, and in the end, they *become* stone. Their bodies persist — they do not decay, because there is nothing in the Hollow to decay them, no bacteria, no fungi, no scavengers. A ceased Seeper simply remains, in place, forever, its dorsal plate slowly mineralizing further as the warm water deposits new layers over the old, until the distinction between the Seeper's body and the stone it sits on is purely academic.

The basin floor is full of them. Hundreds. Thousands, perhaps, accumulated over the entire history of the Hollow. They are among the structures — sometimes they *are* the structures, their bodies incorporated into channels and basins and enclosures by living Seepers who worked around them, over them, through the spaces they occupy, treating them as features of the landscape, which is exactly what they have become. A ring of stones around a junction might include, among its fitted pebbles, the body of a Seeper that ceased there a thousand years ago. The floor of an enclosure might be paved, in part, with the dorsal plates of the Seepers that once rested in it. The dead are not buried. They are *built in*. They are part of the architecture.

This is not memorial. The living Seepers do not honor the ceased ones, do not visit them, do not mark them in any way that differs from how they mark any other stone. But it is also not indifference. It is *continuity*. The ceased Seepers are still present. They are still part of the basin. They are still, in the most literal sense, *holding* the water — their bodies shape the flow, their domes redirect the seepage, their presence alters the micro-topography of the floor in ways that living Seepers respond to and build around. The dead continue to participate in the life of the Hollow, not as memories but as *structure*, as *landscape*, as the shaped stone that shapes the water that shapes the Seepers that shape the stone.

## What They Know

This is the question that cannot be answered and cannot be avoided.

The Seepers are not intelligent in any way that maps onto human categories of intelligence. They do not solve problems. They do not learn from experience in the sense of modifying behavior based on outcomes. They do not have memories that can be accessed and reflected upon. They do not have plans, goals, desires, or fears. They do not, as far as any observation can determine, have *minds*.

And yet.

They built the channels. They built the basins. They built the arches and the enclosures and the junction-stones. They made the patterns — the spirals, the circles, the dot-grids — that serve no function and express nothing but *presence*. They did this over spans of time that dwarf any individual life, through the coordinated (but not coordinated, not planned, not directed) activity of thousands of individuals, each one following the same simple set of responses: follow the water, deepen the groove, rest where it pools, grind where it flows.

The result is a landscape that looks like the work of intelligence. Not because it is complex — it is not, structurally, much more complex than a termite mound or a coral reef — but because it is *attentive*. Every structure in the Hollow is a response to a specific feature of the landscape. Every channel follows a real drainage path. Every basin sits at a real pooling point. Every arch bridges a real rise. The structures are not random accumulations. They are *accurate*. They describe the basin's hydrology with a fidelity that a human cartographer would envy. The entire network is a three-dimensional map of the water's behavior, built not from above, by a surveyor with instruments, but from *within*, by creatures who knew the water by touching it.

Is this knowledge? Is a Seeper's embodied, ventral-surface, chemosensory understanding of water flow *knowledge*? It does not feel like knowledge. It does not present itself as knowledge. The Seeper does not know that it knows. But the channels are there, and they are right, and they persist, and they are the product of a relationship between creature and landscape so intimate, so sustained, so utterly *absorbed*, that the word "knowledge" seems simultaneously too grand and too small.

The patterns — the spirals, the dot-grids — complicate this further. If the Seepers' channel-building can be explained as pure mechanical response to water flow, the patterns cannot. They depart from function. They depart from hydrology. They are *gratuitous* — unnecessary, unmotivated by any external pressure, arising from something internal that has no name. A Seeper grinding a spiral into stone beside its enclosure is doing something that serves no purpose except — except what? Except the expression of a shape that arose in the slow, mineral-rich, water-saturated process of being a Seeper in a particular place for a particular length of time.

Is this art? Is a pecked spiral art if the creature that made it has no concept of art, no audience, no intention, no aesthetic sense? Is it art if it was produced by the same grinding motion that deepens channels, just applied in a different pattern? Is it art if it is beautiful — and the spirals *are* beautiful, in the way that nautilus shells are beautiful, in the way that river deltas seen from above are beautiful, in the way that anything made by the interaction of simple rules and long time is beautiful?

The Seepers cannot answer these questions. The questions may not have answers. But the patterns are there, pecked into stone, persistent, unchanging, and they will not go away.

## The Long Now

A Seeper's experience of time — if a Seeper experiences time at all — must be something like the experience of the Hollow itself: an endless, undifferentiated present. No past, because the past is not recorded (the strata on the dorsal plate record *mineral deposition*, not events; a Seeper cannot look at its own back and remember the day the water ran slightly richer in iron). No future, because there is no anticipation, no planning, no projection. Only now: the warmth, the wetness, the mineral taste of the water, the texture of the stone beneath the lips, the faint, barely-perceptible gradient of flow that says *this way, downhill, toward the center*.

This is not a limitation. It is not a poverty of experience. It is a different *kind* of experience — one in which the boundary between self and environment is as thin as the film of water on the stone, in which the creature is so completely embedded in its world that the question of what it *knows* is inseparable from the question of what it *is*. A Seeper is the basin, in miniature: warm, wet, stone-backed, water-fed, slow, patient, present. It is the basin's own awareness of itself, concentrated into a body small enough to move, to grind, to shape, to build. The Seepers are the Hollow's way of touching itself.

And the Hollow, through them, has become something more than a depression in the ground where water collects. It has become a *work* — not a work of art, exactly, not a work of engineering, exactly, but a work of *attention*. A landscape that has been noticed, thoroughly and patiently and without any agenda beyond the noticing, by the creatures that live in it. Every channel is a sentence in a language with no grammar, no vocabulary, no speaker — only the slow, mineral-deep, water-carried utterance of stone responding to water responding to stone.

The Seepers do not know this. They do not need to. They are too busy — not busy, they are never busy, they are too *present* — following the water, deepening the grooves, resting in the enclosures, grinding the spirals, living their long, warm, amber-lit lives in a world that does not change, among the stone bodies of their ancestors, under a sky that has not moved in longer than anyone could say.

A drop falls. *Tick.* The silence absorbs it.

A Seeper's lips ripple against stone.

Nothing changes. Everything continues.
