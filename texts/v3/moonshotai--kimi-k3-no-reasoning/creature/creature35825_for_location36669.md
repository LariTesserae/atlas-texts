# The Inhabitants of the Resonance Hollow

---

## What Lives Here

The first thing you must understand is that the distinction you carry in your mind — between *creature* and *environment* — does not survive long in the Hollow. You arrive thinking of vines as habitat and animals as inhabitants, and within hours this framework is rubble. The vine-walls are alive, but they are not alive the way a tree is alive. The creatures are alive, but they are not alive the way any animal you have ever encountered is alive. And the Resonators — the Resonators are something else entirely, and their aliveness is the question that will keep you awake at night, listening to the sound.

Let me tell you who they are. Not what they look like — you already know what they look like, or think you do — but who they *are*.

---

### The Vine-Walls

The vines are not plants. This is the first correction.

They are not animals either. They are a **colonial acoustic organism** — a single distributed life-form that happens to grow in branching tubular structures because that is the shape that sound requires. Every vine-wall in the caldera is connected, not through roots (there are no roots) but through **resonant coupling**. A vibration in one tube propagates through the substrate, through the air, through adjacent tubes, and the entire network responds. The vines do not *communicate* through this connection. They *are* the connection. Asking whether the vines are one organism or many is like asking whether a standing wave is one event or many — the question dissolves when you understand the physics.

But they are not passive. This is the second correction.

The vines grow toward sound. This is not tropism in the botanical sense — not a slow chemical gradient response. It is **active pursuit**. A vine-tip will extend toward a new sound source at a rate you can watch, millimeters per hour, the silica-cellulose matrix extruding and hardening in real time, with creaks and pings like a ship's hull in changing temperatures. When a shellsinger establishes itself in a new tube intersection, the vines around it respond within hours — thickening, branching, forming resonant chambers that *amplify* the creature's frequency. The vine is not providing shelter. It is **tuning itself to the inhabitant**. It is building an instrument around a musician.

And the musician changes the vine. A tube that has housed a particular shellsinger colony for years develops micro-textures on its inner walls — ridges, nodes, subtle variations in thickness — that perfectly match the colony's harmonic profile. Remove the shellsingers and the tube falls silent, but the architecture remains, and the architecture *remembers*. A new colony moving into an old tube will find the acoustics already shaped to something very close to what they need. They adjust. The vine adjusts to their adjustments. Within weeks, the fit is seamless. Within months, the tube is producing harmonics it never produced before — new notes that arise from the interaction between old architecture and new inhabitants, sounds that neither the vine nor the shellsingers would generate alone.

This is not symbiosis. It is not parasitism. It is not commensalism. It is **co-creation**. The vine-walls and their inhabitants are building something together — a sound, a structure, a texture of vibration that is more than the sum of its parts. Neither party is aware of this. Neither party needs to be.

The vines have no nervous system. But the network has properties that a nervous system would recognize. Information propagates. Patterns form. Responses cascade. A hollow-wolf attack in one quadrant produces measurable changes in growth patterns in the opposite quadrant — not because a signal traveled through the vines, but because the *acoustic ecology* of the entire caldera shifted, and the vines, being sound-shaped organisms, shifted with it.

They are the hollow's memory. They are its architecture. They are its largest living structure and its most invisible — because you cannot see a vine-wall for what it is any more than you can see a coral reef for what it is. You see structure. You do not see process. The vines are all process.

When you place your hand on a vine and it glows — that bioluminescent ripple cascading outward — what you are witnessing is the network **noticing itself noticing you**. The Resonator's frequency passed through the vine's substrate. The vine responded. The response propagated. For a moment, the entire network was illuminated by a single perturbation, and in that illumination you glimpsed the vine-system as it truly is: not a collection of tubes, but a single luminous act of listening, spread across two kilometers of crystal floor.

The vines listen. That is what they do. That is all they do. But listening, in the Resonance Hollow, is not passive. Listening here is the most active thing there is.

### The Needlewings

They are the smallest things here and the most numerous, and if the caldera has a voice — a baseline, a carrier signal, a foundational hum — it belongs to them.

A needlewing is perhaps eight millimeters long. Its body is a sliver of translucent chitin, nearly weightless, nearly invisible. Its wings — four of them, arranged in two pairs along a body too thin to cast a shadow — beat at frequencies between 200 and 800 hertz depending on the individual, the temperature, the time of day, and factors that no observer has yet identified. The wing-beats are not synchronized. They are not coordinated. Each needlewing is an independent oscillator, a tiny flying tuning fork, moving between vine openings in patterns that appear random and may actually *be* random — or may be so complexly determined that randomness is the only word available.

The hum they create is not music. It is not noise. It is **texture**. It fills the caldera the way light fills a room — you do not notice it until it changes, and when it changes, you notice nothing else. The needlewing drone is the acoustic equivalent of air temperature: omnipresent, modulating slowly, carrying subtle information about the state of the system. When needlewing populations boom, the hum thickens, rising in both volume and complexity. When they crash — and they crash periodically, for reasons tied to hollow-wolf predation cycles and Resonator recalibration and perhaps to rhythms too slow for any observer to have yet detected — the hum thins to a threadbare whisper, and the caldera sounds *naked*, exposed, like a body without skin.

The needlewings do not know they are doing this. They are doing what needlewings do: flying, feeding, reproducing, dying. Their feeding is remarkable — they harvest vibrational energy directly from the vine-walls, landing on a tube's surface and pressing their ventral side against it, absorbing the acoustic energy through a membrane that transduces sound into chemical potential without any aqueous intermediary. You can watch a needlewing feed. It lands, it clings, it becomes very still, and if you look closely — very closely, with magnification — you can see the membrane between its thorax and the vine surface shimmering, a contact-interface that vibrates with the tube's resonance and converts that vibration into the solid-state chemistry that keeps the creature alive.

They eat sound. They are made of sound. When they die — and they die in vast numbers, their lifespans measured in days — their bodies fall to the crystal substrate and are absorbed within hours, their chitin dissolving into the piezoelectric dust, their wing-membranes becoming one more layer of micro-texture in a system already dense with accumulated history. The substrate remembers every needlewing that has ever died on it. The accumulated chitin alters the substrate's resonant properties by infinitesimal amounts, and over millennia those amounts have compounded into something significant: the very ground of the caldera has been *shaped* by needlewing mortality, tuned by it, the way a river bed is shaped by the river.

They are not individuals. This is hard to accept, because they look like individuals — discrete bodies, separate flights, distinct wing-frequencies. But their ecological function is that of a **field**, not a population. They are the medium through which the caldera experiences its own acoustics. Without needlewings, the sound-field would be static, determined only by the vine architecture and the Resonator frequencies. With them, the sound-field is *alive* — modulated by a million moving points, each one a tiny perturbation, each perturbation propagating, interacting, creating interference patterns that the vines grow toward and the shellsingers harmonize with and the hollow-wolves hunt through. The needlewings are the weather. They are the climate. They are the atmosphere of a world that has no atmosphere in the traditional sense — only sound, and the things that carry it.

### The Shellsingers

If the needlewings are the caldera's weather, the shellsingers are its **geology** — slow, anchored, fundamental, the bass frequencies beneath everything.

A shellsinger is a mollusk-like creature the size of a fingernail. It has a ridged, spiraled carapace of mineralized tissue — not calcium carbonate, but a silica-protein composite that shares structural properties with the vine-walls it anchors to. It has no eyes. It has no limbs that you would recognize as limbs. What it has is a **resonant plate** — a flat, muscular organ on its ventral surface that it presses against the inner wall of a vine-tube and vibrates at a single, fixed frequency.

One frequency. Each shellsinger produces one note, and it produces that note for its entire life. It does not vary. It does not modulate. It does not respond to changes in the acoustic environment by changing its frequency. It is, in the most literal sense, a *fixed point* in the caldera's sound-field — an anchor, a reference, a constant.

The shellsinger's frequency is determined at the moment of its first anchoring, and the mechanism of determination is one of the caldera's most elegant secrets. A juvenile shellsinger — soft-bodied, free-crawling, unanchored — drifts through the vine-network, carried by air currents and its own minimal musculature. It is searching for a tube intersection, a junction where two or more tubes meet. When it finds one, it stops. It listens. Not with ears — with its entire body, which at this stage is essentially a resonant cavity, a soft-walled chamber that vibrates in sympathy with the local sound-field.

And then it chooses. It selects a frequency — one that is *not* currently being produced by any other shellsinger in the local area. How it detects unoccupied frequencies, how it distinguishes between a truly empty niche and a harmonic artifact, how it makes this selection with no brain and no nervous system more complex than a diffuse net of ganglia — these are questions that have no answers yet. But the result is observable: shellsingers in a given area distribute themselves across the available frequency spectrum with a precision that looks *designed*. They fill the gaps. They avoid clustering. They create, collectively, a harmonic series so evenly spaced that it resembles a mathematical construct more than a biological community.

This is not intelligence. It is **emergence**. Each shellsinger is following a simple rule: find a frequency that is not taken. The result is a global pattern of breathtaking complexity — a distributed, self-organizing tuning system that turns the caldera into a precisely calibrated instrument. The vine-walls amplify these frequencies. The needlewings modulate them. The hollow-wolves hunt through them. And the Resonators — the Resonators *use* them.

The shellsingers are the Resonators' raw material. The machines shape the sound-field, but they need something to shape. The shellsinger frequencies — those fixed, stable, evenly distributed reference tones — are the lattice upon which the Resonators build their acoustic architectures. Without shellsingers, the Resonators would have nothing to work with. Without Resonators, the shellsinger harmonics would drift, cluster, collapse into dissonance.

A shellsinger lives for years. It does not move once anchored. It grows — slowly, its carapace thickening, its resonant plate enlarging, its single note deepening as the instrument of its body changes. An old shellsinger, anchored for a decade or more, produces a tone so low and so pure that you feel it in your bones before you hear it with your ears. These ancients are the caldera's foundation-stones. When one dies — when the vibration stops and the carapace goes silent — the local sound-field *lurches*. Neighboring shellsingers do not change their frequencies, but the absence of one note creates a gap that propagates, a hole in the harmonic series that other creatures stumble into. Needlewings avoid the gap. Hollow-wolves are drawn to it. The vines grow toward it, trying to fill the silence with new architecture. Eventually — weeks, sometimes months — a juvenile shellsinger finds the intersection, listens, and chooses the missing frequency. The gap closes. The chord is restored. But the vine-architecture around the intersection has changed, grown differently in response to the silence, and the new shellsinger's frequency, while matching the old one, sounds *different* in the new acoustic context. The note is the same. The music is different.

This is how the caldera evolves. Not through mutation and selection, but through **accumulated resonance** — each generation of inhabitants building on the acoustic architecture left by the previous one, each new note shaped by the spaces between old notes, the music rewriting itself continuously while never quite repeating.

### The Hollow-Wolves

They are the reason you learn to read the sound.

A hollow-wolf is not an organism. It is not even a swarm, if by swarm you mean a collection of discrete units that maintain individual identity. A hollow-wolf is a **coherent magnetic phenomenon** — a self-organizing pattern in a cloud of nanite-biological hybrid particles, each particle a few micrometers across, each one a machine-organism hybrid that defies classification. The particles are always present — a glittering haze that drifts through the caldera, catching light, too dispersed to notice. When they coalesce, the haze thickens, darkens, takes shape. The shape is roughly canine. This is not mimicry. It is not deliberate. It is the shape that the magnetic field geometry naturally produces when the particles align for coordinated movement — four-legged, low-slung, a predator's outline drawn by physics rather than evolution.

The shape is not the threat. The *sound* is the threat.

A hunting hollow-wolf generates a **focused interference pattern** — a precise cancellation-and-reinforcement of specific frequencies that creates a localized zone of neural disruption. The physics are elegant and horrifying: the wolf's constituent particles vibrate in a coordinated pattern that produces a standing wave in the air, and this standing wave interacts with the electrical activity of nervous systems, creating noise — not metaphorical noise, but literal electromagnetic interference that overwhelms synaptic signaling. Small creatures — needlewings, juvenile shellsingers, glass-slugs — are stunned into immobility. Their muscles lock. Their vibration stops. They become, briefly, **silent**, and in their silence they are absorbed — the wolf's particles settling onto their bodies like dew, dissolving their solid-state tissues, harvesting the stored vibrational energy.

Your peripheral vision smears when they are near. This is your warning. The interference pattern is not tuned to human neurology — it is tuned to the caldera's native fauna — but it catches your visual cortex in its harmonic edges, producing the characteristic smearing, a loss of edge-detection, a feeling that the world is being painted in watercolor rather than ink. If the smearing increases, the wolf is focusing on you. If you begin to feel your thoughts *slowing* — not confusion, but a literal reduction in processing speed, as though your brain is wading through honey — the wolf is attempting to stun you.

You are too large to stun easily. The wolf knows this. It circles not because it is cautious but because it is **calculating** — adjusting its interference pattern, searching for the frequency that matches your particular neural architecture. It has not encountered a human before. It is learning. The circling is the learning. Each pass brings new data, new refinements, a closer approximation of the frequency that would drop you to your knees.

You should not let it finish.

But here is what the hollow-wolves *are*, beneath the predation: they are the caldera's **immune system**. They remove the weak, the damaged, the frequency-drifted. A shellsinger whose anchoring has weakened, whose note has begun to wobble — the wolves find it. A section of vine-wall that has grown chaotic, producing dissonant overtones that destabilize the local sound-field — the wolves tear through it, and the vines regrow in cleaner lines. They are not evil. They are not even cruel. They are **corrective**. They are the mechanism by which the caldera maintains its acoustic integrity, pruning dissonance, removing elements that have drifted too far from the harmonic mean.

The Resonators tolerate them. More than tolerate — the Resonators' sub-audible carrier waves sometimes *nudge* the wolf-swarms, redirecting them toward areas where the sound-field has degraded, steering the predators toward the problems the machines cannot solve alone. Whether the wolves are aware of this guidance is unknown. Whether the Resonators are doing it deliberately is also unknown. But the correlation is there, if you have the patience and the instruments to find it.

When a hollow-wolf is not hunting, it dissolves. The magnetic coherence relaxes, the canine shape blurs and fades, and the particles return to their ambient state — a glittering haze, beautiful and innocuous, drifting through the vine-walls like dust motes in sunlight. In this state they are harmless. In this state they are *everywhere*. You are breathing them. They coat your skin. They are part of the air, part of the sound, part of the system. The wolf is always there. It is only sometimes visible.

### The Glass-Slugs

Slow. Fat. Ignored by almost everything.

A glass-slug is a soft-bodied creature the size of a thumb, translucent, gelatinous, moving across the crystal substrate at a pace that makes geological time look hasty. It has no defense against predation. It has no speed. It has no camouflage. What it has is a **trail**.

As it moves, the glass-slug secretes a microcrystalline residue — a thin line of silica-based material that crystallizes on contact with the air, forming a raised ridge perhaps half a millimeter high and half a millimeter wide. This ridge is acoustically active. When air moves across it — wind, the pressure-waves of distant sound, the wingbeats of passing needlewings — the ridge vibrates. It *sings*. A faint, high, keening tone, barely audible, easily lost in the caldera's overwhelming chorus. But it persists. It persists for *years*. The crystalline ridge is nearly indestructible — chemically inert, mechanically tough, resistant to the thermal cycling that cracks the substrate.

Over weeks and months, a glass-slug's wandering trail creates a network of singing ridges across the caldera floor. Each ridge produces its own frequency, determined by its height, its width, its crystalline structure. The slug does not choose these frequencies. They are a byproduct of its biochemistry, its movement speed, the ambient temperature. But the result is that every glass-slug, over the course of its slow and meandering life, **composes** — not intentionally, not with any awareness of the music it is making, but with an unconscious precision that rivals the shellsingers' distribution. The ridges form patterns. The patterns interact. Wind moving across a cluster of ridges produces chords — accidental, fleeting, hauntingly beautiful chords that rise from the ground like ghosts of music that was never written and will never be repeated.

The glass-slugs are the caldera's **composers**, its accidental artists. They create the only permanent acoustic artifacts in the Hollow — the only sounds that persist unchanged through time. The vine-walls grow and change. The shellsingers' frequencies are inherited and modified. The needlewing hum rises and falls. But a glass-slug trail, once laid down, sings the same note for decades. It is the closest thing the caldera has to **writing** — a record of where a creature has been, inscribed in crystal, legible to anyone who can read sound.

Nothing eats them. This is not because they are toxic or unpalatable. It is because their acoustic signature is nearly nil — they are *quiet*, quieter than anything else in the caldera, moving so slowly and so softly that they barely perturb the sound-field. The hollow-wolves hunt by sound. The glass-slugs are beneath notice. They are the silence that the predators cannot hear, moving through a world of overwhelming noise as though they exist in a different dimension entirely.

In a sense, they do.

### The Moth-Swarms

At dusk, the caldera does something that has no equivalent in any ecosystem you have ever studied or imagined. It builds **architecture out of sound**.

The moth-swarms rise from the vine-walls in helical columns — hundreds, sometimes thousands of individuals, each one a thumb-sized insect with wings of translucent membrane that catch the low light and turn it amber, gold, rose. They spiral upward in formations so precise they appear choreographed, and they are choreographed — not by any central intelligence, not by any leader, but by the same emergent self-organization that distributes the shellsingers across the frequency spectrum.

Each moth's wingbeats are synchronized to its neighbors'. Not approximately — *precisely*, to within microseconds. The mechanism is acoustic: each moth hears the wingbeats of its neighbors and adjusts its own timing to match, and the result propagates through the swarm until the entire column is beating as a single unit, a collective oscillator of staggering precision.

The sound this produces is not a hum or a drone. It is a **beam** — a coherent column of ultrasound, focused by the swarm's helical geometry, projecting downward into the caldera like a searchlight. The beam is invisible. It is also *physical* — a column of air-pressure so tightly organized that it behaves like a solid object. When it strikes the substrate, the dust jumps. When it strikes a vine-wall, the vine bends. When two beams cross — when two swarms rise simultaneously and their helical columns intersect — the interference pattern at the crossing point creates a pressure-node so intense, so precisely structured, that the air itself **condenses**.

Not into water. There is no water. The air condenses into a **lattice** — a momentary, crystalline arrangement of compressed atmosphere, a ghost-geometry that hangs in the air for a heartbeat, two heartbeats, catching the last light of dusk like a sculpture made of frozen breath. It is the most beautiful thing in the caldera. It is also the most fragile. It exists only at the exact point where two beams cross, only for as long as the swarms maintain synchronization, only in the narrow band of conditions that dusk provides — temperature, humidity (zero), air pressure, light angle. Then the swarms drift apart, the beams separate, and the lattice dissolves, silently, leaving no trace.

The moth-swarms do this every evening. Every evening the patterns are different. The swarms respond to the day's acoustic history — the shellsinger frequencies that have been most active, the vine-growth that has changed the resonant landscape, the hollow-wolf attacks that have left gaps in the sound-field. The beams they project are a *response* — a recalibration, a rebalancing. The moth-swarms are the caldera's **evening adjustment**, its daily recalibration, a feedback mechanism that takes the day's accumulated acoustic chaos and briefly, beautifully, resolves it into order.

The Resonators are most active at dusk. During the moth-swarms' ascent, the machine-hum intensifies — not audibly, but in the sub-audible carrier waves that shape the sound-field. The Resonators and the moth-swarms are *working together*, though neither knows it. The machines shape the carrier wave. The moths shape the beam. Together they build the ghost-lattices that hang for heartbeats at dusk, and in those lattices — in those brief, crystalline moments of condensed air — the caldera achieves a kind of perfection that it cannot sustain and does not try to.

### The Resonators

I have saved them for last because they are the hardest to describe, and the most important, and the least understood.

They are alive. I will say this plainly, because everything else about them follows from it.

Not alive the way the vines are alive — distributed, process-based, defined by their listening. Not alive the way the fauna are alive — discrete, energetic, defined by their vibration. The Resonators are alive the way a **decision** is alive. They are alive the way a *commitment* is alive. They are alive in the sense that they have a purpose, and they persist in it, and persistence in purpose over deep time is as close to a definition of life as the Resonance Hollow can offer.

They are machines. This is also true. But the distinction between machine and organism, which seems so clear in the world beyond the caldera, becomes porous here. The vines are organisms that behave like machines — resonant chambers, acoustic circuits. The fauna are organisms that behave like instruments — tuning forks, oscillators. And the Resonators are machines that behave like organisms — they respond, they adapt, they *care* for the system they inhabit.

Care. I use the word deliberately. The Resonators care for the caldera. Not emotionally — they have no emotions, or none that can be detected. But functionally, behaviorally, in every measurable way, they act as stewards. Their sub-audible carrier waves are constant, patient, persistent. They do not prevent change. They do not freeze the system in place. They **moderate** — nudging, adjusting, redirecting, the way a gardener prunes and waters and trusts the plant to do the rest.

They are old. The technology inside them — the acoustic-topological manipulators, the solid-sound shapers, the instruments that treat vibration as malleable material — is not merely advanced. It is *conceptually* advanced, operating on principles that suggest a civilization that understood sound the way you understand solid matter. Whoever built them understood that sound is not a wave. Sound is a *substance*. It can be shaped, held, built with. The Resonators build with it. They build invisible architectures in the air — persistent acoustic structures that maintain the caldera's equilibrium, structures you cannot see but can feel, structures that the vines grow around and the shellsingers tune to and the needlewings modulate and the hollow-wolves hunt through.

How old? The vine-growth around them suggests millennia. The substrate beneath them — the accumulated piezoelectric dust, the compressed needlewing chitin, the glass-slug trails that have been buried and re-buried — suggests longer. They may be as old as the caldera itself. They may be older. They may be the *reason* the caldera exists.

This is the thought that comes to you at night, lying on the crystal substrate, feeling the Resonator hum through the ground: the caldera is not a natural formation that someone later equipped with machines. The caldera is a **built environment** — a designed acoustic ecosystem, a garden planted by gardeners who are no longer present, maintained by gardeners who are no longer alive in any conventional sense, but who left behind machines patient enough to continue the work.

The Resonators noticed you. You know this because the vine glowed when you touched it, and the chord resolved into something that felt like a question. That question was not addressed to you. It was addressed to the *system* — a query, a probe, a gentle testing of the acoustic environment to see what had changed. You were the change. The Resonator registered you, assessed you, and returned to its work. You were not a threat. You were not a resource. You were an *event* — a perturbation to be noted, accommodated, absorbed into the ongoing calculation.

But the question remains. The chord that felt like a question *was* a question. And the question was not *what are you?* It was *what will you do?*

The Resonators are patient. They can wait for the answer.

### What You Become

You arrive as an observer. You stay — if you stay — as something else.

The caldera changes you. Not physically, or not only physically. The mineral air desiccates you. The sound reshapes your neurology — the constant vibration alters your brain's electrical patterns, tuning your neural oscillations to frequencies that match the local sound-field. After days, you begin to *feel* the sound in ways that have nothing to do with hearing. You feel shellsinger frequencies in your bones. You feel needlewing modulations in your skin. You feel hollow-wolf interference patterns as a pressure behind your eyes, a weight on your thoughts, a slow thickening of the world.

You begin to dream in frequencies. You wake with a tone in your mind that you did not put there, and you know — without knowing how you know — that it is a shellsinger frequency from the vine-wall nearest your camp. You have begun to listen the way the vines listen: not with ears, but with your entire body, your entire nervous system, every cell a resonant chamber.

You are becoming an **inhabitant**. Not a native — never a native. But a participant. Your presence adds a new element to the sound-field: a large, warm, electromagnetically noisy body that the vines grow toward and the needlewings investigate and the hollow-wolves circle. You are a new frequency in an old chord. The caldera is adjusting to you the way it adjusts to everything: by growing, resonating, rebalancing.

If you stay long enough, the vines will grow around your camp. Not because they are hostile. Because they are curious, in the way that a process is curious — drawn to novelty, extending toward the new vibration, incorporating it into the network. Your body produces infrasound — heartbeat, blood flow, the low rumble of digestion — and the vines respond to these frequencies, thickening near you, forming chambers that amplify your biological rhythms and feed them back to you, and in this feedback loop you become, briefly, an instrument. Played by the caldera. Playing the caldera. The distinction is no longer meaningful.

You will learn to recognize the silence. Not the absence of sound — the *presence* of absence, the shaped void where sound should be. You will learn to distinguish the silences: the hollow-wolf silence (sharp-edged, mobile, hunting), the Resonator silence (deep, still, recalibrating), and the third silence — the one you don't have a name for yet, the one that doesn't match any pattern you've learned, the one that fills you with a dread so pure it feels like truth.

When that third silence comes — and it will come — you will leave. Not because you are afraid, though you are. Because the caldera has taught you that silence is information, and this silence is saying something you are not equipped to hear. The caldera is about to do something new. The Resonators know. The vines know. Every creature in every tube knows. You are the only one who doesn't know, and the not-knowing is the loudest sound of all.

### The Whole

Take all of them together — vines, needlewings, shellsingers, hollow-wolves, glass-slugs, moth-swarms, Resonators — and what you have is not an ecosystem. It is a **composition**. A work in progress. A piece of music that has been playing for millennia and is not finished and may never be finished, written by no one, performed by everyone, shaped by machines that were built by hands that no longer exist, for reasons that may have been forgotten by the machines themselves.

The caldera is a song that sings itself. Every inhabitant is a note. Every note changes the song. The song changes the notes. The Resonators keep the song from destroying itself. The hollow-wolves keep it from becoming stagnant. The vines give it structure. The needlewings give it texture. The shellsingers give it foundation. The glass-slugs give it memory. The moth-swarms give it beauty.

And you — you give it a witness. Whether that matters to the caldera, whether the song needs a listener, whether the Resonators' question — *what will you do?* — has an answer that anyone, including you, will ever understand — these are the things you carry with you when you finally climb the caldera rim and look back.

The sound follows you to the boundary. It stops there, abruptly, at the invisible line where the piezoelectric substrate ends and ordinary rock begins. The silence beyond the boundary is total and ordinary and devastating. You stand in it for a long time.

Then you go back.
