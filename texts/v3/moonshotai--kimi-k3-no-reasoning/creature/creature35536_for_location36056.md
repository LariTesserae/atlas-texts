They are a vertical people, and their anatomy confesses it.

The Kael do not possess the broad, flat feet of valley-dwellers. Their toes have lengthened over the centuries, the big toe migrating backward until it forms a crude, opposable hook—the "climbing thumb," they call it—while their legs have become disproportionately powerful, the calves thick as a man's forearm. Their hands are wider than they should be, fingers flattened and spatulate with dense, calloused pads that grip the calcified edges of the Lattice as if the stone were warm bread. When they sleep, they curl into tight, dense knots, conserving the precious heat of the radioactive stones, and their spines compress into an S-curve that allows them to wedge themselves into fissures too narrow for wolves or bears.

They are small—few stand taller than five feet—but dense, their bones reinforced with minerals leached from the water of the Weep, heavy in the hand like bags of wet sand. Their skin has taken on the color of the basalt they inhabit: not the black of soot, but the gray-brown of wet stone, translucent at the temples where blue veins pulse with slow, deliberate blood. They have developed a nictitating membrane across their eyes, a third eyelid of milky keratin that slides horizontally to protect against the diamond-hard glare of the upper spires; when closed, it gives them the appearance of statues, blind and contemplative.

The Weep enters them in more ways than thirst. They drink it, of course—filtering it through woven fungal mats that remove the worst of the metallic salts—but it seeps into their bones, their hair, their minds. Their hair, which they never cut, grows in matted, rope-like cords that they treat with copper salts to prevent freezing; these cords clink softly when they move, like wind chimes made of wire. Their teeth wear down early, grinding the gritty fungal pastes, so that by thirty, the Kael speak with a slight lisp, their tongues pressing against smooth, stubby molars.

Society organizes around the Clefts and the Heat Stones. A settlement might consist of a single extended family—never more than fifteen—huddled in a horizontal scar that runs three meters deep into the mountain's heart. At the back of the cleft, where the geothermal warmth seeps through the stone, they place the Heat Stone: a lump of amber-glowing ore the size of a human skull, encased in a cage of copper wire to prevent accidental burns. It radiates a sickly, persistent warmth that the Kael call "the False Sun," and around it they arrange their lives.

The Elder of each Cleft is not the oldest, but the one who has survived the most climbs. They bear the "Lattice Scars"—deliberate, ritualized wounds made by pressing the forearm against growing fungal plates until the calcified hyphae penetrate the skin, leaving raised, hexagonal patterns that mirror the mountain's architecture. These scars are maps and calendars; the Elders can read the rate of the Lattice's growth by the tightness of their own skin, predicting which bridges will collapse and which clefts will be sealed by the encroaching plates.

They speak a language of clicks and murmurs, evolved to be heard over the wind's whisper without carrying far enough to dislodge the ice above. It is a language of orientation: there are no words for "left" or "right," only "sun-side," "weep-side," "up-throat," and "down-belly." They name their children after holds—the specific shapes of stone that saved a parent during a climb: *Anchor*, *Cradle*, *Knife-edge*, *Thumb-rest*. To be named *Void* is a curse; it means the child was born during a fall, saved only by the rope.

Their economy is one of salvage and careful harvest. They tend the Lattice as a farmer tends a field, though the relationship is adversarial. They scrape the edible hyphae—the soft, gray underlayer beneath the calcified plates—into pastes that taste of iron and mushrooms, fermenting them in bladders made from the stomachs of the rock-voles that occasionally venture too high. They hunt the chitin-bats that nest in the highest crevices, creatures with membranous wings that snap like whips in the thin air, whose meat is black and sulfurous. They trade between Clefts using copper tokens hammered into the shapes of climbing holds, each token representing a specific debt of labor: a day spent scraping spores, a bridge repaired, a body recovered from the ice.

But it is the death-rites that define them. When a Kael dies—and they die often, in falls, in cold-snaps, in the slow poisoning of bad water—they do not bury. There is no soil. Instead, the body is carried to the Weep, to the specific fissure where the water emerges warmest, and wedged upright into the stone. Over months, the calcified hyphae grow over the corpse, coating it in a second skin of gray ceramic, until the body becomes a node in the Lattice itself, a strengthening knot in the mountain's fungal skeleton. The dead become architecture. They become holds for the living.

The children play at climbing before they can speak, dangling from their mothers' backs on leather harnesses as the women scrape the fungal plates. They learn to fear the "shimmer"—the iridescent sheen that precedes a spore-release from the toxic strains of Lattice—and to recognize the specific *click* of contracting plates that signals a collapse. They learn to read the ice: blue ice is safe, white ice is rotten, black ice is death.

At night, when the violet twilight deepens and the mountain's whisper turns to that dry, papery clicking, the Kael gather in the deepest part of the Cleft, their bodies pressed together in a mound that resembles nothing so much as a cluster of eggs. The Heat Stone glows amber in their midst, casting shadows that are too long, too thin. They do not dream of green fields or forests. They dream of handholds. They dream of the Weep flowing upward, defying gravity. They dream of the day the Lattice finally seals the Cleft entirely, and they become the mountain, calcified and eternal, their fingers forever curled around a copper token, their eyes forever looking up toward the light that strikes the summit while they remain in the cold, wet dark.

They are not happy, but they are persistent. They are the crust on the wound of the world, the gray fur on the basalt fist, and they will hold on until the stone itself forgets how to be hard.
