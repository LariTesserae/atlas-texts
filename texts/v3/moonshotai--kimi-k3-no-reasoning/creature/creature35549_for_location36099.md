# Those Who Live in Thren

## The Threnware

They are small people. Not remarkably so — not a separate race, nothing fantastical — but generations of dense living and a diet drawn from pool and garden have produced a population that tends toward compact frames, low centres of gravity, quick hands. The average adult stands perhaps five foot four. They are strong in the particular way of people who work with fibre and water: forearms and shoulders developed, fingers thick-knuckled and deft, backs that bend easily and often. Their skin is weathered but not sun-darkened — the diffuse light of Thren doesn't tan so much as *wear*, and the Threnware carry the pale, slightly weathered look of people who live outdoors in a place where the sun rarely reaches them directly. Their skin is perpetually damp. Everyone's is. You stop noticing.

Their hair is dark, almost universally so, and most wear it long and tied back with woven halg fibre, because loose hair in Thren catches on everything — tendrils, thorns, the hooked seeds of a half-dozen plants that use passing mammals as dispersal vectors. Children's hair is shaved close for the first several years of life, a practical measure against the small biting insects that inhabit the root mat and the canopy. Adults sometimes weave small ornaments into their hair — polished stones, beads of hollowed bone, tiny carvings of frogs or fish — but nothing metal. There is no metal in Thren, and the Threnware do not miss it. When traders occasionally come over the rim from the dry valleys beyond, carrying copper ornaments and iron tools, the Threnware examine these things with polite curiosity and do not trade for them. Metal is cold. Metal does not grow. Metal, in Thren's humid, plant-saturated atmosphere, would rust within a season.

They wear little. The climate does not demand heavy clothing, and heavy clothing in Thren would be a burden — always wet, always heavy, always in the way. Most adults wear a simple wrap of woven fibre around the waist, sometimes a second piece across the shoulders in cooler months. The weave is fine and surprisingly soft, made from the inner bark fibre of the highland willow, processed through a technique of soaking and beating that the Threnware have refined over generations. The cloth is naturally water-resistant, not because of any treatment but because of the tightness of the weave and the slight oiliness of the fibre. It dries quickly. It lasts. When it wears out, it is composted into the root mat, where it decomposes within a season and feeds the very plants from which the next batch of fibre will be harvested.

Children often wear nothing at all. This is practical: children in Thren are in and out of water constantly, and clothing would be perpetually soaked. They run through the settlement barefoot, their feet hardened to the texture of the root mat, their bodies lean and quick, their shrieks adding to the ambient soundscape. They are astonishingly at home in the environment. A Threnware child of five can navigate the settlement in thick mist by sound alone. A child of seven can identify thirty species of plant by touch. A child of ten can weave a functional fish trap. These are not remarkable achievements in Thren. They are the baseline of education.

## The Texture of a Life

A Threnware life is shaped, above all, by the material reality of the settlement: the growth, the water, the sound, the density. These things are not background conditions. They are the substance of daily existence, the medium through which every hour is lived.

The day begins before light. Not with an alarm — there are no clocks in Thren, no bells, no mechanical timekeeping — but with the body. The Threnware sleep lightly, on thin mats of woven rush laid directly on the warm root-mat floor, and the transition from sleep to waking is gradual, synced to the shifting acoustic landscape. The first sign of morning is a change in the frog chorus — a thinning, a modulation, as the night species fall silent and the day species have not yet begun. This interval, this brief pocket of relative quiet, is the closest thing Thren has to silence, and the Threnware wake into it as naturally as breathing.

The first tasks of the day are domestic and immediate. Water is drawn from the nearest channel — every dwelling in Thren is within a few paces of flowing water, because the settlement's circulatory system is designed that way. Food is prepared: a porridge of ground seeds and dried fish, eaten from gourd bowls, sometimes supplemented with fruit or edible shoots. The preparation is quick and communal. Several families typically share a cooking fire — a small, carefully managed blaze in a clay-lined pit, fed with dried halg prunings and deadfall from the canopy above. The smoke is channelled upward through a gap in the woven roof, and the Threnware sit close to it, grateful for the warmth in the cool morning air.

Then the work begins.

## The Work

Work in Thren is not a separate category of activity, not something you go to and come home from. It is woven into the fabric of the day as thoroughly as the halg is woven into the fabric of the buildings. The Threnware do not have jobs. They have *responsibilities* — ongoing, shifting, seasonal obligations to the settlement and to each other that are understood rather than assigned, that emerge from skill and inclination and need rather than from any formal system of labour allocation.

The most fundamental responsibility is maintenance. This is not glamorous work, and it is never finished. The halg grows. Always. Every wall, every roof, every floor platform is alive and expanding, and if the growth is not managed, the settlement will consume itself — not dramatically, not in a single catastrophic event, but gradually, inevitably, the way a forest reclaims a clearing. So every day, in every part of the settlement, people are pruning. They work in pairs or small groups, moving through the settlement with stone blades and hardwood pruning hooks, trimming back tendrils, thinning walls that have grown too dense, opening windows that have begun to close over, redirecting growth away from pathways and doorways.

The pruning is skilled work, more skilled than it appears. A careless cut can damage a load-bearing member and compromise an entire structure. A well-placed cut can redirect growth in a way that strengthens a wall or opens a new space. The best pruners — and the Threnware recognize and respect this skill — are those who can read the growth patterns of the halg, who can look at a wall and see not just what it is but what it is *becoming*, and who can intervene at the right moment and in the right way to guide that becoming in a useful direction. These people are called *halgspeakers*, though the term is informal, more a description than a title, and it carries no authority — only respect.

The water system requires its own maintenance. The channels must be kept clear — roots removed, sediment redistributed, banks reinforced where the rushes have thinned. This is wet, muddy, often cold work, done by wading through the channels with bare hands and simple tools. It is also essential. A blocked channel can flood a dwelling, starve a fish pool, or disrupt the irrigation that keeps the halg walls hydrated. The Threnware who tend the channels — the *waterwalkers*, as they are sometimes called — know the system intimately. They can tell by the sound of a channel whether it is flowing correctly. They can predict, from the colour of the water and the behaviour of the fish, when a blockage is forming upstream. They are the settlement's circulatory specialists, and their work is unglamorous and vital.

Food production occupies a significant portion of every day. The fish pools must be tended — fed with aquatic vegetation and insect larvae, harvested with traps and nets, monitored for signs of disease or overpopulation. The garden plots — small, intensively cultivated patches of edible plants grown in raised beds above the water table — must be weeded, watered, harvested, replanted. The Threnware grow a variety of crops: starchy root vegetables, leafy greens, a protein-rich legume that climbs the halg walls and produces pods in enormous quantities, several species of fruit-bearing shrubs and vines. The gardens are not separate from the settlement; they are integrated into it, growing on rooftops, along walls, in the spaces between buildings, anywhere there is light and a foothold for roots.

Gathering supplements cultivation. The surrounding moorland and the slopes of the bowl provide wild foods — berries, nuts, edible fungi, the tender shoots of certain ferns. The Threnware range widely in their foraging, sometimes spending entire days outside the bowl, returning with woven baskets full of food and with information — the condition of the trails, the movement of animal herds, the state of the streams that feed the bowl's water system.

There is also the work of making. The Threnware produce everything they use: baskets, nets, traps, containers, tools, clothing, mats, screens. The materials are all local — plant fibre, bone, stone, clay, gourd. The skills are passed down through families and through informal apprenticeship, and they are held in common. There is no concept of proprietary knowledge in Thren. If you know how to make a better fish trap, you show others. If you discover a new use for a plant, you share it. The settlement's survival depends on the collective knowledge of its inhabitants, and hoarding that knowledge would be understood — correctly — as a threat to the community.

## Social Architecture

The Threnware have no chiefs, no elders' council, no formal government of any kind. This is not because they are primitive or disorganised. It is because the scale and nature of their settlement makes formal hierarchy unnecessary and, arguably, impossible.

Four hundred people. That is the population of Thren, more or less, fluctuating with births and deaths and the occasional arrival or departure. Four hundred people living in a space four hundred paces across, in a state of continuous interaction, where privacy is limited and every action has visible consequences. In such an environment, hierarchy is redundant. Everyone knows everyone. Not in the vague sense of a small town where people recognise faces, but in the deep, specific sense of people who have worked together, eaten together, slept within earshot of each other, resolved conflicts and celebrated births and mourned deaths together, for their entire lives.

Decisions are made by conversation. This is not a euphemism for chaos or indecision. The Threnware are practical people, and their conversations about communal matters — where to redirect a channel, whether to abandon a structure that has grown unmanageable, how to respond to a fish die-off — are focused, informed, and efficient. The people with the most relevant knowledge speak first and are listened to most carefully. The waterwalkers speak about water. The halgspeakers speak about growth. The foragers speak about the land beyond the bowl. Others contribute as their experience allows. Consensus is not formally sought but naturally emerges, because the decisions are usually practical rather than ideological, and the correct course of action is usually evident to anyone who understands the situation.

When consensus does not emerge — when there is genuine disagreement about a course of action — the Threnware have a custom that serves the function of governance without its structure. They call it *the weaving*. The parties to the disagreement gather, along with anyone else who wishes to participate, in one of the communal structures — a large, multi-chambered building at the settlement's centre that has grown over generations into a sprawling, organic complex of interconnected rooms. They sit in a circle on the warm root-mat floor. They speak in turn, each person holding a length of woven halg fibre while they talk — a physical token that gives them the floor, that prevents interruption. The fibre is passed from speaker to speaker. The conversation continues until a resolution emerges, or until it becomes clear that no resolution is possible, in which case the matter is set aside and revisited later.

The weaving is not a debate. There are no winners and losers. The goal is not to prevail but to *understand* — to arrive at a shared picture of the situation that is complete enough to support a collective decision. The Threnware are patient with this process. They understand that complex problems require complex conversations, and that the time invested in reaching genuine understanding is time saved in avoiding the consequences of poorly understood decisions.

Conflict between individuals is handled similarly, though on a smaller scale. Disputes are common — in a settlement as dense as Thren, friction is inevitable. Boundaries between dwellings shift as the halg grows. Resources are shared and sometimes contested. Personal grievances accumulate. The Threnware address these conflicts directly, through conversation, usually with the help of a mutually trusted third party who serves as a facilitator rather than a judge. There is no punishment in Thren. There is only the ongoing, sometimes exhausting work of maintaining relationships in a community where you cannot avoid the people you disagree with.

Exile exists, but it is self-imposed and rare. A person who cannot or will not maintain the web of relationships that Thren requires will sometimes choose to leave — to cross the rim and descend into the dry valleys, to join the trading communities there or to live alone in the moorland. This is understood as a personal choice, not a sentence. Some who leave return, after a season or a year or a decade, and they are welcomed back without ceremony or recrimination. The bowl holds whoever is in it. It does not chase those who leave, and it does not bar those who return.

## Family and Intimacy

Threnware family structures are fluid, shaped by the same principles of adaptability and impermanence that govern the built environment. The basic unit is the *hearth* — a group of people who share a dwelling and a cooking fire. A hearth might consist of a couple and their children, or several related adults and their collective offspring, or a group of unrelated individuals who have chosen to live together. Hearths form, dissolve, merge, and split with a fluidity that would be disorienting to someone from a culture of fixed households. The architecture encourages this: rooms are added and abandoned, walls grow together and are pruned apart, and the physical structure of a dwelling can be modified to accommodate changing social configurations.

Romantic partnerships are common but not formalised. There is no marriage ceremony, no contractual bond. Two people who wish to be together simply begin sharing a dwelling, and the community acknowledges this without fanfare. The dissolution of a partnership is equally informal. This is not to say that the Threnware take relationships lightly — the emotional bonds are as deep and complex as in any human culture — but they do not surround these bonds with legal or religious scaffolding. A relationship is what the people in it make it, for as long as they choose to make it.

Children belong to the settlement as much as to any individual hearth. This is not an abstract principle but a practical reality. In a community of four hundred people living in dense proximity, children are constantly in the presence of adults who are not their parents, and the responsibility for their care and education is broadly distributed. A child who is hungry will be fed by whichever hearth is nearest. A child who is hurt will be comforted by whichever adult reaches them first. A child who needs to learn a skill will be taught by whoever possesses that skill and has the patience to share it.

This does not mean that parent-child bonds are weak. They are, if anything, strengthened by the distributed nature of care, because parents are freed from the anxiety of sole responsibility. A mother in Thren knows that her child is safe, fed, and watched over even when she is working in the channels or foraging on the moor. This knowledge allows a degree of personal freedom that parents in more isolated family structures do not enjoy.

Sexuality in Thren is matter-of-fact, unburdened by shame or elaborate regulation. The dense living conditions make true privacy rare, and the Threnware have adapted to this with a combination of tact and acceptance. Certain areas of the settlement — the deeper, more enclosed chambers of the communal structures, the sheltered pools along the channel system — are understood to be available for intimate use, and others respect this without being told. Adolescents explore their sexuality with a freedom that is guided but not restricted by the adults around them. The primary concern is consent, and this is taught early and reinforced constantly.

## The Sacred

The Threnware are not religious in any organised sense. They have no temples, no priests, no scripture, no formal theology. But they have a relationship with their environment that is deeply, pervasively spiritual — a way of understanding the world that infuses every action with meaning and that shapes their behaviour in ways both large and small.

The core of this relationship is the concept of *reciprocity*. The Threnware understand, at a level that is intuitive rather than intellectual, that they are sustained by the bowl — by its plants, its water, its animals, its soil — and that this sustenance creates an obligation. They take from the bowl, and so they must give back. This giving-back is not transactional, not a system of payments or sacrifices. It is a posture, a way of moving through the world that acknowledges dependence and expresses gratitude through care.

The orange-spotted newt is sacred. This is not because the Threnware believe it to be a deity or a messenger of deities. It is sacred because it is *vulnerable* — a slow-moving, brightly coloured amphibian in a world of fast predators and accidental tramplings — and because its vulnerability mirrors the vulnerability of the settlement itself. Thren exists because its inhabitants tend it. The newt exists because its inhabitants protect it. The parallel is not lost on the Threnware, and they honour it by stepping around the newts, by teaching their children to do the same, by treating the protection of the smallest and most defenceless creature in the bowl as a measure of their own worthiness to inhabit it.

The walker — the large, hoofed animal that visits the settlement at night — occupies a different place in the Threnware's spiritual landscape. It is not sacred in the way the newt is sacred. It is something older and stranger, a presence that the Threnware do not fully understand and do not attempt to. They leave offerings at the rim — salt, grain, sometimes a woven basket of fruit — not because they believe the walker needs these things but because the act of leaving them acknowledges a relationship. The walker comes. The walker goes. The Threnware do not know where it lives or what it eats or why it visits. They know only that it is *there*, that it is *large*, and that it has been coming to the bowl for as long as anyone can remember. The offerings are a way of saying: *we see you, and we are not afraid, and we hope you are well.*

The Threnware mark the passage of time not with calendars or festivals but with biological events: the first emergence of the newts after the autumn rains, the ripening of the legume pods, the arrival and departure of migratory birds, the annual flooding of the channels during the heaviest rains. These events are noticed, discussed, and responded to, but they are not celebrated with ceremony. The Threnware do not separate the sacred from the mundane. Every act of pruning, every channel cleared, every fish harvested is an act of participation in the bowl's life, and participation is the only worship they require.

Death is handled with the same pragmatic reverence. The dead are composted. This is not a euphemism or an indignity. It is a deliberate, carefully managed process: the body is placed in a shallow pit lined with the same decomposer-rich soil that feeds the root mat, covered with a layer of moss and leaves, and left to return to the bowl. Within a season, the body has been absorbed into the settlement's living substrate. The Threnware find this beautiful. The person you loved becomes the ground you walk on, the food you eat, the walls that shelter you. There is no afterlife in Threnware belief, but there is continuation — a literal, physical, biological continuation that is more comforting to them than any promise of paradise.

## The Senses of the Threnware

Living in Thren shapes the senses in specific ways.

Hearing is the most developed sense. The Threnware live in a wall of sound, and they have learned to parse it with extraordinary precision. They can identify individual voices in a crowd. They can distinguish the calls of a dozen frog species and use them to gauge the time of day, the weather, the season. They can hear a blockage forming in a channel before it becomes visible. They can detect the approach of a stranger by the change in the ambient soundscape — the sudden silence of birds, the altered rhythm of insect noise. Children develop this ability early, and by adulthood it is so deeply ingrained that the Threnware are often unaware of it. It is simply how they perceive the world.

Smell is similarly refined. The air in Thren is thick with olfactory information — the scent of different plants, the smell of water at different temperatures and flow rates, the musky odour of the root mat, the sharp green smell of freshly cut halg. The Threnware read these smells as fluently as they read sounds. They can tell, by smell alone, whether a patch of root mat is healthy or diseased, whether a fish pool is overstocked, whether rain is coming. Their vocabulary for smell is more extensive than that of any outside culture, with specific words for dozens of olfactory nuances that outsiders would struggle to distinguish.

Touch is constant and unremarkable. The Threnware are a tactile people, comfortable with physical contact in a way that outsiders sometimes find disconcerting. They touch each other frequently — a hand on a shoulder, a brief embrace, a casual lean against a friend while talking. This is partly cultural, partly practical: in a settlement where every surface is wet and yielding, where pathways are narrow and spaces are shared, physical contact is unavoidable, and the Threnware have normalised it.

Vision is the least relied-upon sense. The light in Thren is diffuse and green, and visibility is often limited by mist, vegetation, and the sheer density of the built environment. The Threnware see well enough, but they do not depend on sight the way outsiders do. They are comfortable in conditions of near-blindness — thick fog, the gloom of the deep understory, the darkness of a moonless night — because their other senses compensate.

## The Inner Life

What is it like to be Threnware? To grow up in the bowl, to know no other world, to live inside a place that is simultaneously a settlement and an ecosystem and a single, vast, breathing organism?

It is, above all, a life of *immediacy*. The Threnware do not dwell on the past or plan extensively for the future. The environment does not permit it. The settlement changes too quickly, too unpredictably, for long-term plans to be meaningful. A dwelling that is comfortable today may be overgrown next season. A food source that is abundant this month may be scarce next. The Threnware respond to this impermanence not with anxiety but with presence — a quality of attention to the current moment that is both practical and philosophical.

This presence is not a spiritual discipline, not something the Threnware cultivate deliberately. It is simply the natural consequence of living in a place where the environment demands constant attention and constant response. You cannot ignore the halg tendril that is growing across your doorway. You cannot postpone the clearing of a blocked channel. You cannot put off the harvest. The bowl's rhythms are immediate and non-negotiable, and the Threnware have learned to move with them rather than against them.

The result is a culture that is remarkably free of the psychological burdens that plague more settled, more predictable societies. The Threnware do not worry about the future in the abstract way that agricultural or industrial peoples do. They do not accumulate possessions — there is no point in accumulating things when everything you own will eventually be absorbed by the root mat. They do not plan for retirement or legacy or the long-term consequences of their actions beyond the immediate season. They live in the bowl, in the present, in the dense, wet, sound-filled, green-lit *now* of Thren, and this gives their lives a quality of vividness and engagement that outsiders sometimes find enviable and sometimes find exhausting.

They laugh easily. This is one of the first things an outsider notices. The Threnware are quick to find humour in situations — in the unpredictable behaviour of the halg, in the antics of the frogs, in the absurdity of trying to maintain a straight pathway in a settlement that is determined to curve. Their humour is gentle, self-deprecating, and deeply tied to the specific conditions of their environment. A joke in Thren is often a shared acknowledgment of the bowl's indifference to human intention — a wry, warm recognition that they are not in control, and that this is fine.

They grieve deeply but briefly. Death is common in Thren — the infant mortality rate is high, accidents happen, disease moves through the dense population with speed — and the Threnware have learned to hold their grief with a kind of fierce tenderness that does not linger into bitterness. The composting ritual helps. There is something deeply consoling about returning a loved one to the bowl, about knowing that their body will nourish the very plants that will shelter and feed the people they left behind.

They are not curious about the outside world. This is perhaps the most striking thing about the Threnware to an outsider. They know that other places exist — the dry valleys, the trading settlements, the vast world beyond the moor — and they do not care. Not with hostility, not with fear, but with a calm, settled indifference. Thren is enough. Thren is more than enough. Thren is, in the most literal sense, everything.

## The Old Ones

The eldest Threnware — those who have survived past sixty, past seventy — occupy a special place in the settlement. They are not leaders. They are not authorities. They are *rememberers*.

In a culture without writing, memory is the only archive. The old ones carry the history of the settlement in their bodies: the great flood of thirty years ago, the year the fish pools failed, the winter the canopy froze and the settlement went hungry, the time a child was lost in the mist and found three days later on the rim, alive and unharmed, having been guided by the sound of the settlement's voice. These memories are not formalised into stories or histories. They are simply part of the old ones' conversation, surfacing naturally when relevant, providing context and precedent for current decisions.

The old ones are also the most skilled practitioners of the settlement's crafts. Decades of working with halg, with water, with fibre, with stone have given them a depth of knowledge that cannot be taught quickly. They are patient teachers, and they are sought out by younger people who wish to learn. But they are not revered. They are not set apart. They live in the same dwellings, eat the same food, do the same work — slower, perhaps, and with more frequent rests, but without special treatment. The bowl does not grant privileges based on age. It only grants the opportunity to accumulate knowledge, and the old ones have accumulated more than anyone.

When an old one dies, the loss is felt as a diminishment of the settlement's collective memory. The Threnware speak of this with a particular sadness — not the sharp grief of losing a loved one, but the quieter sorrow of losing a library. Something that was known is now unknown. Something that was remembered is now forgotten. The bowl absorbs the body, but the knowledge — the decades of accumulated, embodied, irreplaceable knowledge — is gone.

This is why the old ones talk so much. They talk constantly, to anyone who will listen, filling the settlement's air with their memories and their knowledge and their observations, pouring their archives into the ears of the young in a continuous, urgent, generous act of transmission. They know that their time is limited, and they spend it freely.

## The Children

Threnware children are feral. This is said with love.

They are born into a world that is wet, warm, dense, and alive, and they respond to it with a ferocity of engagement that is both delightful and slightly alarming. They climb everything — the halg walls, the willow trunks, the rock faces of the bowl. They swim in every pool and channel. They catch frogs and fish and insects with their bare hands. They eat things that make their parents wince. They disappear into the undergrowth for hours and return covered in mud and moss and bearing some treasure — a bird's egg, a particularly beautiful stone, a handful of edible mushrooms — that they present with enormous pride.

They are not supervised in the way that children in more structured societies are supervised. The settlement is their playground, and the entire adult population is their collective guardian. A child who wanders into a dangerous situation — too close to a deep pool, too high in the canopy — will be redirected by the nearest adult, gently and without alarm. A child who is injured will be treated by whoever has the relevant knowledge. A child who is hungry will be fed.

This freedom produces children who are remarkably competent and remarkably confident. A Threnware child of eight can navigate the settlement blindfolded. A child of ten can identify every edible plant in the bowl. A child of twelve can weave a watertight basket, tend a fish pool, and prune a halg wall. These are not exceptional children. These are ordinary children, raised in an environment that demands competence and provides the freedom to develop it.

They learn through participation, not instruction. There are no schools in Thren, no formal lessons, no curriculum. Children learn by doing — by watching adults and imitating them, by asking questions and receiving answers, by making mistakes and correcting them. The learning is continuous and embedded in daily life. A child who is curious about how a fish trap works will be shown, not told. A child who wants to know why a particular plant grows in one place and not another will be taken to see, to touch, to smell, to taste.

The result is a generation of children who are deeply, intimately connected to their environment — not as an abstract concept but as a lived, physical reality. They know the bowl the way you know your own body: not perfectly, not completely, but with a familiarity and an ease that comes from constant, unmediated contact.

## The Strangers

Occasionally — not often, perhaps once or twice a year — someone comes to Thren from outside. A trader from the dry valleys, following a rumour of the green settlement in the bowl. A wanderer, drawn by curiosity or desperation. A young person from one of the surrounding communities, seeking something they cannot name.

The Threnware receive these strangers with a warmth that is genuine but not effusive. They offer food, shelter, and the freedom of the settlement. They answer questions patiently. They show the stranger how to navigate the root mat, how to drink from the channels, how to step around the newts. They do not proselytise. They do not try to convince the stranger to stay. They simply open the bowl, as they open it to everyone, and let the place speak for itself.

Most strangers stay for a few days, a week at most, and then leave. They are grateful, often moved, sometimes transformed by the experience, but they leave. Thren is too much for them — too dense, too wet, too loud, too alive. They carry the memory of it with them, and some of them return, years later, for another visit, another immersion in the green light and the sound and the warmth. But they do not stay.

A few do. A few strangers arrive in Thren and do not leave. They feel, immediately or gradually, that they have come home. They learn the skills, adapt to the rhythms, shed their outsider habits like a snake shedding skin. Within a year, they are indistinguishable from the born Threnware — not because they have erased their origins but because the bowl has absorbed them, as it absorbs everything, into its dense, breathing, beautiful complexity.

These adopted Threnware are treasured. They bring new knowledge, new perspectives, new skills. They remind the born Threnware that their way of life is a choice, not a default, and that the choice is worth making. They are, in a sense, the settlement's immune system — a small, regular influx of new material that keeps the community resilient and adaptable.

## What They Are

The Threnware are not noble savages. They are not primitive. They are not a romantic ideal. They are people — flawed, funny, practical, tender, occasionally petty, frequently kind, always busy, always wet — who have made a specific and extraordinary choice about how to live, and who remake that choice every day, with every cut of the pruning blade, every cleared channel, every shared meal, every child taught, every newt stepped around, every body returned to the earth.

They are four hundred people in a green bowl in the high country, living inside a place that is trying, always, to grow beyond them and through them and past them, and they are holding their own — not against the growth but within it, as part of it, as one more expression of the bowl's relentless, generous, indifferent, magnificent life.

They are the weirs of Thren: not barriers against the flow, but structures within it, shaping it, being shaped by it, channelling the water of the world into patterns that sustain and shelter and nourish.

They are alive in a way that most people are not.

They are home.
