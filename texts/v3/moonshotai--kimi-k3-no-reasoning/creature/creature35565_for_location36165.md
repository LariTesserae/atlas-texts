# The Inhabitants of the Farr Anthrac

---

## The Veil — The Living World

To understand what lives on the Farr Anthrac, you must first abandon the distinction between the place and the life it contains. On other worlds, in other landscapes, this separation is easy: here is the ground, here are the things that grow on it. On the Farr Anthrac, the boundary dissolves. The Veil is not merely *on* the plateau — it is *of* it, so intimately bonded to the mineral substrate that to speak of one without the other is to speak a half-truth.

The Veil is not one thing. It is not even one category of thing. It is a phylum — perhaps several phyla — of organisms so alien to water-based life that the usual taxonomic categories strain and buckle under the attempt to contain them. They are not plants, not fungi, not lichens, not bacteria, though they perform functions analogous to all of these. They are a form of life that has taken the fundamental chemistry of living systems and rebuilt it around a solvent that is not water — something ammoniacal, something that flows sluggishly in the cool, thin air, something that carries nutrients in configurations that no terrestrial biochemistry would recognise as food.

At the cellular level — if "cellular" is even the right word — the Veil is organised around membrane-bound compartments filled with this ammoniacal solvent. The membranes themselves are not lipid bilayers but something more resilient, more mineral — a silicate-organic hybrid that is rigid enough to maintain structure at low temperatures but flexible enough to permit growth and movement. Within these compartments, the chemistry of life proceeds at a pace that would seem glacial to a water-based organism. Reactions that would take microseconds in a terrestrial cell take minutes here. Metabolic cycles that would complete in seconds take hours. The Veil does not hurry. It has never needed to.

The photosynthetic apparatus of the Veil is its most distinctive feature — the system that defines its colours, its distribution, its entire relationship with the light that bathes the plateau. The pigments are not chlorophyll. They are not carotenoids or phycobilins or any of the light-harvesting molecules that terrestrial biology has produced. They are something else — complex, conjugated molecular structures built around metal centres, tuned to absorb in the near-ultraviolet and the deep violet, in the wavelengths that pass through the thin, unshielding atmosphere of the Farr Anthrac with the greatest intensity.

These pigments are what give the Veil its colours — or rather, the *absence* of absorption in certain wavelengths is what gives it colour. The deep violet patches, the ones that verge on black in the shadowed troughs, are absorbing almost everything — hoarding the light, converting it with an efficiency that borders on the total. These are the conservative species, the slow growers, the ones that have adapted to the slightly dimmer conditions of the troughs and the lee sides of the undulations. They do not waste. They do not reflect. They take what the star gives and hold it.

The pearl-white patches are the opposite. They reflect almost nothing and appear, to the human eye, almost luminous — as if they are generating light rather than reflecting it. This is an illusion, but it is a productive illusion: these species are the most efficient photosynthesizers on the plateau, converting stellar radiation into chemical energy with a thoroughness that leaves almost no visible residue. They are the foundation of the ecosystem's energy economy, the primary producers that feed — directly or indirectly — everything else that lives here.

Between these extremes lies the spectrum of the Veil's diversity. The mauves and lavenders, the dusty roses and pale ambers, the silver-greys with their iridescent sheen — each represents a different photosynthetic strategy, a different set of pigments tuned to a slightly different slice of the available spectrum. The Veil has partitioned the light the way a tropical rainforest partitions vertical space: each species occupying a slightly different niche, each community a mosaic of complementary strategies that together extract the maximum possible energy from the environment.

The mosaic is not random. Walk across the plateau — walk for days, for weeks — and you begin to see the pattern. The polygonal cells of the pavement, each a few metres across, each slightly domed, each bounded by its crack of black magnetic dust, host distinct communities of Veil. Some cells are dominated by a single species — a vast, monochrome expanse of violet or rose or pearl. Others are mixed — a patchwork of species growing side by side, their boundaries marked by thin lines of darker pigmentation, as if they are delineating their territories with deliberate precision.

The growth patterns within each cell are equally varied. Some species form concentric rings, expanding outward from a central point at a rate of perhaps a millimetre per year. The rings are visible as subtle variations in colour and texture — growth bands, like the rings of a tree, recording years and decades and centuries of slow accumulation. Some of the oldest patches have hundreds of these rings, each one a record of a year's growth, a year's light, a year's slow, patient photosynthesis.

Other species spread in dendritic patterns — branching, fractal forms that fill their cells like frost on a window, like the veins of a leaf, like the branching of a river delta seen from above. These patterns are efficient — they maximise surface area, maximise exposure to light, maximise the contact between the organism and the substrate from which it draws its mineral nutrients. They are also beautiful, though the Veil does not know this and would not care.

Still other species form what can only be described as *blooms* — irregular, organic shapes that spread across their cells like ink in water, like mould on bread, like the slow, amoebic crawl of something that is alive but has no particular shape it needs to be. These are the opportunists, the colonisers, the species that move into disturbed or unoccupied cells and fill them quickly — quickly by the standards of the Farr Anthrac, which means over the course of decades rather than centuries.

The Veil communicates. This is not speculation — it is observable, measurable, verifiable. The communities within each polygonal cell are not isolated; they exchange chemical signals across their boundaries, releasing and detecting molecular messengers carried in the ammoniacal solvent that permeates their surface layer. These signals coordinate growth, regulate density, trigger reproductive cycles. When a dust event deposits fresh mineral particles on the surface, the Veil responds — not immediately, not dramatically, but with a slow, coordinated shift in metabolic activity that propagates across the affected area like a wave, like a whisper passed from cell to cell.

The signals are chemical, but they are also, in a sense, *optical*. Certain species of Veil can alter their reflectance in response to environmental cues — brightening or dimming, shifting their hue, modulating their iridescence. These optical signals are visible to neighbouring patches, and there is evidence — circumstantial but compelling — that the Veil uses them to communicate across distances greater than chemical diffusion alone would allow. A patch of pearl-white Veil brightens in response to increased light; its neighbours detect this change and adjust their own photosynthetic activity accordingly. The result is a slow, rippling coordination of metabolic activity across vast areas of the plateau — a continental-scale symphony of light and chemistry, conducted without a conductor, performed without an audience.

## The Spires — Monuments of the Veil

The spires deserve their own accounting, because they are not merely a growth form of the Veil — they are its most extraordinary achievement, its most visible statement, its most mysterious behaviour.

A spire begins as a thickening — a slight elevation in the surface of a Veil patch, barely perceptible, no more than a millimetre above the surrounding crust. Over the course of years, this thickening grows — not upward, at first, but *inward*, consolidating, densifying, transforming the soft, living tissue of the Veil into something harder, something more permanent. The Veil cells in this region begin to extrude a rigid, translucent material — a silicate-organic composite, similar in composition to their cell membranes but far denser, far more structured. This material is deposited in layers, each layer slightly different in orientation from the last, creating a laminated structure of extraordinary strength and optical complexity.

Over decades, the thickening becomes a nub. Over centuries, the nub becomes a protuberance — a small, rounded dome, perhaps ten centimetres high, rising from the surface of the Veil like a blister, like a bud, like the first tentative push of something that has not yet decided what it wants to become.

Over millennia, the protuberance becomes a spire.

The mature spire is a branching, articulated structure of translucent material, rising one to two metres above the surface of the plateau. Its form is not random — it follows a consistent architectural logic, a pattern of growth that is repeated across all spires with minor variations. The base is thick and sturdy, flaring slightly where it meets the ground, anchored to the substrate by a network of Veil tissue that extends deep into the cracks of the polygonal pavement. From this base, the spire rises in a series of segments, each segment slightly narrower than the last, connected by flexible joints that allow the structure to sway — very slightly — in the thin wind.

At intervals along its length, the spire branches. The branches are not random — they emerge at specific angles, following a pattern that is related to the golden ratio, to the Fibonacci sequence, to the mathematical principles that govern branching structures in biological systems across the universe. Each branch is itself segmented, itself articulated, itself capable of further branching. The result is a fractal structure — a form that repeats itself at every scale, from the overall silhouette of the spire down to the finest terminal branches, which are no thicker than a human hair.

The material of the spire is translucent — not transparent, but translucent, allowing light to pass through but scattering it, refracting it, splitting it into its component wavelengths. When the star is high and the light is at its most intense, the spires catch this light and transform it. Spectral bands — thin, ghostly rainbows — paint the surrounding ground with faint streaks of colour. The colours shift as the star moves, as the light changes, as the angle of incidence varies. A spire is never the same twice. It is a living optical instrument, a crystal organ played by the light.

But the spires are not merely decorative. They are functional — essential, in fact, to the Veil's survival in ways that are only partially understood. The branching structure maximises surface area, and the surfaces of the spire are covered in a thin layer of living Veil tissue — a continuation of the crustose growth that covers the ground, extended upward into the air. This aerial tissue is photosynthetically active, capturing light at angles and intensities that the ground-dwelling Veil cannot access. It is also the primary site of gas exchange — the point at which the Veil absorbs the trace atmospheric gases it needs and releases the waste products of its metabolism.

More than this: the spires are the Veil's reproductive organs. At certain times — on cycles that may be annual, may be decadal, may be tied to some fluctuation in the star's output that no one has yet identified — the terminal branches of the spires swell and darken. Within these swollen tips, the Veil produces its propagules — small, lightweight structures that are released into the wind and carried across the plateau. These propagules are not seeds, not spores, not any terrestrial category. They are packets of living Veil tissue, encased in a protective shell of the same translucent material as the spire itself, small enough to be lifted by the thin wind but large enough to survive the journey.

The release of propagules is one of the few events on the Farr Anthrac that could be described as *dramatic*. It does not happen often — perhaps once every few years for any given spire — but when it does, the air around the spire fills with tiny, glittering particles, each one catching the light, each one a potential new colony. They drift on the wind in glittering veils, visible from a distance as faint, shimmering clouds, like dust motes in a sunbeam, like the breath of the plateau made visible. Most of them will land on unsuitable ground and die. A few will land in an unoccupied polygonal cell, or on the edge of an existing patch, and take hold, and begin the slow, patient process of becoming the Veil.

The interior of a spire is not solid. It is hollow — or rather, it is *channeled*, permeated by a network of fine tubes and cavities that run through the branching structure like the vascular system of a plant, like the tracheal system of an insect. These channels serve multiple functions. They transport the ammoniacal solvent and dissolved nutrients from the base of the spire to its extremities. They carry gases — the waste products of photosynthesis, the chemical signals that coordinate the spire's growth. And they provide habitat — shelter, living space — for a community of organisms so small and so specialised that they exist nowhere else on the plateau.

## The Spire-Dwellers — Life Within Life

Within the hollow channels of the spires, in the dark, protected interior where the light filters through translucent walls and arrives soft and diffuse, there exists a community of microscopic organisms that represent a distinct branch of the Farr Anthrac's evolutionary tree. They are not Veil — not exactly. They share the Veil's basic chemistry, its ammoniacal solvent system, its silicate-organic membranes. But they have abandoned the crustose, spreading growth form of their surface-dwelling relatives and adopted a different strategy: they live *inside*.

These spire-dwellers are small — the largest are a fraction of a millimetre across, the smallest barely more than complex molecular assemblies. They are not photosynthetic. They are heterotrophs — consumers, feeders, organisms that derive their energy and nutrients not from light but from the chemical bounty that flows through the spire's channels. They feed on the dissolved nutrients in the ammoniacal solvent — trace minerals, organic compounds, the waste products of the spire's own metabolism. They are, in effect, the spire's microbiome — its internal ecology, its gut flora, its complement of symbionts and commensals and, occasionally, parasites.

The spire-dwellers are diverse. Some are sessile — anchored to the inner walls of the channels, filtering nutrients from the passing solvent with delicate, feathery structures that extend into the flow. Others are motile — moving through the channels with slow, undulating motions, propelled by cilia-like structures or by changes in the surface tension of their membranes. Some form colonies — clusters of individuals that cooperate in the capture and processing of nutrients, sharing resources through direct chemical exchange. Others are solitary — lone organisms drifting through the spire's interior like fish in a dark river.

The relationship between the spire-dwellers and the Veil that hosts them is complex and not fully understood. In some cases, it appears to be mutualistic: the spire-dwellers consume waste products that would otherwise accumulate to toxic levels, and in return they receive shelter and a steady supply of nutrients. In other cases, the relationship is more ambiguous — the spire-dwellers consume living Veil tissue, boring into the channel walls, weakening the spire's structure. Whether this constitutes parasitism or simply a form of nutrient recycling is a question that the Farr Anthrac does not answer.

What is clear is that the spire-dwellers are ancient. Their genetic material — and they *do* have genetic material, though it is not DNA, not RNA, not any nucleic acid that terrestrial biology would recognise — shows evidence of a long, independent evolutionary history. They have been living inside the spires for as long as there have been spires to live inside. They are as much a part of the Farr Anthrac as the Veil itself, as the substrate, as the light.

## The Drifters — Ghosts of the Plateau

The drifters are the most visible animals on the Farr Anthrac, which is to say they are barely visible at all.

A drifter is a small, translucent, jelly-like organism, roughly the size of a human hand — perhaps fifteen centimetres across when fully extended, though they are rarely fully extended. Their bodies are composed primarily of the same ammoniacal solvent that fills the cells of the Veil, held in a flexible, semi-permeable membrane that is more mineral than organic — a thin, tough, nearly invisible skin that gives the drifter its shape and protects its interior chemistry from the harsh conditions of the plateau's surface.

Beneath this membrane, the drifter's body is a gel — a thick, viscous, translucent substance that fills the interior space and serves as both structural support and metabolic medium. Within this gel, the drifter's organs — if they can be called organs — are suspended: a central digestive cavity, a network of channels that distribute nutrients throughout the body, a rudimentary nervous system that is little more than a loose web of light-sensitive and chemical-sensitive cells. The drifter has no brain. It has no eyes. It has no appendages. It is, in essence, a living drop — a self-contained pocket of chemistry that moves and feeds and reproduces with a simplicity that borders on the miraculous.

The drifter's transparency is its most striking feature. Its body is the same refractive index as the surrounding air — nearly — which means that light passes through it with minimal distortion. A drifter at rest is almost invisible. You could stand a metre from one and not see it, unless the light caught it at the right angle, unless the slight difference in refractive index between its body and the air produced a faint shimmer, a ghostly outline, a suggestion of presence.

When a drifter moves — and it does move, slowly, constantly, with a patience that makes the Veil look hasty — it becomes more visible. The movement is peristaltic: a wave of contraction that passes through the drifter's body from rear to front, pushing it forward across the surface of the Veil at a rate of perhaps a centimetre per minute. The movement creates subtle distortions in the drifter's translucent body — ripples, bulges, areas of compression and expansion — and these distortions catch the light and refract it, producing small, moving spectral flashes: brief rainbows that slide across the drifter's surface like oil on water, like light through a prism, like the ghost of a ghost.

These spectral flashes are often the first sign of animal life that a traveller notices on the Farr Anthrac. You are walking across the plateau, surrounded by silence and stillness and the vast, pale expanse of the Veil, and something catches your eye — a flicker, a shimmer, a brief flash of colour that seems to come from nowhere. You stop. You look. You see nothing. And then — there — another flash, slightly to the left, slightly closer. You focus, and gradually, slowly, your eyes adjust to the subtlety of what you are looking at, and you see it: a drifter, crawling across the surface of the Veil, leaving a faint trail of disturbed surface cells behind it, its translucent body catching the light with every slow, peristaltic pulse.

The drifter feeds on the Veil. This is its sole source of nutrition, its sole interaction with the ecosystem beyond the simple fact of its existence. The feeding apparatus is located on the drifter's ventral surface — the underside, the part that is in contact with the ground. It is not a mouth in any conventional sense. It is a *rasping organ* — a broad, flat area of specialised tissue that secretes a weak ammoniacal enzyme capable of dissolving the outer layer of the Veil's crustose growth. The drifter positions itself over a patch of Veil, lowers its ventral surface, and releases the enzyme. The outer cells of the Veil dissolve — slowly, gently, without drama — and the liberated nutrients are absorbed directly through the drifter's membrane.

The process is slow. A drifter may spend hours on a single feeding site, dissolving and absorbing, dissolving and absorbing, moving only when the local supply of Veil is depleted. The damage to the Veil is minimal — a shallow depression in the crust, a few millimetres across, which the Veil will repair in a matter of weeks. The drifter is not a destroyer. It is a grazer — a gentle, patient, almost apologetic consumer of the ecosystem's bounty.

Drifters reproduce by budding. When a drifter has accumulated sufficient nutrients — when it has fed well and grown large and its internal chemistry has reached a threshold of complexity that triggers the reproductive cycle — a small bulge appears on its dorsal surface. Over the course of days, this bulge grows, differentiates, develops its own membrane and its own internal chemistry. Eventually — after a week or more — the bud separates from the parent and begins its own independent existence. The parent drifter is diminished by the process but not destroyed. It will feed, recover, and bud again, given time. Given the pace of life on the Farr Anthrac, there is always time.

The lifespan of a drifter is unknown. They do not age in any way that is visible to an outside observer. They do not senesce, do not weaken, do not slow down. They simply continue — feeding, moving, budding — until something kills them. And on the Farr Anthrac, there is essentially only one thing that kills them.

## The Shades — The Apex of a Slow World

The shades are the apex predators of the Farr Anthrac, and they are among the strangest organisms this world has produced.

To call a shade a predator is to use a word that carries connotations of speed, of aggression, of the chase. None of these apply. A shade does not chase. A shade does not hunt in any active sense. A shade *waits* — with a patience that is not a virtue but a fundamental property of its biology, a patience that is as much a part of what it is as its colour, its shape, its chemistry.

A shade is, essentially, a shadow. It is a flat, nearly two-dimensional organism that spreads across the surface of the Veil like an inkblot, like a spill of dark liquid, like a hole in the light. Its body is a thin, flexible membrane — rarely more than a few millimetres thick — that lies flush against the ground, conforming to the contours of the substrate with a precision that makes it nearly impossible to see. Its colour is dark — deep violet to near-black, a colour that matches the darker patches of the Veil so closely that the distinction between organism and substrate is, at a glance, meaningless.

This is camouflage, but it is more than camouflage. It is *identity*. A shade is not hiding in the Veil. A shade *is* a piece of the Veil — a piece that has made a different evolutionary choice, that has opted out of photosynthesis and into predation, that has traded the slow, patient accumulation of solar energy for the sudden, concentrated bounty of animal flesh.

The shade's body is a single, continuous membrane — no segmentation, no appendages, no distinct organs visible on the surface. The membrane is dark because it is filled with a dense concentration of light-absorbing pigment — the same class of pigments that the Veil uses for photosynthesis, but repurposed. The shade does not use these pigments to capture light for energy. It uses them to *eliminate* light — to absorb every photon that strikes its surface, to render itself invisible, to become a gap in the visual field, a piece of darkness in a luminous world.

The size of a shade varies. The smallest are perhaps ten centimetres across — barely larger than the drifters they prey upon. The largest are a metre or more — vast, dark patches that sprawl across multiple polygonal cells, their edges blending imperceptibly into the surrounding Veil. The size of a shade is a function of its age and its feeding success. A well-fed shade grows — slowly, imperceptibly, adding a millimetre to its diameter over the course of a year. A poorly fed shade shrinks — reabsorbing its own tissue, reducing its surface area, becoming smaller and darker and more invisible.

The shade's hunting strategy is simplicity itself: it does nothing. It lies on the surface of the Veil, motionless, invisible, a patch of darkness among patches of darkness. It may remain in this position for days, for weeks, for months. It does not sleep — it has no brain to sleep with. It does not dream. It simply *is* — present, patient, a piece of the landscape that happens to be alive.

And then a drifter crawls onto it.

The drifter does not see the shade. It cannot see — it has no eyes. It navigates by chemical sensing, by detecting the subtle variations in the Veil's surface chemistry that tell it where to feed. The shade's surface chemistry is — by design or by evolutionary accident — indistinguishable from the Veil's. The drifter crawls onto the shade's membrane as if it were crawling onto any other patch of ground. It settles. It begins to secrete its digestive enzyme.

And the shade *contracts*.

The contraction is the fastest event on the Farr Anthrac. It is, by the standards of this slow world, an explosion — a violent, shocking, almost obscene burst of speed. The shade's membrane, which has been lying flat and motionless for days or weeks, suddenly *folds* — rippling inward from the edges, converging on the drifter at the centre, wrapping around it with a speed that takes less than a second from initiation to completion. The drifter is engulfed — enclosed in a pocket of shade-membrane, cut off from the outside world, trapped.

The shade then secretes its own digestive enzymes — ammoniacal compounds far more potent than anything the drifter produces. These enzymes dissolve the drifter's membrane, its gel, its internal structures. The process takes minutes. The drifter does not struggle — it has no capacity for struggle. It simply dissolves, its translucent body breaking down into its component chemistry, its nutrients absorbed directly through the shade's inner membrane.

When digestion is complete, the shade unfolds. It returns to its flat, motionless, invisible state. It is slightly larger than before — slightly darker, slightly more substantial. The nutrients from the drifter will sustain it for weeks. It will not need to feed again for some time. It settles back into the landscape, a shadow among shadows, waiting.

The shade's reproductive biology is poorly understood. They do not appear to bud, as drifters do. They do not appear to produce propagules, as the Veil does. The leading hypothesis — and it is only a hypothesis, based on limited observation and a great deal of inference — is that shades reproduce by *fission*: when a shade grows large enough, it splits. Not dramatically, not suddenly, but slowly — a line of weakness develops across the centre of the membrane, and over the course of days, the shade gradually separates into two smaller shades, each of which crawls — with agonising slowness — to a new location and resumes its patient vigil.

Whether shades have any form of awareness — any capacity for sensation, for experience, for anything that could be called consciousness — is a question that cannot be answered. They respond to stimuli: they contract when a drifter lands on them, they adjust their position in response to changes in light intensity, they avoid areas of the plateau where the Veil is too thin or too sparse to conceal them. But these responses are automatic — reflexive, chemical, mechanical. There is no evidence of anything more.

And yet. When you watch a shade — when you find one, invisible on the ground, and sit beside it for hours, watching it do nothing, watching it be nothing, watching it exist in its patient, silent, two-dimensional way — you cannot shake the feeling that there is something *there*. Not a mind, not a consciousness, not a self. But a *presence* — a quality of being that is more than the sum of its chemistry, more than the mechanical responses of its membrane. The shade is alive, and its aliveness is palpable, even in its stillness. Perhaps especially in its stillness.

## The Crack-Dwellers — Life in the Margins

Between the polygonal cells of the pavement, in the shallow cracks filled with black magnetic dust, there exists another community of organisms — smaller, less visible, less understood than the Veil or the drifters or the shades, but no less real, no less alive.

These are the crack-dwellers — burrowing forms that live within the fissures of the pavement, in the thin layer of dark dust that accumulates in the cracks. They are small — the largest are a few millimetres long, the smallest microscopic. They are not translucent like the drifters; they are dark — black or deep grey, matching the dust they inhabit. They are not flat like the shades; they are cylindrical, worm-like, elongated — shaped for movement through the narrow, confined spaces of the cracks.

The crack-dwellers feed on the dust itself — on the fine, magnetic, mineral particles that the wind deposits in the fissures. Their bodies contain specialised structures — not quite mouths, not quite filters — that extract trace nutrients from the dust: metal ions, organic compounds deposited by the wind, the waste products of the Veil that have been carried by the thin atmosphere and settled in the cracks. They are the decomposers of the Farr Anthrac — the recyclers, the processors of the ecosystem's detritus.

Their role in the ecosystem is essential. Without the crack-dwellers, the dust in the fissures would accumulate indefinitely, filling the cracks, altering the drainage patterns of the plateau's surface, disrupting the delicate balance between the Veil and its substrate. The crack-dwellers process this dust — digest it, transform it, excrete it in a form that the Veil can absorb. They are the intermediaries between the geological and the biological — the organisms that bridge the gap between the mineral world and the living one.

They are almost impossible to observe. They spend their entire lives within the cracks, rarely emerging onto the surface. They are sensitive to vibration — the footfall of a traveller sends them retreating deeper into the fissures, beyond the reach of observation. They are best detected by their absence: by the fact that the dust in the cracks never accumulates beyond a certain depth, by the faint chemical signature of their metabolic processes, by the occasional, rare glimpse of a dark, wriggling form retreating into the depths as a shadow passes overhead.

## The Airborne — The Breath of the Plateau

The atmosphere of the Farr Anthrac is thin, but it is not empty. It carries — in addition to its trace gases and mineral dust — a biological component: a suspended community of microscopic particles, some living, some dormant, some in the ambiguous borderland between the two.

These are the airborne — the spores and propagules and drifting cells of the plateau's ecosystem, lifted by the wind and carried across vast distances before settling. They are not a single species or a single type. They are a *assemblage* — a diverse, heterogeneous collection of biological particles that share only the common property of being small enough and light enough to be borne aloft by the thin wind.

The most numerous of the airborne are the Veil propagules — the tiny, glittering packets of living tissue released by the spires. During a propagule release event, the air around a spire fills with these particles, and they drift outward on the wind in glittering veils, visible from a distance as faint, shimmering clouds. The propagules are hardy — their protective shells of translucent, silicate-organic material shield them from the harsh radiation and the extreme dryness of the plateau's atmosphere. They can survive in the air for days, for weeks, drifting on the wind, waiting for the chance encounter with suitable ground that will allow them to germinate and grow.

But the airborne community is more than Veil propagules. It includes the reproductive cells of the drifters — small, buoyant cysts released during the budding process, carried aloft before the parent drifter has finished separating from its offspring. It includes the metabolic byproducts of the shade — microscopic particles of digested drifter tissue, expelled from the shade's membrane after feeding, carried by the wind to who knows where. It includes fragments of Veil tissue — tiny pieces of the crustose growth, broken free by the wind or by the thermal stress of the substrate's expansion and contraction, lifted into the air and carried across the plateau.

And it includes things that are harder to categorise — particles that are clearly biological in origin but that do not correspond to any known organism. These may be the spores of species that have not yet been observed — organisms that exist in some part of the plateau that has not been explored, or that live in a form so different from anything yet catalogued that their propagules are unrecognisable. Or they may be something else — something that challenges the very definition of "organism," something that exists in the borderland between living and non-living, between biology and chemistry.

The airborne community is visible, under the right conditions. When the light is at the right angle — when the star is low on the horizon and the light comes in from the side, grazing the surface of the plateau — the airborne particles catch the light and become visible as a faint, glittering haze, a thin veil of luminescence that hangs in the air like a breath, like a sigh, like the exhalation of the plateau made visible. This is most common during propagule release events, but it happens at other times as well — spontaneous, unpredictable, brief. The plateau breathes, and for a moment, its breath is visible.

## The Centre — What Lives in the Bowl

No one has reached the centre of the spiral. No one has descended into the vast, shallow depression that lies at the plateau's heart. What lives there is unknown — genuinely, completely unknown.

What is known — from spectral analysis, from the instruments of orbiting probes — is that the centre is *different*. The Veil's chemistry shifts. The colours change. The energy regime is altered. Something is happening in that bowl — something biological, something chemical, something that the instruments cannot fully resolve — that has no analogue on the rest of the plateau.

The colours at the centre are the most intense. The spectral data shows wavelengths that do not appear anywhere else on the Farr Anthrac — emissions in ranges that suggest either a different class of photosynthetic pigment or a different photochemical process altogether. The light that is reflected from the centre is not the white, mineral light of the outer plateau. It is something else — something with a quality that the instruments describe as *anomalous*, which is the scientific word for *we do not understand what we are looking at*.

The biological activity at the centre is also different. The density of life — measured by the spectral signatures of metabolic processes — is higher than anywhere else on the plateau. The centre is, relative to the rest of the Farr Anthrac, *crowded* — a concentration of biological activity that suggests either a richer energy source, a more favourable microclimate, or some other factor that has drawn life to this point and concentrated it.

What forms this life takes is a matter of pure speculation. Perhaps the Veil at the centre is different — taller, denser, more complex, freed from the constraints of the outer plateau's extreme conditions by some local factor — a slightly thicker atmosphere, a slightly higher temperature, a slightly different chemistry. Perhaps the spires at the centre are taller — true towers, rising ten metres or more, their branching forms more elaborate, more articulated, more *deliberate* than anything on the outer plateau. Perhaps the drifters and the shades have central analogues — larger, more complex, more active forms that have evolved in response to the centre's unique conditions.

Or perhaps the centre is home to something entirely different — something that has no analogue anywhere else on the Farr Anthrac, something that represents a distinct branch of this world's evolutionary tree, a form of life that arose in the centre and never spread outward, confined by the same barriers that prevent outside life from reaching in.

The Farr Anthrac does not answer these questions. It does not answer any questions. It simply *is* — vast and dry and bright and alive, turning slowly in its spiral, breathing its thin breath, ticking its slow clock. What lives at its centre lives there whether or not anyone ever sees it, whether or not anyone ever knows. The plateau does not need to be known. It does not need to be understood. Its inhabitants — the Veil, the spires, the drifters, the shades, the crack-dwellers, the airborne, and whatever lives in the centre — do not need to be catalogued or classified or explained.

They live. They persist. They endure. On a world without water, in a light without warmth, in a silence without end, they have found a way to be. And that, perhaps, is the most extraordinary thing about them — not their strangeness, not their alienness, not their difference from everything we know. But their simple, stubborn, undeniable *presence* — the fact that they are *there*, on that high, pale, windswept plateau, living their slow, patient, luminous lives, whether or not anyone is watching.
