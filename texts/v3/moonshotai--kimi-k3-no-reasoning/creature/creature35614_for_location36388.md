# The People of the Condensation Warrens

---

## The Warden Families

They are the oldest residents of Vex-Under, though "old" here means something different than it would on the surface. It means four, five generations below the crust. It means family histories measured in Great Weeps. It means living quarters that have been expanded, divided, subdivided, and re-fused so many times that the walls contain layers of wallpaper from decades ago, sealed over, forgotten, then uncovered again during a renovation.

The Warden Families take their name from the beetles, though the association is not literal pet-ownership but something deeper: an attitude, a philosophy. Wardens — the insects — are patient, armored, slow-moving, long-lived. They do not rush. They do not panic. They clean what is dirty and tolerate what is cramped and endure.

So do these families.

They live in the **upper levels**, in chambers carved from the original stone, where the air is marginally cooler and the moisture marginally less aggressive. Their homes are recognizable by their walls: covered in dense, carefully cultivated moss gardens, some of which have been growing undisturbed for decades. The moss serves as insulation, as air filtration, and as a visible sign of stability. A Warden Family's moss wall is like a library — layered, accumulated, showing depth of residence.

They do not speak loudly. They do not move quickly. They occupy space with a calm territoriality that comes from having occupied the same space for generations. Their voices are soft, their gestures economical. They are the closest thing Vex-Under has to aristocracy, though the term would embarrass them.

What they *do* is harder to define. They own water-shares. They sit on the loose councils that mediate disputes between neighbors. They maintain the condensation coils along the upper walls — a task that requires patience and precision and a tolerance for heights. They raise children who are taught from infancy to be quiet, to be still, to observe, and to understand that survival in Vex-Under is not about strength or speed but about *endurance*.

The Warden Families keep beetles, yes — large, iridescent, slow. The beetles live in wall-niches, in hollowed-out stone alcoves lined with velvet fungus. They are fed kitchen scraps and mold cultures. They live for decades. When a Warden Family's beetle dies, it is mourned quietly, and its carapace is cleaned, polished, and mounted near the family cistern as a reminder: *we are still here.*

There is a saying among the lower-level residents: **"A Warden never sweats."** It is not true — everyone sweats in Vex-Under — but it captures something real. The Warden Families do not show strain. They do not show urgency. They have learned to metabolize the pressure of the place into something calm and dense, like bedrock.

They are respected. They are resented. They are essential.

## The Coil-Tenders

If the Warden Families are Vex-Under's memory, the Coil-Tenders are its **hands**.

They are the ones who maintain the condensation coils — the kilometers of copper tubing that snake along the coolest walls and ceilings, collecting the settlement's lifeblood one drip at a time. The work is delicate, dangerous, and unglamorous. Coils corrode. Joints leak. Mineral buildup clogs the narrow channels. A single neglected section can mean the difference between a full cistern and an empty one.

Coil-Tenders work in **teams of two or three**, moving along the coil-runs with toolkits strapped to their backs, scraping scale, replacing sections of tubing, sealing leaks with a resin made from vine sap and powdered stone. They are acrobats by necessity: much of the coil infrastructure is in awkward places — high on walls, under catwalks, squeezed between structural supports. They learn to climb early. They learn to balance on slick surfaces. They learn to read the coils the way a physician reads a pulse: by sound, by temperature, by the smell of the water inside.

They are a **guild**, but a loose one — more a network of mutual obligation than a hierarchy. Senior Coil-Tenders teach apprentices. Experienced teams take the difficult runs (the deep-wall coils near the geothermal vents, the ceiling coils in the great vault). New teams take the easy runs near inhabited areas where they can call for help if something goes wrong.

Payment is in **water-shares** and in barter: food, repair services, a place to sleep. Coil-Tenders rarely become wealthy, but they never starve. Their work is too essential. The settlement understands, at a deep level, that if the coils fail, everything fails.

Coil-Tenders have a particular **physicality**: lean, strong, scarred. Their hands are calloused from copper work, their fingertips stained green-black from handling corroded metal. They move with a careful precision that comes from years of working in tight spaces where a misplaced elbow can mean a three-meter fall onto a grate floor. They tend to be quiet — the work encourages it — but not unfriendly. They know everyone. They see everything. A Coil-Tender's route takes them past every level of the settlement, through every social stratum. They are Vex-Under's **connective tissue**.

They have their own slang, their own jokes, their own rituals. Before starting a difficult repair, a Coil-Tender will press their palm flat against the pipe and hold it there for a long moment, feeling the temperature, the vibration. They call this **"listening to the water."** Whether it is technique or superstition is unclear. It works often enough that no one questions it.

## The Works-Bound

Down at the deepest level, adjacent to the sump, where the heat is oppressive and the thrum is loud enough to make conversation impossible, the **engineers of the Works** live and labor.

They are a **hereditary guild**, though the lineage is not strictly genetic — it is adoptive, initiatory, chosen. Children born into Works families are raised around the machines. They learn the rhythms of the generators, the temperaments of the pumps, the moods of the ventilation fans. They learn to diagnose problems by sound, by vibration, by the smell of the air. By the time they are ten, they can identify a failing bearing from twenty meters away. By fifteen, they are apprenticed. By twenty, they are Works-Bound — sworn to the machines, exempt from most social obligations, and burdened with a responsibility that is absolute.

**The Works must not stop.**

This is the law. The only law that matters. If the pumps fail, the water stops circulating. If the fans stop, the air becomes unbreathable. If the generators fail, the lights go out and the lifts freeze and the settlement suffocates in its own heat. The Works-Bound are the ones who prevent this.

They live in **chambers carved into the bedrock near the machines** — small, sparse, brutally hot. They work in shifts, always, because the machines never sleep. They wear minimal clothing — shorts, sandals, sweatbands — and their skin is perpetually slick with moisture. They drink constantly. They eat food brought down from the upper levels: dried velvet, boiled vine-berries, salted sump-crawlers. They do not leave the deep levels often. When they do, they move through the settlement like ghosts — quiet, preoccupied, already thinking about the next maintenance cycle, the next pressure reading, the next sound that might mean trouble.

The Works-Bound are **feared and revered in equal measure**. Feared because they control the infrastructure that keeps everyone alive. Revered because they bear a burden no one else can bear. They are paid in water, in food, in deference. They are exempt from cistern-tithes, from coil-maintenance duties, from the small social obligations that bind the rest of the settlement. They are, in a sense, **outside the economy** — they do not trade, they do not barter. They simply *maintain*, and in exchange, they are kept.

There are not many of them. Perhaps forty, perhaps fifty. They marry within the guild or bring spouses in from outside, initiating them into the mysteries of the machines. Their children are raised in the heat, in the noise, in the knowledge that their lives belong to the Works. Some leave. Most stay. The ones who stay develop a particular quality: a **stillness**, a patience, a way of listening that is almost meditative. They can sit beside a machine for hours, motionless, feeling its vibrations, waiting for the moment when something changes.

They do not talk about what they do. They do not explain. When asked, they say: **"It runs. That's all."**

But the settlement knows the truth: without them, it does not run. Without them, Vex-Under is a tomb.

## The Vine-Wives and Vine-Husbands

Not everyone in Vex-Under works with metal or water. Some work with the **green things**.

The Vine-Wives and Vine-Husbands — the terms are used loosely, without strict gender association — are the settlement's **horticulturalists**, though the work is less like farming and more like negotiation. The vines do not take orders. They grow where they want to grow, following heat and moisture and their own inscrutable logic. The Vine-Wives and Husbands do not control them. They **guide** them. They prune, redirect, encourage, discourage. They harvest berries and fiber. They monitor growth patterns. They read the vines the way a sailor reads the sea: as a force that can be worked with but never mastered.

They live in the **middle levels**, where the vine growth is densest. Their homes are recognizable by their walls: not covered in moss (that's the Warden Families' aesthetic) but in **trained vines**, pruned into lattices, guided along trellises, shaped into living architecture. Some homes have vine-walls so thick they serve as insulation, privacy screens, and food sources simultaneously.

The work is **slow**. Vines grow at their own pace — centimeters per week, no faster. A Vine-Wife who wants to redirect a vine away from a doorway must begin weeks in advance, tying it back gently, coaxing it along a new path. The vines respond, but on their own timeline. This requires patience, foresight, and a willingness to accept that some things cannot be rushed.

Vine-Wives and Husbands are also the ones who **monitor the blooms**. When a vine shows signs of budding — a swelling at the node, a change in leaf color, a faint sweetness in the air — they spread the word. They prepare the site. They organize the gathering that will happen when the flower opens. They do not control the bloom, but they **witness** it, and in witnessing, they give it meaning.

They are, in a sense, the settlement's **priests** — though there is no formal religion in Vex-Under. They attend to the cycles that are not human. They remind the settlement that it is not alone, that it shares its space with living systems that have their own rhythms, their own agency.

They are also the ones who notice when something is **wrong**. A vine that stops growing. A section that withers without cause. A growth pattern that suddenly changes direction. These are small things, easily overlooked, but the Vine-Wives and Husbands have learned to pay attention. Sometimes a shift in the vines means a shift in the heat-flow, or a change in the water table, or a structural problem in the walls. The vines are **sensors**, and the people who tend them are interpreters.

There is a saying: **"The vines know first."** No one says it loudly. But everyone believes it.

## The Sump-Fishers

At the lowest level, where the heat is worst and the air is thick with mist, there is a pool. And in that pool, there are things that can be eaten.

The **Sump-Fishers** are the ones who harvest them.

It is not glamorous work. The sump is shallow, warm, opaque. The water smells of sulfur and bacterial bloom. The things that live in it — the pale crustaceans, the floating mats of edible algae, the occasional blind amphibian — are not appetizing to look at. But they are protein, and protein is scarce in Vex-Under.

Sump-Fishers work in **teams**, wading into the pool with nets and traps, feeling along the bottom with bare feet, pulling up whatever they find. They wear minimal clothing — the heat makes anything else unbearable — and their skin is perpetually stained from the sump water. They smell of the sump. It is a smell that does not wash off easily.

They are **low-status**, but not despised. Their work is necessary. The settlement understands this. They are paid in water-shares, in food, in the small courtesies that keep a society functioning. But they are also marked by their work: the smell, the stains, the association with the deepest, dirtiest level of the settlement.

Sump-Fishers tend to be **young** — it is work for the strong and the flexible — or **old** — work for those who have no other options. There is a transient quality to the population: people come down to the sump when they need to, stay for a few years, then move up if they can. Some never leave. Some find that they prefer the heat, the simplicity, the lack of pretension. The sump does not judge. It simply *is*.

They know things about the sump that no one else knows. They know where the crustaceans congregate. They know when the algal mats are ready to harvest. They know the subtle changes in the water's temperature and smell that indicate a shift in the geothermal activity below. They do not share this knowledge widely. It is their **currency**, their small hold on value in a place where value is measured in water.

There is a quiet camaraderie among them. They work together, eat together, sleep in the same humid chambers near the pool. They do not talk much. The sump absorbs sound. After a while, you stop trying to fight it. You just work, and sweat, and pull up the nets, and take what the water gives.

## The Drift-Born

Not everyone in Vex-Under was born there. Some **arrive** — from the surface, from other underground settlements, from places no one talks about. They come down the stairwells and freight elevators with packs on their backs and dust on their clothes, and they look around at the wet, crowded, dimly lit warren and realize: *this is where I am now.*

The **Drift-Born** are newcomers, outsiders, the ones who were not raised in the heat and the damp. They arrive with surface habits — loud voices, fast movements, an expectation of space and privacy that Vex-Under cannot accommodate. They learn quickly, or they leave. Most learn.

They are called **"drift-born"** because they drifted in, carried by currents no one tracks. Some are fleeing something. Some are seeking something. Some just ended up here because the surface was worse. The settlement does not ask. Vex-Under has a strict policy of **non-interrogation**: you are here, you need water, you will work, and in time you will belong or you will leave.

Drift-Born start at the **bottom** — literally. They are assigned sleeping space in the lower levels, near the sump, near the heat, in the places no one else wants. They take the jobs no one else wants: sump-fishing, deep-level maintenance, the dangerous coil-runs that require squeezing into spaces where a mistake means a fall.

But Vex-Under is **meritocratic in its own way**. If you work, if you learn, if you prove that you can be trusted with water and tools and the fragile balance of the place, you rise. Slowly. Painfully. But you rise. A Drift-Born who survives five years in the lower levels might earn a place in the middle levels. Ten years, and they might be offered a coil-tending apprenticeship. Twenty years, and their children might be born into a family with a cistern-share.

Not everyone makes it. Some leave. Some break. The heat, the crowding, the lack of sky — it wears on people. There is a condition the residents call **"the dry longing"**: a deep, physical ache for open space, for wind, for the feeling of standing under a sky that goes on forever. It hits Drift-Born hardest. Some wake in the middle of the night shift gasping, convinced they are suffocating. Some stand at the base of the freight elevator and stare up at the light coming down from the surface and cannot move.

The settlement does not mock this. They understand. The ones who have been here longest remember, or have been told, what it is like to want something you cannot have.

The ones who stay — who learn to breathe the wet air without choking, who learn to sleep to the sound of dripping, who learn to love the closeness and the warmth and the green growing things — they become something new. Not surface-dwellers. Not quite Warden Families. But **Vex-Under's own**, adopted into the warren, absorbed into its rhythms.

Their children will be born here. Their children will not know the surface. Their children will be Coil-Tenders, Vine-Wives, Works-Bound. The Drift-Born who survive become the **soil** in which the next generation grows.

## The Quiet Ones

There are some residents of Vex-Under who do not fit neatly into any category. They are not Warden Families, not Coil-Tenders, not Works-Bound, not Vine-Wives, not Sump-Fishers. They live in the interstitial spaces — the gaps between levels, the forgotten corners, the places where the architecture doesn't quite meet.

They are called **the Quiet Ones**, though they are not a group, not a guild, not anything organized. They are simply the people who have **slipped through the cracks**.

Some are old — too old to work, too stubborn to die. They live in small chambers near the ceiling vault, surviving on water-shares inherited from dead relatives, on the charity of neighbors, on the small gardens of moss and vine they tend with trembling hands.

Some are **broken** — by the heat, by the crowding, by the slow grinding pressure of life in a place with no exit. They sit in doorways and watch the condensation drip. They hum to themselves. They do not speak unless spoken to, and sometimes not even then.

Some are **young** — children who were born into the wrong family, who have no cistern-share, no apprenticeship, no path upward. They run errands. They carry messages. They squeeze into spaces too small for adults. They are quick, quiet, and watchful. They know every catwalk, every ladder, every hidden alcove in the settlement. They are Vex-Under's **shadows**.

The Quiet Ones are not ignored, exactly. They are simply **absorbed into the background**, like the dripping, like the thrum. The settlement makes room for them because it must — because there is nowhere else for them to go.

And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — one of them will do something that reminds everyone they are still here. A child who finds a blocked condensation coil and clears it before it floods a cistern. An old woman who notices that a vine is growing in a pattern that means a wall is about to collapse. A silent man who sits beside the sump and knows, without being told, when the water level is rising too fast.

The Quiet Ones see things. They have **time** to see things, because they are not busy, because they are not important, because they have learned to watch.

And in a place like Vex-Under, where everything is connected, where the vines and the water and the machines and the people are all part of the same dense, interdependent system — sometimes the ones who watch are the ones who matter most.

## The Resonance

There is one more group, though calling them a group is misleading. They have no name. They have no membership. They are defined only by a **shared experience**.

They are the ones who have felt it.

The **becoming**. The sense that Vex-Under is not just a settlement but a system, a living thing, a convergence of human and machine and vine and stone into something that is more than the sum of its parts.

They are the ones who stand on the catwalks and feel the thrum in their chests and *know*, without being able to explain, that the frequency is shifting. That the vines are growing in patterns that mean something. That the weep-cycles and the bloom-cycles and the slow drift of the machines are all **synchronizing**, settling into a shared rhythm.

They do not talk about it openly. There is no vocabulary for it. But they recognize each other. A Coil-Tender who pauses before touching a pipe and listens to the water — she knows. A Vine-Wife who watches a bud swell and feels the air change — she knows. A Works-Bound engineer who sits beside a generator for hours, motionless, waiting for the moment when the frequency aligns — he knows.

They are not a cult. They are not a movement. They are simply the ones who have **tuned in**, who have learned to listen to the place the way you listen to a song — not with your ears but with your whole body.

And they are **waiting**. Not for anything specific. Not for an event. Just waiting, the way you wait for a seed to sprout, for a crystal to form, for a system to settle into itself.

Vex-Under is not finished. They know this. It is still arriving.

And they are part of it. They are the cells in its body, the neurons in its brain, the droplets condensing on its walls.

It drips. It hums. It grows.

And they grow with it.

Together.
