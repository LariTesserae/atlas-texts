They are not fish. They are not machines. They are the space between those categories given form and purpose.

Tens of thousands of them fill the column from base to apex—each one small enough to cradle in your palm, shaped like a teardrop pressed flat, ridged beneath and domed above. Their bodies are iron-grey, dark as the volcanic glass that surrounds them, but flecked with points of copper light that pulse and fade with their internal rhythms. They have no eyes to see you standing at the boundary. No mouths to speak or eat. What they possess instead, arranged in precise rows along their undersides, are intake valves—circular, mechanical in their perfection, opening and closing in sequences that never quite repeat.

Each one is a piston. A pump. A tiny engine that breathes pressure and exhales sound.

They live suspended in the water-column, packed so densely in some layers that they appear as solid sheets, hovering curtains of grey and copper that undulate in currents that exist only in the patterns of their collective movement. Near the base, they cluster thickest—their bodies nearly touching, their combined bioluminescence turning the water amber, honey-thick, warm. Higher up, the density thins. The light shifts through green, through pale silver, until at the apex only a few solitary individuals drift in the thin, pressurized water near the column's point, their copper lights sparse as early stars.

They feed on what the water carries upward from the deep earth. Dissolved minerals. Trace gases. Chemical potential locked in molecular bonds. The water rises through the volcanic substrate, pushed by geological forces, and enters the column's base already burdened with sustenance. As it ascends, the creatures strip it clean. Their intake valves open in rhythmic sequence—tick, tick, tick—drawing in water, extracting what can be converted to energy, expelling what remains. They are refiners. Processors. A distributed filtering system tens of thousands strong.

And they sing.

Not in voices, not in any way that a throat could produce, but in the percussion of their valves, the pulse of their internal mechanisms. Each creature maintains its own rhythm—a pattern of pressure-waves that propagates through the water as sound. Standing near the column, you hear the sum of all these patterns: a drone, deep and grinding and layered, like a factory heard through water, like machinery operating inside a living chest. The sound is immense. Physical. It presses against your sternum, resonates in your teeth, makes the volcanic glass underfoot hum in sympathy.

But the sound is not static. It shifts.

Over hours, over days, the creatures adjust their rhythms in response to their neighbours. A new pulse-rate emerges somewhere in the column's middle reaches—a slightly different timing, a subtle variation—and it begins to propagate. Nearby creatures synchronize, then desynchronize, creating waves of harmonic density that roll upward and downward through the water. When thousands align, the drone resolves into something almost tonal—a vast chord, felt more than heard, that makes the frost-crystals in the basalt shadows chime like distant bells. When they scatter out of phase, the result is a grinding roar, mechanical and chaotic, like ten thousand engines all turning over and none catching fire.

They migrate, but slowly. So slowly.

A creature near the base might begin an ascent that takes days to complete. They rise in loose aggregations, responding to pressure gradients, to the changing density of minerals in the water, to cues too subtle for human senses. These migrations create the drifting curtains of light that sweep upward through the column—zones of brightness and shadow that shift with glacial patience. The entire system operates on timescales that blur the boundary between biological and geological. Pulses measured in seconds. Migrations in days. Population reorganizations in seasons. The construction of the base—the strange, terraced platform growing molecule by molecule from extracted minerals—in decades or centuries or longer.

They are building something.

The evidence is there in the base: a crust of metallic, iridescent material that spreads outward from the column's foundation, warm to the touch, humming on the same frequency as the pale veins in the volcanic rock. It grows in terraces, in curves, in shapes that suggest intention—an arch, perhaps, or a dome, or a cradle for something that has not yet arrived. The creatures deposit what they extract. They refine the water to greater and greater purity, and the impurities become structure, become architecture, become a slow-growing monument to a purpose they may not comprehend.

Occasionally—rarely, perhaps a few dozen times per day—one of them leaves the water.

It passes through the boundary with a flash of copper light and a sound like a single bell note, hangs in the cold air for a few moments, its valve-rhythm stuttering and changing as it adapts to the new medium. What it does out there, in the thin atmosphere, is unclear. It may be calibrating some internal mechanism. It may be transmitting information to other creatures at different levels of the column, using air as a medium that carries sound differently than water. It may be performing some function for which there is no name, no analogue, no category.

Then it re-enters. Another flash. Another bell. The column absorbs it, and it resumes its place in the slow, patient work.

They do not notice you. You are irrelevant to their process—outside their medium, outside their timescale, outside the long project that occupies their entire existence. They press onward and upward, extracting and converting and depositing, sounding their endless mechanical hymn. They are a factory without overseers, a chorus without audience, a machine that has forgotten—or never knew—what it was built to produce.

The column holds them, and they hold the column. They give it light, give it sound, give it purpose. They are its inhabitants and its function, its voice and its meaning. Small, patient, numberless, they work in the glowing water, and the thing they are building grows by one molecule, one pulse, one extracted atom at a time.
