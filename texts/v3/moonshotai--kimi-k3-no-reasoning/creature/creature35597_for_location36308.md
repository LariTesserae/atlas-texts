# The People of Rootwater

## The Calloused Ones

Their hands tell you everything. The people of Rootwater have palms like bark—thick, cracked, layered with callus that never fully softens because it never gets the chance. From the age a child can walk, they climb. Not with ropes or harnesses but with fingers and toes pressed into the crevices of ancient wood, reading the texture of bark the way others might read weather. By adulthood, their fingerprints are worn almost smooth, replaced by crosshatched patterns of old scars and new blisters. They do not shake hands when they greet. They press palms together, and in that brief contact, they can tell: *you belong here, or you don't.*

They are small people, mostly. The tree selects for this—not deliberately, not genetically, but practically. A body over six feet tall is a liability on a narrow branch in dim light. The heavy-boned fall more often. Those who remain, those who thrive, tend toward compactness: short limbs, low centers of gravity, feet that can curl around a rope and hold a body's weight while both hands work. Their toes are almost prehensile, the big toe set slightly apart from the others, a product of generations of gripping. When they walk on flat ground—on the rare occasions they descend to the plain—they hobble, uncomfortable, like sailors after too long at sea.

Their eyes are adapted to the gloaming. Wide pupils, an almost reflective quality in low light, a tendency to squint and turn away from bright sun. The amber-filtered light of Rootwater has shaped their vision the way the tree has shaped everything else. They see well in shadow. They see movement before they see detail. Colors are muted for them, except at the edges: the brilliant blue of a tree-frog, the shocking red of certain fungi, the iridescent green of beetle shells—these register as almost painfully vivid against the brown-and-amber backdrop of their world.

They are lean. Not starving—Rootwater provides, if you work—but there is no excess. Every calorie is earned through climbing, gathering, building, tending. Their muscles are functional rather than impressive: strong forearms, powerful thighs, cores that can hold a body rigid against a vertical trunk for hours. They move slowly and deliberately, conserving energy, wasting nothing. A Rootwater person does not fidget. Fidgeting is a luxury of the well-fed and the safe. Here, every unnecessary motion is a small risk, and the people have bred risk-aversion into their bodies the way other cultures breed courtesy.

## The Families

There are no surnames in Rootwater, only branch-names. You are *of the Third Branch* or *of the Seventh*, and this tells people where you live, who your neighbors are, what part of the tree you call home. The branches are not equal. The First and Second are lowest, closest to the trunk, where the wood is thickest and the light is dimmest. They are the oldest settlements, the most densely built, home to families that trace their residence back through dozens of generations. The people of the lower branches are considered *deep-rooted*—conservative, traditional, resistant to change. They speak an older dialect, thicker with glottal stops and the rhythmic clicking sounds borrowed from insect speech.

The higher branches—Sixth through Ninth—are younger settlements, more spread out, less crowded. The people here are lighter, both literally (they tend to be smaller, thinner, adapted to the more precarious footing) and figuratively (they are more willing to experiment, to try new building techniques, to trade with the rare climbers from below). The Ninth Branch families are considered slightly wild, touched by the strangeness of the upper canopy. They keep odd hours. They know bird calls that the lower families have never heard. Some of them have gone above the Ninth Branch, despite the rule, and come back changed—or not come back at all.

Families are large but diffuse. A child might sleep in one relative's hollow, eat with another, work alongside a third. The concept of *household* is fluid, defined more by proximity and cooperation than by blood. This is partly practical: space is so limited that no single structure can hold a large family. But it is also cultural. The tree does not belong to any one person, and neither does a child. The branch raises its young collectively. A child of the Fifth Branch is a child of the Fifth Branch, regardless of who bore them.

They marry late, by the standards of the plain. There is no rush. The tree is patient, and the people have learned patience from it. Courtship is a long process of working alongside someone, learning their rhythms, testing whether their weight balances yours on a narrow bridge. Marriages are not ceremonies but agreements, recognized when two people begin building together—a home, a water-collection system, a garden of bark-fungi. The building *is* the marriage. If the structure holds, the marriage holds. If it fails, they salvage what they can and begin again.

## The Work

Everyone in Rootwater works. There is no leisure class, no specialization so narrow that you can afford to do only one thing. But there are roles, tendencies, gifts that certain people develop over years of practice.

**The Water-Tenders** are the most essential. They manage the collection systems: the absorbent pads, the clay jars, the slow networks of fiber channels that guide seeping moisture to where it can be stored. Water-tending requires patience beyond even the Rootwater standard. A water-tender might spend an entire day adjusting a single pad, testing its position against the bark, moving it a fraction of an inch to catch a better flow. They know the tree's moods better than anyone. They can feel a change in moisture through their fingertips before any visible sign appears. They are quiet people, mostly, with a meditative quality that comes from spending long hours alone with the slow drip of water.

**The Builders** work with what the tree provides. They gather shed branches, bark plates, hardened resin, and shape them into structures that must be both light enough to avoid overloading the branches and strong enough to withstand the tree's constant, subtle movement. A Rootwater builder thinks in terms of decades, not days. They select materials with obsessive care, testing each piece of wood for density, flexibility, grain direction. They know how the tree moves—how each branch sways slightly differently, how the trunk expands and contracts with moisture cycles—and they design their structures to accommodate these rhythms. A good builder's work is invisible: the structure seems to be part of the tree, to have grown there rather than been placed.

**The Foragers** range across the entire vertical territory of the town, from the lowest branches to the upper reaches of the Ninth. They know where the edible fungi grow, which insects are safe to eat, which birds' eggs can be collected without damaging the population. They tend the tree's gardens: the patches of tough, slow-growing plants that have been coaxed into existence in the bark crevices. Foraging requires an encyclopedic knowledge of the tree's ecology and an almost supernatural sense of balance. The best foragers are part acrobat, part botanist, part hunter. They carry nothing but a woven bag and a short stick, and they move through the branches with a fluidity that makes the rest of the town look clumsy.

**The Resin-Tappers** tend the insect colonies that produce the raw materials for much of Rootwater's construction. This is delicate, dangerous work: the insects are small, easily disturbed, and their resin is produced in cycles that must be timed precisely. A resin-tapper knows the insects' behavior the way a water-tender knows the tree's moisture. They can read the temperature of a colony by holding their hand near it, judge the readiness of a resin deposit by its smell. They wear no gloves—gloves would dull their sensitivity—and their hands are perpetually stained amber, the resin worked so deeply into their skin that it never fully washes away.

**The Bridge-Keepers** maintain the rope bridges and walkways that connect the nine branches. This is the most public of roles: a bridge-keeper's work is visible to everyone, and a failure is immediately apparent. They inspect the ropes daily, testing for fraying, rot, insect damage. They adjust tension as the tree shifts, loosening here, tightening there. They are acrobats by necessity, comfortable hanging upside-down from a rope a thousand feet above the ground, splicing fiber with their toes while their hands work above their heads. They are also diplomats: when a dispute arises between branches, the bridge-keepers are often the first to know, for they cross between territories constantly and hear everything.

## The Children

Children in Rootwater learn to climb before they learn to talk. This is not an exaggeration. A Rootwater child's first words are typically not *mother* or *father* but directional: *up*, *down*, *hold*, *safe*. They are strapped to their parents' backs for their first year, feeling the constant motion of climbing, the shift of weight, the reach and grip and release. By the time they are two, they are climbing on their own—slowly, clumsily, but with an instinct that is already deeply ingrained.

They learn the tree by touching it. A child's education in Rootwater is almost entirely tactile: feel this bark, is it living or dead? Press this resin, is it ready or still soft? Grip this branch, will it hold your weight? The adults teach through demonstration and then step back, letting the child explore, make mistakes, learn. Falls are common. Most are short—a few feet, caught by a lower branch or a safety line. But every family has stories of children who fell farther, and these stories are told plainly, without melodrama, as part of the education. *The tree holds you or it doesn't. Pay attention.*

By the age of seven or eight, a Rootwater child can navigate the entire town—nine branches, dozens of bridges, hundreds of structures—with complete confidence. They know the routes the way other children know their own bedrooms. They have favorite spots: a particular hollow where the light falls just so, a bridge that sways at the perfect frequency, a crevass where a colony of luminous beetles pulses with greenish light at night. They have enemies too: other children, rivalries that form along branch-lines and are conducted through elaborate games of tag and chase that range across the entire vertical territory of the town, breathless and dangerous and utterly thrilling.

## The Elders

The oldest people in Rootwater are not the most powerful but the most *listened to*. They have lived through the most cycles, seen the most changes, accumulated the most knowledge about the tree's long-term patterns. They no longer climb much—their joints are stiff, their balance less certain—but they sit in their hollows or on their platforms, and people come to them.

They are the keepers of the stories. Rootwater has no writing, but its oral tradition is vast and meticulous. The elders remember genealogies stretching back twenty generations. They remember the great sheddings: times when the tree dropped major limbs, reshaping the town's geography overnight. They remember droughts: periods when the tree's moisture slowed to almost nothing and the people rationed water so severely that the old and the sick died of thirst. They remember invasions: swarms of insects that swept through the branches like fire, or flocks of birds that arrived in such numbers that they stripped the crevices of every edible thing.

The elders tell these stories not as entertainment but as *instruction*. Each story contains practical knowledge: how to prepare for a drought, how to reinforce a structure before a shedding, how to recognize the signs of an insect swarm before it arrives. The children listen. The young adults listen. Even the water-tenders and builders, who think they know everything, listen. Because the tree is old, older than memory, and it moves in cycles that span generations, and the only way to see those cycles is through the accumulated testimony of those who have lived long enough to see them repeat.

The elders also serve as judges, of a sort. Disputes in Rootwater are rare—there is little to fight over, and the consequences of violence in a place where everyone lives on branches above a long fall are self-evident—but they do arise. Property disputes, mostly: who has the right to build in a particular hollow, who controls a particular water-collection site, who may harvest from a particular garden. The elders mediate, drawing on precedent and practicality. Their decisions are not law, but they are rarely ignored. The social fabric of Rootwater is too small, too interconnected, to sustain open conflict. A person who refuses to accept the elders' judgment finds their ropes fraying faster than they should, their water-pads mysteriously repositioned, their bridges developing small but unnerving creaks. The community corrects itself.

## The Climbers

There is a subset of Rootwater's population that defies easy categorization. They are called the Climbers, though the name is inadequate—everyone in Rootwater climbs. The Climbers are those who *go down*.

The descent to the plain is the most dangerous thing a person from Rootwater can do. It takes days. The trunk below the First Branch is sheer, almost featureless, the bark harder and smoother than it is higher up. There are no branches to rest on, no crevices to shelter in. A Climber carries everything they need: rope, water, food, tools. They descend in stages, sleeping in hammocks strung to the trunk, exposed to the open air, the wind, the terrifying visibility of the desert stretching to every horizon.

They go down for stone. The tools of Rootwater—adzes, drills, scrapers—are made from stone, and stone comes from the plain. The best stone comes from a particular outcrop two days' walk from the tree's base, a formation of hard, fine-grained rock that holds an edge better than anything found nearby. The Climbers make the descent, load their packs with as much stone as they can carry—fifty pounds, sometimes more—and then climb back up. The ascent with a heavy pack is excruciating. It takes a week, sometimes longer. Some Climbers do not make it.

Those who do, and who do it repeatedly, become something more than stone-carriers. They become the town's connection to the outside world, such as it is. They see the plain. They know what grows there (little), what lives there (almost nothing), what changes (nothing). They bring back this information along with the stone: *the plain is the same, the plain is always the same, there is nothing new below.* This news is received with a kind of relief. The desert is not a threat. It is simply *there*, eternal and irrelevant, a fact as stable and unremarkable as the sky.

The Climbers are marked by their journeys. Their hands are different from other Rootwater hands—more scarred, less sensitive, hardened by the sheer brutality of the descent. They develop a distant look, a habit of staring at the horizon that unsettles the branch-dwellers, who never see farther than the next branch. They are respected and slightly feared. They have been *outside*, and they have come back, and this makes them strange.

## The Quiet Practice

There is no formal religion in Rootwater, but there is a practice—unnamed, unstructured, but universal—that fills the space where religion might be. It is simply this: *listening to the tree*.

Every person in Rootwater does this, though few would call it a practice or give it a name. It happens in the quiet moments: a water-tender pausing with her hand on the bark, feeling the vibration of sap moving through the xylem. A builder sitting cross-legged on a branch, eyes closed, listening to the subtle groans and pops of the wood adjusting to its own weight. A child lying belly-down on a thick limb, ear pressed to the bark, hearing the deep, slow pulse that is not a heartbeat but serves the same function: proof that the tree is alive, that it is still drawing water from the deep stone, that it has not yet finished.

The tree's sounds are the closest thing Rootwater has to scripture. The elders teach the young to distinguish between them: the normal groaning of settling wood (safe, ignore it), the sharper cracking that indicates a branch under stress (pay attention, reinforce or abandon), the deep, resonant thrumming that sometimes emanates from the trunk itself (the tree is *doing something*, drawing water, growing, shifting its vast internal chemistry). This last sound is rare and significant. When it occurs, people stop what they are doing. They listen. They place their hands on the bark. Some of them weep, though they could not say why.

There are no priests, no temples, no rituals. But there is a shared understanding that the tree is not merely a resource, not merely a habitat, but something *more*—something that exists on a timescale so vast that human lives are like the insects that hatch and die in a single day in the water-filled crevices. The tree does not need the people. The people need the tree. This asymmetry is understood and accepted without bitterness. It is simply the shape of things.

And in the quiet moments, when a person sits alone on a branch and feels the slow, patient pulse of the tree beneath them, they experience something that might be called gratitude, or awe, or love. They do not speak of it. There are no words in their language for what the tree means to them. But they stay. They build. They tend the water and the bridges and the gardens. They raise their children in the branches. They listen. They endure.

## The Dead

When someone dies in Rootwater—and people do die, though less often than you might expect, for the tree selects for caution—the body is given to the tree. This is not metaphorical. The dead are placed in hollows in the trunk or in the deep crevices between major branches, wrapped in woven fiber, and left. The tree does what it does. Insects arrive first, then fungi, then the slow chemical processes of decomposition. Within a season, the body is reduced to bone. Within a year, the bones themselves begin to soften and merge with the wood, absorbed by the tree's patient biology.

The families do not mark these sites, but they know where they are. Every branch has its dead, layered into the wood like the annual rings of the tree itself. The oldest parts of the town—the First and Second Branches, the lower trunk—contain the remains of hundreds, maybe thousands of people, absorbed over generations into the living tissue of the tree. The people of Rootwater live among their dead the way they live among the birds and the insects and the fungi: as part of the ecosystem, another layer in the dense, vertical community of the branches.

A child's first lesson in mortality is not a funeral but a hollow. *Your grandmother is in there,* a parent might say, pointing to a crevice that looks no different from any other. The child nods. The tree holds everyone, eventually. This is not sad. It is simply the way of things, the same slow cycle of growth and decay and renewal that governs everything in Rootwater.

The dead become the tree. The tree sustains the living. The living become the dead. The cycle continues, patient and unhurried, on a timescale that makes individual lives seem like the brief, bright flash of a beetle's wings in the amber light.

## The Strangers

Occasionally—very occasionally—someone climbs up from below who is not of Rootwater. These strangers are rare enough that their arrival is an event, discussed for days across all nine branches. They come for various reasons: curiosity, desperation, the rumor that there is water in the tree, the rumor that there is wealth. They are usually wrong about both.

The people of Rootwater receive them with a mixture of wariness and hospitality. There is no formal process for accepting a stranger, no citizenship test, no oath. The tree decides. If the stranger can climb, if they can learn the rhythms, if they can find a place in the dense, interlocking web of work and obligation that holds the town together, they stay. If they cannot, they leave—or they fall, and the tree absorbs them like it absorbs everything else.

Those who stay are slowly, imperceptibly transformed. Their hands develop calluses. Their eyes adapt to the gloaming. Their bodies lean out, their movements slow down, their footsteps become quiet on the bark. They learn the sounds of the tree, the moods of the water, the names of the birds and the fungi and the insects. They marry into families, adopt branch-names, build homes in hollows that have held other people's dead. They become Rootwater.

It takes about ten years, the elders say. Ten years for the tree to accept you. Ten years for the town to stop thinking of you as *the stranger* and start thinking of you as *the person who lives in the hollow by the Fourth Branch bridge*. Ten years for your body to reshape itself to the demands of vertical life, for your mind to adjust to the slowness, for your hands to forget whatever they used to do and learn the work of the tree.

After that, you are simply here. You have always been here. The tree does not distinguish between those who were born in its branches and those who climbed up later. It holds them all with the same patient, indifferent embrace. It sheds its bark. It seeps its water. It groans in the night. It grows so slowly that no one notices.

And the people of Rootwater—born or adopted, young or old, water-tender or builder or forager or bridge-keeper or climber or child—they continue. They wake in the dim amber light. They climb. They work. They listen to the tree. They tell their children the stories. They place their dead in the hollows. They maintain the bridges. They collect the water, drop by patient drop.

They are not remarkable. They are not heroic. They are simply people who live in a tree, and have lived in a tree for longer than anyone can remember, and will live in a tree for as long as the tree allows.

The tree allows. That is enough.
