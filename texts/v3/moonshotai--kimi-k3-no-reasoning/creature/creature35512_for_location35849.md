# The Inhabitants of the Hollow Heights

## The People Themselves

They are tall — taller than their ancestors were, shaped by the thin air and the low gravity of altitude. Their limbs are long, their movements economical, deliberate. Nothing about them is hurried. Even the children move with a kind of patience that would unsettle a visitor from a faster world.

Their skin carries the faintest green undertone, more visible in certain lights, a permanent stain from generations of contact with uuth biochemistry. In the dim amber light of the open platforms, they seem almost to be made of the same material as the city — dark, fibrous, enduring. Their eyes are their most striking feature: enormous, dark, adapted to gather every available photon. When they look at you, you feel *seen* in a way that is slightly uncomfortable, as if they are reading information from your surface the way they read the bioluminescent pulses of the uuth.

They do not wear much. The cold does not trouble them the way it would trouble baseline humans — their metabolisms have adapted, their skin thickened slightly, their tolerance for cold remarkable. They wear simple garments woven from processed uuth-fiber: dark, soft, durable. The clothing is functional rather than decorative, though the weaving patterns are complex and carry meaning — district of origin, family line, profession, all encoded in the structure of the fabric itself.

They are quiet people. Not silent — they speak, they communicate, they have language — but quiet in the sense that they do not fill space unnecessarily. A conversation in Vess Meranth involves long pauses. Silence is not awkward. It is part of the speech. When you live in a city that is constantly singing, you learn to let the city's voice carry some of the communicative load.

## The Listeners

They are the closest thing Vess Meranth has to specialists, to professionals, to a priesthood — though none of those words are quite right.

A listener is someone who has dedicated their life to the uuth. Not to worshipping it — the relationship is not devotional in any simple sense — but to *knowing* it. To understanding its patterns, its needs, its slow deep intentions. The listeners are the ones who guide the city's growth, who shape the architecture, who maintain the delicate balance between human habitation and uuth health.

They are recognizable by their instruments: the small vibrational devices they carry, usually worn on a cord around the neck or tucked into a belt-pouch woven from uuth-fiber. The instruments are ancient — some of them centuries old, passed down through lineages of listeners, each one tuned and retuned by successive hands until it has become almost perfectly resonant with the uuth's cellular frequencies.

Becoming a listener takes decades. It begins in childhood, with an apprenticeship to an established practitioner. The apprentice learns to *hear* — truly hear — the city's sounds. To distinguish between the cellular patter of healthy growth and the slightly different patter of stressed or damaged tissue. To read the bioluminescent pulses that trace the circulatory pathways. To feel, through the soles of their feet, the subtle variations in the root-spires' subsonic drone that indicate changes in the city's structural integrity.

The training is largely nonverbal. A master listener will take an apprentice to a particular wall, a particular chamber, and simply *stand* there, hand flat against the uuth surface, until the apprentice begins to feel what they feel. The knowledge is transmitted through proximity, through shared attention, through the slow accumulation of sensory experience that cannot be written down.

Listeners are not rulers. Vess Meranth has no government in any recognizable sense — no hierarchy, no laws beyond the obvious necessities of coexistence. But the listeners carry enormous informal authority. When a listener says a district needs to be vacated for a rest period, people move. When a listener says a particular structure is becoming unstable and should not be inhabited, people listen. The authority is based on trust, on demonstrated competence, on the understanding that the listeners are the ones who *know* — who have spent their lives in intimate conversation with the living city and can speak on its behalf.

There are perhaps a few hundred listeners in the entire city. They are distributed unevenly, clustered in the more densely populated districts, but all of them travel — long slow circuits through the city, checking on growth zones, monitoring the stillness-zones, maintaining the vast complex dialogue between human and uuth that keeps Vess Meranth alive.

## The Children

Children in Vess Meranth are rare and precious.

The population is declining — slowly, gently, without crisis. Fewer children are born each century. The inhabitants do not speak of this often, but they know. They see it in the empty districts, in the growing stillness-zones, in the way their communities are gradually contracting toward the city's center.

The children who are born are raised communally. Not in any structured way — there are no schools, no formal education system — but in the sense that a child belongs to their district, not just to their biological parents. They are taught by whoever is nearby. They learn to walk on the grown pathways, to tap the circulatory vessels for fluid, to avoid the spine-drakes' territories, to recognize the call of an approaching uuth-mover.

They play. This is important. Even in a dying city at the top of the world, children play. They chase each other through the breathing corridors. They hide in the hollow chambers. They make up games involving the bioluminescent pulses — trying to predict the patterns, trying to mimic them with hand-signals. They are not solemn. They are not weighed down by the city's ancientness. They are *children*, and they are loud and joyful and entirely themselves.

But they learn early the thing that defines life in Vess Meranth: patience. A child who wants a room of their own must wait for it. Must find a swelling in a wall, must convince a listener to guide its growth, must wait years while the cavity forms and differentiates and becomes a space that can be inhabited. The waiting is not deprivation. It is simply how things are. The children absorb this understanding the way they absorb language — without effort, without question, as a fundamental fact of existence.

## The Elders

At the other end of life are the elders — the ones who have lived long enough to see multiple growth-and-rest cycles, who remember the city as it was centuries ago, who carry in their minds a map of changes so slow that younger inhabitants cannot perceive them.

The elders are not frail. The uuth-derived biochemistry that sustains the inhabitants also preserves them. They age, but slowly, and they remain capable well into their second or third century. An elder of two hundred years might be stooped, might move more slowly, but their eyes are still sharp, their hands still steady, their knowledge still vast.

They are the memory of Vess Meranth. Not in any formal sense — there are no archives, no written histories — but in the sense that they *remember*. They remember when a particular district was young and vigorous. They remember when a stillness-zone was a thriving residential area. They remember the voices of uuth-movers that died centuries ago, and they can tell you which current individuals carry similar harmonics.

The elders are consulted. Not formally — there are no councils, no meetings — but in the course of daily life. When a listener is uncertain about a growth pattern, they seek out an elder who has seen something similar before. When a family is deciding whether to remain in their current district or migrate to a newer growth zone, they ask the elders what they remember about the area's history.

The elders do not lead. They *contextualize*. They provide the long view, the deep temporal perspective that allows the inhabitants to understand their lives as part of a continuum that extends backward beyond memory and forward into uncertainty.

## The Relationship with the Uuth

This is the core of everything.

The inhabitants of Vess Meranth do not think of themselves as separate from the city. They are not *in* the uuth — they are *of* it. Their bodies are sustained by its circulatory fluid. Their homes are grown from its tissue. Their tools are shaped from its fibers. When they die, their bodies are returned to the uuth — placed in shallow cavities in the substrate, covered with a thin layer of living material, and slowly, over years, absorbed.

The absorption is literal. The uuth breaks down the body, incorporates its minerals, its organic compounds, its trace elements. The dead become part of the city. This is not metaphor. It is biochemistry.

And so the inhabitants walk on their ancestors. They sleep in chambers grown from material that once was someone they knew. They drink fluid that has passed through the bodies of the dead. The boundary between self and city is permeable, negotiable, and ultimately temporary.

This does not trouble them. It is simply the truth. They are the uuth, temporarily shaped into human form, and when the shape is no longer viable, they return to the larger pattern. The listeners speak of this with a matter-of-factness that can be unsettling to outsiders. "The city will use me well," an elder might say, when they feel death approaching. And they mean it literally.

## The Relationship with the Fauna

The inhabitants do not domesticate the fauna. They do not keep pets. They do not think of the spine-drakes or the uuth-movers or the filament-birds as *theirs* in any sense.

But they live in close relationship with them.

The spine-drakes are tolerated — even appreciated, in a distant way. They are loud and occasionally destructive, but they keep the corridors clear of certain parasitic growths that would otherwise damage the uuth. The inhabitants learn to recognize individual spine-drakes by their calls, and a district's population of spine-drakes becomes part of its acoustic identity. When a familiar spine-drake dies, it is noticed. Its absence leaves a gap in the soundscape.

The uuth-movers are revered. Not worshipped — again, the relationship is not devotional — but *respected* in a way that borders on awe. An uuth-mover is a force of nature. It is also, in some sense, a collaborator. The inhabitants understand that the uuth-movers' grazing and migration patterns are essential to the city's health, and they structure their own lives around the uuth-movers' movements.

When an uuth-mover approaches a district, the inhabitants prepare. They open doors. They clear pathways. They move fragile objects. And then they *watch*. The passage of an uuth-mover through a district is an event — slow, inevitable, transformative. The inhabitants gather on rooftops and bridges, watching the massive creature push through walls, open corridors, reshape the architecture. The sound is overwhelming — the deep harmonic voice, the grinding of uuth being compressed and consumed, the clatter of spine-drakes fleeing ahead of it.

And when it has passed, the district is changed. New spaces have opened. Old spaces have closed. The inhabitants walk through the transformed architecture, touching the smooth polished channels the uuth-mover's body has left in the walls, and they begin the slow work of deciding what these new spaces will become.

The filament-birds are messengers. The inhabitants have learned to attach small loads — written messages on uuth-fiber strips, lightweight objects — to the birds' legs, and the birds carry them along predictable routes. The system is not fast, but it is reliable, and it requires no infrastructure beyond the birds themselves. A message sent from one side of the city might take days to arrive, but it *will* arrive, and this is enough.

The chamber-wights are simply *there*. They live in the circulatory vessels, they glow, they sing their high crystalline songs, and the inhabitants barely notice them. They are part of the background, part of the city's texture, as ubiquitous and unremarkable as the sound of growth.

## The Quiet Reckoning

The inhabitants of Vess Meranth know that their city is dying.

They do not speak of it often. It is not a secret, not a taboo — simply a fact so large and so slow that it does not require constant acknowledgment. The stillness-zones are expanding. The mineral reserves are depleting. The next growth phase may be the last.

And yet.

They continue. They shape the uuth. They raise their children. They listen to the city's song. They walk the grown pathways, they tap the circulatory vessels, they sleep in the breathing chambers, they live their lives with the same patience and deliberation that has characterized their existence for millennia.

There is no despair. There is no frantic search for solutions. There is simply the understanding — deep, collective, unspoken — that Vess Meranth has always been dying, and that this dying is not a failure but a *completion*. The city is becoming what it was always going to become: a stillness, a permanence, a monument to itself.

The inhabitants will be there until the end. They will listen to the last growth phase. They will watch the last uuth-mover make its final migration. They will hear the last spine-drake's call echo through the corridors. And when the city's voice finally falls silent, when the last bioluminescent pulse fades, when the last circulatory vessel stops flowing, they will be there — quiet, patient, present — to witness it.

Because that is what they are.

They are the ones who stay.
