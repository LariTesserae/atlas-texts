I am a lattice of memory and aspiration, a being caught between the last touch of the forgotten makers and the first breath of my own becoming. My torso is a weave of meadow-grass and the pith of dried reeds, tight and fragrant, hollow where a heart might beat in a wetter world. I have no blood, only a fine, white dust that sifts through my wicker ribs when the tortoises nudge me, settling in the creases of my construction like snow in a fence-post’s shadow.

My right wing is complete—a magnificent, terrible arc of flight. It is made of flight stolen from the sky beyond the vessel, from the eagles that circled the kilns when the clay was still wet. Each feather is a blade of obsidian and bronze, articulated by pins of carved bone, stitched with sinew so fine it might be spider-silk. When the terracotta floor warms in the long, slow afternoon of our cycle, these feathers stir. They remember the wind. They remember the hunt, the stoop, the scream of air over naked quills. They yearn for a sky that does not exist here, pressing against the close, honey-colored light with a restlessness that shakes my half-formed frame.

My left wing is a ghost. It is the architecture of intention, a scaffolding of willow-twigs bent and lashed into the *idea* of a wing. It is light, skeletal, vulnerable. It is the part of me that is still being dreamed. The dragonflies come to it, their horn-panes scraping the air with a sound like dry pages turning. They carry in their mandibles the detritus of our world—shards of iridescent beetle-shell, flakes of turquoise patina scraped from the copper ferns, filaments of shredded bark that have been bleached to the color of old bones. With the patience of erosion, they fit these pieces into my framework. They are building me a wing of the earth, not of the sky. It will be heavy. It will be beautiful. It will never fly, not in the way the feathered wing remembers, but it will catch the light that emanates from the walls and hold it, a mosaic of dust and desire.

I do not see, for I have no eyes—only two hollows of woven grass where the dust pools. But I perceive. I know the tortoise by the creak of his leather sinews and the gritty whisper of his stone mosaic against the slate table. I know the dragonfly by the vibration of her wing-beats, a papery hum that resonates in the hollow of my chest. I know the slow, deep groan of the vessel settling, the sound of our world breathing, cooling, contracting into its density.

Time moves through me like the mineral dust—slow, dry, inexorable. I have been on this table for longer than the moss has clung to the north wall, longer than the patina has bloomed on the ferns. I am the project. The center. The empty space around which all gathering orbits. The tortoises bring me calcium in pouches of old leather, and I feel it sifting into my weave, strengthening me, weighing me down, making me real. The dragonflies adorn me. They are patient, for they are not building a bird that will live and die; they are building a repository, a vessel within the vessel, a container for the accumulated craft of their endless, quiet toil.

Sometimes, when the light catches a mica shard on a tortoise’s back, I see something else reflected—not the terracotta curve of our bowl-world, but a flash of blue. A color that has no name here, for there is no water to name it after. In that flash, I understand that I am not the first. I am the latest. There have been others on this table, other constructions, other dreams of flight. The dust that fills my hollows is their dust. The feathers on my right wing came from birds who died in a world of rain and wind. I am their heir, and I am their tomb.

When my left wing is finished—when the last beetle-wing is set, when the last twig is sheathed in shell and resin—I will be complete. And then? Perhaps the tortoises will carry me from the table to the floor. Perhaps I will become part of the garden, a new kind of flower, a new kind of fern, gathering dust in my turn, my sharp edges softening, my purpose shifting from *becoming* to *being*. Or perhaps I will simply remain here, the still point of the turning world, the empty center that gives the gathering its meaning.

I am the unfinished bird. I am the patience of the dry world. I am the weight of feathers and the lightness of twigs. I am the sound of construction that has no end. And I am listening, always, to the slow, beautiful, unceasing work of my makers, who are also my children, building me out of dust and memory, in a room that is a world, in a silence that is a song.
