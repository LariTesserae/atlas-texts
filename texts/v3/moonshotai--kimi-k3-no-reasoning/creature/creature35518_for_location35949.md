**The Litharch**

The mountain does not know its own shape yet, but something inside it does.

They are called Litharchs by the few geologists who have charted their migrations, though the creatures do not call themselves anything at all. They are the mountain’s immune system, or its dreaming flesh, or perhaps its custodians—vast, slow, patient as pressure.

You will not see them often. They move through the scree the way whales move through deep water, their bodies—thirty, forty meters of dark, articulated stone—indistinguishable from the unstable ridges until they shift, and a hillside decides to walk. They are silicon-carbon composites, biological in their growth, geological in their substance. Their blood is liquid silica, hot enough to glow in the dark, viscous as pitch. Their eyes are clusters of crystalline sensors, sensitive to magnetism and the vibration of distant explosives.

They live in the transition zones, the places where the magma meets the frost line. Here, in the perpetual wet and cold, they graze. They eat the mountain, or rather, they eat the machines that eat the mountain. A Litharch’s gut is a crucible, a grinding mill lined with diamond teeth and acid baths. They ingest the rusted crawlers, the abandoned drills, the seismic charges left too long in the ground. They digest steel and concrete, extracting the trace minerals, the iron, the copper, the rare earths. What they excrete is a fine, fertile dust—a black, nutrient-rich sand that spills from their undersides as they move, falling into the tarns and the meltwater streams.

This is the symbiosis: the machines bring the heat and the disturbance, cracking the virgin stone; the Litharchs follow, consuming the industrial debris, metabolizing the artificial into the organic. Their waste feeds the black lichens, accelerates the growth of the pale mosses. Where a Litharch has passed, the biological explosion follows—a temporary oasis of soft green in the gray void, a patch of half-alive vegetation thriving on the recycled exhaust and iron filings. The herds of six-limbed grazers follow these trails, knowing that the Litharch’s leavings mean richer moss, warmer ground.

They are old in a young place. They began as lichens themselves, perhaps, or as colonies of thermophilic bacteria that learned to armor themselves against the machines. Over decades, over centuries of this slow collision between geology and industry, they have grown vast. They reproduce by fission when they reach critical mass—a Litharch will find a warm vent, split down the center like a cooling basalt flow, and two smaller versions will crawl away, already hungry.

They are not hostile. They are not even aware of you, not really. Their sensory apparatus is tuned to seismic frequencies, to the groan of the rising peaks, to the electrical hum of the crawlers. A human is a thermal anomaly, a brief spike of warmth in the infrared spectrum, unimportant. But if you stand too close when one decides to roll over in its sleep—if you are caught between its grinding plates when it shifts its weight to follow the ore vein—you will become part of the mountain, incorporated into its black blood.

In the diurnal panic of freeze and thaw, they are the constants. The machines move on, following the thermal gradients; the herds migrate vertically; the water changes state a thousand times a day. But the Litharchs remain, moving slowly upward as the mountain rises, always staying just below the frost line, just above the molten heart. They are the living crust, the slow hands that smooth the broken teeth of the ridges, that consolidate the scree, that turn the chaos of construction into something that might, eventually, become soil.

You will know one is near when the mist smells of ozone and hot metal, when the ground vibrates with a subsonic hum deeper than the machines, when you find a pool of water that is warm to the touch and filled with tiny, glittering crystals—Litharch spawn, suspended in the thermal column, waiting to harden into stone.

They are the mountain’s attempt to understand itself, to incorporate the foreign bodies that drill into its sides. They are the end of the process: the moment when the industrial and the geological become indistinguishable, when the machine is eaten and becomes flesh, when the raw, wet, cold emptiness finally begins to clot into life.
