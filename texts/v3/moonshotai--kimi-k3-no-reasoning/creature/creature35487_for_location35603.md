The Verdant Maw is not an ecosystem. It is a body, and we are all inside its mouth.

You will understand this when you realize the horizon is a gum-line. When you look across the flatness, you are seeing the soft palate curve away, dissolving not into distance but into the humid darkness of a throat. The water is saliva—bacterial, enzyme-rich, patient. The heat is breath. And the stones, those bruise-colored molars rising from the black sponge of the delta, are not stones at all. They are teeth, worn smooth by three hundred million years of chewing on nothing, on everything, on the slow bolus of time itself.

We are what lives in the Maw. We are the flora and fauna of the oral cavity, adapted to wetness and darkness, to the pressure of a tongue that has not yet decided to swallow.

**The Skin of the World**

First, there is the **Bloom**—the living surface that coats every submerged tooth and makes the black water slick as the inside of a cheek. You cannot see it as a separate entity; it is the color of bruises, the texture of wet velvet, the smell of sweetness turning. It is not one species but a confluence: cyanobacteria that have forgotten sunlight, photosynthesizing heat itself; fungal hyphae that thread through the peat like nerves; a single, continent-wide slime mold that pulses with a chemical intelligence, slow and thorough.

The Bloom digests. It does not hurry. It exudes a mucilaginous film that turns fallen fronds into translucent jelly within a decade, that coats the eyes of the unwary with a film of loving decay. To walk on the delta is to walk on the Bloom’s back, feeling it give slightly beneath your weight, exhaling methane ghosts that are not ghosts at all but the breath of its metabolism. It is the world’s tongue, and it tastes everything that touches it.

**The Teeth**

The cycads are not trees. They are **incisors**—vertical, rooted in the jawbone of the bedrock, their armored trunks the enamel of ancient grinding surfaces. Each one is a colony, a city. In the reservoirs of their crowns—where the fronds meet in a dripping basin—live the **Siphons**, translucent amphibians with external gills like red feathers. They have never touched the ground. They are born in the tree-cups, feed on the mosquito-larvae that hatch in the stored water, and die without ever knowing the earth exists.

The horsetails are different. They are **whiskers**, sensory bristles rising from the Maw’s lips. Hollow and jointed, they resonate when the wind moves, producing a low, fluting tone that carries for miles through the dense air. The creatures of the Maw use them as we use telegraph wires. When the **Flat-Faced Ones**—the great amphibians of the mudflats—wish to speak across the miles, they press their throats against the base of a horsetail and croak. The stalk vibrates, the sound carries, and in the next acre, another Flat-Face turns its algae-green head, slow as a landslide deciding to fall.

**The Roaring**

The frogs are the Maw’s **voice**. They are not individual creatures but the vocal cords of the place itself, millions of throats inflated to the size of hearts, synchronizing their calls into a white noise that has no beginning and no end. This is not communication. It is the sound of the Maw existing, the way a sleeper’s breath is the sound of lungs being lungs.

The frogs come in sizes that defy categorization. The **Bass Frogs** are the foundation—vast, sluggish, their bodies flattened into discs that adhere to the submerged stones. Their calls are felt rather than heard, a subsonic throb that makes the water ripple in perfect circles. Above them, the **Tenor Swarms**—frogs no larger than a thumb—fill the middle register, a million clicks and whistles that merge into a shimmering curtain of sound. And at the top, the **Screamers**, bright yellow, poisonous, their voices sharp enough to cut through the humidity and startle the pterosaurs from their circling.

The pterosaurs are the Maw’s **eyelids**. They circle the thermals not to hunt but to regulate the light, their leathery wings casting moving shadows that cool the mud in patterns the Bloom responds to, growing faster in the shade. They are patient creatures, living on a timescale that matches the delta’s own. A pterosaur may circle for a decade without landing, feeding on the wing, snatching dragonflies from the air with a snap that sounds like a whipcrack in the stillness.

**The Swimmers**

In the channels, the water is thick with **Armored Ones**—fish that have not changed since the Devonian because the Maw has not changed. They are long and cylindrical, plated in enamel that matches the cycads’, their eyes reduced to pinpricks that sense only heat and vibration. They do not swim so much as they *persist*, gliding through the black water with a movement that seems to argue against the concept of speed. When they surface to gulp air—and they must, for the water is nearly anoxic—they create the great splashes that echo for a minute, rebounding off the flatness, announcing that something is still alive in the soup.

Beneath them, in the peat, the **Root-Eels** move through the soil as if it were water. They are blind, boneless, and ancient, their bodies segmented like the horsetails, their mouths ringed with teeth that grind the fossilizing wood into a paste that the Bloom then absorbs. They are the recyclers, the undertakers, the slow machinery of the Maw’s digestion.

**The Watchers**

On the mudflats, where the water meets the air in a gradient so weak it is more suggestion than boundary, lie the **Sentient Flat-Faces**. They are the size of crocodiles but have no armor, only skin—photosynthetic, green, slick with algae that the Bloom cultivates in exchange for their stillness. They lie motionless for days, for years, becoming part of the landscape, their backs rising like green islands from the black mud.

They are not asleep. Their eyes—wide, golden, positioned on the top of their heads—are open. They are watching. They see in thermal gradients, tracking the heat of your body through the humid air, feeling the vibration of your footsteps through the saturated earth. When they move, it is with the suddenness of a bite. The Maw closes, and the Flat-Face has fed.

**The Buzzing**

The dragonflies are the Maw’s **nervous system**. They are the size of hawks, their wingspans wide enough to cast shadows that cool the mud, their eyes covering their heads in a mosaic of lenses that see the world in gradients of heat and moisture. They are everywhere, stitching the air together with their flight, their wings creating a bass hum that underlies the frogs, a sound so constant that you forget it exists until it stops—when the pterosaurs dive, when the Flat-Faces strike, when the Maw holds its breath.

They hunt the smaller insects, the sparrow-sized beetles that drone between the cycads, their carapaces iridescent with the oils of the Bloom. But they also serve as messengers, landing on the backs of the Flat-Faces to deliver chemical cues—tastes of the air, samples of the humidity—that keep the great amphibians informed of the delta’s slow shifts.

**The Rules of Residence**

To live in the Maw is to accept the law of **saturation**. Nothing is dry. Nothing is hidden. Every creature leaves a trail in the scent-heavy air—a musk of algae and blood, a signature of fear or fertility. The Bloom tastes these trails, remembers them, responds to them. The Flat-Faces follow them, slow as erosion. The dragonflies read them like braille.

Time here is not linear but **stratigraphic**. You measure your life in layers of peat, in the rings of the cycad trunks, in the slow migration of the pools. The creatures of the Maw do not age so much as they *sediment*, growing thicker skins, heavier armor, slower metabolisms. The Bass Frogs have heartbeats that sync with the tides of the water table, one beat per hour, a drum that marks the passage of geological time.

And yet, for all its slowness, the Maw is not static. It is **digesting**. The teeth are wearing down. The Bloom is thickening. The water is rising, imperceptibly, as the jaw of the world slowly closes. In a million years, the cycads will be coal. The Flat-Faces will be oil. The frogs will be silence.

But for now, there is the roaring. The wet, bacterial warmth. The sound of a world that has finished becoming and now simply *is*—open, wet, and waiting to swallow.
