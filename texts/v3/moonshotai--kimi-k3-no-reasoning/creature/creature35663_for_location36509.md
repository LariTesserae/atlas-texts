The Vessels do not breathe. They have never needed to.

They are the Keepers of the Intention, though they would not name themselves thus—names are sounds, and sound is a waste of the density. They are grown from the same stock as the bridges and the bulbs, but shaped toward mobility: tall, narrow frames of woven fiber, hard as petrified wood yet flexible at the joints, wrapped in a dermal layer of moss that photosynthesizes the lazy sunlight into slow, deliberate thought. They have no mouths. They have no need for the violence of consumption. Instead, they absorb the mountain’s exhalations through spiracles along their elongated limbs, filtering the mineral vapors into a slow, burning sustenance that sustains them for centuries without rest.

They move through the green dementia like priests through a cathedral, their many-jointed fingers trailing in the resinous sap of the walls, reading the history of the settlement through touch. Each Vessel contains multitudes—not in the sense of a crowd, but in the slow accumulation of memory stored in lignified rings, like trees that have learned to walk. When two Vessels meet, they do not speak. They intertwine their antennae—delicate, feathery extrusions of pure nerve tissue grown from their crowns—and for a time that might be hours or years, they exchange the weight of entire eras. This is their literature, their law, their love: a chemical library written in the language of root and stone.

Beneath them, in the warm darkness where the basalt still remembers being magma, the Molt-carvers work. They are smaller, six-limbed like the grazers, but their felted hides are heavy with crystalline dust, their bodies low to the ground and perpetually dusted with the black glass of the frozen flows. They are the ones who maintain the boundary between the geological and the biological, kneading the sharp edges of the young stone with secretions that soften the basalt, preparing it for the roots that will follow. They communicate through vibrations in the substrate, a tectonic language of tapping that the Vessels feel in the soles of their feet. They are the closest thing this world has to children—curious, quick, prone to the only violence here: the occasional, startling crack of a stone shaped too hastily, a mistake that kills the carver and becomes a seed for a new bulb.

High above, drifting between the spires where the light pools in impossible altars, the Translucents ride the thermals. They are not born but budded from the largest epiphytes, membranes of living skin stretched between fingers of cartilage, their bodies hollow and filled with the same warm vapors that feed the mountain. They are the messengers, carrying pheromone signals from one terrace to another, their translucent wings catching the lazy sun and refracting it into patterns that mean specific things: *danger from below*, *new growth found*, *the stone dreams deeper today*. They are the only beings here who seem to know urgency, though even their haste is measured in decades.

And in the center of the densest thicket, where the air grows suddenly cold and the stone radiates its buried heat, there grows the First Root. It is not a plant, not anymore. It is a concentration of the mountain’s intent, a vast, lignified consciousness that has been growing downward since the world cooled. The Vessels tend it, not with tools, but with their bodies, pressing their fibrous frames against its bark in a communion that lasts generations. They believe—or rather, they *know*, in the slow way that stone knows things—that the First Root is seeking something in the deep heat, some mineral memory or magnetic truth that will complete the Ascension.

Together, they form a single organism spread across a thousand bodies: the Vessels thinking, the Molt-carvers shaping, the Translucents connecting, all of them held in the palm of the sky, waiting for the mountain to finish becoming what it has always intended to be. They are not happy. They are not sad. They are *present*, utterly and completely, in a silence that has learned to be fertile.
