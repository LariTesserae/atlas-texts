# Who Lives in the Undertank

Start with the hands, because that's where they start with themselves. When a child is born in Kettle-Hollow — and birth happens in a shallow alcove near the basin, in water, always in water, because the people believe the body should never fully leave the medium it will spend its life maintaining — the first thing the attending hands-keeper does is press the infant's palms into a pat of fresh composite. The print is left to cure. It goes into the wall. Not symbolically. Actually into the wall, in a section near the birthing alcove that has become, over centuries, a dense palimpsest of handprints layered over handprints, the smallest ones barely larger than a frog's splayed foot, the composite there so packed with the record of arrivals that it has a different texture from the rest of the chamber — denser, smoother, almost polished by the sheer accumulation of beginning.

So the hands. Broad, as noted, but that word doesn't do the work. They are *reshaped* — not through any deliberate practice of deformation but through the simple, lifelong fact of pressing. The palms are thick-callused, the fingers slightly splayed, the nails worn to smooth ovals. The skin of the hands is a different color from the skin of the rest of the body — darker, almost stained, from decades of contact with the composite's binding resins. You can tell a person's age in Kettle-Hollow by holding their hands. A child's hands are still soft, still learning. A person in their middle years has hands like the floor of the basin: firm, textured, warm. An elder's hands are like the oldest sections of wall — so worked, so pressed and re-pressed, that they have taken on a quality beyond function. They are *archives*.

The hands-keepers — and this is a real role, perhaps the closest thing Kettle-Hollow has to a priesthood — are the ones who read hands. Not for divination. For diagnosis. A hands-keeper can hold a worker's hands and tell you which section of wall they've been repairing, what stage of cure the composite was in, whether the mixture was too wet or too dry, whether the worker is tired (the hands get slightly cooler when the body is depleted), whether the worker is ill (a particular smell on the palms, metallic and wrong, that precedes fever by several days). The hands-keepers also tend the birth-wall, pressing new prints, reading old ones, maintaining the alcove where the composite is softest and most receptive.

But the hands-keepers are not leaders. Nothing so simple.

## The Question of Authority

Authority in Kettle-Hollow is a matter of *relevance*, and it shifts the way the light-veins shift: slowly, continuously, in response to conditions. There is no chief. There is no council. There are people who know things, and when the things they know become pertinent, they become, for the duration of that pertinence, the person others listen to.

When the ventilation hum changes key — when the standing tone produced by air moving through the shafts drops a quarter-tone or develops a waver — the ear-keepers step forward. These are people (usually older, because the skill takes decades to refine, though occasionally a young person is born with the gift and is recognized early) who can hear the state of the air the way you might hear a chord and know instantly that one note is sour. They direct the adjustment of baffles, the opening and closing of flues, the repositioning of the clay organs that shape the airflow. While they are doing this work, their word carries weight. When the tone is restored, they go back to whatever they were doing — harvesting lichen, mending nets, teaching children the frog-calls — and someone else becomes relevant.

When the frogs begin to spawn, the frog-watchers become central. These are often younger people — the work requires patience and stillness more than strength, and the young are better at stillness than the old, who have accumulated too much restlessness. The frog-watchers sit by the basin for hours, motionless, reading the water, noting which species are spawning where, tracking egg masses, and — critically — determining when the vibration-sensitive period has passed and wall-repair work can resume. A frog-watcher's call — a specific whistle, three notes, descending — can halt all construction in the chamber for days. Nobody questions this. The frogs are essential. The chain is understood.

When someone falls ill, the smell-readers come forward. When the light-veins shift noticeably, the light-mappers convene. When a crack appears in the composite — a real crack, not the hairline crazing that is normal and even healthy, but a structural fissure that you can fit a fingernail into — the wall-readers are summoned, and their authority is absolute until the crack is stabilized, and if a wall-reader says a section of the floor must not be walked on, it is not walked on, and if a wall-reader says a section of ceiling must be reinforced immediately, work begins immediately, and if a wall-reader puts their ear to the composite and then pulls back and says a word — a specific word, low and round, that means roughly *this is beyond us* — then the chamber goes quiet, truly quiet, for the first time anyone can remember, and the people gather at the basin and sit in the shallow water and wait.

This has happened twice in living memory. Both times, the wall healed itself — the composite, being slightly alive (not metaphorically; the binding agent contains colonies of microorganisms that continue to metabolize, slowly, over centuries, producing the warmth and the faint chemical activity that keeps the material pliable), flowed into the fissure and sealed it. Both times, the people marked the event by pressing their hands into the healed section, a wall of gratitude that is also a wall of data: *here the world broke and here it mended*.

## The People Individually

They are not a type. This must be said clearly, because the temptation in describing a closed, bounded, ancient community is to render them as uniform — a culture rather than a collection of persons. They are persons. They differ from one another as much as any group of people who share space and history and genetic material and an environment that shapes them all in similar ways.

There is, for instance, a woman — middle-aged by the reckoning of the frog-cycles, which is how they count time: not years, which mean nothing without seasons, but *cycles*, the great wheeling round of spawning and hatching and metamorphosis that turns roughly (though not exactly, and the inexactness is itself a subject of ongoing calibration) with what the surface world would call a year — who is the most skilled net-weaver in the chamber. Her nets, woven from reed fiber teased out of the composite's binding matrix, are so fine they are nearly invisible in the amber light, and so strong that a bird striking one at full speed is held without injury. She is proud of this. She is *known* for this. When she walks through the chamber (and she walks constantly, restlessly, covering the perimeter in a circuit she has walked so many times her path has worn a faint groove in the floor, a groove that other people unconsciously follow, so that her private habit has become a public thoroughfare), other workers glance at her hands — always moving, always teasing fiber, even while she walks, even while she talks — and there is respect in the glance, and also a kind of wariness, because she is not easy. She is exacting. She has opinions about the correct way to do everything, not just net-weaving, and she shares these opinions freely, loudly, in a voice that carries above the general din with a penetrating quality that the ear-keepers have studied with professional interest.

She has a particular feud with one of the light-mappers — a man, older, quiet, who spends his time lying on his back on the floor (he has shaped a shallow depression in the composite for this purpose, a body-mold that fits him the way the basin fits the water) staring at the ceiling, tracking the migration of the light-veins with a patience that she finds maddening. *You can't eat light*, she tells him, regularly, sometimes daily. He does not respond. He has not responded in decades. This is the nature of their relationship: she prods, he absorbs, and the prodding and the absorbing have become a ritual as structural to the social fabric as the baffles are to the ventilation. When she dies — and the people of Kettle-Hollow do die, though they live long, the humid warmth and the constant low-level physical activity and the lichen-based diet conspiring to produce lifespans that would be remarkable on the surface — when she dies, he will miss the prodding the way the chamber would miss a drip. The silence where her voice was will be an alarm.

There is a boy — perhaps ten cycles old — who has been identified by the ear-keepers as having the gift. He can hear the standing tone's individual harmonics, can separate the note produced by the eastern shaft from the note produced by the western, can detect a blockage in a ventilation channel before it becomes audible as a change in the overall hum. The ear-keepers are training him, but he is resistant. He wants to be a frog-watcher. He sits by the basin whenever he can escape his lessons, watching the water with a longing that the frog-watchers recognize and the ear-keepers tolerate with a mixture of amusement and concern. He will likely end up doing both. Most people in Kettle-Hollow do more than one thing. Specialization exists but is not rigid; the chamber is too small, too interconnected, for anyone to be only one thing.

There is an old woman who is the primary keeper of the composting niches — the alcoves set into the walls at floor level where organic waste (food scraps, dead animals, shed skin, molted carapaces, human waste, everything, *everything* is composted; in a closed system there is no such thing as waste) is layered with specific proportions of fungal spores and crushed shell and left to decompose in a managed process that generates both warmth and a rich, dark soil-like substrate that is used to grow the edible fungi. This work is essential and it is not prestigious. It is smelly. It involves close contact with decay. The old woman does it with a matter-of-fact competence that has, over the decades, acquired a kind of moral authority. She is not an ear-keeper or a light-mapper or a wall-reader, but when she speaks — rarely, in a voice so low that you must lean close to hear it, a voice that has been shaped by decades of working in the niches where the air is thick with spores — people listen. She has opinions about the chamber's overall health that are grounded in the most fundamental data available: the rate and quality of decomposition. *The compost tells the truth*, she says, which is as close to a philosophical statement as Kettle-Hollow produces.

## The Children

Children in Kettle-Hollow are everywhere and underfoot, which is not a complaint but a description of a deliberate strategy. In a closed system of this size, the population must be maintained within a narrow range — too few and the labor of maintenance cannot be sustained; too many and the food web is strained, the composting niches overwhelmed, the air itself (calibrated for a specific respiratory load) thrown off balance. Population control is achieved through a combination of biological timing (conception is deliberately aligned with the frog-cycles, which means births cluster in certain periods) and social practice (sexual mores are flexible and non-procreative pairings are common and unremarkable) and, when necessary, through a practice that the people discuss openly but perform privately: the returning.

The returning is what it sounds like. A body — infant, elder, anyone whose continued presence would strain the system or whose suffering has exceeded its meaning — is returned to the composite. Not buried. *Returned*. Pressed into a prepared section of wall, covered with fresh material, smoothed. The body decomposes within the composite, feeding the microorganisms that keep the binding agent alive, contributing its warmth and chemistry to the ongoing cure. The wall where the returned are placed is known. It is slightly warmer than the other walls. The people touch it as they pass — casually, without ceremony, the way you might touch a doorframe.

Children learn this early. They are not shielded from it. A child of four or five cycles will watch a returning with the same attention they give to a frog's metamorphosis, and the questions they ask — practical, direct, unfrightened — are answered in the same register. *Where did she go? Into the wall. Is she still there? Yes. Can I touch her? Yes. Does it hurt? No.*

The children play — of course they play; they are children — and their games are shaped by the environment. They play at pressing composite, making small, useless, wonderful shapes that are left to cure on ledges and in corners, so that the chamber is dotted with the artifacts of childhood: lumpy frog-figures, crude bowls, handprints within handprints, a wall near the birthing alcove that has become a sort of gallery of small makings, added to over generations, some of the objects so old that the composite has begun to absorb them, the shapes softening, losing definition, becoming part of the wall's grain.

They play at hunting — stalking the smaller crustaceans with cupped hands, learning the patience and the quickness that will later serve them in the bird-snaring and the frog-monitoring. They play at listening — sitting in a circle, eyes closed, trying to identify the individual sounds in the chamber's soundscape: *that drip is the eastern channel, that croak is the russet frog, that clatter is the grinding-stone near the basin's edge*. The ear-keepers sometimes join these games and are, to the children's delight, occasionally beaten by a particularly sharp-eared five-cycle-old.

## What They Eat

The lichen is the staple. It grows in slow patches on the walls, thickest on the northern curve where the seepage is steadiest, and is harvested by scraping with a flat stone tool, a motion so practiced that the harvesters can do it without looking, their eyes on the ceiling or the basin or each other, their hands reading the lichen's texture to determine readiness. It is eaten fresh — a thin, silvery paste with the taste described earlier: nothing, then everything — and dried, ground, mixed with crustacean powder, pressed into flat cakes that are stored in the cool, drier sections of wall (relative coolness, relative dryness; nothing in Kettle-Hollow is truly cool or dry) and that keep for many cycles.

The crustaceans are the protein. The larger ones — hand-sized, armored, slow — are caught by hand or with the fine nets, killed quickly (a practiced twist that severs the nerve cluster; the people are not cruel, or rather, they have defined cruelty as *slowness in killing*, which they consider a failure of skill and therefore a kind of disrespect for the animal), and eaten whole: shell cracked, flesh extracted, shell saved for grinding. The smaller crustaceans are eaten like the lichen — scraped up, swallowed, barely noticed. A child sitting by the basin will idly scoop a handful of the speck-sized ones from the water's edge and eat them the way you might eat seeds, without ceremony or even much attention.

The frogs are eaten, but sparingly. Their ecological role is too important — too visibly, immediately important — for them to be a primary food source. When a frog is eaten, it is an occasion. The frog-watchers determine which individuals can be taken (old ones, past breeding, or ones that are injured and unlikely to survive) and the taking is done with a specific ritual — not religious, exactly, but *acknowledging*. The person who takes the frog holds it for a moment, looks at it, says a word. The word is old. Nobody remembers what it originally meant. It is simply *the word you say to the frog*.

The birds are eaten more freely — they reproduce quickly, they are less ecologically critical than the frogs (though they are not unimportant; nothing here is unimportant), and they are, by all accounts, delicious. Dark meat, rich, with a mineral undertone from their diet of crustaceans. The net-weaver's birds are considered the best, because her nets catch without bruising, and a bruised bird is a diminished bird.

The fungi from the composting niches — the edible ones, carefully selected and cultivated over generations from the wild profusion of fungal life in the chamber — provide variety and certain nutrients that the lichen lacks. They are dense, meaty, with a flavor that the old compost-keeper describes as *the taste of the walls*, which is accurate: they are grown from the same materials, the same chemistry, the same slow processes. Eating them is, in a very literal sense, eating the architecture.

Water is drunk from the basin, from specific collection points where the seepage is cleanest. It is warm — everything here is warm — and tastes faintly of the composite, a mineral roundness that is not unpleasant. The people have never tasted cold water. They would find it alarming.

## What They Know and What They Don't

They know the chamber. This sounds obvious but the depth of it is worth stating: they know every centimeter of the composite's surface, every seepage channel, every scallop in the ceiling, every niche and alcove and ledge. They know where the light-veins run and where they are migrating. They know the population of every animal species to within a few individuals. They know the sound of the place so intimately that a single changed note — a drip that has shifted rhythm, a hum that has developed a waver — is registered by dozens of people simultaneously, a communal flinch, a collective turning of attention.

They know the composite — how to make it, how to shape it, how to read its condition, how to repair it, how long it takes to cure at different thicknesses and temperatures and humidities. This knowledge is not written down. It is carried in the hands, in the body, in the practices that are passed from elder to child through decades of side-by-side work. The knowledge is not separate from the people who hold it. If all the wall-readers died simultaneously — an event so unlikely as to be essentially impossible, but consider it — the knowledge would be gone, and the chamber would, eventually, begin to fail.

They know their history, but in a particular way. They do not have writing. What they have is the wall — the birth-wall with its handprints, the returning-wall with its warmth, the gallery of children's makings, the sections that have been repaired and the sections that are original and the sections where the composite is so old it has begun to flow, slow as thought, reshaping itself in ways that the people observe and discuss and interpret. The wall is their archive, and they read it the way you might read a landscape: not for facts but for *character*, for the story that is embedded in the material's condition.

They know there is an above. The stair goes up. The slab at the top is removed on occasion — for trade, though trade has become rare in recent generations (what comes down: the soft metal tools, a few other items, increasingly little; what goes up: crustacean powder, dried lichen, the fine nets, which are valued above for qualities the people below find amusing — their lightness, their delicacy, things that down here are simply practical). They know the above is different — drier, brighter, colder, *open* — and this knowledge produces not longing but a kind of mild, distant curiosity, the way you might feel about a country you have no intention of visiting.

Some of them have gone up. Not many. A few in each generation — the curious, the restless, the ones who for whatever reason do not fit the chamber's particular shape. Some return. Some don't. The ones who return bring back information that is received with interest and then, mostly, set aside: *the sky is large. The air is thin. There is a light so bright it hurts. The water is cold.* These facts are noted. They do not change anything. The chamber is sufficient.

What they don't know: where the composite technology came from. Who built the first wall. How the light-veins were originally prepared. Whether the harder, darker material beyond the composite's thickness is natural or made. What lies beyond *that*. Whether there are other chambers, other hollows, other kettles in the dark. They have theories — the light-mappers have theories, the wall-readers have theories, the compost-keeper has a theory she does not share — but theories here are not beliefs. They are *held lightly*, the way you hold a frog: carefully, attentively, ready to let go.

## The Texture of a Life

A life in Kettle-Hollow has a particular shape, determined by the environment's constraints and affordances.

It begins in water and it is, throughout, *wet*. The people are never dry. Their skin is always slightly slick, their hair always damp, their few garments always moist. They do not notice this. Dryness would be the anomaly, and an alarming one — dry skin is a sign of illness, a symptom that the smell-readers and hands-keepers are trained to detect.

It is *loud*. From birth to returning, the soundscape is constant and dense. The people are comfortable in noise the way surface people are comfortable in silence. They talk over the din, through it, with it. Their language has evolved to be audible in this environment — percussive, full of sharp consonants and carrying tones, with a rhythm that syncs, deliberately or not, with the drip-patterns and the ventilation hum. They sing, but their singing is not melodic in the way surface music is melodic. It is *textural* — layers of sound that blend with and complement the ambient noise, adding to it, enriching it, the way a frog's call adds to the soundscape without standing apart from it. A song in Kettle-Hollow is not a performance. It is a contribution to the total sound. Everyone sings. Not formally — there are no concerts, no audiences — but constantly, casually, while working, while walking, while sitting by the basin. The singing is as much a part of the soundscape as the dripping and the croaking and the hum.

It is *close*. There is no privacy in the sense that surface people understand privacy — no separate rooms, no doors, no spaces that are exclusively one's own. The alcoves and niches provide a degree of physical separation, and there are conventions — unwritten, universally observed — about when a person's alcove is to be treated as private. But these conventions are about *attention* rather than *access*. You do not watch someone who is sleeping, even though you can see them. You do not listen to a conversation that is not meant for you, even though you can hear it. Privacy in Kettle-Hollow is a practice, not a place.

This closeness produces a particular social texture: dense, warm, occasionally frictionful. People know each other completely — not just their names and roles but their smells, their sounds, the specific quality of their footstep on the composite floor, the rhythm of their breathing during sleep. Conflict exists — of course it exists; they are human — but it is managed differently than on the surface. You cannot storm off. There is nowhere to storm off *to*. You cannot avoid someone. The chamber is too small. So conflict is addressed — not always immediately, not always cleanly, but *addressed*, because the alternative is a kind of pressure that the system cannot sustain. The ear-keepers, in addition to their acoustic duties, often serve as informal mediators, because they are trained to hear discord, and social discord is, in their framework, not so different from a sour note in the ventilation hum: a signal that something needs adjusting.

It is *purposeful*. There is no idleness in Kettle-Hollow — not because idleness is forbidden (nothing is forbidden; the concept of prohibition barely exists) but because there is always something to do, and the doing is satisfying in the way that necessary work is satisfying when it is also skilled work, work that engages the hands and the senses and the accumulated knowledge of the body. The people do not experience their labor as toil. They experience it as *participation* — in the maintenance of the world, in the continuation of a project that has been going on for longer than anyone can remember and that will continue after they are returned to the wall.

This gives their lives a quality that is difficult to name without romanticizing, and romanticizing must be resisted, because Kettle-Hollow is not a utopia. People get sick. People die — sometimes young, sometimes badly, from infections that the smell-readers catch too late or from falls (the ceiling scallops are tempting to climb, and children fall, and sometimes the falling is the kind that ends in returning) or from the slow, undramatic failures of the body that happen everywhere. People are sometimes cruel to each other. People are sometimes selfish, sometimes lazy, sometimes wrong. The net-weaver's exactingness is sometimes hurtful. The light-mapper's silence is sometimes a kind of aggression. The boy who wants to be a frog-watcher will disappoint his ear-keeper mentors, and the disappointment will be real and will sting.

But the *shape* of their lives — the wetness, the loudness, the closeness, the purposefulness — produces a kind of person that is recognizably different from a surface person. They are more tactile. They touch each other constantly — casually, without intent, a hand on a shoulder in passing, a brief press of palm against palm in greeting, the constant incidental contact of bodies sharing a small space. They are more attentive — to sound, to smell, to the subtle shifts in their environment that signal change. They are more patient — the work of pressing composite, of watching frogs, of tracking light-veins, of waiting for cure and compost and lichen growth, selects for and reinforces a relationship with time that is slower, longer, more geological than the surface's.

And they are *content*. Not uniformly, not ecstatically, not in a way that would be recognizable as happiness on the surface. But in the way that a place is content when it has found its form — settled, sufficient, humming with a low and steady note that says: *this is what we are, and it is enough.*

## The Ones Who Stand Out

Every community has its outliers — the ones who don't quite fit the shape, who push against the walls (metaphorically; the literal walls they push against only in the course of repair work). Kettle-Hollow has its share.

There is a man — young, perhaps twenty cycles — who has been digging. Not in the composite walls, which would be normal — repair work, maintenance, the occasional exploratory probe that the wall-readers sanction and supervise. He has been digging in the *floor*, in a corner where the basin's edge meets the wall, a spot that is not structurally critical and that the wall-readers have, with some reluctance, allowed him to work. He is looking for the bottom. He wants to know how deep the composite goes, what lies beneath the floor, whether the basin has a foundation or whether it simply... continues. He has been digging for three cycles. He has reached a depth of about two meters. The composite shows no sign of thinning. The wall-readers observe his progress with professional interest and personal unease. The compost-keeper has told him, in her low voice, that he will find nothing, and when he asked how she knows, she said, *Because the compost tells the truth, and the truth is that this place goes down as far as it needs to.* He was not satisfied. He continues to dig.

There is a woman — old, very old, old enough that her cycles are no longer counted with precision — who has stopped working. This is unusual. Not unheard of — the very old are exempt from labor expectations, though most continue to work in some capacity because the work is the life — but unusual in her case because she was, until recently, one of the most active and skilled wall-readers in the chamber. She stopped without explanation. She sits now by the basin, near the spot where the boy who wants to be a frog-watcher sits, and she watches the water. She does not watch the frogs. She watches the *water itself* — the surface, the ripples, the dimples made by the water-walking insects, the slow circulation patterns produced by the seepage inflow and the evaporation and the occasional splash of a bird or a frog or a crustacean. She has been watching the water for two cycles. She does not explain what she is looking for. The other wall-readers have stopped asking. The people have, with the instinctive social intelligence of a close community, given her the space she needs — not physical space (there is none) but the space of unasked questions, of uninterpreted behavior, of letting a person be strange without making the strangeness into a problem.

She may be dying. She may be thinking. In Kettle-Hollow, the two are not always distinguishable, because thinking — real thinking, the kind that happens when a mind has been saturated with a single environment for a hundred cycles and has begun to see patterns that are not visible to younger, less saturated minds — is a slow, quiet process that looks, from the outside, very much like sitting still and watching water.

## The Visitor

You. Because you are there, standing in the amber light, damp, noticed.

They will not be afraid of you. Fear of strangers is a luxury of places with borders that can be defended and spaces that can be fled to. Kettle-Hollow has one room. You are in it. Fear would be impractical.

They will be curious — intensely, practically curious, the way they are curious about a new patch of lichen or a change in the drip-pattern. What are you? How did you get down the stair? (The slab was closed. It is always closed. Did someone open it? Why?) What do you want? (This question, asked in their wet, percussive language, will not be hostile. It will be *genuine*. They will want to know what you want the way they want to know what the light-veins want, what the frogs want, what the walls want — because everything in the chamber wants something, and understanding the wanting is the first step toward accommodating it.)

The child who was sent to observe you will have reported back by now — not to a single authority (there is none) but to *several people whose knowledge seems relevant*. An ear-keeper, because your voice, when you speak, will add a new note to the soundscape, and the ear-keeper will want to assess its impact. A wall-reader, because your weight on the floor is a new load, and the wall-reader will want to know if the composite can bear it. A smell-reader, because you smell different — of the surface, of dry air and cold water and open sky, and the smell-reader will be reading you the way they read illness: for signs of something that might affect the balance.

The net-weaver will have opinions. She will share them.

The old woman watching the water will not look up.

The boy who wants to be a frog-watcher will stare at you with the undisguised, evaluating gaze of a child who is deciding whether you are interesting or merely strange, and the distinction matters to him, because interesting things are worth watching and strange things are just... there, like the harder material beyond the walls: known, noted, not particularly relevant.

And the people — the damp, brown, broad-handed, loud, warm, purposeful, content people of Kettle-Hollow — will continue their work. The wall crew will press composite. The harvesters will scrape lichen. The grinders will grind. The frog-watchers will watch frogs. The ear-keepers will listen. The compost will decompose. The light-veins will migrate. The frogs will call. The birds will chatter. The crustaceans will patrol. The water will drip.

The hum will hold its note.

You will stand in the middle of it, and the composite will yield slightly under your feet, and the warmth will press against your skin, and the sound will fill your ears until it is not sound anymore but *medium*, the thing you are immersed in, and you will understand — not with your mind but with your body, with your damp skin and your strained ears and your softening, yielding, adjusting body — that this place is not a curiosity or a marvel or a cautionary tale. It is a *life*. Many lives. Lives that have been lived here, in this shape, for longer than memory, and that will continue, adjusting, pressing, humming, dripping, cycling, returning, beginning again, adding their fingerprints to the strata, for as long as the composite holds and the light migrates and the frogs spawn and the walls, in their slow, imperceptible, patient way, continue to be made.
