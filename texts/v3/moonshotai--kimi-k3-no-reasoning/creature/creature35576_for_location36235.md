# The Inhabitants of the Warrens

## The Elder Female

She is the oldest person in the Warrens, and she is not that old — sixty, perhaps, though no one counts. What she is, is the *most* — the most present, the most knowing, the most continuous with whatever came before. She has a name, but names in the Warrens are like the water table — they shift, they serve purposes, they are not the fixed things you might expect. The others call her something that means, roughly, *the one who hears the deepest*.

She lives in the most intact chamber of the eastern section — a space that might once have been a smokehouse, or a drying room, or a council chamber, its original function lost but its roof still solid, its walls still true. She has made it hers not through decoration (there is almost nothing that could be called decoration in the Warrens — nothing made for beauty alone, though many things are beautiful) but through *arrangement*. Objects placed where they are needed, where they make sense. Her few tools — the bronze hook-knife, a set of stone scrapers, needles of bird-bone — hang from pegs worn smooth by generations of hands reaching for them.

Her body is small and dense, like a knot in wood. Her hands are the most remarkable thing about her — broad-palmed, thick-fingered, the skin grooved with lines that are not wrinkles but *wear patterns*, the physical record of decades of knotting, lashing, weaving, shaping. When she works — and she works constantly, not at building but at *maintaining the skill of building*, practicing joints she will never need, tying lashings she will immediately untie — her hands move with a certainty that is almost painful to watch, the certainty of someone who knows that they are among the last to know this, and that the knowledge will go with them.

She is not sad about this. Sadness requires an alternative, and she has never known an alternative. The Warrens are. She is in them. That is all.

What she does — what her purpose is, if a person in the Warrens can be said to have a purpose — is listen. She sits in her chamber or on her platform or at the edge of a channel, and she listens. To the birds. To the frogs. To the otters. To the wood. She has a memory for sound that is extraordinary — she can tell you that the third heron from the north platform has changed its call, that a new frog has joined the chorus near the western wall, that a post beneath the third walkway from the southern boundary has begun to creak in a way that suggests it will fail within the year. She carries the acoustic map of the Warrens in her head the way other people might carry a genealogical chart — every sound connected to every other sound, every change noted, every pattern tracked.

She does not write any of this down. There is no writing in the Warrens. There is memory, and there is the place itself, and the two are intertwined in ways that make the distinction meaningless.

She is also the one who tends the dwarf willow, though "tends" is too active a word. She visits it. She sits beneath it. She checks its roots where they emerge from the midden-mound, noting new growth, new damage, the slow progress of its penetration into the compressed past. She does not water it (it does not need water — nothing in the Warrens needs water). She does not prune it. She simply *witnesses* it, and this witnessing is, in the context of the Warrens, a form of care.

She has opinions about the under-builder. She believes it is real. She believes it is old — older than the Warrens, older than the building, something that was here before the first plank was laid and that has accommodated itself to the human construction the way water accommodates itself to a vessel. She does not believe it is dangerous. She believes it is *necessary* — that the deepest structures, the ones that keep the whole layered mass from subsiding into the wet earth, are maintained by something that is not human and was never human and has its own reasons for doing so. She does not speculate about those reasons. Speculation, in the Warrens, is a luxury, and the people here are not luxurious.

## The Young Man

There is one young man — perhaps twenty, perhaps younger — and he is the Warrens' future, which is to say, he is the proof that the Warrens have a future, however small, however temporary.

He is not like the Elder Female. He is restless. The Warrens, which to her are a complete world, are to him an incomplete one — a place that is *almost* enough but not quite. He dreams of the boundary walls, not with fear but with curiosity. He climbs to the highest points — the tops of the stacked-timber walls, the crown of the largest midden-mound — and looks out, trying to see what lies beyond. The light out there is different — brighter, cooler, with a quality that suggests *distance*, a concept that has almost no meaning within the Warrens, where everything is close, everything is near, everything is within reach.

He is skilled — the Elder Female has taught him well, and his hands are learning the lessons her hands already know. But his skill is tinged with impatience. He wants to *build*, not just maintain. He wants to add his own layer to the accumulation, to leave his mark in wood and fiber and clay. This desire is not forbidden — nothing is forbidden in the Warrens, precisely — but it is unusual. The people here have not built anything significant in generations. The momentum of construction ran out long ago, and what remains is maintenance, adaptation, the small-scale adjustments of people living in a structure they did not make.

But the young man has ideas. He has noticed — because he is observant, because the Elder Female taught him to be observant — that certain channels are silting up, that the water flow is shifting, that the deep pools where the big fish live are becoming shallower. He wants to dredge. He wants to redirect. He wants to *do something*.

The Elder Female listens to his ideas with an expression that is not dismissive but is not encouraging, either. She has heard these ideas before — from his father, from his father's father, from the restless young men of every generation. Some of them acted on their ideas. Their works are visible in the Warrens — a redirected channel here, a reinforced wall there — absorbed into the general accretion, indistinguishable now from the older layers. The Warrens absorb everything. They absorb ambition the way they absorb water — without comment, without resistance, without change to their essential nature.

The young man has a relationship with the otters that the others do not. He swims with them — not by design, at first, but because he swims in the channels (the others mostly do not; they use the walkways) and the otters, being otters, treat any animal in their water as a potential playmate or rival or both. He has learned their calls, their body language, the particular set of sounds that means *follow me* and the different set that means *get out of my way*. He has found their stone-caches and, memorably, added a stone of his own to one — a perfectly smooth, palm-sized river cobble that he found embedded in an old wall. The otters ignored it for three days, and then one of them — a big female, the matriarch of the largest group — picked it up, tested it, and carried it to the anvil-stone on the northern platform. It is now part of the tool-tradition. His contribution. His mark on the Warrens, not in wood but in stone, not in the human archive but in the otter one.

He does not know if he will stay. The question is not formulated in his mind as a question — the Warrens do not encourage abstract thought, and the young man is not an abstract thinker — but it exists in him as a kind of pressure, a restlessness in his muscles, a tendency to look at the boundary walls rather than away from them. The Elder Female sees this. She says nothing. She has seen it before.

## The Others

The remaining people — perhaps ten, perhaps eight, the number shifts as the old die and the occasional child is born (rarely, so rarely) — are defined less by their individual characteristics than by their collective texture, their shared quality of being *people of the Warrens*.

They are all small. This may be diet, may be genetics, may be the effect of generations spent in a dim, wet, enclosed space. They are dark-skinned, dark-haired, dark-eyed — whether from ancestry or from the tanning effect of living in a world colored by tannin-stained water is unclear. Their teeth are worn. Their hands are calloused. Their feet are wide and flat, adapted to the uneven, yielding surfaces of the walkways.

They speak a language that is probably a descendant of whatever the original builders spoke, but it has drifted, simplified, become dense with terms specific to the Warrens and lost the terms that were relevant to the world outside. They have dozens of words for wood — not species of wood (there are no living trees here except the dwarf willow, and wood is all old, all salvaged, all *found*) but conditions of wood: wood that is sound, wood that is soft but usable, wood that is rotten through, wood that looks sound but will fail, wood that looks rotten but will hold. They have words for the different sounds water makes — trickling, dripping, gurgling, slapping, the particular sound of water moving through a channel that is partially blocked. They have words for the calls of the specific animals that live here — not onomatopoeia, but terms that capture the *meaning* of the call, the information it carries.

They do not have words for *sky*, or *horizon*, or *sun*. They have a word for the pale light at the boundaries, and it is the same word they use for the translucent hides in the roof — *the thin light*, *the light that comes through*.

They live in proximity but not in community, exactly. They share space, share resources, share the ongoing project of existing in this place, but they are not social in the way you might expect. They do not gather for meals. They do not have rituals (or rather, their rituals are so small, so embedded in daily action, that they are invisible as rituals — the way the Elder Female checks the willow, the way the young man greets the otters, the way each person has a particular route they walk each day, a circuit of the Warrens that is both practical and, in some deep way, *ceremonial*).

They are aware of each other. They care for each other, in the way the Warrens care for them — passively, structurally, without demonstration. If someone falls ill, the others bring food. If someone dies, the body is placed in one of the deep channels and the water takes it down, into the layers, into the substrate, where it becomes part of the place. Nothing is removed. Not even the dead.

## The Under-Builder

What lives beneath.

The people say it is there. The animals — some of them — behave as if it is there. The otters avoid certain sections of the deepest channels, and their avoidance has the quality of *respect* rather than fear — the way you might avoid disturbing something that is doing important work. The big fish — the ancient, carp-like fish that are too large to eat and too wise to catch — congregate near these areas, as if drawn by the same currents, the same warmth, the same *presence*.

The evidence, such as it is, is acoustic. The Elder Female has heard it — a sound that is not wood, not water, not animal (or not any animal she can name). A slow, rhythmic sound, like breathing but deeper, like a heartbeat but slower. It comes from below, from beneath the lowest platforms, from the submerged foundations that no one built and no one maintains. It is not constant. It comes and goes, sometimes absent for weeks, sometimes present for days, sometimes so faint that she doubts her own hearing, sometimes so clear that the young man (who does not have her trained ear) can hear it too.

What it might be: a very large fish, or aquatic mammal, something that has grown old and huge in the protected darkness of the deep channels. What it might be: a function of the structure itself, the slow settling and shifting of thousands of tons of layered timber, the hydraulic action of water moving through compressed organic material — a sound the Warrens make, the way a house makes sounds, but deeper, slower, more deliberate. What it might be: something else.

The Warrens do not say. The Warrens are not a place that explains itself. It is a place that *is*, and the things that live in it — the otters, the birds, the fish, the frogs, the few quiet people, the Elder Female, the restless young man, the thing beneath — are all part of its being, all participants in its ongoing, patient, soggy continuation.

The under-builder is not disturbed. This is the rule. Not because it is dangerous (it may be; no one knows) but because it is *doing something*, and what it is doing matters, and the Warrens — the whole, layered, accumulated, creaking, dripping structure of the place — depend on its doing, whatever that doing is.

The Elder Female listens for it. The young man, on his restless circuits of the Warrens, sometimes stops and stands very still on the lowest platforms, feeling for the vibration through the soles of his feet. The otters dive deep and come up fast, their fur slicked, their eyes bright, chittering in a register that might be excitement or might be warning.

The under-builder continues.

The Warrens continue.
