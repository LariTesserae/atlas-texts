**The People Who Grow With the Mountain**

They are called the Vel Maru, though this is a convenience — they call themselves nothing, for they have never needed to distinguish themselves from anyone else. There is no one else. The mountain and the water and the interval between them constitute the entirety of the known world, and the people who live within that interval simply *are*, the way the stone simply *is*, the way the green simply *is*. If pressed — by what force or interlocutor it is impossible to imagine, for there are no visitors, no traders, no curious foreigners — they might say they are the ones who live here. The ones who are living here. The present participle is important. It is not a place they occupy. It is a process they participate in.

They are, in the most immediately visible sense, adapted to their environment in ways that would mark them as unmistakably *other* to anyone from a brighter, drier, faster world.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely so — not the bulging, light-hungry eyes of deep-sea creatures — but noticeably larger than is common among peoples of sunlit places, with pupils that dilate wide and dark in the permanent amber gloom, swallowing the iris. They see well in dimness. They see in gradations of shadow and phosphorescence that would be invisible to unadapted eyes, distinguishing texture and depth in the near-darkness of the understory or the green murk below the waterline with the ease of long practice and longer evolution. Bright light — the rare, knife-thin shaft of direct sun that occasionally penetrates the cloud layer and strikes the highest ridges — is painful to them. They wince, avert their faces, raise broad, calloused hands to shield their eyes. They prefer the soft, diluted, perpetual twilight. It is what they are made for.

Their skin is smooth, almost hairless, and carries a faint, perpetual sheen — not of sweat, though the air is warm enough for sweat, but of the ambient moisture that condenses on every surface in Vel Maru. They are always slightly damp. Their skin has a mineral quality to it, a faint crystalline roughness at the elbows, the knees, the heels, the palms — places where the water's mineral content has deposited microscopic layers of stone over years of contact. The oldest among them have skin that seems almost fossilized at the extremities, ridged and banded with the same rust-red and bone-white deposits that mark the mountain's youngest stone. This is not considered a deformity. It is considered a kind of *ripening*. The eldest are said to be "taking the stone," and the phrase carries no sadness. It means they are becoming more a part of the mountain, which is what everything in Vel Maru eventually does.

Their hair is dark, thick, and worn long, often braided or coiled and secured with pins of shaped antler or polished stone. It grows quickly in the humid warmth, and it absorbs the minerals in the water, so that over time it takes on a faint iridescence — a greenish shimmer in certain lights, like the sheen on a beetle's wing. Some cultivate this deliberately, washing their hair in the most mineral-rich pools to encourage the effect. Others ignore it. Hair is hair. It grows. The mountain grows. Everything grows.

They are not large people. The dense, humid air and the low light and the limited protein of the deep-eye fish have produced bodies that are compact, efficient, lean. They are strong — the architecture-as-agriculture of Vel Maru demands physical labor, the constant maintenance of channels and scaffolds and accretion surfaces — but their strength is the strength of endurance rather than power. They can work for hours in the dripping, suffocating heat of a cliff-face accretion site, bent over a channel, coaxing mineral water across a surface with the patience of someone who measures progress in years. They are also, without exception, extraordinary swimmers. The water is their second medium. Children learn to swim before they learn to walk, or at roughly the same time, and there is no meaningful distinction in their culture between land-dweller and water-dweller. They are both. The waterline is not a boundary. It is a seam.

Their lungs are large. Their capacity for breath-holding is remarkable. A skilled diver — and most adults are skilled divers — can descend for four, five, six minutes on a single breath, moving through the submerged levels of the old city with an unhurried, meditative calm, harvesting materials, tending the Builder colonies, checking the structural integrity of drowned walls. The deepest divers — the ones who descend past the last recognizable structures, into the warm darkness where the vents glow and the nameless serpentine things circle — are a specialized class, half artisan, half mystic. They are called the Rememberers, and their role is complex and not entirely understood even by the people themselves.

**How they live.**

The basic unit of Vel Maru is not the family, though families exist and are important. It is not the individual, though individuals are distinct and valued. It is the *terrace* — a level, a layer, a band of habitable space between the water and the summit, bounded above and below by the slow, implacable processes of growth and submersion.

A terrace is typically home to between thirty and eighty people, organized loosely around kinship but more tightly around shared infrastructure. The people of a terrace share accretion channels, share water sources, share the labor of maintaining the structures that the mountain is in the process of growing around them. They eat together, work together, dive together. They know each other's bodies with the unselfconscious intimacy of people who live in close quarters in a humid climate where clothing is minimal and privacy is architectural rather than cultural.

Clothing, such as it is, is woven from the processed fibers of the canopy plants — a lightweight, quick-drying fabric that serves primarily as a surface for carrying things rather than for covering the body. Pouches, loops, straps, harnesses: the people of Vel Maru wear their tools, their containers, their small personal objects, distributed across their bodies in arrangements that are practical first and decorative second. Ornaments exist — carved beads of the translucent mineral that veins the young stone, polished fragments of deep-eye fish bone, small containers of bioluminescent paste that glow faintly against the skin — but they are integrated into the functional harness rather than set apart as jewelry. In Vel Maru, the distinction between *useful* and *beautiful* is not considered meaningful. A thing that is useful is, by definition, beautiful. A thing that is beautiful but not useful is puzzling, slightly unsettling, an anomaly.

Food is simple and labor-intensive. The deep-eye fish are the staple, caught in traps or speared by divers, eaten raw or cooked over the small, carefully managed fires that are one of the few uses the people have for the woody vines that grow along the cliff faces. Fire is precious here — the humid air makes combustion difficult, and dry fuel is scarce — and cooking is a communal activity, centered on shared hearths that serve an entire terrace. The fish are supplemented by the broad, starchy tubers of certain canopy plants, which are harvested with great care (the canopy is both garden and roof, both food source and structural element) and by the dense, mineral-flavored eggs that the gliding creatures lay in crevices on the highest ridges. The eggs are a delicacy, gathered by specialists who climb the exposed upper ridges where the light is brightest and the stone is newest.

The people eat slowly. This is not etiquette but necessity — the tubers require extensive chewing, the fish are bony, and meals are long, low-voiced affairs conducted in the dim warmth of communal spaces, accompanied by the constant ambient music of dripping water.

**The oral map and the ones who carry it.**

The most important institution in Vel Maru — if "institution" is not too rigid a word for something so fluid, so distributed, so deeply embedded in daily life — is the oral map.

The oral map is not a map in the conventional sense. It is not a representation of terrain. It is a *living record* of the relationship between the people and the mountain, maintained through constant observation, constant narration, constant correction. It exists in the collective memory of the entire population, distributed across hundreds of minds, each of which carries a portion of the whole — some specializing in the terrain of their own terrace, others in the routes between terraces, others in the submerged levels, others in the newest exposed ridges.

Children begin learning the map almost as soon as they can speak. The learning is not formal — there are no schools, no designated teachers — but is woven into daily life with the same seamlessness as everything else in Vel Maru. A child walks with an elder, and the elder narrates: *This ledge was wider last season. The crack above the third stair has deepened. A new pool has formed where the old path turned left. The water was here —* a hand placed flat against the stone at chest height — *when I was your age. Now it is here* — the hand dropped to knee level. *Remember.*

And the child remembers. The memory of Vel Maru's people is trained to a degree that would seem almost supernatural elsewhere. They hold complex, three-dimensional spatial information in their minds with an ease that comes from lifelong practice and from the absolute necessity of the task. The terrain changes. The map must change with it. A person who misremembers the location of a new ledge, the depth of a flooded passage, the condition of a load-bearing accretion pillar — such a person is a danger to everyone.

The oral map is recited, in whole or in part, on a regular cycle. Each terrace has its own recitation, a low-voiced, rhythmic narration of the current state of the terrain, conducted at intervals that correspond roughly to the pace of change — every few weeks for the stable upper levels, every few days for the active accretion zones near the waterline. The recitations are not performances. They are *updates*, practical and specific, and they are attended with the same quiet attentiveness that characterizes all communal activities in Vel Maru.

There is a special category of people — not a caste, not a priesthood, but a recognized role — who carry the deepest parts of the map: the levels that have been submerged for generations, the structures that no living person has seen, the terrain that exists only in memory, passed down through chains of narration so long they have acquired a quality of mythology. These are the Rememberers, the same deep divers who descend into the warm darkness where the vents glow. Their role is double: they maintain the physical connection to the submerged city, retrieving materials, monitoring the Builder colonies, checking the structural integrity of the deep foundations; and they maintain the *narrative* connection, updating the map of the underwater levels with each dive, correcting inherited memory against present observation.

The Rememberers are chosen young. Not by any formal process — a child who shows an unusual capacity for breath-holding, an unusual comfort in the deep water, an unusual memory for submerged terrain, will find herself drawn gradually, organically, into the company of the older Rememberers, accompanying them on dives, learning the routes, the protocols, the specific quality of attention required to navigate in the dark. The apprenticeship lasts years. It is not complete until the young diver has descended to the deepest level and returned — a dive that takes her past the last recognizable structures, past the Builder colonies, into the zone where the vents glow amber and the water is almost hot and the great serpentine shadows move in the darkness beyond visibility.

What happens on that dive — what the Rememberer sees, feels, experiences — is not shared with the general population. Not because it is secret, but because it is inarticulable. The Rememberers return with a quality of quietness about them, a depth of stillness that the other people recognize and respect. They have been to the warmest water. They have been closest to the mountain's heart. They have felt the sensation of *arrival* that all divers report but that the deepest divers feel most intensely, and they have brought something back from that experience — not knowledge, exactly, but a kind of *orientation*, a recalibration of the self in relation to the mountain, the water, the long slow processes that contain them all.

**The builders of buildings.**

The people who guide the accretion — who lay the reed-and-wood frameworks, who channel the mineral water, who coax the stone into the shapes they need — are the closest thing Vel Maru has to an artisan class. They are called, simply, Growers, and their work is the slowest, most patient, most intricately skilled labor in a culture that is already defined by slowness, patience, and intricate skill.

A Grower's training begins in childhood and never truly ends. The fundamentals can be learned in a few years: how to read the mineral content of water by taste and texture, how to judge the rate of accretion on a given surface, how to construct the reed-and-wood frameworks that will guide the stone into desired forms. But the mastery — the ability to anticipate how the stone will behave over decades, to design structures that will remain sound as the terrain shifts beneath them, to integrate new growth with old in ways that strengthen both — this takes a lifetime, and even then, the best Growers speak of their work with a humility that borders on the spiritual. They do not build. They *persuade*. The stone grows; the Grower suggests a direction. The result is a collaboration, and like all collaborations, it is unpredictable, full of compromise, shaped as much by the material's own tendencies as by the Grower's intent.

Growers are recognizable by their hands. The constant contact with mineral-saturated water deposits stone in the creases of their palms, along the ridges of their fingerprints, in the beds of their nails, so that their hands become gradually encrusted — not disabled, not stiffened, but *textured*, as though the skin itself is becoming a geological surface. The oldest Growers have hands that look like the youngest stone: banded, layered, warm to the touch, faintly iridescent. They can, it is said, feel the accretion rate of a surface simply by pressing their palms against it. They can feel where the stone wants to grow and where it resists. They can feel the difference between stone that is healthy and stone that is brittle, stone that will hold and stone that will crack.

This tactile sensitivity extends beyond their craft. Growers are often the first to notice changes in the mountain's behavior — a shift in the mineral content of the water, a change in the accretion rate of a particular terrace, a subtle alteration in the quality of the warmth that rises from below. They are, in a sense, the mountain's interpreters, the ones most attuned to its slow, geological language.

**Children, the old, and the dead.**

Children in Vel Maru are raised communally. Not by design or ideology but by the simple fact that in a settlement this dense, this vertical, this intimately interconnected, the distinction between *my child* and *your child* is architecturally meaningless. Children wander. They climb. They explore. They fall into pools and are fished out by whoever is nearest. They fall asleep in the warm dimness of a neighbor's dwelling and are covered with whatever woven fabric is at hand. They are fed by whoever is cooking. They are corrected by whoever notices them doing something dangerous.

They are, from an early age, astonishingly capable. By the age of five or six, a child of Vel Maru can swim, dive, climb, identify edible plants, distinguish sound stone from unsound, and navigate the terraces of their home level with unerring accuracy. By ten, they have begun to specialize — showing aptitude for growing, for remembering, for diving, for the delicate work of harvesting canopy tubers without damaging the plants. By fifteen, they are full participants in the life of the terrace, contributing labor, carrying portions of the oral map, and beginning the long, slow process of learning a craft.

The old are revered, but not in the way that word usually implies. They are not set apart, not venerated, not treated as fragile or precious. They are simply *valued* — for their memories, which extend further back than anyone else's; for their hands, which carry the mineral deposits of decades of contact with the mountain; for their eyes, which have seen the water rise further than anyone else's. They work as long as they are able, and when they are no longer able to work, they sit in the warm dimness and narrate. They tell the map as they learned it, as they have corrected it, as they have watched it change. They are living archives, and they know it, and they carry the knowledge with a quiet, unsentimental pride.

The dead are returned to the water. This is not a ceremony so much as a *continuation* — the body is carried to the waterline by the people of the terrace, spoken over briefly (a recitation of the dead person's name, their terrace, their role, their contributions to the map), and then released. The water takes them. The Builders colonize them. The mineral deposits begin to form. Within a generation, the body is stone — a small, human-shaped accretion on the submerged slopes, indistinguishable from the mountain except to those who know where to look.

The people do not grieve loudly. They grieve the way they do everything: quietly, patiently, with the understanding that the process is not an ending but a transition. The dead person has not left. They have *changed state*. They are part of the mountain now, part of the stone, part of the slow upward growth that pushes new ridges above the waterline even as the water claims the old terraces below. To mourn them as *gone* would be to misunderstand the fundamental nature of Vel Maru, which is that nothing is ever gone. Everything is transformed. Everything continues.

**The inner life.**

The people of Vel Maru do not have religion in any sense that a person from a harder, brighter world would recognize. They have no gods, no temples, no scriptures, no clergy. What they have is a *relationship* — with the mountain, with the water, with the processes that surround and contain them — that is so total, so pervasive, so seamlessly integrated into every aspect of daily life that it does not require a separate category.

The Singing is not a divine message. It is the mountain humming. The Remembering is not a miracle. It is a light that comes from below. The Builders are not sacred. They are *essential*. The distinction between the sacred and the practical does not exist here, because everything that is essential is sacred, and everything that is sacred is essential.

They do have rituals, of a sort. The offerings dropped into the deepest pools for the serpentine things in the warm darkness — carved stone beads, bundles of dried herbs — are ritualistic, performed on a schedule that is more habitual than calendrical. But the offerings are not prayers. They are not requests. They are *acknowledgments*. The people drop the beads and the herbs into the warm dark water not because they believe the deep creatures will respond but because the act of acknowledgment itself is important — a way of saying *we know you are there*, of maintaining a relationship with the unknown that is based on respect rather than fear, on coexistence rather than control.

Similarly, the practice of touching the stone — of placing a bare hand on a new accretion surface and holding it there until the stone warms — is not a religious act. It is a *conversational* act. The stone responds to presence. The people respond to the stone. The exchange is mutual, unhurried, and entirely devoid of the anxiety that characterizes so many human relationships with the natural world in other places. The people of Vel Maru do not worry about the mountain. The mountain is not a problem to be solved or a threat to be managed. It is a *partner*, slow and warm and alive, and the partnership requires nothing more than attention, patience, and the willingness to adapt.

**What they dream.**

The people of Vel Maru dream of light.

Not of sunlight — they have never seen direct, unfiltered sunlight, and would not know what to do with it if they did. They dream of *brightness*, of a world in which the dim, strained, filtered glow they live by is somehow *more* — more present, more generous, more evenly distributed. They dream of rooms that are fully illuminated, of faces seen clearly, of colors that are vivid rather than muted. These dreams are not painful. They are not expressions of dissatisfaction. They are *longings*, gentle and persistent, for something that the dreamer knows does not exist but cannot help imagining.

The hunger for light is the closest thing the people of Vel Maru have to a collective sorrow. It is not a sharp sorrow. It is not a grief. It is a *tenderness*, a quiet ache for the one thing their world does not provide in sufficient quantity. They have adapted to the dimness. Their eyes have adapted. Their plants have adapted. Their culture has adapted. But the adaptation is not complete — something in them still reaches for brightness, still orients toward the pale, luminous gray-green of the sky, still positions dwellings and workspaces and gathering places to capture the maximum possible share of the insufficient light.

This hunger is expressed in the architecture. The dwellings on the highest ridges — the ones closest to the sky, the ones bathed in the pearly luminescence that passes for brightness in Vel Maru — are the most coveted, the most carefully maintained, the most lovingly grown. Families that live high are not wealthier in any material sense — there is no currency in Vel Maru, no trade, no accumulation of goods — but they are *richer* in the only currency that matters: light. They wake to a marginally brighter dimness. They eat in a marginally clearer gloom. They see each other's faces with a marginally greater sharpness. The margin is small. The difference is everything.

And it is expressed in the bioluminescent paste that some people wear on their skin — small dabs of cold light at the wrists, the temples, the hollows of the throat. This is not decoration. It is *supplementation*. A way of carrying a little extra light with you, of being a little more visible, a little more present, in a world where visibility and presence are scarce resources.

**The ones who are strange.**

Every terrace has one or two people who are, by the standards of Vel Maru, slightly *off* — not disabled, not ill, not mad, but oriented differently, attuned to frequencies that the rest of the population does not perceive.

These are the ones who claim to hear the Singing when no one else can. Who see the Remembering glow in places where no glow has been reported. Who dream vividly, almost prophetically, of changes in the terrain — a new ledge, a collapsed arch, a shift in the water level — that later prove accurate. They are not shamans or seers. Vel Maru has no such categories. They are simply people whose sensitivity to the mountain's processes is slightly higher than average, whose perception of the slow, geological timescale is slightly more acute, whose relationship with the stone and the water is slightly more intimate than is typical.

They are treated with a mixture of affection and mild wariness. They are useful — their observations about terrain changes are incorporated into the oral map, their warnings about unstable accretion are heeded — but they are also slightly *uncomfortable* to be around, in the way that any person who perceives more than the consensus reality is uncomfortable to be around. They tend to be solitary, not by choice but by temperament. They spend long hours alone on the high ridges, or in the deep water, or in the narrow, dripping gorges where the vines hang in curtains and the light is at its most amber and the silence is at its most attentive.

They are the ones who speak most often of the *arrival* — the sensation reported by deep divers, the feeling of coming home to a place they have never been. For the strange ones, this feeling is not limited to deep dives. It comes at unexpected moments: while walking a familiar path, while sitting in a communal meal, while waking from sleep in the dim warmth of a shared dwelling. A sudden, overwhelming sense of *rightness*, of being exactly where they are supposed to be, of fitting into the world with a completeness that is both ecstatic and slightly terrifying.

They do not talk about this often. When they do, they speak quietly, in the soft, liquid language of Vel Maru, and the people who listen nod slowly and say nothing, because they understand. They have all felt it, at least once. The mountain is alive. The water is warm. The stone responds to touch. And sometimes — rarely, unpredictably — the whole system seems to *notice* them back, to acknowledge their presence with a warmth that goes beyond the thermal, beyond the mineral, beyond anything that can be explained or named.

This is the strangest thing about the people of Vel Maru: they live in a world that is, by any objective measure, barely habitable — dim, wet, warm, slowly drowning, geologically unstable, ecologically sparse — and they are, by any subjective measure, *content*. Not resigned. Not stoic. Not making the best of a bad situation. Content. Genuinely, deeply, quietly content, in the way that only people who have never known anything else, and who have built their entire civilization around the acceptance of what is, can be content.

They do not wish for more light. They do not wish for less water. They do not wish for harder stone or faster accretion or a world that is finished and stable and safe. They wish for nothing, because they have everything — not in abundance, but in *sufficiency*, which they understand, with the deep, bone-level wisdom of a people who have lived for uncounted generations in the narrow interval between creation and dissolution, to be the same thing.

**The ones at the edge.**

There are, in every generation, a few who leave.

Not far — there is nowhere to go. Vel Maru is the world. The mountain and the water and the dim green light are the totality of the known. But the mountain has edges — the highest ridges, where the new stone pushes up through the clouds and the light is at its most direct and the wind, when it blows, carries unfamiliar scents — and the water has edges — the deepest, warmest, most mineral-rich depths, where the vents glow and the great slow shapes circle in the darkness — and a few people in every generation are drawn to these edges with a pull that is not curiosity, exactly, and not rebellion, exactly, but something more fundamental: a need to see the *limit*, to stand at the boundary of the known and look outward, even if outward is only more cloud, more water, more dimness, more nothing.

The ones who climb to the highest ridges report a strange experience: the clouds thin, the light strengthens, and for a few moments — a few precious, blinding moments — they can see *far*. Not to another land, not to a horizon, but to the *shape* of the world: the vast, warm, green-lit body of water stretching in all directions, unbroken, unbounded, with the young mountain rising from it like a single, wet, magnificent tooth. There is nothing else. No other peaks. No other land. Just the mountain and the water and the clouds and the dim, patient, eternal process of rising and drowning, of arriving and departing.

Some of the ones who see this are comforted. The world is small. The world is complete. The world is *enough*.

Others are not. They return to the terraces with a restlessness that never quite leaves them, a sense that the world should be *more* — more varied, more extensive, more surprising. They do not speak of this often. Vel Maru has no vocabulary for discontent, no tradition of longing for elsewhere. But the feeling persists, low-level and chronic, like the humidity, like the dimness, like the slow, patient, unrelenting rise of the water.

These are the ones who become the deepest divers, the most dedicated Rememberers, the ones who push the boundaries of breath and depth and darkness, descending further than anyone else into the warm, amber-lit depths where the vents glow and the serpentine shadows move. They are looking for something. They do not know what. The deep water offers no answers, only warmth, only pressure, only the faint phosphorescence of the vents and the slow, rhythmic tremor of the Singing and the occasional, inexplicable, amber glow of the Remembering rising from the oldest, deepest, most forgotten levels of the drowned city.

They go down. They stay as long as their breath allows. They come back up. They report what they have seen to the oral map. They eat. They sleep. They dream of light.

And the water rises. And the stone grows. And the mountain hums. And Vel Maru continues, and the people of Vel Maru continue with it, emerging and submerging, building and drowning, arriving and departing, in the warm, dim, patient, permanent interval between the beginning and the end.
