The architecture does not end at the lacquered tuber-walls; it merely becomes sentient in different gradations. To map the Burrow-Markets by species is to misunderstand the ecology—here, the categories of *citizen*, *livestock*, *structure*, and *tool* blur into a continuum of negotiated consciousness. They live in the damp interstices of classification, each adapted to the amber gloom and the relentless digestive pressure of the cavern.

**The Root-Bound** (the speaking people) came first, or claim they did. They are not a single species but a coalition of the originally digitate: badger-things grown soft and hairless after generations of burrowing through looser soil, their claws now spatulate and sensitive as fingertips; salamander-folk with external gills that filter the yeasty air, their skin perpetually moist and patched with symbiotic lichen; and the pale descendants of surface-dwellers who fled downward during the Excavation, their eyes reduced to silvered pits that perceive only the blue-shifted glow of the cultivated fungi. They have no word for *ownership*, only for *tending*. Their skin secretes a lipid that encourages specific moss growth, and they communicate in the **Moss-code**—not merely reading with their feet, but *writing* with their toes, pressing territorial claims and love letters into the street-moss that are read by the sensitive pads of their neighbors hours later, a slow, tactile conversation that outlasts the ephemeral spoken word.

They dwell inside the **Hollowed Tubers**, yes, but the tubers are not inert. A tuber-house is a living entity with a slow metabolism, its walls composed of cells that remember being part of the World-Oak. When a Root-Bound family "builds," they are actually grafting—taking cuttings from their own hair or nail-clippings and inserting them into the tuber’s vascular bundle. The house recognizes their genetic signature. It breathes when they breathe, its pores dilating to admit the moist air, and when they sleep, the walls contract slightly, maintaining warmth. To be homeless in the Burrow-Markets is not to lack a roof; it is to be *unrecognized* by any substrate, to have no living wall that will absorb your heat and return it. The very old among the Root-Bound eventually merge with their houses entirely, their spines softening into the tuber’s pith, becoming **Threshold-Ancestors**—semi-conscious doorways that open only for their descendants, their voices reduced to the creak of expanding cellulose.

The **Pipe-Keepers** are a distinct caste, perhaps a distinct species. They are small, no larger than a cat, with elongated snouts and teeth of self-sharpening copper—adaptations for scraping the rust-lichen from the steam-pipes without damaging the metal beneath. They move through the city in a perpetual crouch, their backs armored with overlapping scales of chitin that click like castanets when they squeeze through the maintenance gaps between the Fermentation Engines. They have domesticated the **rust-lichen** (or been domesticated by it; the relationship is unclear), carrying cultures of the orange growth in pouches on their bellies, seeding it onto new pipes to prevent catastrophic oxidation, then scraping away the excess before the metal is entirely consumed. They speak in whistles that mimic the pressure-release valves, a language of steam and tension. When a Pipe-Keeper dies, its body is fed to the lichen it tended, and the resulting bloom of rust-red growth is harvested as a pigment for the Moss-code—death becoming literal text.

In the high arches, where the parasitic figs have created lattices of shadow, the **Echo-Wrights** roost. They are not birds, though they have wings of layered, papery bark, and they are not plants, though they germinate from seed-pods that drop from the ceiling-orchids. They are the city’s memory made flesh. Each Echo-Wright is a living phonograph, its hollow chest cavity a resonating chamber that captures and stores vibrations. They absorb the sounds of the Burrow-Markets—the churning of the yeast-beasts, the specific rhythm of a particular citizen’s gait, the arguments in the Moss-code translated into drumming feet—and they replay them at random, decades later. A Root-Bound might hear her grandmother’s laughter, distorted by time and the Wright’s woody larynx, coming from the darkness above. The Echo-Wrights are territorial, solitary, and deaf to the present moment; they live entirely in the archived past, their eyes glazed with a film of resin that preserves them against the humidity. When they die, they fall from the arches and burst, releasing a cloud of spores that carry the recorded sounds into the compost-floor, where the memories are eaten by the fungi and, eventually, expressed again as faint phosphorescent patterns in the mushroom-caps—history rewritten as biology.

The **Yeast-Beasts** in the Fermentation Engines are not merely livestock; they are citizens of a sort, blind and massive, slug-like oxen with grinding plates where teeth should be. They are the digestive system of the city, and they know it. In the semi-darkness of their vats, they hum—not with their throats, for they have none, but with the vibration of their gut-bacteria, a subsonic drone that the Root-Bound feel in their molars. The Yeast-Beasts are tended by the **Brew-Wardens**, a limbless offshoot of the salamander-folk who swim through the alcoholic slurry, immune to the intoxicating fumes, their bodies translucent enough that the glow of the bio-luminescent yeast shines through their organs. The Wardens do not control the Beasts; they negotiate with them, offering specific cellulose scraps in exchange for continued fermentation. When a Yeast-Beast dies of old age—which happens rarely, for they can live centuries—it is not butchered but *retired*, moved to a private vat where it is allowed to ferment itself into a distilled spirit that is said to contain the dreams of the World-Oak.

Beneath the moss-pavement, in the warm dark where the compost-floor generates its own heat, live the **Mole-Sages**. These are not the Light-Tenders—though they share the silvered eyes and the mole-like physique—but a deeper, more reclusive lineage. They are pale, naked, and covered in sensory pits that read the chemical signatures of the soil. They are the city’s archivists, but they do not use writing. Instead, they taste. The Mole-Sages consume specific combinations of fungi and mineral deposits, allowing the compounds to interact in their gut to produce hallucinatory visions of the past—chemical memory. They are the ones who remember the pre-Excavation ore-hauler, not as a machine, but as a flavor profile of rust and ancient oil that still lingers in the taproot’s growth rings. They surface only during the surface storms, when the lightning runs down the root-fibers and electrifies the cavern; the current stimulates their nervous systems, allowing them to communicate with each other in a language of direct neural transmission, a temporary network of shared hallucination that serves as the city’s legislative body.

In the drainage channels, where the **centipede-hounds** hunt during the rains, there are also the **Silt-Weavers**—spider-like creatures the size of dinner plates, their bodies composed of iridescent chitin that refracts the fungal light into rainbows. They are not predators but gardeners. They cultivate the **glow-worm gardens** mentioned in the stagnant pools, tending the larvae with their spinnerets, weaving them into silk tubes that amplify their light. The Silt-Weavers are the only creatures that can safely approach the centipede-hounds during the hunting frenzies; they secrete a pheromone that mimics the vibration-patterns of wounded prey, distracting the hounds while the Weavers harvest the silk from the hounds’ own legs—silk that is stronger than steel and used to reinforce the spider-silk membranes that seal the tuber-houses during floods. They are the middlemen of violence, turning predator into textile.

And everywhere, moving through the streets in single file, are the **Armored Burrowers**—the scaled, eyeless descendants of badgers, yes, but changed. They are the true natives, perhaps, the original inhabitants who were here before the Root-Bound arrived. They carry tubers on their backs not as burdens but as symbiotic partners; the tubers root into the Burrowers’ armor, drawing nutrients from their blood while providing camouflage and a mobile food source. The Burrowers do not speak. They communicate through the **Moss-code** in a way that suggests they invented it, their claws clicking in patterns that the Root-Bound have learned to interpret as *yield*, *stop*, *danger above*. They are the city’s nervous system, their slow migrations through the streets serving as a living circulatory system, distributing nutrients and information. When an Armored Burrower dies, its body becomes a planter; the tubers on its back consume the corpse and bloom into a temporary garden of bioluminescent flowers, marking the spot as sacred for exactly one year, until the flowers fade and the armor is harvested for tools.

The **Light-Tenders**, finally, are not merely mole-like gardeners. They are the city’s chronologists. The flickering of the bracket fungi—the shadow-and-gold rhythm that measures local time—is not natural; it is the result of the Tenders’ feeding patterns. They are nocturnal in a place without day or night, their circadian rhythms dictating the city’s pulse. Each Light-Tender tends a specific shelf of fungi for its entire life, learning the individual metabolism of each mushroom, feeding it specific mineral salts to adjust its brightness, pruning it to control its flicker-rate. They are solitary, territorial, and communicate only through the modulation of light. When two Light-Tenders meet, they do not speak; they adjust their fungal shelves in tandem, creating a light-morse that can be read across the cavern, a silent conversation of brightness and shadow that determines when the Fermentation Engines should churn faster, when the Weep-Holes are likely to open, when the citizens should retreat to their membranes.

None of these entities live in isolation. The Root-Bound’s exhaled carbon dioxide feeds the fungi that the Light-Tenders prune; the fungi’s glow attracts the cave-crickets whose phosphorescent mating signals are eaten by the Echo-Wrights, who replay the sounds to the Yeast-Beasts, whose churning vibrates the moss that the Armored Burrowers read with their claws. It is a single organism wearing many faces, a distributed consciousness that dreams in the language of decay and regrowth. To ask what lives here is to ask what the Burrow-Markets are *thinking* at any given moment—and the answer is always: *we are becoming soil, and soil is becoming us.*
