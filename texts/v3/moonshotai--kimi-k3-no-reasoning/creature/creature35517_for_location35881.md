The people who live here—call them the **Slate-Born**, though they call themselves something that sounds like water striking a hollow stone, *Kith-ren*—do not build so much as negotiate. Each dwelling is an argument made in basalt against the mountain’s tendency to shed its skin. They are short-limbed, deep-chested people, their lungs adapted to the thin air in ways that make lowlanders gasp and weep; their skin carries the constant humidity in a perpetually dewed sheen, and their hair, black and thick as moss filament, is never dry, worn plastered to skulls or twisted into heavy ropes that drip steadily down their backs.

**How they dwell:**

A Kith-ren house is not a shelter from the plateau but an extension of it. They enter through low lintels that force a bow—not from reverence, but to press the spine into a curve that matches the ceiling’s arc. Inside, the single room is divided by hanging weavings of dyed fern-fiber and cured moss, porous partitions that breathe. There are no hearths; the volcanic stone holds the mountain’s geothermal memory, radiating a faint warmth that keeps the interior at the temperature of blood. They sleep on pallets of compacted, living moss that they rotate weekly, letting one bed regenerate while another is in use. When a Kith-ren dies, they are not buried—the ground is too hard, too wet—but placed in a spring-fed cistern, where the water’s mineral content calcifies the body into a white, branching sculpture within a decade, joining the architecture.

Their days are organized not by sun but by **flow-rate**. The plateau’s hydraulics pulse with a subterranean rhythm—three strong surges each day, when the pressure from deep aquifers finds release. During these *breaths*, the town mobilizes. Mill wheels are engaged, rice paper is pressed from beaten philodendron fiber and spread on sloping stones to catch the surge’s force, and the intricate system of bamboo valves and stone weirs is adjusted by children small enough to crawl into the conduits. The Kith-ren speak a language that is half-whistle, half-click, designed to carry over water-noise without startling the silence; a conversation sounds like rain beginning, like pebbles settling.

**The unseen ecology:**

Though the great birds and insects are absent, the water itself is inhabited. Translucent, eyeless shrimp—pale as root-tips—swarm in the catchment basins, filtering minerals and tended like livestock. The Kith-ren harvest them with sieves woven from their own hair, eating them raw or drying them into a chalky protein paste that tastes of iron and rain. Larger, slower things move in the deep cisterns: blind salamanders with fronded gills, their skin the blue-white of cave fish, cultivated in sacred pools where the water is warmest. These are the *Under-Shepherds*, creatures that live for decades and grow to the length of a human arm, their eggs a delicacy eaten only during the stone-splitting festivals.

The vegetation, too, is husbanded. Every pocket of emerald is owned, maintained, and visited daily. The Kith-ren are master grafters, encouraging the tree ferns to grow in specific shapes—arched bridges, living walls, baskets that form over decades as the ferns’ aerial roots are trained through stone lattices. They have no metal, so surgery is performed with obsidian flakes, and binding is done with strips of waterproof bark that tighten as they dry. A Kith-ren’s wealth is measured in **root-hours**—the labor invested in training a plant to serve as a gutter, a door, or a storage vessel.

**The silence as discipline:**

They have no word for "sound" as a category; there are only specific waters—the *tiss* of drip, the *thrum* of fall, the *hiss* of steam escaping a thermal vent. To speak loudly is considered a form of violence, an assault on the mountain’s equilibrium. Children learn to move without footsteps, placing the ball of the foot first, rolling to the heel, testing each stone for stability before committing weight. This creates a population that appears and disappears like mist; you might think a courtyard empty until a dozen figures shift their weight, revealing they have been standing still as basalt columns, listening.

Their religion—if it can be called that—is hydraulic. They do not worship water but **pressure**, the force of the confined. Their priests are engineers, reading the language of the stone through moisture: where damp appears, where moss thickens, where the basalt begins to sing a higher note under stress. They prophesy not futures but **tolerances**—how much more weight the slope can bear, which retaining wall will next surrender, when the mountain’s slow uplift will crack the aquifer and change the town’s circulatory system forever.

**The frozen moment:**

Because the dynamic parameter holds them in stasis, the Kith-ren have developed a culture of radical presence. They do not tell stories of the past or plan for distant futures. A child is not "growing up" but is **at the age of seven waters**, the age of twelve stones. They perfect the moment: the angle of a slate shingle to shed a specific raindrop, the weave of a basket to hold exactly one tide of shrimp. Innovation happens, but glacially—someone spends thirty years perfecting a knot that resists rot in exactly this humidity, and the knowledge spreads not through teaching but through observation, through hands tracing the knot in the dark of a storage cistern.

When they mate, it is with the same hydraulic inevitability—two bodies finding the path of least resistance, creating heat in the constant cool. They are not fertile in the way of lowland peoples; conception requires specific mineral balances, certain pressures of the aquifer. A pregnancy is celebrated not as a new life but as a **new settlement**, a fresh pocket of biomass that must be carefully grafted into the existing architecture. Children are rare enough that each is assigned a spring, a personal source of water that they must learn to read before they learn to walk.

To encounter them is to find, on a sudden ledge, a figure crouched in the mist, wearing nothing but a coating of clay and algae, holding a bamboo tube to their ear, listening to the mountain’s deep arteries. They will not startle. They have heard you breathing for three ridges. They will simply turn, their eyes wide and dark from the constant dim light under the leaf-canopy and cloud-cover, and offer you a cup of water so fresh it burns with cold, so mineral-heavy it coats the teeth. They will wait for you to drink, to acknowledge the stone’s generosity, before they speak—if they speak at all. Most often, they simply point, directing you to the next water, the next stone, the next moment of the plateau’s eternal, saturated equilibrium.
