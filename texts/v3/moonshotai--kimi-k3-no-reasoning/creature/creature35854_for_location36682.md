# The Ones Who Live Here

## The First Thing You Notice

You notice them by their absence first.

You're walking through a chamber — say, one of the mid-level markets, where fungal plates hang from hooks and the smell of roasted keth threads through the air like smoke that has forgotten how to rise — and you realize that the space is *full*, densely full, but nothing is shouting. No one is calling prices. No one is laughing loudly or arguing. The chamber holds perhaps sixty people, and the sound they make together is barely louder than the dripping. It takes you a moment to understand what you're hearing: a conversation conducted at the volume of thought.

Then you begin to see them.

They are pale — not the pallor of illness, not the gray of cave-fish, but a warm, faintly luminous paleness that seems to borrow its quality from the stone itself, as though generations of living inside this light have made their skin into a lesser version of the walls. Their eyes are large, with wide dark pupils and irises that tend toward gray and amber and a strange, milky violet that is prized as a mark of beauty. Their heads are smooth — some fully hairless, some with fine, downy hair so pale it catches light like spider-silk. Their hands are broad, the fingers long and thick-jointed, calloused along the palms and fingertips from a lifetime of reading the world by touch. Their feet are wide, soft-soled, silent on the stone.

They are not tall. The ceiling is low in many places, and the ceiling has opinions.

They move with a quality you might mistake for slowness if you came from a faster world. It is not slowness. It is *deliberation* — the same quality the vanduul have, the quality of a creature that has learned, down in its marrow, that the world is old and patient and will still be here when you arrive, and that haste is a waste of heat. They pick things up by closing their hands around them gradually, as though making friends with the object before committing to the grip. They set things down the same way. They do not drop things. Dropping things is a surface habit; here, everything is stone, and stone remembers impact.

They call themselves the *Keth-na* — which is usually translated as *the people of the keth*, but this is wrong, or at least incomplete. The word *keth* in their language carries a root that means something like *that which persists through change*, and the *-na* suffix means *those who belong to*. So: *those who belong to that which persists through change*. They named themselves after the swarm not because they resemble it but because they aspire to its central quality: the ability to be always in motion, always adapting, and yet always, recognizably, *itself*.

## The Shape of a Life

A Keth-na child is born into a *har* — a word that means something between *household* and *district* and *fungal strain*. A har is not a family in the surface sense, though it contains families. It is a unit of place. You belong to the har that tends the chambers you were born in, and the har belongs to the chambers, and the chambers belong to the city, and the city belongs to the stone. This is not a metaphor. This is the architecture of belonging, as literal as the archways.

A har might comprise forty people, or four hundred, spread across a cluster of connected chambers that share a water source, a fungal garden, and — crucially — a *pattern*. The pattern is the har's identity made spatial: a particular arrangement of carved marks, fungal plantings, and keth-management practices that make a district recognizably *itself*. You can walk from one har's territory into another's and feel the difference underfoot — a change in the texture of the floor carving, a shift in the smell of the fungal mats, a different density of keth clicking through the walls. The borders are not walls. They are gradients. The Keth-na are comfortable with gradients; their entire world is one.

Children are raised by the har, not by parents alone. This is partly practical — in a world where the stone flows and chambers flood and the breathing can change the shape of your home in a season, redundancy of care is survival. But it is also philosophical. The Keth-na believe — not as doctrine, but as assumption, the way you believe in gravity — that a person is not a unit but a *node*, a point of intersection between lines of relationship that extend in every direction. A child raised by one pair of parents would be like a stone column resting on a single point: unstable, prone to cracking under load. A child raised by a har is a column with a wide base, spread like the ancient columns in the old districts, bulging at the bottom with the weight of many hands.

They learn to read before they learn to speak fluently. This surprises no one here. The tactile script — the raised bumps pressed into fungal sheets — is the first language of the fingers, and the fingers learn faster than the tongue. By the time a child is five (they measure years by the breathing, each full cycle of seep-and-flow counting as one *turn*), they can read the basic inscriptions that cover every surface: directional markers, water-quality indicators, the names and histories of chambers, warnings about unstable ceilings, and the small, dense, devotional texts that are pressed into the walls at eye level in certain quiet corridors — texts that no one reads aloud, ever, and that are read by passing your fingertips over them without stopping, the way you might trail your hand along a banister.

Their education is practical, but *practical* here encompasses things that would seem esoteric elsewhere. A Keth-na child learns:

- How to read the water: its mineral content by taste, its flow rate by sound, its seasonal patterns by the smell of the air near seep-points. This is not a hobby. This is the difference between a har that thrives and a har that drowns.
- How to manage keth: which species to encourage, which to discourage, how to lay fungal barriers that redirect swarm-flow, how to read the carpet-patterns in the old districts for changes in population density that might signal a boom or a crash. The keth are not pests and not pets. They are a system, and you are inside the system, and you had better understand it.
- How to work halik: where to cut, how deep, at what angle, how to account for the stone's creep so that a doorway carved today will still be a doorway in thirty turns. This is engineering, sculpture, and divination simultaneously. The best stone-workers are also the best musicians, and also — though this is never said directly — the closest thing the Keth-na have to priests.
- The sign-language, which is not a supplement to speech but a full parallel language, with its own grammar and its own vocabulary, some of which has no spoken equivalent. There are concepts that can only be expressed in sign, just as there are concepts that can only be expressed in the percussion-language of halik-tapping, and a fluent Keth-na moves between all three modes — voice, hands, tapping — as naturally as breathing.

Which is to say: as naturally as the city breathes. The metaphor is not lost on them.

## What They Look Like, Closely

Let me be precise, because the details matter.

Their skin is smooth, almost poreless-looking, with a faint translucence in the thinner places — the inner wrists, the temples, the webs between fingers — where you can sometimes see the tracery of veins, pale blue-violet, like the seep-channels in the walls. It is cool to the touch, always, a few degrees below what you would expect, as though their bodies have acclimated to the ambient temperature of the stone rather than fighting it. This makes their body heat, when it comes — in fever, in exertion, in the rare flush of strong emotion — startling, almost shocking. You can feel a Keth-na's anger from inches away, radiating like a banked coal.

Their eyes are the most striking thing about them, and the most various. Large, yes — adapted to the even, shadowless light, which requires no squinting, no adjustment, no darkness-adaptation. But the *colors*: the gray is the most common, a soft mineral gray like wet halik. Then amber, warm and deep, the color of the light itself concentrated into an iris. Then the violet — rare, prized, unsettling to outsiders — which is not really violet but a kind of milky lavender that seems to shift depending on the angle of the ambient glow, and which is associated, in their stories, with the ability to see things that are not visible: the creep of stone before it becomes apparent, the early signs of a fungal blight, the intentions of a vanduul before it moves.

Whether this is true or whether it is a self-fulfilling prophecy — people with violet eyes being trained from birth to be *noticers*, and therefore becoming excellent noticers — is a question the Keth-na find more interesting than its answer.

Their ears are slightly larger than you'd expect, slightly more mobile, capable of subtle directional adjustments that they use constantly and unconsciously, the way you might adjust your gaze. In the quiet of the Under-Commons, hearing is not a passive sense. It is an active search. A Keth-na sitting still in a chamber is listening to the water, to the keth, to the structural sounds of the stone — the faint creaks and ticks that indicate stress, movement, the slow creep. They are listening to the city's breathing, and their own breathing slows to match it.

Their hands deserve a second mention, because the hands are where the culture lives. The fingers are thick-tipped, the pads broad and densely nerved. The nails are short, hard, kept clean — a Keth-na's nails are a point of personal pride and social legibility, because dirty or ragged nails signal someone who does not read by touch, and someone who does not read by touch is someone who has withdrawn from the community, and withdrawal from the community is the closest thing the Keth-na have to a concept of sin.

They wear simple clothing: wraps and tunics of fungal fabric, pale and soft, faintly luminous where the material is thin enough to transmit the ambient light. The fabric is layered in ways that indicate har affiliation, craft specialization, and personal history — the way a surface-dweller's clothing might signal region, class, and taste. But there is no fashion here in the surface sense. Styles change over decades, not seasons, and the changes are driven by material availability — a new fungal strain that produces a finer sheet, a shift in the water chemistry that affects the dye-minerals they use.

They wear beads. Halik beads, carved and polished, worn on cords of mycelial fiber at the wrists, the ankles, the throat. The beads are not decorative. They are *records* — each bead carved with a tactile pattern that encodes a piece of information: a birth, a death, a flood survived, a skill mastered, a debt owed, a promise made. An elder's bead-cords might hang to their knees, a clicking, stone-heavy autobiography that they carry on their body because the body is the one thing the stone cannot take.

When a Keth-na dies, the beads are removed, read aloud (which is to say: read by touch, then spoken, so that the record exists in both modes), and then distributed — some to the har's archive, some to individuals who shared the events recorded, some returned to the stone, placed in seep-channels where the water will carry them, over years, down through the city's levels, until they come to rest in the deep cisterns where the vanduul live.

No one has ever asked the vanduul what they think of this. But the vanduul are always there, in the deep water, and the beads are always arriving, and the Keth-na find this arrangement satisfactory in a way they do not examine.

## The Day, Such As It Is

There is no day. There is no night. The light does not change.

The Keth-na sleep in shifts determined not by light but by the breathing — the water cycle — and by the rhythms of their har's work. A typical sleep-period is about six hours, though this varies by individual, by season, and by the demands of the moment. They sleep in alcoves carved into the walls — small, rounded, womb-like spaces that are re-carved every few decades as the stone flows and the openings sag shut. The alcoves are lined with dried fungal mat, soft and faintly sweet-smelling, and they are almost always shared. Sleeping alone is unusual, and vaguely pitied. The Keth-na are tactile sleepers — they sleep in piles, in heaps, in the comfortable tangle of a har that trusts itself. Children sleep in the center, where it is warmest. Elders sleep near the opening, where the air is coolest, because they have learned to prefer it.

When they wake, the first thing they do is listen. This is not a ritual. It is a reflex, as automatic as blinking. They listen to the water. They listen to the keth. They listen to the stone. If all three are normal — if the drip-rate is steady, if the clicking is at baseline density, if the stone is making its usual small settling sounds and nothing more — then they rise, and the day begins.

If something is wrong, everything changes. The har mobilizes. Plans are adjusted. The city has taught them that flexibility is not a virtue but a condition of existence, and they have learned the lesson so thoroughly that they no longer perceive it as a lesson.

The morning meal (they do not call it morning; they call it *first-waking*) is typically fungal: a plate of dried, seasoned mycelial sheet, crisp at the edges and chewy at the center, eaten with a paste of ground keth and mineral salt. It is dense, savory, slightly bitter, and it sustains. They eat communally, from shared plates, using their fingers, and the meal is accompanied by quiet conversation — half spoken, half signed — about the work ahead.

The work is the maintenance of the world.

This is not hyperbole. Every Keth-na, from the youngest apprentice to the eldest bead-laden archivist, spends some portion of every day on the tasks that keep the Under-Commons from collapsing, flooding, or being consumed by its own biology. Carving. Channeling. Tending the fungal gardens. Managing the keth. Shoring up ceilings. Re-cutting doorways. Reading the water. Repairing the fungal-fiber props that hold up the more precarious vaults. The work never ends because the stone never stops moving, and the water never stops seeping, and the keth never stop swarming, and the city is a boat that must be bailed continuously, forever, and they are the bailers, and they know it, and they do not resent it, because the alternative is not something they can imagine.

Can a fish resent water? Can a bird resent sky? The Keth-na do not resent the work because the work is what they *are*. A Keth-na who cannot work — through illness, injury, or age — is not cast out, but they are *diminished* in a way that is painful to witness, because their identity is so thoroughly bound to their function that the loss of function is a loss of self. The Keth-na have no word for *retirement*. The closest concept is *becoming an ancestor*, and this is not something that happens while you are alive.

## The Inner Life

What do they think about, these pale, quiet people, in their glowing stone world?

They think about the stone. Constantly. The stone is their environment, their material, their medium, their constraint, and their obsession. They think about its creep — the rate of it, the direction of it, the way it differs from chamber to chamber, from wall to wall, from era to era. They have a theory — not a scientific theory, but a philosophical one, or perhaps a religious one, though they would not use either word — that the stone's creep is a form of *memory*. That the stone is not merely deforming under geological pressure but is *returning* to some original shape, some pre-carved state, some smoothness that existed before the First Cut. That every doorway they carve is an interruption of the stone's true nature, and that the stone, with infinite patience, is erasing those interruptions one by one.

This theory has a corollary that the Keth-na find both comforting and vertiginous: if the stone is returning to smoothness, then the city itself — the buildings, the streets, the archways, the carved marks and patterns and inscriptions — is temporary. Not temporary in the way that all things are temporary, but temporary in a specific, measurable, observable way. They can see it happening. A lintel installed by their grandmother is now visibly bowed. A staircase carved by their grandmother's grandmother is now a ramp. A face carved by their grandmother's grandmother's grandmother is now a smooth oval with two slight concavities where the eyes might have been.

They live inside a process of erasure, and they know it, and this knowledge shapes everything.

It makes them meticulous. If the stone will erase your work, then your work must be worth erasing. Every cut is precise. Every carving is intentional. Sloppy work is not merely ugly; it is *disrespectful* — to the stone, to the people who will have to maintain it, to the future that will inherit its consequences.

It makes them conservative in their ambitions. They do not build monuments. They do not attempt grand architectural statements. The city has taught them that grand statements are erased first — the tallest columns sag the fastest, the largest chambers collapse the soonest. Their architecture is modest, functional, organic: chambers shaped by the stone's natural contours, doorways placed where the stone is strongest, corridors that follow the seep-channels because the water has already done the hard work of finding the path of least resistance.

And it makes them — this is the part that is hardest to convey — *joyful*. Not happy in the surface sense, not cheerful, not optimistic. Joyful in the way that a musician is joyful in the moment of playing a note that will never be played again. The Keth-na live in a state of permanent, low-level, deeply felt *impermanence*, and instead of despairing, they have learned to find in impermanence a kind of freedom. If nothing lasts, then every act is complete in itself. If the doorway will sag, then the carving of the doorway is not a means to an end but an end in itself — the act of carving, the moment of the chisel meeting the stone, the satisfaction of a clean edge that exists, *now*, even if *now* is all it gets.

They have a word for this: *halik-na*, which is their name for the stone with the belonging-suffix attached. *That which belongs to the stone*. It is the closest thing they have to a philosophy of life, and it cannot be translated, because it contains within it the entire context of the Under-Commons: the light, the creep, the water, the keth, the vanduul, the breathing, the patience. To be *halik-na* is to belong to the stone the way the stone belongs to the earth: completely, without reservation, without the possibility of separation.

## The Ones Who Stand Apart

Not all Keth-na are the same, and the differences matter.

**The Cutters** are the stone-workers, the engineers, the musicians. They are the elite, though the Keth-na would not use that word. They are the ones who understand halik most deeply — its densities, its fracture-lines, its creep-rates, its hidden resonances. A master Cutter can press an ear to a wall and tell you what is on the other side: open chamber, solid stone, seep-channel, keth-warren. They can read the stone the way you read a face, and the stone, in their hands, seems to cooperate — to offer its cleanest cuts, its truest tones, its most stable structures. Whether this is skill or something else is a question the Cutters do not answer. They are a quiet, inward-focused guild, and they carry their knowledge in their fingertips and their ear-bones, and they do not write it down, because writing is impermanent, and the stone's lessons must be re-learned by each generation, fresh, from the material itself.

**The Wetcalled** are the water-readers, the ones who monitor the breathing and predict its changes. They are chosen young — usually by the age of eight or nine turns — based on an aptitude that is partly sensory (an unusually acute sense of taste and smell) and partly something harder to define: a stillness, a patience, an ability to sit by a seep-point for hours and simply *attend*. The Wetcalled are the closest thing the Keth-na have to a priesthood, and they are treated with a mixture of respect and unease, because their knowledge is the knowledge of catastrophe. They are the ones who will know, first, if the breathing is changing — if a flood is coming, if a district must be evacuated, if the Drowning Times are returning. They carry this knowledge lightly, or appear to, but their bead-cords are the heaviest in the city, because they record not just personal events but *the city's* events, the floods and the dry-times and the close calls, and the weight of all that water is a weight that bends their shoulders in old age.

**The Swarmspeakers** are the keth-managers, and they are the strangest of the Keth-na, because their work requires them to think like the swarm — to understand the keth not as individuals but as a system, a flow, a pattern of turbulence that can be predicted and redirected but never controlled. They spend their days in the deep districts, where the keth are densest, walking through the living carpet with a calmness that outsiders find unnerving. They can tell you what the swarm will do — where it will surge, where it will thin, where it will turn — and they are usually right, and the mechanism by which they know is not something they can articulate. It is not science. It is not magic. It is *attunement*, the same quality the Wetcalled have with water and the Cutters have with stone: a deep, wordless, body-level understanding of a system that they are inside of and cannot observe from outside.

The Swarmspeakers have a saying: *The keth do not make decisions. The keth are the decision.* This is not meant to be profound, but it is.

**The Quiet Ones** are not a guild or a craft. They are the ones who have withdrawn — partially or fully — from har life. They live alone, in the outer chambers, in the crawl-spaces and the half-flooded corridors at the city's margins. They carve their own doorways, tend their own small fungal gardens, eat their own keth, and speak to no one for days or weeks or turns at a time. They are not outcasts. They are not punished. They are simply *left alone*, because the Keth-na understand, with the deep pragmatism of a people who live in close quarters, that some individuals need solitude the way others need company, and that forcing a solitary person into communal life would be as cruel and as pointless as forcing a fish to climb a tree.

The Quiet Ones are the ones who go to the First Cut most often. They sit in the empty room, in the glowing silence, and they listen to the water dripping, and they do not pray — the Keth-na do not pray, they have no concept of prayer — but they do something that serves the same function: they *attend*. They pay attention to the place where it started, and they feel the weight of the stone above them, and they feel the creep of the stone around them, and they feel the smallness of their own lives in the context of the city's vast, slow, luminous existence, and they find this comforting.

Or they find it terrifying, and they stay anyway, because the terror is honest, and the Keth-na value honesty above almost everything.

## The Vanduul, Again

I said earlier that the vanduul belong to the city, not to the people. Let me try to be more precise about what this means, because it matters.

The Keth-na do not worship the vanduul. They do not feed them, house them, name them, or attempt to communicate with them. They do not, as a rule, even approach them. When a vanduul surfaces from the deep cisterns — when its pale, armored bulk appears at the edge of a flooded chamber, its many legs moving with that slow, deliberate gait, its cluster of eyes reflecting the ambient light like a constellation of small, steady stars — the Keth-na stop what they are doing. They watch. They wait. If the vanduul moves toward them, they move away. If the vanduul moves past them, they let it pass. If the vanduul settles in a chamber and does not move for days, they route their paths around the chamber and wait for it to leave.

This is not fear. The vanduul have never, in recorded history, harmed a Keth-na. They are not aggressive. They are not even particularly interested in the people. They are interested in the keth — the swarms, the populations, the endless skittering abundance that they consume with the same slow, deliberate patience that characterizes everything they do. A vanduul feeding on a keth-swarm is a sight that the Keth-na find deeply, almost painfully beautiful: the great armored body, still as stone, while the keth flow around it and into its waiting mouth-parts in a stream that seems, from a distance, almost voluntary, as though the swarm is offering itself, as though the transaction is understood and accepted by both parties.

The Keth-na have a story — not a belief, a story, and they are careful about the distinction — that the vanduul were here first. That when the first Keth-na ancestor cut the First Cut and said *here*, the vanduul were already in the deep water, already patient, already watching. That they are the city's original inhabitants, and the Keth-na are guests, and the vanduul tolerate the guests because the guests maintain the fungal gardens that feed the keth that feed the vanduul, and this is a system, and the system works, and the vanduul are nothing if not patient enough to let a working system work.

Whether the vanduul are intelligent — whether they are *aware* in any sense the Keth-na would recognize — is an open question. The Swarmspeakers, who spend the most time in proximity to them, report behaviors that are difficult to explain by simple instinct: vanduul appearing at the edges of flooded districts *before* the floods, as though anticipating the water. Vanduul positioning themselves at the mouths of seep-channels that are about to surge, as though waiting for the keth that will be flushed through. Vanduul — and this is the detail that the Swarmspeakers report most quietly, with the most hesitation — vanduul *looking* at them. Not with the diffuse, environmental awareness of a simple creature, but with a directed, focused attention that lasts for minutes at a time, and that leaves the Swarmspeaker feeling — their word — *read*. As though the vanduul is reading them the way they read the swarm: not as individuals, but as patterns, as flows, as systems within systems.

The vanduul are the city's immune system. The vanduul are the city's memory. The vanduul are the city's — but here the metaphors fail, because the vanduul are not metaphors. They are real, and they are large, and they are patient, and they are *there*, in the deep water, in the flooded chambers, in the places where the light is dimmest and the stone is oldest and the creep is slowest, and they are watching, and they have always been watching, and the Keth-na live their entire lives inside that gaze, and they do not look away.

## What They Fear

The Keth-na fear three things, and they fear them in a specific order.

**First: the Drowning.** Not death by water — they are not afraid of water itself, they live by it — but the *return* of the Drowning Times, the great floods that fill whole districts like cups. The Wetcalled carry the institutional memory of these events, and they speak of them rarely, and when they do, their voices drop to the lowest register, the one used for things that are too heavy for the cavern to carry. The Drowning is not a historical event. It is a *possibility*, always present, always latent, the way a fault-line is always latent in the earth. They live with it the way they live with the stone's creep: as a fact, a condition, a parameter of existence.

**Second: the Silence.** The Under-Commons is never silent — the keth, the water, the stone, the voices — and the Keth-na are so accustomed to this ambient texture of sound that its absence is not merely unusual but *alarming*. Silence means the keth have fled. Silence means the water has stopped. Silence means the stone has gone still, and the stone is never still, and if the stone is still then something is wrong in a way that is beyond their ability to comprehend. The Keth-na have a sign for *silence* in their parallel hand-language — a closed fist pressed against the sternum — and it is the sign they use most rarely, and the one that produces the strongest reaction when used.

**Third: the Smoothness.** The end-state of the stone's creep. The final exhalation. The point at which every doorway has sagged shut, every carving has been erased, every chamber has become a smooth, featureless dome, and the city has returned to the state it was in before the First Cut: empty, glowing, silent, waiting. The Keth-na do not believe this will happen in any timescale that matters to them. But they can *see* it happening, in the oldest districts, in the First Cut itself, in the slow, patient, unstoppable relaxation of the stone toward smoothness, and the knowledge that their city — their home, their world, their *everything* — is temporary in a way that is visible and measurable and ongoing is a weight that they carry the way they carry their beads: on the body, always, clicking softly with every step.

## What They Love

They love the water. Not as a resource — though it is that — but as a *presence*, a voice, a member of the community that happens to be made of mineral and cold. They love the sound of it, the taste of it, the way it carves its own channels through the stone with a patience that makes even the stone's creep look hasty. They love the breathing — the cycle of seep and flow, the way the city inhales and exhales its moisture, the way the chambers that flood are always, eventually, the chambers that dry, and the chambers that dry are always, eventually, the chambers that flood again.

They love the keth. Not sentimentally — they eat them, after all — but *structurally*, the way you love your own skeleton, your own immune system, the parts of yourself that you cannot see but that make you possible. The keth are the city's metabolism, and the Keth-na understand this, and they love the swarm the way you love the beating of your own heart: not with affection, but with *dependence*, with the deep, wordless gratitude of a creature for the processes that keep it alive.

They love the light. Not because it is beautiful — they have no concept of beauty that separates it from function — but because it is *there*, constant, unwavering, the one thing in their world that does not change. The stone creeps, the water floods, the keth surge and crash, the fungal strains shift and hybridize, but the light is always the light, and its constancy is the foundation on which everything else rests. They do not thank the light. They do not worship the light. They simply *live in it*, the way you live in air, and their love for it is the love of the fish for the ocean: total, unconscious, inseparable from existence itself.

They love each other. Quietly. Deliberately. With the same patience they bring to everything else. They love in the way they carve: with precision, with attention, with the knowledge that the work is temporary and the willingness to do it anyway. They love in the way they speak: softly, with gestures, with the understanding that words can be swallowed by the cavern or carried to unintended ears, and that what matters is not the volume but the intention. They love in the way they sleep: in piles, in heaps, in the comfortable tangle of a har that trusts itself, skin to skin, breath to breath, warm in the cool air, pale in the pale light, present in the present moment, because the present moment — this drip of water, this click of keth, this slow exhalation of the stone — is all there is, and all there ever was, and all there ever will be.

And they are enough.

In the glowing stone, in the breathing city, in the patient, luminous, slowly-relaxing world of the Under-Commons, they are enough.
