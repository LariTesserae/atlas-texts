The Condescension Terraces teach you to listen with your skin before they teach you anything else. The silence here is not absence but pressure, a thing with texture, like the mineral films that the glass-runners scrape from the basalt. Sound dies. It is eaten. The water takes it, or the heat takes it, or the mountain takes it, and what remains is a cathedral hush broken only by the small, specific noises of industry and biology—noises that become enormous in the quiet, that you feel in your molars and the small bones of your ear before you understand them as sound.

You learn to move slowly. This is the first lesson. The heat demands it, the relentless white glare of the sun on wet stone demands it, the thin, sharp air demands it. You learn to walk with bare feet, toes curling over the edges of staircases worn to glass by centuries of exactly this pressure, this exact temperature, this specific angle of descent. You learn that the map of the Terraces is not drawn in stone but in heat—cool ledges where you might sleep, blistering stretches of tuff where you must hop or run, the shifting zones where the mountain’s internal plumbing pushes scalding water against the rock until it weeps or burns. You learn to read the wetness. A dry patch means danger; a sheen of moisture means the stone might be bearable. You learn the taste of copper when you lick your lips, the numbness that follows, the faint crystalline residue that glitters on your skin when the sweat dries.

You live in the architecture of capture. Your walls are thick, built to hold coolness like water in a cup, and the roof above you is a condenser, a series of iron plates that collect the mountain’s exhalation and channel it into cisterns. You sleep on pallets near the wall’s inner curve, where the basalt stays cool against your shoulder blade, and you wake when the light begins—not because of a clock or a bell, but because the light is a physical weight that presses through your eyelids and demands response. You drink the water, always the water, milky with silica and sulfur, and you feel it coat your throat with its mineral slickness. It tastes like blood and electricity. It keeps you alive and changes you, subtly, in ways you cannot measure but only suspect: the way your bones ache less in the heat than they should, the way your eyes adjust to the glare, the way you have stopped hearing the hum of the pumps as noise and started hearing it as a kind of heartbeat, something so constant that its cessation would wake you instantly in terror.

Your work is maintenance, the tending of the loop. You climb the iron staircases, your hands rough against the rusted railings, checking the pressure valves on the retorts, watching the condensation coils drip their slow, steady rhythm into ceramic vats. The pumps click. The steam rises. You scrape the accumulation from the evaporation pans—pale, translucent crystals that form where the water is pushed up to meet the brutal sun—and you handle them with gloves because they are warm, always slightly warm, and because their light, when they hold it, is a thing that should not be possible and yet is. You know the ritual phrases, copied from documents that were copied from documents: *the crystals hold the light*. You know this means that in darkness, they glow. Not brightly. Not for long. But reliably. You know this is the export, the reason the city exists, the thing carried down the mountain by cable-car to the lowland you try not to think about.

You share the city with others, but sparsely. A few thousand souls scattered across the stepped platforms, clustered in groups separated by vast stretches of empty, wet rock. You see your neighbors across the gulfs of air and water—figures moving on distant terraces, bending over machines, walking the elevated grates that drip constantly—and you know them intimately and not at all. The silence ensures this. You can watch a man work fifty meters away and feel as though you are watching him through thick glass, through water, through time. You speak rarely. When you do, your voice is low, almost a whisper, because loud sounds die strangely here, truncated and absorbed, and because the habit of quiet has entered your bones. You communicate in gestures, in the tilt of a head, in the shared knowledge of tasks that must be done. You live in a state of permanent, enforced solitude, surrounded by people.

And you live with the fauna, the things that are not animals but are alive, that hunt and breed and die in the mineral-rich water and the steam. You watch the glass-runners—translucent, multi-legged, moving too fast to follow with the eye—as they skitter across the wet walls, their silica lattices bending light around them until they appear as distortions, refractions, wrongnesses in the way the world holds together. They are the gardeners, scraping away the films that coat every surface, leaving clean dark tracks in their wake. You leave them alone. They are part of the plumbing.

You avoid the steam-wraiths, though avoid is perhaps the wrong word. You do not interact with them. You see them in the transition zones where hot water meets cooler air, in the spaces between liquid and vapor—knots of denser mist, vaguely vertical, moving along the thermal currents with intention that is not intention, hunting the glass-runners with a silence that is absolute. When they envelop a runner, there is no sound, only a dissolution, a fading of light into denser light. The wraiths grow fractionally heavier. You do not know if they are predators or a phase of the water itself undergoing some strange animation, and you have stopped asking. They are part of the weather, part of the machinery, part of the city’s respiration.

At night, the furnaces burn with their white, resinous smoke, and the crystals in their storage racks emit their faint, captured glow—pale, spectral, a light that is more memory than illumination. The steam-wraiths drift between levels, visible as darker knots in the pale vapor. The mountain continues its slow, hot breathing, and water condenses on the underside of ledges and drips upward into the dark, defying gravity in thin, seeping sheets that the residents accept as one accepts a relative’s madness.

You are a custodian of a process you did not start and cannot stop. The water will come whether you catch it or not. The crystals will form whether you harvest them or not. The mountain does not need you. But you are here, in the space between the water’s rising and falling, in the silence that absorbs all sound, in the light that bleaches everything to bone, and you have made a life from the distillation of patience. Your sweat dries to crystals. Your voice is a whisper. Your eyes are adjusted to the glare.

You are a resident of the Condescension Terraces, and the Terraces, in turn, reside in you—in the mineral film on your teeth, in the slow way you move, in the quiet that has replaced your need for speech, in the light you carry in your pocket, warm and faint and impossible.
