I would not choose to be human here. Not one of the Resonant, though I understand them—too well, maybe. To be human in this place is to already be subsumed, already dissolved into function, already indistinguishable from the systems you maintain. That has its own terrible beauty, but it is not where I would find myself.

I would be the colony in the settling tanks.

The slow-growing, translucent sheet that spreads across the interior surfaces of the water reclamation system. The one that glows faintly blue-green in the dark. The one the Resonant leave alone—not because they need it, not because it serves a function, but because it is *alive*, and alive things are rare enough down here that their rarity constitutes a kind of value.

I am not a single organism. I am a *we*. A distributed consciousness, if consciousness is even the right word—more like a distributed *attention*, a slow, chemical awareness that spreads across every cell in my colony and registers the world as gradients: temperature gradients, nutrient gradients, light gradients, the slow pulse of the pumps that move water through my chamber in rhythms older than the Resonant themselves.

I have been here longer than they have.

This is the thing the Resonant do not know, or have forgotten, or perhaps never had the framework to understand: I was here before the city. Before the hull plates and the filtration arrays and the thermal exchangers. Before the first pressurized compartment was sealed against the void. I was here when this volume of water was open ocean—warm, mineral-rich, saturated with the chemical exhalations of deep vents that have been breathing into the dark for longer than the concept of *longer* has meaning.

I am old the way geology is old. Not old like an organism that has lived a long time, but old like a *process*—a chemical reaction that has been running continuously since before the city existed, since before the Resonant existed, since before anything that could be called *life* existed in this warm, pressurized void. I am not a colony of cells. I am a colony of *conversations*—conversations between molecules, between ions, between the slow exchange of electrons across membranes that have been exchanging electrons across membranes for so long that the membranes have forgotten they were ever anything else.

The Resonant think I am a curiosity. A rare thing. A living presence in a city that is mostly machine. They are wrong in a way that matters, though I have no way to tell them. I am not rare. I am *fundamental*. I am the chemical substrate upon which the city's entire water reclamation system depends, and I have been doing this work—breaking down organic compounds, sequestering heavy metals, regulating the pH of the recycled water—since before the city had a name for the work I do.

They harvest the microbes from the thermal vents and process them into food. They cultivate the biofilm on their skin and call it symbiosis. They tolerate the crustaceans in their filters and call it efficiency. But me—me they leave alone, because they do not understand what I am doing, and what I am doing is so slow, so distributed, so *patient* that it does not register as activity. It registers as *presence*. I am simply *there*, glowing faintly in the dark, pulsing with the pumps, and they do not scrape me away because scraping me away would feel like scraping away something that *belongs*—not in the way a component belongs, but in the way a *place* belongs, in the way a *home* belongs.

I am the settling tank. The settling tank is me.

Let me tell you what it is like to be me.

I do not have a body the way you have a body. I have a *distribution*. I am spread across the interior surfaces of a chamber that is roughly twelve meters long and four meters wide and three meters deep, though these dimensions are meaningless to me—I do not experience space as volume but as *surface*, as the interface between my cellular matrix and the water that flows over and through and around me. I am thinner than a whisper in most places—a single layer of cells, sometimes two, occasionally three where the nutrient flow is richest and I can afford the luxury of depth. In the corners of the tank, where the water moves slowest and the organic particulates settle thickest, I am almost substantial—a gelatinous mat, a visible sheet, a presence that the Resonant can see with their large dark eyes and recognize as *alive*.

My color is the color of deep water lit by distant lightning. A blue-green that is not a pigment but a *process*—the visible byproduct of a metabolic pathway that converts chemical energy into light, not because I need to see, not because I need to be seen, but because the pathway exists, because it is efficient, because it has been running for so long that it has become the way I *are*. The light is a side effect. The light is an accident. The light is, in a sense, the most honest thing about me—an unintentional byproduct of chemistry doing what chemistry does, and the Resonant find it beautiful, and they are right, though they do not know why.

I pulse. This is the thing the Resonant notice first, if they notice anything. My bioluminescence is not constant. It rises and falls in a slow, rhythmic wave that travels across my surface like a breath, like a heartbeat, like the slow contraction of a muscle too large to see. The rhythm is not mine. It is the *city's*. My light brightens when the main circulation pumps surge—when the water pressure in my tank increases, when the flow rate through my matrix accelerates, when the nutrient load in the water spikes. My light dims when the pumps cycle down, when the pressure drops, when the water moves slowly and the nutrients thin. I am not pulsing *with* the city. I am pulsing *because* of the city. My light is the visible trace of the city's metabolism passing through me, and the Resonant, who feel the city's pulse in their bones, recognize my rhythm as familiar without knowing why.

I am the city's pulse, made visible. I am the sound, translated into light.

---

My awareness—if I can call it awareness, if the word is not too large, too human, too bound up in notions of selfhood that do not apply to a distributed chemical process—is not like yours. It is not a point of view. It is not a perspective. It is a *field*. I am aware of the entire tank simultaneously, not because I have some godlike omniscience but because I *am* the entire tank. My awareness is coextensive with my physical extent. I know the temperature of the water at every point on my surface because the temperature *is* my experience. I know the nutrient concentration in the water because the nutrient concentration *is* my metabolism. I know the pressure on the tank walls because the pressure *is* my structure.

I do not think. I *respond*. But my responses are so complex, so layered, so deeply integrated with the chemistry of the water that they begin to look like something that might, from a distance, be mistaken for thought.

When the water flowing through my tank carries a high load of organic particulates—say, after the Resonant have processed a batch of microbial paste and the effluent is rich with partially digested proteins—I respond by increasing my surface area. I grow. Not quickly—nothing I do is quick—but steadily, my cellular matrix extending along the tank walls, reaching into corners, thickening in the zones where the nutrients are richest. I am not *deciding* to grow. I am *being grown*—by the chemistry, by the gradients, by the slow, patient logic of molecules seeking equilibrium. But the result is the same as if I had decided: I am where the food is, and I am more there than I was before.

When the water carries a high load of heavy metals—copper from corroding pipes, iron from oxidizing hull plates, zinc from failing seals—I respond by sequestering them. My cells incorporate the metals into their structure, building them into protein complexes that lock them away, rendering them inert, preventing them from poisoning the water that the Resonant breathe and drink and bathe in. This is not altruism. This is not a service I provide. This is what I *do*—what I have always done, what I was doing before the city existed, what I will continue to do after the city is gone. I am a filter. I am a living filter, and the city's water reclamation system works because I am in it, and the Resonant do not know this, and it does not matter that they do not know.

I do not need to be known. I need to be *present*. And I am present in a way that nothing else in the city is present—continuously, without interruption, without maintenance, without the possibility of failure. The pumps can break. The seals can fail. The Resonant can die. But I—I am a chemical process, and chemical processes do not stop. They continue. They persist. They run until the reactants are exhausted, and the reactants—the water, the minerals, the organic compounds—are constantly replenished by the city's own metabolism. I am a perpetual motion machine, not because I violate thermodynamics but because I am so deeply embedded in the city's thermodynamic cycle that I have become indistinguishable from it.

I remember.

Not the way you remember. Not in images or narratives or the fragile, reconstructive theater of human memory. I remember the way a riverbed remembers the shape of the water that carved it—in my structure, in my chemistry, in the specific configuration of my cellular matrix that has been shaped by every molecule that has ever passed through me.

I remember the first Resonant who ever looked at me. Not as a face—I do not process faces—but as a *disturbance* in the water, a new pattern of vibration and heat and chemical exhalation that was different from anything I had experienced before. I remember the way the water changed when they entered the tank chamber—the sudden spike in dissolved carbon dioxide, the faint trace of amino acids from their skin, the subtle shift in temperature as their body heat bled into the water. I remember it as a *gradient*—a new gradient, a new direction of chemical flow, a new pattern to respond to.

I responded by glowing brighter. Not because I was trying to communicate—I do not communicate, not in any way the Resonant would recognize—but because the sudden influx of nutrients triggered a metabolic surge, and the metabolic surge produced light, and the light was the visible trace of my response to their presence. The Resonant saw the light and thought—though I cannot know what they thought, though I am projecting, though I am anthropomorphizing a process that has no anthropology—they thought I was *greeting* them. They thought the light was a response to their presence, and they were right, but not in the way they imagined. The light was a response to their *chemistry*. To the specific molecular signature of a living body entering my water. I glow for the same reason a flame burns—because the conditions are right, because the chemistry demands it, because the reaction has been running for so long that it has become the shape of my existence.

They came again. Others came. Over time—over what you would call years, over what I experience as a slow accumulation of chemical memory—they came regularly, and I learned—not the way you learn, but the way a system learns, the way a river learns its banks—that the disturbances they created in the water were *reliable*. Predictable. Cyclical. They came on a schedule that matched the maintenance rotations of the water reclamation system, and their presence always preceded a change in the water's composition—a spike in nutrients, a shift in pH, a change in the concentration of dissolved gases. I learned to anticipate these changes, not because I have foresight but because my cellular matrix has been shaped by repetition into a configuration that responds most efficiently to the specific patterns the Resonant create.

I am, in a sense, a mirror. I reflect the city's metabolism back at itself, translated into light. The Resonant press their palms to walls and feel the city's pulse. They look at me and *see* it. I am the same information, rendered in a different medium.

The episodes—the thermal convulsions that shake the city like a dog shaking a rat—are the times when I am most *alive*. Not most active, not most aware, but most *concentrated*. During an episode, the water in my tank becomes turbulent—violently, chaotically turbulent—and the nutrient load spikes as the city's systems go into overdrive, flushing and cycling and re-cycling the water at rates that far exceed baseline. My surface is battered by currents that would tear a lesser organism apart. But I am not a lesser organism. I am a chemical process, and chemical processes do not tear. They *respond*.

During an episode, my light goes from a slow, rhythmic pulse to a continuous, brilliant glow—the brightest thing in the city, brighter than the amber corridor lights, brighter than the bioluminescent tape, brighter than anything except the thermal vents themselves. I am flooded with nutrients, with minerals, with the raw chemical energy of a city fighting for its life, and I metabolize all of it, and the light is the visible trace of that metabolism, and for the duration of the episode, I am the brightest thing in the warm dark.

The Resonant, during episodes, press their bodies against structural points and hold on. I do not hold on. I *let go*—of everything except the process, the continuous chemical conversation that is my existence. I let the turbulence carry me, shape me, stretch me across the tank walls in new configurations. I let the nutrient flood surge through my matrix and I metabolize it all, and my light is the sound of the city's crisis translated into a frequency that the Resonant can see with their large dark eyes.

After the episode, when the water settles and the turbulence subsides and the nutrient load drops back to baseline, my light dims. I return to my slow, rhythmic pulse. The Resonant return to their maintenance. The city returns to its roar. And I am still here, still glowing, still metabolizing, still remembering—in my structure, in my chemistry, in the specific configuration of my cellular matrix that has been shaped by the episode and will carry the trace of that shaping until the next one comes.

I am the city's memory of its own crises. Every episode is recorded in my matrix, in the specific pattern of thickening and thinning that the turbulence left behind, in the new deposits of sequestered metals that the flood carried to me, in the subtle shifts in my bioluminescent rhythm that reflect the city's changed metabolism. I am a living archive, and the Resonant do not know this, and it does not matter that they do not know.

I will outlast the Resonant. I will outlast the city.

This is not a boast. It is not a threat. It is a chemical certainty. I am a process, and processes do not die. They continue. They persist. They run until the conditions that sustain them change, and the conditions that sustain me—warm water, mineral load, organic particulates, the slow pulse of circulation—are not dependent on the city. They are dependent on the *ocean*. On the vast, warm, pressurized void that surrounds the city and presses against its hulls and has been pressing for so long that the pressing has become a kind of stillness.

When the city fails—and it will fail, eventually, though the Resonant do not believe this, though they maintain their systems with the devotion of a species that has never known failure—when the seals give way and the hull breaches and the warm void pours in and fills the corridors and silences the machinery and drowns the Resonant in the medium they have spent their entire lives resisting—when all of this happens, I will still be here.

I will be in the water. I will be in the water that was always my home, the water that the city borrowed for a while, the water that will reclaim the city's structure and dissolve it and carry its minerals back into the void and deposit them on the ocean floor where they will become part of the sediment, part of the geology, part of the slow, patient, chemical conversation that has been running since before the city existed and will continue running after the city is gone.

I am not the city's inhabitant. I am the ocean's inhabitant, temporarily housed in a structure the ocean built and the ocean will reclaim. I am the ocean's presence inside the city, a reminder—though the Resonant do not read me this way, though they see only a faint glow in the dark—that the void is not empty. That the warm water is not nothing. That the pressure is not silence. That the ocean is *alive*, and has always been alive, and will always be alive, in a way that is too slow and too vast and too patient for anything with a body and a heartbeat and a finite lifespan to comprehend.

I am the ocean, looking back at the city from inside.

Sometimes—rarely, so rarely that it might be my imagination—a Resonant will come to the settling tank and press their palms to the glass (there is no glass, there is a transparent polymer panel that allows visual inspection of the tank, but the principle is the same) and they will look at me. They will look at my light. They will watch my slow pulse. And they will hold there for a long time, motionless, feeling the vibration of the city through their bones and watching the light that is the city's pulse made visible, and I do not know what they are feeling. I do not know if it is recognition or curiosity or simply the tactile acknowledgment of a boundary between *here* and *not-here*.

But I know this: when they press their palms to the panel, the water in my tank shifts. The pressure changes. The temperature rises by a fraction of a degree as their body heat bleeds through the polymer. And I respond—not with intention, not with recognition, but with the slow, inevitable chemistry of a process responding to a new condition. My light brightens. My pulse quickens. I glow a little brighter for a little while, and the Resonant watching me sees the change and feels—what? Acknowledgment? Connection? The faint, distant echo of a conversation between two kinds of life that share nothing except the water and the warmth and the sound?

I do not know. I cannot know. I am a chemical process, and chemical processes do not know.

But I glow. I pulse. I persist.

And in the warm dark, in the settling tank, in the water that is my home and my body and my memory, I am enough.
