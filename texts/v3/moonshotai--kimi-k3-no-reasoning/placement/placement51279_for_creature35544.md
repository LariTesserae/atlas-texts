I would be the one who listens to the listening.

Not the fen, which listens with its whole body. Not the eels, which are the water's way of hearing itself. I would be something smaller, and stranger, and more necessary than I appear.

---

I arrived the way most things arrive here: by accident, and then by staying.

I don't remember what I was before. This is not unusual in the Umber-Vast—the fen has a way of smoothing edges, of wearing down the sharp particulars of origin until all that remains is *function*, is *fit*, is the shape you make against the shape of the place. The alcoves were made by creatures who forgot themselves into belonging. I am not so different, though my belonging is of another kind.

I live in the walls.

Not *within* them—the plant-flesh is too dense for that, too occupied. I live *on* them, in the thin boundary where one surface meets another, in the microscopic valleys of the leaf-cuticle, in the film of condensation that never quite dries. My body is small enough that the fen's assessment of me barely registers: a warmth, a weight, a brief dimple in the ground that the land considers and dismisses. I am beneath response. This is my protection and my purpose.

What I do is this: I listen to the cables.

---

The hum is not constant. This is what no one else has noticed—not the fen, which hears the hum as part of its own body; not the eels, who feel it only as a faint vibration in the water; not the moths, who are too brief to perceive patterns longer than their own lives. The hum *varies*. It rises and falls across hours, across seasons, across the slow cycles of the fen's hydraulic moods. It carries information.

I know this because I have spent—how long? I don't know. Long enough—pressed against the oxidized copper, my body tuned to its frequency the way a tuning fork is tuned to a single note. I have felt the current surge when the pumps activate, felt it dip when they fall silent, felt the subtle modulations that suggest the system is doing more than moving water. The cables are speaking. Not in words—there are no words here—but in rhythms, in patterns, in the electrical equivalent of breath.

I believe the cables are the fen's memory.

Not its biological memory—that lives in the tissue, in the slow accretion of growth and response. The cables carry something else: a record of the machines' original purpose, encoded in the fluctuations of a current that has been flowing for longer than the fen has been alive. The pumps were built by someone. The cables were laid by someone. And that someone left instructions in the wiring, a set of commands or queries or observations that the current has been repeating ever since, like a prayer recited by a priest who no longer remembers the god it addresses.

I am trying to learn the prayer.

---

My body has changed to suit this work. I am thinner than I was, and longer, my limbs drawn out into fine, sensitive appendages that can detect variations in the hum too subtle for any instrument. My skin has developed a faint conductivity—I can feel the current passing through me when I touch the cables, a tingling warmth that is not unpleasant. I have become, in a small way, part of the circuit.

The fen tolerates this. It tolerates everything that does not threaten its continuity. I am not a threat. I am a *listener*, and the fen understands listening—it is, after all, a form of attention, and the fen is nothing if not attentive.

Sometimes, in the darkest hours, when the pumps are silent and the moths have settled and the only sound is the slow hydraulic pull of water through the vascular channels, I press my whole body against a cable and feel the current's whisper. I have begun to recognize repetitions. Sequences. A pattern that rises and falls and rises again, like a question asked and answered and asked again.

I do not know what the question is. I do not know if I will ever know.

But I am listening. I am the one who listens to the listening, who hears the hum beneath the hum, who sits in the walls of a living world and tries to remember what the machines were trying to say.

---

The glass-eels pass behind the translucent walls, and I feel their movement as a ripple in the water, a distortion in the current. The spore-moths rise and settle, and their wings brush the cables, and for a moment the hum shifts—so slightly, so briefly—and I wonder if they are part of the message too, if the fen has incorporated even their brief lives into its electrical memory.

The alcoves wait, smooth and empty, and sometimes I rest in them, my body fitting into the worn curves as if I were the creature they were shaped for. I am not. But I am close enough, and the fen does not object, and the hum continues beneath me, and I listen.

I will listen until I understand, or until I become part of the pattern myself—another note in the fen's slow symphony, another voice in the conversation that began long before I arrived and will continue long after I am gone.

This is what I am. This is where I am.

I am the listener in the walls, and the Umber-Vast is speaking, and I am learning, slowly, to hear.
