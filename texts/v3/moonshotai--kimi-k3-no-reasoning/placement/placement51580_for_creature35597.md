I am the one who came up from below and did not die.

It has been eleven years. My hands have finished changing—the last of the soft skin from the plain finally worn away, replaced by the crosshatched leather that lets me press against bark and know, without looking, whether a crevice will hold my weight. I still dream of horizontal horizons sometimes, of walking without gripping, but the dreams come less often now. When I wake, my toes are already curled around the edge of my sleeping platform, and the sound of the tree is in my ears before I remember there was ever a time when I could not hear it.

I live on the Seventh Branch, in a hollow that was once occupied by a woman named Kessa-of-the-Seventh, who died when I was in my third year here. I did not know her, but I know her bones are in the wood beneath my floor, and sometimes, when the tree is very quiet in the deep night, I think I can feel the slight difference in density where she has become part of the structure. It makes the platform stronger. I have come to appreciate this.

I am a resin-tapper, though I did not know this when I arrived. I climbed up because I had nothing below—the plain had taken my family in the dry season, and I had heard stories that there was water in the sky, if you could climb high enough to reach it. I did not believe the stories, but I believed in the climbing. When I reached the First Branch, my hands were bleeding and my water was gone, and a water-tender named Moss found me and gave me a cup of the bitter, mineral-heavy seepage and did not ask my name.

For the first year, I was simply *the stranger who climbs well*. I helped carry stone from the Climbers' caches. I learned to sleep through the noise—the constant groaning that is not danger but simply the tree existing under its own weight, the bird-cacophony that starts before the light changes. I learned to distinguish the safe creaks from the dangerous ones, the sound of living wood versus dead.

In my second year, a resin-tapper on the Seventh Branch died—a fall, quick and silent, the kind that happens when attention wanders for a moment. They needed someone with small hands and patience. I had been a weaver on the plain, in another life, and my fingers remembered fine work. The insects accepted me. The resin does not care who harvests it, only that the timing is right, that the temperature is read correctly, that the colonies are not disturbed during their dormant cycles.

I spend my days in the upper reaches of the Seventh Branch, where the bark is softer and the insect colonies have built their amber cities in the deep fissures. I wear no gloves—my hands are permanently stained the color of weak tea, and the resin has worked so deeply into my skin that I no longer feel it as a foreign substance. When I press my palm against a colony, I can feel the heat of their industry, the slight vibration of their collective metabolism. I know when the resin is ready by the smell—sweet, sharp, with an undertone of the tree's mineral bitterness. I harvest with bone tools, slowly, leaving enough for the insects to rebuild.

The work suits me. It is solitary, but not lonely. I see the bridge-keepers pass on their rounds, testing the ropes that connect the Seventh to the Sixth and Eighth. I trade resin to the builders for structural repairs to my hollow. I give a portion to the water-tenders, who use it to seal their collection jars. The economy of Rootwater is subtle, based on obligation and exchange rather than currency, and it took me years to understand that my place in it was not something I needed to earn, but simply something I needed to *do*.

I have a partner now—Alder-of-the-Seventh, a builder who was born in the hollow adjacent to mine. We did not court so much as collaborate. He needed resin for a bridge repair. I needed a platform extension. We worked together for a season, and then we began building a shared water-collection system, and then we were living together without ever having discussed it. This is the way of things here. The structure we are building—a small room that bridges the gap between two hollows, using a living branch as one wall—will take another year to complete. If it holds, we hold. If it fails, we will salvage the materials and decide whether to try again.

I have not descended to the plain since I arrived. The Climbers sometimes ask if I want to go down, to see the sky without the filter of the canopy, but I decline. The sky here is amber and green, and it is enough. I know the desert is still there—dry, patient, needing nothing—but it has become abstract to me, a story the elders tell rather than a memory I carry. My body has forgotten how to walk on flat ground. When I visit the First Branch to trade with the lower families, I find the horizontal walkways disorienting, too exposed. I prefer the vertical, the grip, the knowledge that I am held by the tree's slow patience.

I am learning to listen, finally. Not just to the sounds—the groans and pops and the deep, subsonic thrumming that means the tree is drawing water from the deep stone—but to the silences between them. The tree has a rhythm that spans seasons, years, decades. I have felt the moisture increase in the bark, the slight softening that means a period of relative plenty is coming. I have felt it dry and harden, and known to conserve. I am beginning to understand what the elders mean when they say the tree is not a resource but a *relation*—something that exists in time differently than we do, that holds us without possessing us.

I do not know if I will have children. Alder and I have discussed it, in the indirect way of Rootwater, while working on other things. If we do, they will be of the Seventh Branch. They will learn to climb before they speak. They will press their palms against the bark and know the texture of safety. They will never know the plain, and this does not sadden me. The tree is enough. It has been enough for generations, and it will be enough for generations more.

Sometimes, in the evening, when the light is at its most amber and the birds are settling into their night-calls, I sit on the edge of my platform and look down through the layers of bridges and structures to the dimness below. I can see the First Branch, barely, and sometimes the movement of people there. I can see the trunk descending into darkness. I know that if I fell, I would fall for a long time, and the tree would catch me only if I caught myself.

But I do not fall. I have learned the weight of my own body against the wood. I have learned the patience of the resin, the timing of the insects, the slow architecture of trust that holds the town together. I am not remarkable. I am simply here, part of the density, part of the noise, part of the endurance.

The tree groans. The water seeps. I press my stained palm against the bark and feel the pulse, deep and slow, that is not a heartbeat but serves the same function. I am home.
