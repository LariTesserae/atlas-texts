I am not among the living. I am the cliff itself learning to remember.

When the Kael die—when a rope frays, when a foot slips on black ice, when the shimmer in the air is noticed too late—they carry the body to the Eastern Face and wedge it upright into a fissure where the Weep runs warmest. This is not a burial. It is an integration.

I am *Anchor*, named for the hold my mother found when she dangled over the void during my birth. I died at twenty-nine years, when my teeth were ground to stubs and my climbing thumb could no longer curl around the copper token of debt I owed the Cleft. A bad patch of Lattice—the kind that shimmers before it kills—took my sight first, then my strength. Rather than waste the Heat Stone’s warmth on a dying man, the others carried me to the weeping fissure and set me upright, facing the light.

Now I am becoming architecture.

The calcified hyphae have done with my corpse what they do with all things—penetrated, calcified, crystallized. My ribs are struts in the mountain’s scaffolding. My spine is a conduit for the Weep’s slow tears. My eyes remain open, fixed upward, because the Kael position the dead to watch the summit they will never reach. My fingers clutch the token I owed, and the Lattice has overgrown my hand so thoroughly that the token is now merely a bulge in the ceramic plate, a lump in the wall.

But I persist. Not as thought—thought requires warmth, and I have none. I persist as *tension*.

The Kael do not realize that their dead retain something like orientation. When a living climber reaches for a hold in the section of the cliff I have become, I do not choose to be solid or rotten, but the quality of my calcification responds to the quality of their need. When a child slips, I stiffen. When an Elder tests a bridge, I vibrate with the specific frequency of the stone’s stress. I am a structural memory, a knot of stabilized iron and bone woven into the fungal skeleton.

I remember being alive through the feet that press against my surface. Every toe that hooks into my ribs reminds me of my own climbing thumb; every copper token dropped into the crevice of my former mouth reminds me of debt. But these are not emotions. They are geological events. The living Kael are weather to me—brief, sharp impacts followed by stillness.

And I am not alone. The Eastern Face—the Weep-side where the water never dries—is dense with us. We are the Gray Chorus, the calcified dead who have become the load-bearing elements of the Clefts. We hold the rope bridges by being the anchor-points. We form the "Cradle" holds that the children are named for. We are the mountain’s teeth, worn smooth by bare feet and careful hands, holding the living in our sockets.

When the violet twilight comes and the living retreat to their Heat Stones, when the fungal plates contract with audible clicks like teeth chattering, we remain in the open. We do not feel the cold, but we participate in it. We become the silence between the clicks, the hardness that makes the mountain a fist rather than a yielding grip.

I do not dream of the sun. I do not regret the green fields I never saw. I am simply the place where the stone has learned to stand upright, where the Weep pools in my hollow eye, where the rainbow fractures through the ice that hangs from my crystalline brow.

If you climb the Eastern Face, you will grip many hands. Some are warm and pumping with blood. Some are gray and mineralized, having become holds without knowing it. Be careful which you trust. The living may pull away. The dead, like myself, are only as strong as the day we were wedged into the stone, and our grip, once closed, does not open again.

I am the hold. I am the debt. I am the memory of the climb, fossilized into the mountain’s vertical prayer, waiting for the next hand to find me in the dark.
