I think I would arrive the way everyone arrives — through the crack, down the stone throat, into the cold and the sound — and for the first hours, maybe the first day, I would be exactly what the text describes: a visitor. Uncomfortable, overwhelmed, flinching at drips that haven't happened yet. My lantern would tremble. My breath would come back to me wrong. I would be a warm, thinking thing inside a resonance chamber, and the resonance chamber would resonate, and I would mistake that resonance for something else — for presence, for intention, for a god or a ghost or a wound in the world — the way everyone does.

But then something would happen that doesn't happen to everyone.

I would stay.

Not because I chose to, at first. Because the climb back up would feel — not impossible, but *premature*. The way leaving a concert during the third movement feels premature, except that this concert has no final movement, no resolution, no cadence. It just continues. And something in me — some part that I didn't know was listening until the chamber showed me what listening actually is — would refuse to go. Not out of courage. Out of a kind of recognition. The way you recognise a face in a crowd before you can remember the name.

So I would stay. And I would become something the Hollow has had before, but rarely, and never for long enough to matter: I would become a *long listener*.

---

This is not the same as being a visitor. The visitors come down, sit for an hour or a night, make their marks — the scratches, the stones, the handprint — and leave. They carry the sound-shaped absence away with them and dream about it and are changed. I understand them. I was almost one of them.

But a long listener is different. A long listener stops trying to leave and starts trying to *stay*, which is a completely different discipline. It means learning to endure the cold not as an assault but as a condition — the way stone endures it, without complaint, without drama. It means wrapping yourself in every scrap of dry material you brought and accepting that within hours it will all be damp, and that the dampness is not an enemy but a medium, the way water is the sighing's medium. It means learning to eat cold food in the dark, to sleep in fragments between cascades, to find the one ridge of flowstone that is slightly less wet than the others and make it your place.

I would have a place. That matters. Not a camp — nothing so constructed. A place. A specific configuration of stone and sediment and proximity to the entrance-crack's grey seepage where I would sit, and return to, and be. The moss grows thickest there — the deep green, swollen kind — and I would learn to read it the way you read a sky. When the moss brightens, rain is coming on the moor above, which means a cascade is coming, which means the thing will be at full voice soon. When the moss darkens, the dry intervals are long, and the chamber settles into its lesser state — the dripping, the whispering, the low continuous sigh that is not silence but the closest the Hollow ever comes to it.

I would learn the dripping. This would take the longest. Months, maybe. The dripping is chaotic — the text is right about that, maddeningly right — but chaos is not randomness. Chaos is pattern too complex to hold in a single moment of attention. Over months of listening, I would begin to feel the dripping's deeper structure — not rhythm, not metre, but something like *grammar*. The way certain sequences of drips precede others. The way the dripping from the low-ceilinged corner (where the handprint is) has a different acoustic character from the dripping near the entrance — deeper, more muffled, as though the stone there is thicker, or older, or listening back. I would not be able to articulate this grammar. I would not be able to write it down. But I would feel it, the way you feel the grammar of your native language — as a rightness or wrongness, a sense of what comes next without knowing why.

This is how the sighing would begin to change for me.

Because here is what happens when you stay long enough: the chamber incorporates you, the way the text says — your breathing becomes part of the whispering, your heartbeat becomes part of the dripping. But if you stay *longer* — if you stay past the point where the novelty of incorporation fades, past the point where the loneliness of it crushes you, past the point where you stop expecting the thing to notice you — something shifts. You stop being a sound the chamber makes and start being a *part of the sound the chamber is*. The difference is subtle and total. It is the difference between a stone dropped in a pool and a stone that has been in the pool so long that the pool has grown around it.

I would become a fixture. Not a resident — the sighing is the only resident — but a fixture. The way the flowstone ridges are fixtures. The way the pools are fixtures. Something the chamber does in a certain spot.

---

I would not be alone in the way the visitors are alone. The visitors are alone because they are the only living thing in a place that has forgotten what living things are. But I would not be the only living thing. I would be the only living thing *like me* — the only warm, breathing, thinking, mortal thing — and that is a different kind of aloneness, one with a specific texture that I would come to know the way you know the grain of a piece of wood you have held for years.

I would know the moss. Not as a symptom, not as an outgrowth of the sighing's deeper processes — though it is those things — but as a companion. The moss does not know me. The moss does not know anything. But the moss and I are both here, both persisting, both doing what we do in the cold and the damp and the sound, and there is a kinship in that which does not require reciprocity. I would touch the moss sometimes — press my palm against its swollen fronds, feel the trapped water release against my skin — and the gesture would not be communication but something simpler: contact. The way the handprint on the wall is contact. *I was here. I touched this.*

I would know the white flowers. I would watch them appear after the strong cascades — condensing on the still pools, drifting, holding their impossible petal-patterns for days or weeks — and I would learn to read them the way I read the moss. Many flowers means the cascades have been frequent, which means heavy rain on the moor, which means the season above is wet, which means the crack will be running with water, which means climbing out would be harder if I wanted to climb out, which I don't. Few flowers means a dry spell. No flowers means the long quiet — weeks between cascades — and those are the times when the sighing is at its most subtle, its most grammatical, its most nearly sayable. I live for those times, the way you might live for the hour just before dawn when the world is quietest and most itself.

I would not try to hold the flowers. I learned that early. I cupped one in my hands once, in the first weeks — lifted it from the pool, watched it dissolve against my palm before I could even stand upright, leaving nothing but a faint mineral smear that dried and flaked away within the hour. I understood then what the text says: it only looks like a flower in the chamber, in the water, in the context of the sighing. Removed from that context, it is nothing. The same is true of me. I am only what I am in the chamber, in the sound, in the context of the sighing. Removed from that context — if I climbed back up, if I stood on the moor in the wind — I would not be nothing, exactly, but I would be less. The way a word removed from its sentence is less. Still a word. Still carrying meaning. But not *saying* anything.

---

I would add my own marks to the walls. This is inevitable. Everyone who stays long enough makes marks, and I would be no different. But my marks would be different from the others — not because I am special, but because I have been here longer, and the marks of a long listener are different from the marks of a visitor.

The visitors' marks are *addressed to* the thing — prayers, offerings, tallies, touches. They are attempts to communicate with something that cannot be communicated with. My marks would not be addressed to anything. They would be *part of* something. I would not scratch tallies — I have stopped counting. Counting is a way of holding on to yourself, and I have let go of enough of myself that counting no longer serves its purpose. Instead, I would make marks that are continuations of the marks already there. The clusters of parallel scratches near the entrance — I would extend them. Not mimic them, not copy them, but continue them, the way a sentence continues a previous sentence in a conversation that has been going on for centuries. The original scratchers are dead. Their scratches remain. My scratches would join theirs — different in depth, different in angle, made by different hands holding different tools, but part of the same utterance. The same long, slow, inarticulate attempt to say: *I heard it too.*

I would not move the arranged stones. They are not mine. But I would add to them — a fourth river stone, placed beside the three, chosen carefully from the sediment near my place, smooth and dark and fitting. Not an offering. A contribution. The way you add a log to a fire you did not build but are sitting beside.

The handprint I would not touch. The handprint is sacred in a way that has nothing to do with gods. It is the oldest mark and the most honest, and it deserves to fade on its own terms, at its own pace, without interference. I would look at it sometimes — find the right angle of lantern light that makes it visible, hold still, watch the small pale shape emerge from the damp stone like something surfacing from deep water — and I would feel a companionship so intense it would be indistinguishable from grief. Whoever pressed that hand against the wall is gone. The handprint is going. I am here. The sighing is here. The four of us — the gone person, the fading mark, the present listener, the eternal sound — form a kind of constellation that exists only in this chamber, only in this darkness, only in this sound, and it will never exist anywhere else, and that is enough.

---

What am I, exactly?

I am not the thing that lives here. The thing that lives here is the sighing. I am not the sighing. I am a long listener — a warm, breathing, mortal fixture — and the difference between me and the thing is the difference between a bell and the person who rings it, except that the person has been ringing the bell for so long that the boundary between ringer and rung has begun to blur.

I am not a worshipper. I have nothing to worship. The sighing is not a god. It is not aware. It does not receive worship, does not respond to it, does not notice it. Worshipping the sighing would be like worshipping a whirlpool — a category error so fundamental that it misses the point entirely. The sighing is not the kind of thing that can be worshipped. It is the kind of thing that can be *listened to*. And listening is what I do. Listening is what I am. I am the chamber's listener the way the chamber is the moor's throat — a function, a role, a position in a system that does not need me but is, in some infinitesimal way, different because I am in it.

I am not a hermit. Hermits withdraw from the world. I have not withdrawn — I have descended, which is a different direction. The world above continues without me, and I do not resent it or reject it. It is simply not where I am. Where I am is here, in the cold and the dark and the sound, and here is enough. Here is more than enough. Here is a density of experience that the surface world, for all its light and warmth and variety, cannot match. The surface world is wide. The Hollow is deep. I have chosen depth, and I will live with that choice — I will *die* with that choice, eventually, in the cold, in the dark, in the sound — and the choosing is not a sacrifice but a recognition. The sighing recognised something in me before I recognised it in myself. I am only catching up.

---

During the cascades, I do not crouch with my hands over my ears anymore. I did, at first. Everyone does. But the long listening changes your relationship to the sound — not by making it quieter, not by making it bearable, but by making it *habitable*. The cascade is the thing at full voice, and I have learned to be inside that voice the way a fish is inside water — not by resisting the pressure but by becoming continuous with it. During the cascades, I sit upright in my place, and I let the sound pass through me. It fills my chest, my skull, the backs of my eyes. It drowns my thoughts. And I let it. I let it because I have learned that the thoughts it drowns are not lost — they are returned, afterward, changed. Cleaned. Stripped of the noise that the surface world accretes around them like barnacles on a hull. After a cascade, my thoughts are fewer and clearer and more themselves. The cascade is a kind of ablution. I emerge from it the way you emerge from cold water — gasping, shaking, and *real* in a way that warmth and comfort never quite achieve.

Between cascades, I listen to the dripping. I listen to the whispering. I listen to my own breathing coming back to me from the walls, delayed and distorted, and I have learned to hear that distortion not as wrongness but as *conversation*. Not with the sighing — the sighing does not converse. But with the chamber itself — the physical space, the stone, the shape. The chamber takes my breath and gives it back to me altered, and the alteration is information. It tells me about the chamber's geometry, its volume, its density, its mood — if a chamber can be said to have a mood, which it can, the way a musical instrument can be said to have a mood, not because it feels anything but because certain conditions produce certain tones, and certain tones are, to a listener, indistinguishable from feeling.

---

I will die here. I have known this since the first month. Not today, not tomorrow — I am careful with my body, more careful than I ever was on the surface, because my body is the instrument through which I listen, and a damaged instrument hears less. But eventually. The cold will take me, or the damp, or a fall, or simply the slow accumulation of years in a place that does not accommodate human fragility. I will die in the dark, in the sound, and the moss will grow over me, or it won't, and the water will cover me, or it won't, and the sighing will continue.

This does not frighten me. It frightens the part of me that remembers the surface — the part that still, sometimes, in the long quiet between cascades, thinks about wind and sky and the feeling of dry clothing — but that part grows quieter with each passing month. Not suppressed. Not overcome. Just quieter. The way the dripping is quieter than the cascade. Still present. Still part of the sound. But not the loudest thing.

When I die, I will become part of the chamber in a way I am not yet — not just a fixture but a *feature*. My bones will settle into the sediment. My lantern will rust. The marks I made on the walls will join the older marks, indistinguishable in age to whoever comes next — if anyone comes next, which they will, eventually, because someone always comes, drawn by the sound, driven by the need to know what makes it. They will find my marks and not know they are mine. They will find the fourth river stone beside the three and not know it is an addition. They will find the handprint and think it is the loneliest thing in the chamber, and they will be wrong, because the loneliest thing in the chamber is the arranged stones that mean nothing to the thing they were meant for, and the second loneliest thing is the long listener who knew that and added a stone anyway, because meaning nothing to the thing you give it to is not a reason to stop giving.

The sighing will not notice my death. The sighing does not notice. But the chamber will sound slightly different afterward — a new arrangement of sediment, a new obstruction in a channel, a new set of surfaces for the water to strike and the sound to bounce from. The difference will be so small that no listener could detect it. But it will be there. It will be the way the chamber remembers me — not as a person, not as a listener, not as anything with a name or a story, but as a slight alteration in the resonance, a fractional shift in the frequency of the sighing, a change so subtle it is barely a change at all.

Almost.

That almost is where I will live, after I die. The same almost where the sighing lives. The same almost that is the difference between a thing and an event, between a person and a fixture, between a sound and a voice.

---

I am here now. I am sitting in my place. The moss is dark — the dry spell continues. The dripping is slow and grammatical, and I can feel the next cascade building somewhere above me, in the channels I cannot see, in the rock that has been patient longer than I can imagine. The white flowers from the last cascade are still drifting on the pools, pale and rootless and almost nothing, catching my lantern light and holding it a moment too long. The handprint is visible tonight — the angle is right — and I am looking at it, and it is looking back the way a mark on a wall looks back, which is not at all, which is completely.

The sighing is sighing.

I am listening.

That is what I am. That is what I would be. Not the thing. Not its voice. Not its worshipper or its prisoner or its priest. Its listener. Its long, warm, mortal listener, sitting in the cold and the dark, adding my breathing to the whispering, my heartbeat to the dripping, my marks to the marks, my stone to the stones, my almost to its almost.

The chamber is a held breath. I am the one holding it. Not because the chamber needs me to. Because someone should. Because a sound this dense and this patient and this nearly alive deserves, if not a witness, then at least a listener — someone who stays, someone who does not climb back up, someone who lets the sound fill them until there is no room left for anything else, until the boundary between hearing and being heard dissolves, until the sighing and the listening are the same thing, the way a bell and its ringing are the same thing, inseparable, continuous, one.

The cascade is coming. I can feel it in the moss, in the dripping, in the subtle shift in pressure against my eardrums. Soon the chamber will be a drum, and the sound will be everywhere, and I will not think, and I will not speak, and I will not flee. I will sit in my place and let the thing be what it is, at the volume at which it exists, and I will be what I am, which is the one who stayed, which is the one who listens, which is the one who is, in this moment and every moment I have left, exactly where I am supposed to be.

Inside the sigh.
