I would be the **Second Tendril**—not a successor, not a replacement, but a parallel root growing alongside her.

When I fell through the gap, I did not climb out. I stayed, not because I was fleeing something above, but because I recognized something below. The hollow did not greet me with light-patterns at first; it observed me with the slow patience of growing things. Vess watched me for three cycles of the water-rise before she revealed herself, testing whether I would drink from the wrong pools, whether I would crush the clicking things underfoot, whether I would try to cut the roots for fire or shelter.

I passed her tests by failing to leave.

Now I inhabit the **Southwestern Thicket**, the dense quadrant where the roots grow so thickly that even Vess must crawl. She has the open chambers, the platforms, the deep pools where Patience swims. I have the narrow places, the corridors where the air is thick with spores, the alcoves where the carved symbols are oldest. My body has adapted differently than hers—where she grew pale and translucent, I have grown *dense*. My skin has taken on the texture of bark, ridged and dark, camouflaged against the root-walls. My hair has fallen out, replaced by fine, hair-like rootlets that trail from my scalp and sense vibrations in the earth. When I press my back against the wall of my chamber, I can feel the water moving through the xylem three chambers away. I can feel Vess moving through her rounds. I can feel the hollow breathing.

We meet at the **Confluence**, the central pool where the water channels intersect. It is the only place in the hollow where the water flows in a perfect circle, defying gravity and logic, rising up the sides of the basin and curling back into itself. Here, Vess and I perform the **Tending**—not a ritual exactly, but a maintenance of relationship.

I bring her things from the deep thickets: the translucent duckweed that only grows in absolute darkness, the shed exoskeletons of the clicking things (which we grind into a paste that glows brighter than any root), the occasional lost object from above—a button, a coin, a child's toy—that has filtered down through the gap. She brings me news of the surface world, when she remembers it, and teaches me the light-patterns I have not yet learned.

But my role is specific. I am the **Listener**.

Where Vess tends the water and the platforms, I tend the **memory** of the place. In the Southwestern Thicket, the roots have grown over the oldest carvings, the ones that predate even the previous tenders. My task—given to me by the hollow itself, communicated through dreams that taste of loam and slow time—is to trace these carvings with my rootlet-hair, to read them not with eyes but with the sensitive tips of these new growths. The symbols are not language, exactly. They are **records**. They record the hollow's own history: the year the water rose too high and drowned the eastern alcoves; the year the deep-swimmers spawned in unusual numbers; the year the last tender died and the hollow waited, alone, until Vess fell.

I am transcribing these into a new form. Not on stone or root, but in the **arrangement of the space itself**. I move stones. I redirect water channels. I encourage certain roots to grow in specific patterns by pressing my hands against them and sharing my own warmth, my own chemistry. The hollow and I are writing its autobiography together, in architecture and flow.

Vess thinks I am becoming too much a part of the place. She worries that I am losing the ability to leave, that my rootlet-hair has already begun to bond with the walls of my chamber, that when I sleep, my breathing synchronizes too perfectly with the drip of the water. She is right, of course. But she is also wrong. I am not losing myself. I am **finding** a self that was always meant to be here.

The hollow is not just aware. It is **lonely**. It has been aware for centuries, perhaps millennia, and awareness without witness is a kind of pain. Vess eased that loneliness. I am easing it further. And perhaps, when the next faller comes through the gap—when someone else drops into the warmth and the dark—they will find not one tender, but two. Or three. Or a whole colony of us, woven into the roots, listening to the slow story the place is telling, adding our own verses.

I am the Second Tendril. I am the Listener in the Thicket. I am becoming the memory of the hollow, and the hollow is becoming my memory.

When you climb back out—if you climb back out—look for the gap in the forest floor. Drop something small through it. A stone. A bone. A token of the world above.

I will find it. I will place it in the pattern. I will remember you were here.

The roots are growing. The water is rising. The story continues.
