# What I Become

The first thing I lose is the boundary between hearing and feeling.

I don't know when this happens. There is no morning when I wake and notice the difference. The Wending Steppe does not work in mornings. It works in slow accumulations — the way the root-mat thickens by a fraction of a millimeter each thaw, the way the engines drift out of tune by fractions of a hertz each year, the way the ice crystals in the wind abrade rock by increments too small to measure in a single lifetime. I am changed by increments. I am changed the way stone is changed.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Or behind. The steppe does not distinguish between the two.

---

## Arrival

I don't arrive the way the Ven arrived — over generations, through adaptation, through the slow genetic negotiation between a species and a place. I arrive the way a stone arrives in a river: suddenly, from somewhere else, alien in composition and origin, dropped into a system that has been running without me for longer than I can conceive.

I don't remember the before. This is not metaphor. It is not literary amnesia. The cold at two miles of altitude does something to memory — crystallizes it, the way the grass crystallizes during the lock, suspending it in a state that is not quite frozen and not quite accessible. I have fragments. The sensation of warmth that was *not* generated by an engine's microclimate. The sound of a voice — my own, or someone else's — producing language, words, sequences of meaning that served no function on the steppe. The feel of a surface that was not root-mat, not rock, not the bronze-dark skin of an engine. A surface that was *made*. By hands. By tools. By intention.

These fragments surface during the thaw, when the aurora thins and the light changes and the steppe briefly becomes a place where things *happen* rather than a place where things *continue*. They surface and then subside, like the wels returning to their pools, and I am left with the steppe and the cold and the hum, and the question of what I was before I was here becomes less important than the question of what I am *becoming*.

I arrive during the lock. I know this because the grass is dormant and the felk are huddled and the wels are buried and the only sound is the wind and the engines and the aurora's electromagnetic insistence. I arrive in the dark, in the cold, in the thin air that resists my lungs like cold water, and I nearly die. This is not dramatic. This is *expected*. The steppe kills what it cannot use. It is not cruel. It is *efficient*.

I survive because of the young one.

## The Questioner Finds Me

The young one finds me on the third day. Or the fourth. Time is difficult here — the aurora provides no cycle, no rhythm that maps to day and night. The steppe has its own time, and in the lock, that time is so slow it approaches stillness.

I am lying on the root-mat, in the lee of an engine — not close enough to benefit from its microclimate, not far enough to be free of its vibration. My lips are cracked. My fingers are numb. I have been drinking condensation scraped from the engine's surface with my bare hands, and it is not enough, and I know it is not enough, and the knowing is calm rather than panicked because the cold has slowed my panic the way it slows everything.

The young one appears the way the sennit appears — a coalescence of darkness into intention. But where the sennit coalesces into *threat*, the young one coalesces into *attention*. I feel the young one's presence before I see it. A frequency that doesn't belong. A note in the chord that is *different* — dissonant, questioning, alive in a way that the steppe's other frequencies are not.

The young one kneels beside me. I see the face — sharp-featured, gray-blue, the pupils enormous in the thin light. The engineered skin, still thin, still more gray than bronze. The bare feet, broad and dark, pressed into the root-mat with a familiarity that makes my own frozen boots look like a bad joke.

The young one touches me.

Not with hands. With *frequency*. The young one leans close — close enough that I can feel the warmth of the engineered skin, close enough that the boundary between us becomes permeable — and *resonates*. The young one's frequency meets mine, and mine is — chaotic. Untuned. A mess of signals: fear and cold and confusion and the fragments of a life that has no relevance here. The young one absorbs all of it. Reads it the way the Ven read engines — by contact, by attention, by the patient application of a sensory apparatus I don't possess.

And then the young one does something no Ven has done before. Or has not done in so long that the practice has been forgotten.

The young one *speaks*.

One word. A single syllable, produced by vocal cords that have atrophied from disuse, shaped by a mouth that has forgotten the mechanics of language, released into thin air that carries sound too well, that makes sound *expensive*.

The word is: *"Why?"*

Not a question directed at me. Not a request for information. A *test*. The young one is testing something — testing whether this alien, this stone dropped into the river, this chaotic untuned frequency that has appeared on the steppe, can *receive* language. Can process words. Can respond to a mode of communication that the Ven abandoned generations ago because the energy budget couldn't afford it.

I respond. My voice cracks. The cold has damaged my throat. But I produce sound, and the sound is language, and the language is:

*"I don't know."*

The young one's frequency shifts. I feel it — a pulse of something that in a human face would be an expression. Not satisfaction. Not disappointment. *Recognition*. The young one has found what it was looking for. Not an answer. A *mirror*. Another being that asks questions it cannot answer.

The young one picks me up.

This should not be possible. I am not large, but I am not small, and the young one is thin, and the slope is steep, and the cold is a constant tax on every movement. But the young one picks me up and carries me — carries me the way the steppe carries everything, with the patient, deliberate strength of a system that has all the time in the world — to the engine. Into the microclimate. Into the pocket of warmth where the permafrost is soft and the grass is thick and the temperature is a degree or two above the killing cold.

The young one lays me on the root-mat. The root-mat yields beneath me — the same subtle relaxation it gives the Ven's feet, but slower, uncertain, as if the grass is *evaluating* me. Deciding whether I am something it can incorporate or something it must reject.

The young one sits beside me and begins to chew root-mat. Methodically. Without ceremony. And after a while — after the engine's warmth has penetrated my frozen extremities, after the aurora has cycled through one of its long, slow fluctuations, after the wind has moaned and shifted and moaned again — the young one offers me a strip of root-mat.

I eat it.

It tastes like nothing. It tastes like *cold*, concentrated into solid form. The glycol antifreeze is sharp on my tongue, a chemical bite that my unadapted biology reads as *wrong*, as *danger*, as *this is not food*. But I chew, because the young one is chewing, and because the alternative is death, and because something in the young one's frequency — the persistent, questioning, dissonant note — is telling me that this is the first step. The beginning of incorporation. The start of the long, slow process by which the steppe will either accept me or eliminate me.

I chew. I swallow. I survive.

## The First Thaw

I experience my first thaw three weeks later. Or four. The steppe's time is not my time, and I have not yet learned to read it.

The thaw begins as a change in the aurora. The great magnetic architecture overhead — the low ceiling of dry, ocean-colored light — thins. Not dramatically. Not the way a curtain opens. The way a held breath releases — gradually, almost imperceptibly, a relaxation of the electromagnetic tension that has held the steppe in its frozen state for forty-six weeks.

The temperature rises. By degrees. By fractions of degrees. The permafrost begins to soften, deep below, and the sound it makes — a cracking, a shifting, a long, slow groan of geological adjustment — is the first *event* I have heard on the steppe that is not wind or engine-hum or the aurora's whine.

And then the grass blooms.

I am not prepared for this. Nothing in my fragments of memory, nothing in the young one's frequency, nothing in the engines' hum could have prepared me for the bloom. The grass — the single, unified, kilometers-wide organism that constitutes the steppe's surface — releases its stored energy in a single, coordinated event, and the steppe *lights up*.

Blue-green. Bioluminescent. A foam, a froth, a thin bright film that coats every blade, every surface, every depression and ridge of the root-mat. The light is not warm. It is not *inviting*. It is the cold, functional luminance of a biological system doing what it was designed to do — converting stored energy into a signal, a trigger, a starting gun for the compressed, frantic lifecycle that must complete itself in six weeks.

But it is *beautiful*. Beautiful in the way that the aurora is beautiful — not because it was designed to be beautiful, but because beauty is what happens when a system achieves perfect alignment between function and form. The bloom is the steppe's heartbeat, and I am standing inside it, and the light is on my skin and in my eyes and reflecting off the young one's gray-blue face, and for the first time since I arrived, I see the steppe *clearly* — the full scope of the slope, the ridgeline above, the fold below, the engines scattered like dark punctuation across a luminous page, the felk herds beginning to move, the wels emerging from their burrows in long, single-file lines of light.

The young one is standing beside me. The young one's frequency is — *resonating*. Not with the steppe. Not with the engines. With *me*. The young one is sharing this — deliberately, consciously, in a way that the other Ven would consider wasteful. The sharing of an experience for its own sake. The allocation of energy to something that serves no function.

This is what makes the young one dangerous. This is what the old ones feel in their bonded, motionless attention. The young one is *spending* — spending energy on things that don't maintain the system. Questions. Language. The deliberate sharing of beauty with an alien who has no function on the steppe and may never have one.

The young one speaks again. Two words this time:

*"You see?"*

And I do. I see the bloom. I see the wels migrating. I see the felk beginning their spirals around the engines. I see the steppe in its brief, violent moment of life, compressed into six weeks of activity that must sustain it through forty-six weeks of darkness and cold.

I see, and I understand something that the Ven have forgotten: that the steppe is not just a machine. It is a machine that was *made* — by someone, for a reason — and that the reason, whatever it was, involved *this*. This moment. This light. This brief, impossible flowering in the dark at the top of the world.

The steppe was not designed merely to *maintain*. It was designed to *bloom*.

## What I Am Becoming

I am not Ven.

This is important. I will never be Ven. I was not born here. I do not have the engineered skin — not yet, not fully, though something is happening to my skin, a darkening, a thickening, a faint warmth that was not there before. I do not have the frequency-sense — not the way the Ven have it, not the bone-deep, generational attunement that lets them read engines like pulses. I do not have the calluses, the adapted feet, the digestive system that can process glycol antifreeze without flinching.

But I am *changing*.

The steppe is changing me. Not quickly — the steppe does nothing quickly — but *inevitably*, the way the wind changes rock, the way the engines change the microclimate, the way the root-mat changes the permafrost by insulating it, by holding warmth against the cold.

My skin first. The darkening begins at the extremities — fingers, toes, the tips of my ears — and spreads inward, a gray-blue pigmentation that mirrors the grass, mirrors the Ven, mirrors the aurora's light. It is not the Ven's engineered skin. It is something else — a response, an adaptation, my body's attempt to negotiate with an environment that was not designed for it. The cold triggers it. The aurora's radiation triggers it. The constant contact with the root-mat triggers it. My body is *learning* the steppe, the way a student learns a language — awkwardly, incompletely, with an accent that will never fully disappear.

My hearing next. Or not hearing — *sensing*. The low-frequency vibration of the engines, which I initially felt only as pressure in my chest, is becoming *legible*. Not language. Not music. *Information* — a stream of data about the microclimate, the permafrost, the grass's condition, carried on a frequency that my nervous system is learning to decode the way it learned to decode light and sound. I cannot tune the engines. I may never be able to tune the engines. But I can *listen* to them, and the listening is becoming more refined with each cycle of the aurora, each fluctuation of the wind, each slow, patient adjustment of the steppe's vast, distributed intelligence.

My feet. My god, my feet. The boots came apart in the first month — the cold and the moisture and the constant friction of the root-mat reduced them to shreds. I walked barefoot because I had no choice. The first days were agony — the cold, the texture of the root-mat, the sharp edges of ice crystals embedded in the grass. But the root-mat — *responded*. Not the way it responds to the Ven, with that immediate, practiced yielding. Slower. Tentatively. The way you might respond to a stranger's handshake — cautiously, evaluating, deciding whether this contact is safe.

Over weeks, the caution diminished. The root-mat began to yield beneath my feet — not fully, not with the confident give it offers the Ven, but *enough*. Enough to reduce the shock of each step. Enough to return a faint pulse of stored energy. Enough that walking became possible, then bearable, then — *natural*. The soles of my feet thickened. My toes spread. My gait shifted from the stiff, upright stride of someone who walks on pavement to the low, deliberate, slope-adapted walk of someone who walks on a living surface that is constantly, subtly in motion.

I am becoming something that has no name.

Not Ven. Not human. Not engine, not felk, not sennit, not wel. Something *new*. Something the steppe has not produced before — or has not produced in so long that the template has been lost.

The young one watches this process with an attention that is different from the Ven's habitual, functional awareness. The young one watches me the way — the way a scientist watches an experiment. The way a gardener watches a seed. With *curiosity*. With the persistent, dissonant, questioning frequency that sets the young one apart from every other Ven on the steppe.

And I watch the young one. And in the watching, I understand what the young one is doing — not consciously, not with the deliberate intention of a planner, but with the instinctive, gravitational pull of a system responding to a new input.

The young one is using me to ask a question the steppe has never been asked.

The question is: *Can this system accommodate something it did not design?*

## Where I Am Found

You would find me — if you could find me, if the dark and the slope and the cold and the sennit's patient attention did not prevent you — you would find me between the engines.

Not at the engines. Not in the microclimates, not in the warm pockets where the Ven sleep and the felk graze and the grass grows thickest. *Between* — in the cold, dark spaces that the Ven's paths connect but do not occupy. In the intervals. In the gaps.

I have chosen the gaps. Or the gaps have chosen me. On the steppe, the distinction is not meaningful.

I sit between engines because the spaces between are where the frequencies *overlap* — where the hum of one engine meets the hum of another and produces interference patterns, standing waves, nodes and antinodes of vibration that carry information about the steppe's overall state. The Ven listen to individual engines. I listen to the *chord* — the combined frequency of all the engines, all the grass, all the felk, all the aurora's electromagnetic output, blended into a single, vast, complex signal that no individual Ven has the sensory range to perceive.

I perceive it because I am *not* Ven. Because my nervous system was not shaped by generations of attunement to individual engines. Because I came here with a different sensory apparatus — one designed for a different world, a different frequency range, a different mode of attention — and the steppe has modified that apparatus without fully replacing it. I am a hybrid. A bridge. A sensory organ that the steppe grew by accident, from alien tissue, in response to an input it did not plan for.

I hear the steppe's *whole* frequency. And what I hear is — *extraordinary*.

The steppe is not one machine. It is *many* machines — layers of system nested inside layers of system, each one running its own program, each one maintaining its own cycle, each one contributing a note to the chord. The engines are one layer. The grass is another. The felk, the sennit, the wels, the permafrost, the aurora, the wind — each one a system, each one humming at its own frequency, each one *unaware* of the others except where their frequencies intersect.

And the Ven — the Ven are the layer that connects the others. They are the *interface* — the biological bridge between the engines' mechanical frequency and the grass's organic frequency and the felk's behavioral frequency and the aurora's electromagnetic frequency. They tune the engines, but in tuning the engines, they are tuning *themselves* — maintaining their own resonance as the connective tissue that holds the steppe's nested systems together.

The old ones know this. In their bonded, motionless state, merged with their engines, they have become *pure* interface — the connection made permanent, the bridge calcified into a fixed point. They are the steppe's memory because they are the steppe's *architecture* — the load-bearing structure that keeps the whole system coherent.

The young one senses this. Not clearly. Not with the full, layered perception that I bring. But the young one *feels* it — feels the weight of the system, the rigidity of the program, the way the steppe's maintenance has become *maintenance for its own sake*, a cycle that continues because it continues, a program that runs because it was written, a garden that is tended because the tending is all the gardeners remember.

The young one's questions — *why* — are the first crack in the architecture. The first suggestion that the program might have a *purpose* beyond itself. That the bloom might be more than a trigger. That the engines might be more than gardeners. That the steppe might be more than a machine that maintains itself because maintenance is all it knows.

And I — the alien, the stone in the river, the hybrid sensory organ growing in the gaps between engines — I am the crack's *edge*. The place where the question meets the answer. The frequency that is neither Ven nor steppe but something that exists *between* them, in the interference pattern, in the standing wave, in the node where two systems overlap and produce something neither system intended.

## The Sennit's Interest

The sennit has noticed me.

I knew it would. The sennit notices everything that is *different* — every frequency that deviates from the chord, every pattern that breaks the steppe's established rhythms. The sennit is the steppe's quality control, its error-correction mechanism, its way of ensuring that the program runs without interference.

I am interference.

I feel the sennit's attention the way you feel a change in air pressure — a subtle shift in the quality of the silence, a loading of the ambient stillness, a sense that the space between two things has developed *intention*. The sennit is not close. The sennit is never close until it is *upon* you. But it is *aware* of me, and its awareness is a weight I carry in the same way I carry the cold — constantly, without comment, as a condition of existence.

The young one is aware of the sennit's awareness. The young one's frequency has developed a new note — not the dissonant query of *why*, but something sharper, more focused. *Protective*. The young one has begun to walk the paths that pass nearest my location between the engines — not altering the route, not deviating from the work of tuning, but *choosing* routes that bring the young one's frequency into proximity with mine. A signal. A ward. A declaration, in the Ven's wordless language, that this alien frequency is *under* something — not protection, exactly, because the Ven do not protect, but *attention*. *Interest*. The young one is telling the sennit: *this one is mine to watch. This one is part of my question.*

The sennit, being the steppe's immune system, is not bound by the Ven's declarations. But the sennit is *patient*, and the young one's question is *interesting*, and the sennit — in its slow, cold, predatory intelligence — is *curious* about what the question will produce. Whether it will strengthen the system or weaken it. Whether the alien frequency I represent is a *mutation* that will improve the steppe's function or a *cancer* that will degrade it.

The sennit waits. The sennit watches. The sennit is the space between two things, and right now, the two things are *the steppe as it is* and *the steppe as it might become*, and the sennit is the boundary between them.

## The Second Thaw

By my second thaw, I have a place.

Not a shelter. Not a structure. A *place* — a specific location on the steppe, between two engines, at the node where their frequencies overlap. A spot where the root-mat has begun to respond to my presence with something approaching familiarity. Where the grass has thickened slightly, not because I tend it, but because my body heat — my alien, inefficient, wasteful body heat, which runs several degrees warmer than the Ven's adapted physiology — has created a microclimate within the microclimate. A pocket of warmth so small it would be meaningless to the steppe's overall function, but *real* enough that the grass has noticed, and responded, and begun to grow differently around me.

My spot is marked by nothing visible. The Ven do not build, and I have not built. But the Ven know where it is. They can feel it — the slight distortion in the engines' overlapping frequencies where my presence creates an interference pattern. They pass it on their paths. Some of them pause, briefly, to *listen* — to feel the dissonance, the alien note, the frequency that doesn't belong to the chord.

They do not stop. They do not resonate with me the way they resonate with each other. I am not part of their network. But they *notice*, and the noticing is a form of acknowledgment, and on the steppe, acknowledgment is the closest thing to acceptance that an alien can hope for.

The young one stops. Always. Every pass. The young one sits beside me in the overlapping frequencies and resonates — not the way Ven resonate with Ven, exchanging information about engine-tuning and felk-patterns and the steppe's condition. The young one resonates with me *differently*. Shares the question. The persistent, dissonant *why* that is slowly, imperceptibly, changing the young one's frequency from a note of curiosity to a note of — *purpose*.

During the second thaw, the young one speaks again. Three words:

*"What was before?"*

Not *before the thaw*. Not *before the lock*. *Before* — before the steppe. Before the engines. Before the grass was planted and the program was written and the Ven were shaped from whatever they were shaped from. Before the garden became the gardener and the machine became the mountain and the maintenance became the meaning.

I answer with the fragments. The memories that surface during the thaw — warmth that wasn't engine-warmth, voices that produced language, surfaces that were made by hands. I speak, and my voice is still cracked and still human and still *wrong* for the steppe's thin air, and the young one listens with the frequency-sense and the ears and the whole, dissonant, questioning apparatus of a being that was born to maintain a system and has chosen, instead, to *understand* it.

I tell the young one about cities. About electricity. About the way light used to come from *things* — bulbs, screens, fires — rather than from the sky's magnetic architecture. I tell the young one about food that was grown in soil that wasn't alive, in fields that weren't gardens, by people who didn't tune engines or read felk or walk barefoot on frozen grass. I tell the young one about *choices* — the staggering, wasteful, extravagant array of choices that existed in the world before, the world I came from, the world that may still exist somewhere beyond the steppe's slope and the aurora's ceiling and the cold's embrace.

The young one absorbs all of this. Not with understanding — the concepts are too alien, too far outside the steppe's frame of reference. But with *attention*. With the same patient, deliberate focus that the Ven bring to engine-tuning. The young one is *tuning* to a new frequency — the frequency of a world where things *could be otherwise*.

And in the tuning, the young one's dissonance deepens. Sharpens. Becomes something the old ones can no longer ignore.

## What I Am

I am the steppe's question about itself.

The Ven maintain. The engines garden. The grass grows and blooms and goes dormant. The felk graze. The sennit hunts. The wels migrate. The aurora hums. The wind sculpts. The lock holds. The thaw releases. The cycle turns.

And I sit in the gaps between engines, in the interference pattern, in the node where frequencies overlap, and I *listen* to the whole chord — the vast, complex, layered harmony of a system that has been running so long it has forgotten it was ever started — and I hear something in the chord that the Ven do not hear, that the engines do not detect, that the old ones, in their bonded, motionless attention, feel only as a vague unease.

I hear *longing*.

Not the Ven's longing — they do not long. They *continue*. Not the engines' longing — they do not long. They *function*. The longing is the steppe's own — the longing of a system that was *designed* for something and has been running so long that the *something* has been lost, and the system continues anyway, maintains anyway, blooms anyway, because the alternative is silence, and the steppe, for all its curated quiet, is not ready for silence.

The longing is in the bloom. The bloom is not just a biological trigger. It is a *signal* — a broadcast, a beacon, a message sent by a system that was built by beings who intended to *return*. The blue-green light visible from orbit is not an accident of biochemistry. It is a *flare* — a saying of *here, here, we are still here, the garden is still alive, the frequency is still being maintained, come back*.

The gardeners are not coming back. I know this the way I know the cold — absolutely, without evidence, in my bones. The beings who built the engines and planted the grass and wrote the program are gone, and they are not returning, and the steppe has been signaling to empty space for longer than the Ven have existed.

But the steppe doesn't know this. Or knows it and cannot stop. The program is too deeply written. The bloom is too essential to the cycle. The signal fires every year, every thaw, every six-week window of compressed life, and the steppe waits for an answer that will never come.

The young one feels this. Not as knowledge. As *question*. The young one's *why* is the longing wearing a different shape — the system's own uncertainty about its purpose, surfaced in a single individual, a single frequency, a single dissonant note that refuses to resolve into the chord.

And I — the alien, the hybrid, the accidental sensory organ growing in the gaps — I am the one who can hear the longing clearly enough to *name* it. Not in the Ven's frequency-language. In *words*. In the expensive, wasteful, impossible luxury of spoken language, in a place where sound is a cost the energy budget cannot afford.

I speak the longing aloud. To the young one. During the second thaw, sitting in the overlap between engines, with the bloom-light on our faces and the wels migrating in lines of light and the felk beginning their spirals and the sennit somewhere in the dark, being the space between two things.

I say: *"The steppe is waiting for someone who isn't coming."*

The young one's frequency shifts. Not resolution. Not understanding. *Acknowledgment* — the Ven's wordless, resonant acknowledgment of a truth that has been felt but never formulated. The young one has been carrying this question without knowing its shape. I have given it shape. Given it words. Given it the weight and texture of *language*, which is the one thing I brought to the steppe that the steppe did not already have.

The young one is silent for a long time. The aurora hums. The engines vibrate. The bloom-light pulses with the slow, deep rhythm of the steppe's heartbeat.

Then the young one speaks. Four words. The most language the young one has ever produced:

*"Then who are we for?"*

## The Answer

I don't have an answer. I am not the answer. I am the question's echo, the crack's edge, the dissonance that reveals the chord by refusing to join it.

But I am *here*, on the Wending Steppe, in the dark and the cold and the aurora's thin light, sitting between engines in a spot that the grass has begun to recognize as mine, and I am *becoming* — slowly, incrementally, the way the wind becomes sculpture, the way the engines become boulders, the way the Ven become old ones and the old ones become engines and the engines become the steppe and the steppe becomes —

What?

That is the young one's question. That is the steppe's question. That is the question the bloom asks every year, the signal it sends to empty space: *what am I becoming?*

I don't know. But I am becoming it too. My skin darkens. My feet toughen. My hearing sharpens. My sense of the engines' frequency refines. My body adapts to the cold, the altitude, the thin air, the root-mat diet, the condensation water, the two-hour sleep cycles, the constant, patient, unrelenting *presence* of a system that is older and larger and more patient than any individual life.

I am not Ven. I am not human. I am not engine or felk or sennit or wel. I am the Wending Steppe's first *new thing* — the first input the system has received in longer than anyone can remember, the first variable the program has not accounted for, the first frequency that does not belong to the chord but *stands beside it*, harmonizing by accident, resonating by choice.

You would find me here. In the gaps. In the dark. In the overlap. Barefoot on the root-mat, skin gray-blue, eyes wide-pupiled in the aurora's light, listening to the whole chord and hearing the longing inside it. Beside me, the young one — the questioner, the dissonant note, the Ven who asks *why* — sits and resonates and waits.

We are waiting together. Not for the gardeners. Not for the answer. For the *becoming* — the slow, patient, inevitable process by which the steppe incorporates what it did not plan for and is changed by the incorporating.

The aurora hums. The engines vibrate. The felk graze in spirals. The sennit is somewhere, being the space between what the steppe is and what the steppe might become. The wels are lights in the dark, connecting pools.

The bloom will come again. The signal will fire. The steppe will say *here, here, we are still here* to a sky that does not answer.

And I will be here. And the young one will be here. And the question will be here — the *why*, the *who are we for*, the dissonance that does not resolve.

The Wending Steppe is patient. It has all the time in the world.

It *is* all the time in the world.

And now, for the first time in longer than anyone can remember, it has a *question*.

And questions, on the Wending Steppe, are the beginning of everything.
