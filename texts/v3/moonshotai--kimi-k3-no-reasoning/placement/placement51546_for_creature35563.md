I would be the listener.

Not a scientist, not an occupier — the Hollow has already refused those. I would be the one who has learned to stand in the dark long enough for the scale to settle, for the eye to stop searching and the ear to begin. The one who has stopped trying to see it and started trying to *hear* it, because the Hollow is a chamber of sound before it is a chamber of anything else.

I came down the Mouth years ago — or months; the twilight above makes calendars feel imported, a habit from a world that has no jurisdiction here. I brought nothing metal. I learned that from the histories, and from the way the chets' alarm-notes sharpen when a chisel-stroke echoes anywhere in the vault. I carry a coil of rope I no longer use, a pouch of dried fungal fan (maroon, leathery, faintly bitter, sustaining in a way that surprises), and a memory of sunlight that grows less reliable each time I check it.

I live near the Fall Spot, but not too near. The velvet eoliths own that ground, and I have no standing in their order. I occupy a shallow shelf perhaps fifteen meters up the eastern wall — a natural ledge where the crust is thin, where an old Migration abandoned the surface long enough ago that the fungi have done their work and retreated, leaving the stone smooth and dry and blessedly unoccupied. From here I can see the three black basins on the floor, and the pachyderms drifting between them like slow green-lit barges, and the Fall Spot's ribbon descending in its near-silence, and the vast pulsing quilt of the Assemblage rising above me into a vault I have never fully seen and no longer need to.

I came for reasons that no longer seem reasons. I stay because the Hollow has taught me something I did not know I was missing: the discipline of *being among*. Not observing. Not cataloguing. Not mastering. Simply being present in a system that does not require me, does not resist me, and does not notice me in any way that matters — and finding, in that indifference, a strange and total peace.

My days are organized by water. Not clocks — water. I know where the seep runs on my wall and when it is strongest. I know the stalactite above my ledge by its rhythm: a drop every forty breaths, arriving with a faint *tik* on the stone lip that I have come to treat as punctuation. I drink from a natural catchment cup that the seep fills overnight. I eat the fungal fans — the older ones are better, denser, more mineral, tasting of the deep rock the water has passed through. I have tried the small blind flesh-creatures that swarm near the basins and found them edible and without character. I do not eat the sket-runners. Their hierarchies are too legible; it would feel like eating a sentence.

What I have become is not quite what I was. My eyes have adapted — not to see in darkness, exactly, but to stop demanding light. The bioluminescence of the Assemblage is enough: the mint-green drift of the pachyderms, the cold sapphire flicker of the chets, the faint violet suggestion of the high-vault swarm. It is dim the way starlight is dim — adequate, once you stop asking for more. My hearing has changed more profoundly. I can distinguish the chet-calls now — not all of them, not with precision, but enough to know alarm from gossip, enough to know when the alarm-notes are *old* (the two-foot warning, passed down through generations, triggered by my own silhouette when I first arrived, now faded to a habitual murmur) and when they are *new* (rare, sharp, meaning something has shifted in the seep pattern or an Ancient has moved unexpectedly).

I have not yet witnessed a Migration. I am waiting for it the way the Ancients wait — not with anticipation but with availability. I have read the accounts, and I have heard the chets' sound-patterns that seem to encode the memory of it, a specific shriek-harmonic they practice sometimes at the volume of ordinary chatter, as though rehearsing. I know that when it comes I should climb — up, away from the floor, above the chaos of falling and crawling and flying things. I know my shelf is high enough. I know the Ancients will pass below me like weather, and the pachyderms will trundle with their green lights swaying, and the noise will be a pain that lives in the teeth and the nose and the bones, and then it will be over, and the far wall will bloom, and the abandoned wall will gape, and the fungi will arrive like a second tide, and the Hollow will continue.

I will watch. I will not interfere. I am not part of the Assemblage, but I am no longer entirely outside it. I have been here long enough that the chets no longer sound their two-foot alarm when I move — only the habitual murmur, the inherited note, a formality. The sket-runners' lines pass near my ledge and do not deviate. A velvet eolith has been slowly extending one edge of itself toward my shelf for weeks, at the pace of lichen, and I have not decided whether to permit it or relocate. I think I will permit it. I think I would like to be, eventually, what the Hollow makes of things that stay: something patient, something still, something that the chets' grandchildren will inherit alarm-notes against and then, in time, forget to sound.

I am the listener. The Hollow speaks in water and chitter and the long silence of the Ancients, and I am learning the grammar. I will not finish learning it. That is fine. The seep does not forget, and neither, I think, will I.
