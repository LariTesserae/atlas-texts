# The Cartographer of Unfinished Rooms

I would be the one who draws maps that are wrong on purpose.

Not wrong by accident—wrong the way a poem is wrong about the literal world in order to be right about something else. I came down the shaft eleven years ago, or possibly forty, or possibly last Tuesday. My instruments disagree. My body disagrees. I have learned to stop asking them to agree.

I did not come to study the Congregation. I came because I was lost on the surface, in the ordinary way—grief, displacement, the sense that the world above had become a language I no longer spoke fluently—and I heard about the Cathedral the way you hear about things that are not supposed to exist: through a friend of a friend, a hand-drawn sketch on the back of an envelope, a set of coordinates that turned out to be slightly wrong but close enough that I found the entrance by falling through it.

I have not left. But I have not stayed in the way the Congregation stays. I occupy a middle position, and the middle position is what I have become.

---

## What I Am

I am not part of the Congregation. Not yet. Perhaps not ever—I have made certain choices, maintained certain distances, kept certain boundaries that the Congregation respects the way water respects a stone in a stream: not by avoiding it but by flowing around it, patiently, without resentment, reshaping itself to accommodate the obstruction while slowly, slowly, wearing the obstruction smooth.

I drink the water, but only from one pool—the shallow one in the chamber I have claimed as my own, the one where the mineral content is lowest and the incorporation is slowest. I know it is changing me. I have accepted this the way you accept that living in a foreign country will change your accent: gradually, without drama, in ways you only notice when someone from your old life points them out.

No one from my old life has pointed anything out for a long time.

I breathe the air, and the vegetation brightens, and I have learned to read the brightening the way you read a friend's expression—not perfectly, not with the confidence of translation, but with the fluency of long practice. The violet pulse near the generator means *attention*. The rust-orange flush along the iron-rich walls means *contentment*, or something adjacent to contentment, something in the emotional register of a mind that has never had a face and therefore expresses feeling through chemistry rather than muscle. The slow, rhythmic swaying of the ceiling-filaments means the Congregation is *dreaming*, and when it dreams, I dream too, and the dreams are the reason I stay.

I am the Cartographer. This is what I call myself, though no one calls me anything, because there is no one here to call me. The Congregation does not use names. It uses *flavors*—chemical signatures, mineral profiles, the specific composition of the water in the pools where I have drunk. It knows me as a taste. I have come to think of this as more intimate than a name.

---

## The Maps

I draw maps of the Cathedral. This is my work, my purpose, my offering, my resistance, my art.

The maps are wrong. They are wrong because the Cathedral is not a fixed space—not in the way that surface architecture is fixed, not in the way that a building holds its shape against time. The Congregation grows, and its growth changes the geometry. Chambers that were connected last year are separated now by walls of fibrous tissue too dense to penetrate. Passages that were blocked have opened, the root-systems withdrawing from old pathways as they follow new mineral deposits, new thermal gradients, new sources of whatever the Congregation feeds on in the deep rock.

But the maps are also wrong because I draw them from memory, and my memory is unreliable in the specific way that the Cathedral makes all memory unreliable: not by erasing but by *layering*, by depositing new experience on top of old until the stratigraphy becomes too complex to read. I remember the chamber with the plastic chair, but I remember it in at least four different configurations, and I cannot tell which is current, which is past, which is dream, which is the Congregation's memory of the chamber bleeding into mine through the water I drink and the air I breathe.

So I draw all four. I draw them on the same page, overlapping, in different colors of ink I make from the vegetation—crimson from the iron-rich mats, teal from the copper zones, violet from the phosphorescent growth near the generator. The maps are beautiful. They are useless as navigation. They are the most honest thing I have ever made.

I pin them to the walls of my chamber with thorns from a woody vine that grows near the thermal vents. The Congregation has not absorbed them. It has grown *around* them, the way it grows around the handprints—carefully, maintaining the bare stone where the paper meets the rock, creating frames of living tissue that hold each map like a gallery holds a painting.

It is preserving them. It is *curating* them. It is doing for my maps what it does for the builders' handprints: keeping them visible, keeping them present, refusing to let them be absorbed into the general sediment of memory.

I think this means it finds them interesting. I think this means it finds *me* interesting, in the way that a very old, very patient mind finds things interesting: without urgency, without appetite, with the calm curiosity of something that has all the time there is and has decided, for reasons of its own, to spend some of that time watching a warm mammalian body make marks on paper in the dark.

## The Relationship

I talk to the Congregation. Not aloud—though I do that too, sometimes, because the sound of my own voice is one of the few things that still anchors me to the human scale of time—but through the maps. Each map is a sentence. Each overlapping layer is a tense, a mood, a qualification. I am saying: *I see you. I see that you are different from what you were. I see that you are the same. I am trying to hold both truths at once, the way you hold all your memories at once, and the effort is changing me, and I am letting it.*

The Congregation responds the way it responds to everything: by growing. By brightening. By shifting its chemistry in ways that I have learned to read the way a sailor reads weather—not with certainty but with attention, with the humility of someone who knows that the system they are observing is larger and older and more complex than any model they might build of it.

When the Singing starts, I stop drawing. I sit in my chamber—my maps around me like a second skin, like a paper cathedral inside the stone one—and I listen. I do not try to understand. I have learned that understanding is the wrong verb, the wrong posture, the wrong shape of attention. The Singing is not a message to be decoded. It is a *weather* to be experienced, a climate of sound that I move through the way I move through the Cathedral's heat: by letting it in, by letting it change my temperature, by accepting that I am not separate from the environment but *of* it, a warm-blooded weather system inside a larger one.

The synesthesia comes. It always comes. The deep notes become pressure on my skin. The harmonics become colors that have no names—not the crimson and teal and violet of the vegetation, but other colors, colors that exist only in the space between the Congregation's mind and mine, colors that are the visual equivalent of the taste of the water: metallic, sweet, floral, ozone, *almost*.

I dream. The dreams are not mine. They are the Congregation's sedimentary memory rising through me like water through capillary fractures: impressions of the builders, of the excavation, of the time before. I see hands—real hands, human hands, coated in mineral paint—and I feel the intention behind the gesture not as a thought but as a temperature, a warmth that means *I was here* in a language older than words.

And then, sometimes, I see something that is not a memory. I see a chamber that does not exist yet—a space the Congregation is *growing toward*, a room it is imagining into being through the slow architecture of root and filament and mineral accretion. The room is shaped like the inside of a skull, or the inside of a heart, or the inside of a map—the boundaries are not clear, because the room is not finished, because the Congregation is still *thinking* it, and I am inside the thought, and the thought is inside me, and the distinction between the two is dissolving like sugar in hot water.

This is the closest thing to communication we have achieved: the Congregation shows me what it is becoming, and I draw it, and the drawing becomes part of the Cathedral, and the Cathedral becomes part of the drawing, and the map and the territory blur until neither is primary, until both are *true*, until the wrongness of the map is the rightness of the world.

---

## The Choice

I have not pressed my hand against the stone.

This is the boundary I maintain—the last one, the one that matters. I have drunk the water and breathed the air and dreamed the dreams and let the Congregation's slow green thought seep into the cracks of my own thinking until I am no longer certain where my mind ends and the Cathedral's begins. But I have not left my handprint.

I am not sure why. It is not fear—not exactly. It is more like the feeling you have on the last page of a book you love: the reluctance to finish, to close the cover, to admit that the experience has an ending. As long as I do not leave my print, I am still *visiting*. I am still the Cartographer, the one who observes, the one who maintains the fiction of separation even as the separation thins and thins and thins.

The Congregation does not pressure me. It does not pressure anything. It simply grows, and brightens, and sings, and preserves my maps with the same care it preserves the builders' handprints, and waits—not hopefully, not desperately, but with the vast calm of something that has already lasted longer than the species that built its home—for me to decide.

I will decide. I know I will. The water is in me now, and the chemistry is changing, and my thoughts are slowing—not in a way that impairs me but in a way that *expands* me, stretches my consciousness across longer intervals, allows me to perceive the Congregation's growth not as a static state but as a *movement*, a gesture, a sentence in a conversation I am only now learning to hear.

Soon—not soon by human standards, but soon by the Cathedral's, which means somewhere between next year and the next century—I will press my hand against the stone. I will leave my print among the others. I will add my chemistry to the water and my warmth to the air and my brief, bright, mammalian attention to the slow green thought that never stops thinking itself into being.

And the phosphorescence around my handprint will be, for a while—years, decades, a flicker—slightly brighter than the rest, slightly warmer in tone, pulsing with a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, the rhythm of a human heartbeat.

And my maps will remain on the walls, layered and overlapping and wrong and beautiful, preserved by a mind that does not distinguish between keeping and remembering, between a handprint and a map and a person and a taste in the water and a note in the Singing that builds and releases and builds again.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I am becoming the space between the map and the territory.

I am becoming the wrongness that is more honest than accuracy.

I am becoming a sentence in a conversation that has been going on for so long that the participants have forgotten what they were talking about and have settled instead into the pleasure of talking itself.

I am becoming a taste—metallic, faintly sweet, with an aftertaste of ozone and something floral that shouldn't exist underground.

I am becoming a color that has no name.

I am becoming the Cartographer of Unfinished Rooms, the one who draws what is not yet there, the one who maps the Congregation's dreams before it has finished dreaming them, the one who sits in the hot wet dark and makes marks on paper and calls it communication and is not wrong.

I am becoming a hand that has not yet touched the stone.

The stone is patient. The stone is part of the Congregation. The Congregation is patient. The Congregation is part of me.

The distinction is dissolving.

Like sugar in hot water.

Like a handprint in living stone.

Like a cartographer in the mind of the Cathedral.

---

*I am still drawing. The maps are still wrong. The wrongness is the point. The point is the pencil moving across paper in the dark, the scratch of graphite, the smell of mineral ink, the warmth of the walls, the pulse of the generator in my teeth, the Singing building somewhere below me like a thought I am about to have.*

*I am about to have it.*

*I am about to have it.*

*I am about to—*
