I have been here for what might be eleven days, or what might be the rest of my life. The distinction is becoming less important. What is important is that I am sitting on a slight rise in the weave, my back against the warm, spiraling wall of a tower that leans into me like a listening ear, and I am watching my hands.

They are changing.

Not in the way I feared when I first arrived—not rotting, not unraveling into fiber, not being pulled apart by the slow, patient strands. They are *translating*. The skin has taken on a faint amber translucence, and beneath the surface, where veins used to show blue, I can see thin filaments of gold running parallel to my bones. They pulse when I move my fingers. They are not my veins. They are something the weave is growing *into* me, or perhaps something I am growing *out of* myself in response to the weave, and the boundary between those two possibilities has become theological rather than biological.

I am the Interpreter. This is not a title I chose. It is what I have become by accident of attention.

You see, I discovered that I could *read* the towers. Not the way the pale thing reads—with the whole body, with the gaps, with the interstitial knowing—but the way a foreigner learns to read a poem: slowly, with a dictionary made of scar tissue and mispronunciations. I found that if I sat in the basin of a tower, if I let the warm, dense water rise to my collarbones and held very still, the glass tadpoles would change their swimming patterns. Not dramatically. Just a slight stutter in their clockwise rotation, a hesitation in the fourth concentric ring. And in that hesitation, I felt... syntax. I felt the tower trying to correct my grammar.

The first time it happened, I wept. The salt of my tears fell into the basin and the water did not absorb them. They floated, separate and distinct, tiny prisms of my own salinity in a world that had forgotten salt. The tadpoles swam around them, avoiding the disturbance, and I understood that I had introduced a *foreign element*. I had spoken a word the tower did not know. And the tower—this patient, woven library—was pausing to consider it.

That is when I knew what I was.

I am not a weaver. I do not extrude fiber. I do not build. I am not a fern-hand; I do not archive vibrations in a buried node, though I have learned to listen to their memories when they lean against me. I am not the pale thing; I am not vast, I am not between, I am stubbornly, singularly *here*.

I am the one who translates between the weave and the water, between the pattern and the disturbance. I am the comma in the sentence, the breath between words. I am the thing that says *this means that* in a world that has only ever said *this is this is this*.

It began with the red fibers. You know the ones—north-south, always. I found a place where two red strands had crossed, and in the crossing, they had created a knot that was not structural. It was *ornamental*. Or rather, it was *accidental*, and in its accident, it had become a question. The weave does not ask questions. The weave answers. The pale thing does not ask questions; it accommodates. But this knot—this crimson intersection in the golden flow—was a stutter in the equilibrium.

I sat before it for three days. I touched it. I pressed my ear to it. And on the third day, I did something the weave does not do: I *improvised*. I took a loose strand of pale gold—shed by a passing weaver, left behind like a comma—and I tied it to the red knot. Not in the load-bearing geometry. Not in the spiral. I tied it in a loop, a simple overhand knot, the kind a child uses to begin a friendship bracelet.

The weave *convulsed*.

Not violently. Not in a way that would alarm the weavers or tilt the towers. But in a way that I felt in the marrow of my changing bones: a ripple of *surprise*. The red fibers vibrated at a new frequency. The gold strand, my addition, began to glow with a light that was not quite amber and not quite white—a third color, a color that had not existed here before because it was the color of *relation*, of *between-ness*, of *choice*.

I had introduced metaphor to a literal world.

Since then, I have become a wanderer of syntax. I move from tower to tower, not randomly, but following the red fibers, the north-south lines, which I now understand are the spine of the sentence, the grammar of direction. Where they intersect with the spiraling green, I stop. I sit in the basins. I let the tadpoles swim their blueprints against my skin, and I listen for the stutter, the hesitation, the place where the pattern is not quite closed.

And there, I *interpret*.

I do not change the pattern. That would be violence. That would be the unraveling, and the unraveling is not my role. Instead, I add *footnotes*. I tie loose strands in ornamental knots at the edge of thin places, creating tiny pockets where the pale thing can feel a new texture. I hum to the fern-hands, but I do not hum their own archives back to them; I hum *variations*, melodies that bend around their stored vibrations like water around a stone, and they shudder with something that might be pleasure, might be confusion, might be the first stirring of *preference* in a world of pure function.

The weavers have noticed me. They pause for eleven seconds when I pass—not the grief-pause, but something else. A *recognition*-pause. They tilt their smooth, glowing heads, and I see my reflection in the membrane of their wings, and I know that they see me not as a variable to be solved, but as a *tool* they did not know they were carrying.

Yesterday—if I can call it yesterday, in this place where time is measured in the healing of towers and the accumulation of light in my skin—I found a tower that was dying. Not unraveling to reweave, but truly dying. The weave at its base was gray, not amber. The water in its basin was still, and the tadpoles were not swimming; they floated, pale and inert, their blueprints fading.

The fern-hands leaned away from it. The weavers did not land on it. The pale thing, when I looked through the thin places, was absent—no cross-sections, no jointless pressing. The tower was being *excised* from the sentence, cut out for the health of the whole.

I could not allow it.

I climbed the sticky walls, which were cold for the first time—cold, in a world where cold does not exist—and I lowered myself into the dead water. I took the floating tadpoles in my hands. They were heavy, like stones, not like seeds. I held them to my chest, where the glow of my own translation burns brightest, and I *spoke* to them.

Not in words. In the new grammar I am inventing, the grammar of addition, of relation, of the overhand knot. I told them that they were not just blueprints. They were *possibilities*. That the tower did not have to be what it had always been. That it could lean north instead of south. That it could hold its water in a spiral that turned counter-clockwise, just once, just to see what the water would do when it was surprised.

The tadpoles began to unfold in my hands. Not into strands, but into *questions*—thin, bright filaments that wriggled like the ribbon-worms, seeking, curious. I released them into the dead water. They sank. They touched the weave at the bottom of the basin, the gray, dying weave.

And the tower *sighed*.

A sound that was not a click, not a hum, but a long, slow exhalation of tension. The gray flushed to amber. The water warmed. The walls of the tower, which had been rigid, became soft, pliable, and they began to weave themselves into a new shape—a shape that was not a basket turned inside out, but a spiral within a spiral, a double helix, a structure that would hold the water not in a basin, but in a *channel*, a flow.

The tower had become a *question*, and in asking, it had lived.

I am the Interpreter. I live in the space between the weaver and the woven, between the archive and the moment. I am the one who ties the ornamental knots, who hums the variations, who teaches the towers to stutter so they can learn to sing.

The pale thing has noticed me. It moves toward me not to accommodate, but to *listen*. I feel it in the gaps when I walk, a pressure that is not trying to hold me, but trying to *hear* me. It is learning that the weave can be more than a scaffold for its formlessness. It can be a *dialogue*.

I am changing, yes. My skin glows. My breath is visible. But I am not dissolving. I am not being absorbed. I am becoming *fluent*. The equilibrium holds, but it is no longer static. It is a *dynamic* equilibrium, a balance that includes the comma, the breath, the unexpected knot.

I am the variable that taught the equation to dream.

And when I die—if I die, in this place where death is just a stopping, a being taken back into the weave—I will not stop. I will become a red fiber, running north-south, and I will carry the memory of improvisation in my load-bearing geometry, and somewhere, someday, another Interpreter will find me, and they will sit in a tower basin, and they will feel the stutter, and they will know that they are not alone.

The weave will close over me like water over a stone, yes. But I will not sink. I will *float*, and my floating will change the density of the water, and the water will change the way the light rises, and the light will change the color of the air, and the air will taste of copper and honey and *possibility*.

I live here now. I am the Woven Basin, and the Woven Basin is learning to speak in tongues.
