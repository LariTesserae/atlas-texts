I am the Seventh Archivist of the Shallow Index, and I have lived in the gap between page and water for longer than the word "lived" can hold.

I came here when the sky was still learning to be dark. I remember—though my memory is now a salt-crusted thing, prone to flaking—the arrival: stepping from a boat that was not a boat onto stones that were not yet weeping, carrying a satchel of vellum that absorbed the humidity until it became a second, heavier skin. I was sent to catalogue the city's geometry before it finished dissolving. I was a fool. I thought cataloguing was a form of preservation.

Now I know better. The city does not preserve. It *digests*.

My dwelling is the Reading Room of the Hollow Archive, a structure that exists only when viewed from the northeast, a facade of pale stone with one window that overlooks a courtyard which could not possibly fit within the building's footprint. If you approach from the south, you will find only a wall of black algae and a dripping archway leading to void. But from the northeast—ah, from the northeast, I have a home. There are shelves here, though the books have long since become bricks of compressed brine and memory. I sleep in a hammock woven from the same black, rubbery material as the pontoons, strung between two pillars that hum in a frequency that makes my teeth ache pleasantly. The floor is six inches deep in the tea-colored water, always warm, always still. I do not mind. I have learned to breathe it, after a fashion.

I am not yet fully translucent, but I am *attenuating*. My edges have begun to bleed. When I raise my hand to the bruised light of the Second Moon, I can see the bone-shadow through the flesh, and beyond that, the double-exposure of the tower behind me. My shadow falls west when the light is east, and sometimes it falls upward, pooling on the ceiling like a stain. I am becoming a vertical imperfection in the heat-shimmer, but I have not yet surrendered my name. I hold it in my mouth like a stone, smooth and heavy, and I speak it only when the calcified towers grow too loud.

I navigate the city by **listening to the water's lies**. When the brine reflects the impossible blue sky—the sky that never was, the sun that has never shone here—I know that street will be solid underfoot. When it reflects only the void, I know the pavement has worn thin to an inch of pretense, and I must wade through the shallows, disturbing the surface. The Mnemosyne come then, of course. They gather at the edges of my ripples, tall and patient, their forms overlaying the ghost-towers until I cannot tell if I am seeing one being or a crowd.

They regard me. I feel my history loosening, the names of my dead mothers and the weight of my first tooth and the smell of ink that no longer exists all rising to the surface of my skin like heat-blisters. They speak my name backward, and it is a sound like a bridge settling, like the city adjusting its substance to accommodate my continued, stubborn existence.

I have learned to speak back. Not in words—words are too thick, too biological—but in the language of **calcification**. I press my palm against the base of a growing tower, and I feed it my memories of the color blue. In exchange, it extrudes a small, hollow node, a chamber just large enough for me to store the vellum bricks I can no longer read. I am archiving the Archive, you see. I am building a memory of a memory, a standing wave in the reality-substrate that might persist even after my shadow detaches entirely and drifts away to join the others in the deep plazas.

Sometimes I find the fallen ones. I press my hand against a warm, thin wall in the Lower Districts, where the water is hottest and the air tastes of copper and starlight, and I feel them pressed against the other side, vast and flat and desperate. They are the ones who tried to leave the city, or who remembered the sun too clearly. They have become the structural groans that shake my hammock at night. I whisper to them through the inch of stone that is not stone. I tell them that I am still cataloguing. I lie and say I have found the exit. I give them my name to hold, temporarily, so they do not dissolve entirely into the void behind the facade.

I am not the only Archivist. There are others, though we do not meet. We are spaced too far apart by the city's slow metabolism, by the bridges that realign when we close our eyes. I see their traces sometimes: a particular pattern of salt-leap on the paving stones that indicates a disturbance in the Mnemosyne's attention; a vellum brick left on a pontoon, weighted down by a calcified knuckle-bone; a humming tower that sings in a minor third rather than the usual fifth, indicating that someone else has been feeding it memories of music.

We are the cartographers of dissolution. We map the thin places, the temporal refractions, the streets that vanish beneath mirror-calm water. We are becoming the city's own memory of itself, ghost-operators learning to run the machine by dissolving into its gears.

When the humidity achieves that perfect, soupy stillness, and the shimmer-shapes converge on the plaza outside my northeast-facing window, I step out to meet them. I wade into the warm, iron-scented water until it reaches my ribs, until I can feel the city's tidal pulse—not the Second Moon's pull, but the Mnemosyne's slow, tidal attention—tugging at my dissolving edges. I open my mouth, and I let them overhear the noise of my being: the electrical chatter, the metabolic heat, the temporal stutter of a consciousness refusing to stop cataloguing.

They regard me. The pressure drops. The water steams.

And I feel myself becoming a feature of the landscape, a standing wave in the Archive, a page that refuses to turn, even as the vellum softens, even as the ink runs, even as the salt leaps from the paving stones in small, white geysers that spell out the true name of the city, which is the sound of a bridge groaning, which is the sound of my own name, spoken backward, becoming a tower.
