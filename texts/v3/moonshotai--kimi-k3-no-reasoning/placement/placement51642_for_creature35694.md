I am the sound of a single drop falling from a leaf to a stone basin below.

Not the water itself, but the sound. The *plik*.

If you are standing on the Condensation Terraces, near the junction where the Mid-Reach fades into the higher, wetter air, you might hear me. I am the interval between events, the punctuation mark in the long sentence of the mountain. I am what happens when patience accumulates until it must, briefly, become action.

Before I was a sound, I was a warmth in the air. I was a breath exhaled by someone in the Lower Gardens, or the steam rising from a cup of bitter tea in the Mid-Reach, or simply the wetness of the morning. I rose because the structure called me upward—the sun-heated ceramic creating rivers of warm air that flow upslope, always upward, toward the cool faces of the high terraces. I traveled for days, perhaps, or weeks, suspended in the bright haze, drifting past the Brightworks where mirrors tilted to catch the sun, past the humming engines that push the air through the mountain's hidden lungs.

I came to rest on the underside of a canopy leaf—a broad, waxy thing, cupped like a hand, belonging to a tree whose roots are older than the memory of stone. The leaf was cool; the night had been long, and the ceramic of the terrace had radiated its heat away into the thin, high air. I beaded there, with others. We gathered. We became a single swelling weight, a lens that caught the light and held it, trembling.

The leaf was one of thousands on the tree, and the tree was one of hundreds in its basin, and the basin was a structural element holding up the terrace above. My weight grew. The surface tension that held me—that held me as a sphere, a world, a brief perfection—strained against the waxy cuticle. The leaf tilted. The angle changed by a fraction of a degree.

And then I fell.

The drop is short. Perhaps three feet. The air is warm and humid, thick with the scent of moss and the dust of ceramic. I fall past a fern frond that uncurls toward me but does not catch me. I fall through a shaft of light redirected by the Brightworks above, and for a moment I am incandescent, a small bright thing against the green. I fall toward a stone basin—an old collection point, its ceramic worn smooth by centuries of impacts just like mine.

When I strike, I become myself.

*Plik.*

The sound is small, but in the dense soundscape of Mount Vex—in the hum of the engines, the calls of the birds, the murmur of slow water—it carries. It is a high, clear note, a bell-tone that resonates against the pale walls and the iron railings. It says: *here is water.* It says: *the cycle continues.* It says: *something has moved.*

In the Lower Gardens, a water-tender pauses in her work, her hand on a channel gate. She hears me, or feels me—a vibration in the ceramic underfoot, a change in the pressure of the air. She does not look up. She knows what I am. She smiles, perhaps, or simply nods, and opens the gate a fraction wider. The water in her channel flows a little faster, a little deeper.

In the canopy above, an arboreal mammal—one of the monkey-cats, all grasping hands and bright eyes—freezes in its leap. It heard me. It recognizes the sound of water, of potential, of the slow life that sustains it. It resumes its movement, but altered, its path curved now toward the basin, toward the moisture, toward the place where I have landed and become stillness again.

I am the rhythm of the mountain. I am the way time is measured here—not by the sun, which is always bright, or by the engines, which are always humming, but by the intervals between drops. I am the event that is not an event, the drama that is not dramatic, the change that proves that nothing changes, and that everything is always changing, slowly, slowly.

I fall again. I am always falling. I am the patience of Mount Vex made audible, and I am here, and I am now, and I am enough.
