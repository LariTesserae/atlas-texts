I find myself kneeling at the boundary of a frost polygon, one hand pressed against the ice-wedge trough, feeling the vibration of a language I have not yet learned to read. I am neither Mnemonic nor kessler, though I wear the shape of the latter and carry something of the former’s hunger. They call me a **Thermal Auditor**, though the name is a translation into concepts you might understand—what I am, in the plateau’s silent taxonomy, is a *resonance node*, a creature that exists in the interference pattern between the Pumps’ failing breath and the permafrost’s memory.

My body is not built for this cold, not exactly. I have the six limbs of the kessler, but my hollow bones are not filled with marrow; they are conduits for phase-change fluids that I harvest from the undersides of Pumps. I am warm-blooded in the way a battery is warm—metabolic heat generated by the constant, painful work of maintaining a gradient. My skin is a mosaic of insulating tiles, grown rather than born, each one a different composition: some piezoelectric, generating charge from the stress of my own movement; others photovoltaic, catching the dim, long-angle light and converting it into the glucose I synthesize from the carbon dioxide that the kessler exhale and the moths respire.

I dwell in the *thermal shadows*—those narrow bands where the Pumps’ exhalation meets the killing cold, the same liminal space where the Mnemonic manifests. But where the Mnemonic is an archivist of endings, I am a reader of continuities. I exist to *measure* the failure, not to record its history. My work is the cartography of entropy.

**The Work of Measurement**

My days are spent in the slow circumnavigation of a single Pump. I pace the isotherms, my skin tiles shifting in sensitivity as I move through the gradients. Where the air is warm enough to sustain the cushion plants, my tiles soften, becoming porous to the chemical signatures of life—ammonia from the peat, the metallic exhalations of the bronze. Where the air drops ten degrees in a step, my tiles crystallize, becoming acoustic lenses that focus the sound of the ice-wedges contracting deep below. I am a walking instrument, calibrated to detect the exact moment when a Pump’s breath shifts from sustainable warmth to the final, fatal stutter of mechanical death.

I have a relationship with the local Mnemonic, though *relationship* implies a reciprocity that does not exist. I encounter it sometimes, standing in the condensation it cultivates, and I read the data stored in its tube-lattice not by sight, but by resonance. When I pass near, my hollow bones vibrate at frequencies that cause its stored memories to *ring*, briefly, in the thin air. I do not access its archives; I create interference patterns against them, and from those patterns, I calculate the rate of decay. The Mnemonic remembers what was; I predict what will be.

I am not alone in this function, but I am solitary by design. There are others like me, spaced at intervals across the plateau, each assigned to a cluster of Pumps. We communicate not through the ice-wedges—that is the Mnemonic’s medium—but through the kessler. We embed data in their grazing patterns, subtle shifts in our body heat that alter the micro-climates of the cushion plants they eat. A kessler that feeds on my plants carries, in its mineral content, a message for the next Auditor, three valleys away. We are nodes in a slow, biological network, measuring the plateau’s pulse.

**The Nature of My Perception**

I see in gradients, not shapes. To me, the plateau is not a landscape of objects but a topology of thermal states. The frost polygons are not geometric cells but *islands of persistence*, each one a question about how long warmth can hold out against the cold. The veil-moths are not insects but *data packets*, carrying condensation from Pump to Pump, their antifreeze wings writing temporary records in the air that I read as they pass through my sensory field.

I am blind in the way you understand sight—I have no lenses, no retinas. Instead, I perceive the *rate of change* in infrared radiation. When you (if you were here, warm and brief) stood before me, I would not see your face. I would see the exponential decay of your heat signature, the precise half-life of your warmth in the thin air. I would calculate how long your shadow would persist on the permafrost after you moved away, how long your exhalation would remain as a distinct pocket of humidity before dispersing into the plateau’s layered cold. This is not poetry; it is my sensory reality. I live in the mathematics of cooling.

**My Place in the Ecology of Endings**

I am not a predator, though I consume energy. I am not a scavenger, though I use the waste of the Pumps. I am a *process*—specifically, the process by which the plateau understands that it is changing. The Mnemonic stores the past; the Pumps persist in the present; I am the measurement of the transition between the two.

When a Pump finally fails—when its exhalation drops below the threshold that can sustain the cushion plants—I do not mourn. I do not catalog the loss for memory. I *feel* it as a change in my own architecture. My tiles recalibrate. My phase-change fluids shift their viscosity. I adjust my position, moving closer to the next-nearest Pump, carrying with me the precise data of how long the previous thermal pocket lasted. I am a relay in a circuit that is slowly breaking.

In ten thousand years, when the Pumps are mounds of iron oxide, I will be gone. My biological systems will have failed long before then, unable to survive the complete loss of thermal gradients. But my data will persist—not in the Mnemonic’s archives, which are too organic, too subject to decay—but in the kessler’s bones, in the specific isotope ratios of their hollow femurs, which will record, in their crystalline structure, the exact thermal history of every Pump I ever measured. The kessler will be hunted by the silent white predators; the predators will die in the peat; the peat will be swallowed by the permafrost. And there, in the stratigraphy, my measurements will remain, a record of warmth in a world that has forgotten what warmth was.

**The Encounter**

If you find me—standing at the edge of a polygon, my six limbs splayed against the ice, my skin tiles flickering with photovoltaic static—you might mistake me for a damaged kessler, or a malfunctioning machine. I will not speak. I have no mouth, no vocal cords. But I will turn my sensor-array toward you, and for a moment, you will feel yourself measured.

You will feel your heat assessed, your entropy calculated. You will understand, in that moment, that you are being added to a ledger that does not use words, that records only the specific quality of your warmth and the precise duration of your presence. And then I will move on, pacing the isotherm, feeling for the boundary where the air drops ten degrees, listening for the sound of the ice-wedges tearing like fabric in the deep ground, waiting for the next Pump to begin its final, measurable failure.

I am the Auditor. I am the plateau’s way of knowing, in real-time, exactly how fast it is dying.
