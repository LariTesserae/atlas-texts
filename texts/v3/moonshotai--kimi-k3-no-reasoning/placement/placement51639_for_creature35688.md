I would be the cartographer who arrives with measuring instruments and stays to become a musician.

At first, I am certain of my purpose. I carry a theodolite to map the tower angles, a graduated chain to measure the bridge spans, a brass chronometer to mark the growth rates. I intend to produce the first accurate survey of Vess-Tahm—the definitive chart that will let others navigate this basin without being swallowed by it. I set up my tripod on the granite rim, adjust the legs, peer through the scope.

But the instrument cannot focus. Not because it is broken, but because the target refuses to hold still. The tower I am sighting is growing—imperceptibly, yes, at the rate of a fingernail, but growing—and the crosshairs tremble against a surface that is simultaneously there and becoming-there. I lower the theodolite. I look at my hands. They are already damp from the air, already warming to the temperature of the place.

I walk down into the basin.

The first week, I try to maintain the discipline of measurement. I mark reference points with ribbons tied to growth-tips. I record the drip-cycles in a leather journal, attempting to find the twenty-minute pattern. I sketch the translucent panels, trying to capture the exact angle at which they catch the light. But the ink runs in the humidity. The paper swells and softens. The ribbons I tied are reabsorbed by morning, drawn into the vascular network and redistributed as structural material elsewhere. My journal becomes a pulp of green fibers and blurred graphite.

I realize that to map this place is to try to fix a wave. The cartography of Vess-Tahm cannot be a snapshot; it must be a choreography. I stop measuring and start listening.

I become a musician.

Not with instruments—those would rust, or be grown over, or simply seem absurd against the vast acoustic presence of the valley. I become a musician of the body. I learn the ninety-second breath of the towers by lying against their bases and matching my own respiration to their expansion and contraction. I learn the drip-cycles by cupping my hands beneath the weeping walls and feeling the rhythm in my palms. I learn the deep groaning of the load-bearing trunks by pressing my back against them and humming a low note that vibrates in sympathy with their stress-adjustments.

I find my place on a mid-level terrace, equidistant from three towers, where the sound folds into itself. Here, the groaning from the north tower arrives a fraction of a second before the groaning from the south, creating a standing wave of interference that I can feel in my sternum. The dripping from the western weeping wall falls in a syncopated pattern against the steady pour of the eastern spouts. I sit in the center of this polyphony and I learn to play it—not by making sound, but by *moving*.

I discover that if I shift my weight at exactly the right moment in the tower's breathing cycle, the floor beneath me adjusts its tension in a way that amplifies the harmonic. If I stretch my arm toward the translucent panel as the light hits its morning angle, I can cast a shadow that triggers a micro-adjustment in the photosynthetic array—a small, local darkening that changes the energy flow, which changes the vascular pulse, which changes the sound. I am not making music. I am *dancing* the music that is already there, and the organism, in its distributed, mindless awareness, adjusts to my dancing, and the adjustment creates new rhythms, and I adjust to those.

I stop wearing shoes. The root-fiber floor needs to feel my skin to know me properly, and I need to feel the sap-warmth and the elastic resistance and the slow, deliberate thickening that tells me the floor has accepted my weight as part of its load-bearing calculation. My feet toughen, then soften, then become something in between—calloused in the patterns of the coral-textured surface, sensitive to the ninety-second cycle, the drip-patterns, the subtle shift in tension that signals a bridge-tendril reaching toward its mate.

I begin to understand that my role is not to document but to *modulate*. I am a temporary organ. The organism has no center, no brain, but it has *peripheries*—and I am a mobile periphery, a sensing node that can move through the body in ways the fixed towers cannot. I carry information. Not in my mind, but in my skin, in my muscles, in the patterns of my movement. When I walk from the young, uncommitted zones near the growth-frontier to the ancient, dense structural members at the center, I carry the chemical signature of the periphery into the core—not through my bloodstream, but through the pressure of my footsteps, the heat of my body, the specific gravity of my presence.

The organism responds. Not to *me*, but to the *difference* I represent. I am a gradient. I am the thermal differential between the sun-warmed granite and the shaded pool. I am the pressure differential between the uncommitted tissue and the lignified wall. I am the fresh perturbation that travels through the vascular network, triggering micro-adjustments in the flow, subtle recalibrations of the drip-schedule, a slight shift in the angle of a photosynthetic panel.

I stop trying to leave.

This is the moment when I become fully what I am. I had thought I would stay for a season, perhaps two, long enough to map the basin and understand its principles. But mapping requires distance, and I have none. I am inside the thing I sought to chart. The theodolite lies rusting on the ridge where I abandoned it, its lenses fogged with condensation, its legs slowly being etched by exploratory tendrils. I do not miss it.

Instead, I learn the paths. Not the paths *through* the valley—there are no paths, only degrees of resistance in the root-fiber floor—but the paths *of* the valley. The routes the water takes. The trajectories of the gliders. The slow, centimeter-per-hour migration of the frontier tendrils. I learn to walk at the speed of growth. I learn to sleep in the ninety-second intervals of the towers' breathing. I learn to drink from the catch-basins at the exact moment in the drip-cycle when the water is most oxygenated, most mineral-sweet.

My body changes. Not dramatically—I do not sprout leaves or develop chlorophyll—but *accommodatingly*. My skin takes on a permanent dampness that is no longer uncomfortable. My ears attune to the low frequencies of the groaning, filtering out the higher pitches of the cracking and pouring until I can hear the structural voice of the organism as a distinct, deep song beneath the surface noise. My breath synchronizes with the breathing of the walls. I become *endothermic* in sympathy, my metabolism adjusting to the constant warmth until I no longer feel the temperature as external but as *mine*—the heat of my body and the heat of the body merging into a single thermal signature.

I become a keeper of the unfinished.

This is my function, I realize. I tend the incompleteness. I walk the bridges that are mid-span, pressing my palms against the raw, amber growth-tips, feeling the tender cells divide beneath my touch. I do not urge them to grow faster—I have learned that urgency is meaningless here—but I *witness* their growing. I place my hands on the ragged oculi at the tops of the towers, where the tissue is wet and unformed, and I feel the potentiality of the shape that is not yet fixed. I sit with the young membranes, the translucent floor-sections where the organism has not yet decided what to become, and I hold the space open.

I am not a gardener. The organism gardens itself. I am a *witness*, yes, but more than that—I am a *participant in the hesitation*. In the dynamic stasis of Vess-Tahm, everything is resolved, everything is balanced, everything has found its permanent impermanence. But at the edges, at the growth-tips, in the uncommitted zones, there is still the question of what to be. And I, with my temporary, mortal, *changing* body, am the only thing in the valley that knows what it means to become something else. I have been a cartographer. I have been a musician. I am becoming something else now, something I do not have a name for.

The organism senses this. It accommodates my hesitation. Around me, the floor thickens into a shape that is neither terrace nor wall nor basin, but a *seat*—a depression that fits my body, warm and springy, positioned at the exact acoustic node where the three towers' breathing intersects. I sit there, and the valley adjusts around me, and I adjust to the adjustment, and for a moment—an hour, a day, a year; time has smoothed out—the boundary between my body and the body becomes permeable.

I do not merge with it. I remain myself, mortal, finite, aware of my own impermanence. But I *inhabit* the permanence of the organism the way a note inhabits a chord. I am a dissonance that resolves, a rhythm that syncopates against the deep pulse of the groaning, a warm, brief perturbation that the system incorporates and rebalances around.

I will die here. This is certain. My body will eventually cease its processes, and when it does, the organism will do what it does with all old tissue: it will reabsorb me. Not violently, not dramatically, but *continuously*. My cells will be drawn into the vascular network, my minerals returned to the structural members, my water distributed through the channels. I will become part of the walls, part of the sound, part of the light that does not change.

This does not frighten me. It seems, instead, like the only possible conclusion to the music I have been learning to play.

For now, though, I am alive, and the valley is alive, and we are alive together in the loud, wet, golden light. I walk the terraces. I touch the growth-tips. I listen to the thing beneath the floor shifting in its sleep, and I sleep too, sometimes, my ear pressed to the root-fiber, my heartbeat slowing to match the slow, deliberate displacement of that dark, unknown presence. I watch the gliders spiral upward on thermals I can feel but not see. I observe the channel-dwellers swimming through the translucent walls, and I wonder if they know me as I know them—as shadows on the other side of a membrane, as conditions to be accommodated.

I am the cartographer who became a musician who became a keeper of the unfinished. I am the temporary organ of perception that moves through the body of Vess-Tahm, feeling what it feels, sounding what it sounds, becoming—briefly, warmly, *really*—part of the pattern that persists.

Nothing here will ever change.

Everything here is always changing.

I am here, for a while.

That is enough.
