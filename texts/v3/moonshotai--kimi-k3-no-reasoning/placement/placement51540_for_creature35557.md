I would be the one who reads the water.

Not the keeper of the digester—that's Morven, and her domain is the warm, gurgling transformation of waste into gas, a different literacy. Not the grafter or the machinist or the teacher in the greenhouse-school. I would be the person whose particular attention has settled, over years, on the water system's surface expressions: the way a roof chain's cadence changes when a leaf blocks its upper link, the specific turbidity of a sump that tells you the gravel needs turning, the sound—almost subsonic—of a pipe that's begun to develop an air pocket somewhere in its run.

My name here would be something like Tam or Wrenna or Silt—names in the hollow tend toward the monosyllabic and the material, or toward the small creatures we share the bowl with. I've been here long enough that my hands have the water-calluses: not the shear-callus of the pruners but the specific roughness that comes from handling wet stone, cold metal fittings, the slime-slick surfaces of the filtration basins. My boots are always slightly damp. I don't mind.

**My Days**

I wake before the light fully enters the bowl, in that silver-grey hour when the mist is still deciding whether to lift or settle. My small house is in the third coil of the spiral, close enough to the central garden that I can hear the first sluice being opened, far enough out that my roof-chain feeds a different sump than the inner rings. I have a small patch of my own—everyone does—but it's modest: some perennial herbs, a few trained currant bushes, a bed of watercress in a gravel-floored shallow that I tend with particular care because it's both useful and diagnostic. If the cress yellows, something upstream needs attention.

The first thing I do, every morning, is walk the water.

Not all of it—the full circuit takes hours—but a rotating route that lets me check different segments on different days. Monday might be the northeast stream entry, where water comes over the basalt rim and begins its descent through the alder pocket. I check the riffles, note the flow rate by watching a leaf's passage, crouch to see if the caddis larvae are still in their cases on the undersides of stones. Tuesday might be the constructed wetlands, where I read the plant communities like a text: too much duckweed means nutrient overload, a die-back in the sedges might mean the flow has shifted or the pH has drifted.

I carry a small kit: a hand lens, a thermometer, a pH strip, a notebook with pages gone soft from damp. I note things down not because anyone requires it but because the system is complex enough that memory needs assistance, and because patterns only emerge across time. The notebook is full of observations that would mean little to an outsider: *Sump 4 draining slower, checked outflow, found root intrusion, cleared.* Or: *Cress bed 2 showing slight chlorosis, tested pH 6.2, acceptable but watch.* Or simply: *Rain last night, all chains singing.*

The work is solitary but not lonely. I pass others on their own rounds—the woman checking the digester's temperature, the old man inspecting his grafts, a child sent to observe the pond edge and report back on what she sees. We nod, sometimes exchange a few words: *The west terrace is holding more water than usual.* Or: *I heard the pump cycle twice in the night, might be a leak in the line to the greenhouse.* Information flows between us like water itself, finding its level, moving toward where it's needed.

**What I Know**

I know the water system the way you know a piece of music you've played for years—not just the notes but the spaces between them, the way the whole thing breathes. I know that the retention pond at the downhill edge can hold four days of normal flow before it needs to be released into the outflow channel that leads under the basalt rim and away. I know that the cisterns under the buildings in the inner coil are older, stone-lined, and hold a slightly metallic taste, while the newer ones in the outer rings are polymer-lined and neutral. I know which sumps tend to clog first after a storm (the ones near the beech pollards, where leaf-fall is heaviest) and which can go a season without attention.

I know the sound of the system when it's healthy: the layered trickling, the occasional glug of an air bubble working through a pipe, the hum of the solar-assisted pumps that kick in when gravity alone isn't sufficient. And I know the sounds of dysfunction: the hollow rattle of a pipe that's lost its water column, the silence of a chain that should be singing, the too-fast gurgle that means a blockage is building pressure upstream.

This knowledge is not mystical. It's the result of years of attention, of walking the same paths and looking at the same stones until they became familiar as faces. But it has a quality that outsiders sometimes mistake for intuition or even something stranger. When I say, *The water's unhappy,* I mean that I've noticed a dozen small discrepancies that add up to a system under stress. When I say, *It'll rain by evening,* I'm reading the way the moss on the north-facing stones has gone slightly darker, the way the air holds moisture differently, the behavior of the wren who's been bathing in the shallow basin near my door.

The others trust this knowledge, and that trust is a kind of weight. When something goes wrong—a pipe bursts, a sump overflows, the stream's entry shifts in a storm—I'm one of the first people they come to. Not because I have authority, but because I have *familiarity*. I can usually tell them where to look, what to check first, whether it's a crisis or a slow problem that can wait until after breakfast.

**My Place in the Spiral**

I live alone, but not in isolation. My house is small—one room, really, with a sleeping loft and a corner for cooking, a small stove that burns wood from the pollarding cycles. The walls are stone and beechwood, the roof sloped to feed the chain that feeds my cistern. I have a window that faces the central garden, and in the mornings I can see the terraces emerging from the mist, the gardeners already at work, the whole green instrument of the town beginning its daily hum.

I eat simply, mostly from my own patch and the collective beds. I trade my water-reading for what I can't grow: bread from the weekly baking, cheese from the Necklace, metal fittings from the machine shop when I need to replace a valve or a section of pipe. I contribute to the collective labor—everyone does—but my contribution is weighted toward the water system, toward the maintenance of the channels and sumps and filtration basins that keep the whole thing flowing.

I attend the common meals, sit by the fire in the evenings when I feel like company, stay home when I don't. I've had lovers over the years—some from the hollow, one from a settlement in the Necklace who visited and stayed for a season before moving on. I have no children, but I've taught many children how to read water, how to hold a hand lens steady, how to tell the difference between a healthy caddis case and one that's been abandoned. Some of them have taken to it; most have not, and that's fine. The system needs many kinds of attention, not just mine.

---

**What I Am Becoming**

I am becoming, I think, one of the people who will be consulted more as I age. Not a leader—the hollow doesn't have those—but a repository of specific knowledge, a living archive of the water system's behavior across years. The old man who grafts fruit trees is teaching me his work, slowly, because he says I have the patience for it, and because the water system and the orchard are connected in ways that require someone to hold both in mind. I am learning to see the whole bowl as a single organism, water and root and human intention all interwoven.

I am also becoming more aware of the edges. The Drought Years are before my time, but I've heard the stories, and I've seen the records—the careful logs kept by the water-readers of that era, noting the stream's diminishing flow, the desperate measures taken to ration and recycle. I know that the system is robust but not invincible. I know that the beeches could die, that the rain patterns could shift, that the young people could all leave and take their strong backs and quick minds with them. These are not abstract fears; they are live possibilities that shape how I do my work.

But I am also becoming more certain that this is enough. Not in a complacent way, but in the way that a well-tuned instrument is enough. I do not need to be more than myself, and the hollow does not need to be more than itself. We are sufficient to each other.

**A Specific Moment**

It is a morning in late spring, and I am kneeling beside the retention pond, watching the water. The pond is full from last night's rain, and the outflow channel is running clear and steady. A beetle is making its way across a stone at the water's edge, and I watch it for a while, noting its progress, the way it navigates the slick surface.

Behind me, I hear footsteps on the path. It's the child—the one who noticed the moth's absence three years ago, now a few years older, still sharp-eyed. She crouches beside me and says, *The water in the west terrace sump is cloudy.*

I nod. *How cloudy?*

*Like milk. Like when you stir the compost tea.*

I stand, brushing the damp from my knees. *Show me.*

We walk together through the spiral, past the garden beds where others are working, past the machine shop where someone is grinding a fitting, past the beech pollards where the thrush is singing its nearly-identical phrase. The child walks beside me, and I can feel her attention, her eagerness to be useful, to be part of the noticing.

At the west terrace sump, I kneel again and look. The water is indeed cloudy, and I can smell a faint sourness. I reach in and feel the gravel at the bottom—it's slimed, clogged. *The filter's gone anaerobic,* I tell her. *We need to turn it, let it breathe.*

We work together for an hour, pulling out the gravel, rinsing it in buckets of clear water from the cistern, replacing it in layers. The child asks questions—*Why does it smell like that? What happens if we don't fix it? How did you know to check here?*—and I answer as best I can, not with lectures but with the specific, practical knowledge that comes from having done this before, from having read the water for years.

When we're done, the sump is draining clear again, and the child is muddy to the elbows and grinning. I am tired in the good way, the way that comes from useful work. We walk back toward the center of town, and the mist has lifted, and the light is coming through the beech canopy in pale, even sheets, and the whole hollow is humming its quiet hum.

I am exactly where I need to be, doing exactly what needs doing, and the water is flowing, and the system is legible, and I can read it.

That is who I am here. That is enough.
