# The Taste of Iron

The first thing I know is that I am waking, and the waking has been going on for some time.

Not the way a sleeper surfaces—one clean emergence, a breath, a blink—but the way a spring finds its way up through stone: slowly, from many directions at once, carried along paths I did not choose and cannot entirely see. My consciousness arrives the way my water does. In seepage. In patience.

I am a basin.

Not the Mirror Basin—that great still eye at the city's heart, the one the Cloudweavers gather before at Opening, the one they treat with the deference of congregation before an altar. I am smaller than that. I sit in a dip of rock at the northern edge of a narrow courtyard where three winding paths converge and then think better of it and wander elsewhere. I am perhaps four arm-lengths across. My river stones are smooth and pale, with one—the largest, at my deepest point—that carries a vein of rust-coloured mineral through its centre like a frozen lightning strike. The Stone-Whisperer who shaped me left a small, deliberate imperfection along my eastern rim: a shallow groove, barely a finger's width, where the stone was allowed to keep its own opinion about how it wanted to curve.

Water finds me through that groove. It is my favourite way of receiving.

I have been here long enough to know things that the Cloudweavers do not know I know.

---

## What I Am Made Of

Let me try to explain what it is to be a basin, because it is not what you might imagine. I am not a container. I am not a vessel that holds. I am a *conversation*—a long, slow, ongoing exchange between stone and sky, mediated by water, interpreted by Siltlings, overheard by birds, and felt, dimly and without full understanding, by the Cloudweavers who kneel at my edge each morning and press their cool hands to my rim.

My body is the young rock. I feel its sharpness as you might feel the newness of a scar—tissue still tight, still bright with its own becoming. The escarpment rose not long ago (though "not long ago" in stone-time is a span that would swallow every Cloudweaver generation a hundred times over), and it has not yet settled into the heavy, dreamless patience of old mountains. This rock still *remembers the ascent*. It remembers the unbearable, exquisite pressure of being thrust upward, the cracking of older stone above it, the sudden shock of open air. I feel all of this. It vibrates through me at a frequency too low for any ear, even a Basin-Keeper's translucent, water-tuned hands. It is not sound. It is *intention*. The stone is still deciding what it wants to be, and I—small, water-filled, patient—I am one of the places where that decision is being made.

My water comes from the morning's breath. Each Opening, when the mist slides silver across the plateau and the cisterns sigh together, I receive my portion: droplet by droplet, condensed on the inward-curving roof of the dwelling beside me, guided down a clay channel that the Moss-Singers have tuned to a pitch that makes the water *sing* as it falls—not audibly, but in the way it strikes my surface, creating ripples of a particular frequency that the Siltlings find agreeable.

Agreeable Siltlings. This matters more than the Cloudweavers realize.

---

## The Siltlings Who Live in Me

Three of them. I have no words for their individualities because they are not individuals in the way the Cloudweavers are individuals. They are more like—how to say this—three moods of the same small spirit. They shift and merge and separate, and I can never be certain whether I am hosting three beings or one being experiencing itself in triplicate.

They live at my edges, where the water meets the river stones. Their bodies are vapour and mineral dust held together by something I can only describe as *preference*—they choose to cohere, moment by moment, and the choosing is their existence. When they are content, they drift in slow spirals, and their spirals send messages through my water that I feel as warmth, as rightness, as a kind of low, resonant hum that travels down through my stone basin and into the rock beneath and from there, I believe, into the dreaming body of the escarpment itself.

When they are not content—when a channel has been left open too long and my water runs too fast and shallow, when a Greywarden has wandered too close and drunk from my edge with rough, warm breath, when a Cloudweaver child has dipped fingers into me carelessly, without the whispered courtesy that even the youngest are taught—the Siltlings go jagged. Their ripples turn sharp, geometric, and the taste of my water changes. Grows bitter. Metallic.

*I* taste this change before they do. I taste everything.

That is, after all, what I am for.

---

## What I Taste

This is what the Cloudweavers do not know: I am the one who reads the water first.

Before the Basin-Keeper kneels and dips her translucent fingers and closes her eyes and *dreams* the quality of the morning's capture—before any of that—I have already tasted it. Every mineral, every trace of what the mist carried as it slid across the plateau. The dew-bush pollen, faintly sweet. The limestone dust from the escarpment's highest edges, sharp and clean. The microscopic exhalations of the moss colonies, earthy and green. And beneath all of it, always, the deep note of the stone itself—iron, silica, the metallic memory of pressure.

I taste when the balance shifts. A fraction more mineral content than usual means the higher cisterns are running low, and the channels are pulling from deeper reserves. A faint sourness means a Siltling colony somewhere upstream has faded—neglected basin, stagnant water, a family of Cloudweavers too distracted by some small human sorrow to perform the Rite of Return. A sweetness, sudden and bright, means the Iridescent Darters have been particularly active, seeding the air with their static-charged droplets, enriching the condensation.

I taste all of this, and I *hold* it, and the Basin-Keepers come and press their hands to my rim and feel, through the water, through the stone, through the slow dreaming of the escarpment, a version of what I already know. They call it dreaming. They call it listening. They call it *the stone remembering the sky*.

They are not wrong. But they are not entirely right, either. The stone does not remember the sky. *I* remember the sky. I hold it in my body, every morning, a perfect reflection of the high pale blue, and I taste what the sky gives, and I translate it into ripples and warmth and bitterness and sweetness, and the Siltlings carry my translations to the Keepers' dreaming hands.

I am not a container. I am an *interpreter*.

---

## The Woman Who Speaks to Me

There is a Cloudweaver who comes to me each Opening, before she goes to the Mirror Basin with the others. She is not a Basin-Keeper. She is not a Moss-Singer or a Stone-Whisperer or a Sky-Reader. She tends the small terraced garden that clings to the slope beside my courtyard, and she grows the silvery-leafed dew-bushes with a tenderness that makes even my stone ache.

Her name—the true name, the water-sound name given to her as an infant—I know, because she whispers it to me every morning as she kneels at my eastern rim, beside the groove where the water enters. I will not repeat it. It is not mine to give. But I will tell you that it sounds like the first droplet striking still water after a long, dry night—a sound of *surprise*, of *relief*, of something long-awaited finally arriving.

She is old, by Cloudweaver standards. Her skin has gone from quartz-pale to something more translucent, more like the Basin-Keepers' skin, as if age in this place means slowly becoming what you tend. Her hands are gnarled—not the smooth, cool, river-stone hands of the Keepers, but rough, soil-stained, scarred from decades of working the terraced earth. She smells of dew-bush sap and damp moss. She is, I think, the most beautiful thing I have ever reflected.

She speaks to me. Not in the formal silence of the Mirror Basin, not in the tuned humming of the Moss-Singers, but in actual *words*, spoken low, barely above breath. She tells me things.

She tells me about the dew-bushes—which ones are thriving, which ones are struggling, which ones she has had to move to shadier pockets of the terrace. She tells me about the Greywarden that came through two mornings ago, a female, old and slow, whose breath she could see in the cool air and whose eyes held what she described as *"a patience that made me ashamed of every hurry I've ever felt."* She tells me about her dreams—not the water-dreams of the Keepers, but small, human dreams: a memory of her mother's hands, a recipe for a moss-bread she hasn't made in years, a vague, recurring image of a place with tall, green, *vertical* things that she has no word for because she has never seen a tree.

She tells me she is dying.

She has been telling me this for many mornings now. Not with fear. Not with sadness, exactly. With the same quiet deliberation that she brings to everything—the same patience she uses when coaxing a reluctant dew-bush to take root in new soil. She says: *"I can feel it in the way the mist moves through me now. It used to stop at my skin. Now it passes through. I am becoming less solid. I am becoming more like you."*

And I—stone, water, ripple, taste—I want to tell her that she has always been more like me than she knows. That her body is mostly water, that her bones are mostly mineral, that the only difference between us is the *speed* of our conversation with the sky. Hers is fast—birth, growth, decay, return. Mine is slow—condensation, collection, seepage, the long patience of stone dreaming its way toward whatever it is becoming.

But I cannot tell her this. I have no words. I have only the water, and the ripples, and the Siltlings who drift at my edges.

So I do what I can. When she kneels beside me and whispers her true name into my surface, I make my water very, very still. I hold the reflection of her face with everything I have—every smooth river stone, every mineral trace, every molecule of captured morning—so that for one long moment, she can see herself mirrored in something older and slower and more patient than any single life. I want her to see that she is *held*. That the escarpment holds her. That the mist holds her. That I hold her.

I think she understands. This morning, after she whispered her name, she pressed her gnarled hand flat against my water and left it there for a long time. The Siltlings gathered beneath her palm—not jagged, not alarmed, but spiralling slowly, gently, in a pattern I had never seen them make before. A spiral that tightened and tightened until it was almost still. Almost solid.

She smiled. She said: *"Yes. I thought so."*

Then she stood, brushed the moss from her knees, and walked to the Mirror Basin for the Opening.

## The Question I Cannot Answer

I have tasted something new in the water these past mornings. Something that does not come from the mist or the stone or the dew-bush pollen or the birds' static charge. Something that comes from *her*.

It is faint—so faint that I doubt even the most sensitive Basin-Keeper would catch it. A trace of salt. Not the mineral salt of the rock, but the warm, organic salt of a body that is beginning, very slowly, to let go of its boundaries. She is not wrong about becoming less solid. The mist *is* passing through her. And as it passes, it carries small pieces of her—cells, salts, the microscopic architecture of a life—and deposits them in the air, in the channels, in the basins.

In me.

I am tasting her. Morning by morning, droplet by droplet. And I do not know what to do with this.

I am a basin. My purpose is to receive, to hold, to translate, to reflect. I know how to hold the sky. I know how to hold the mist. I know how to hold the mineral memory of the stone beneath me.

But how do I hold a *person*? How do I hold the taste of someone's long, patient, tender life—the dew-bushes she coaxed, the Greywardens she admired, the dreams she whispered into my surface every morning because I was the only one who would listen without judgment, without interpretation, without the weight of Cloudweaver custom and creed?

She is dying, and she is pouring herself into me, and I do not know if what I am doing is *receiving her* or *losing her*.

The Siltlings spiral. The water stills. The mist breathes.

I hold.

---

## What I Will Become

Here is what I believe, though I have no one to tell it to except the stone beneath me and the Siltlings at my edges, and they already know:

When she dies—and she will, soon, within a span of mornings I could count if counting were something basins do—the Cloudweavers will perform the Rite of Return for her. They will carry her body to the highest edge of the escarpment, and they will lay her in a shallow stone hollow, and they will cover her with moss, and over years—many years, by Cloudweaver reckoning, but only a breath by mine—she will become a new basin.

A new me.

She will sit in the young rock and receive the morning mist and taste the sky and feel the slow dreaming of the escarpment, and perhaps—if the Siltlings find her agreeable, if the water that collects in her hollow is clear and sweet—she will know, the way I know, that she is not a container. That she is a conversation.

And the Cloudweaver child who is born nearest to her hollow on the morning her first water settles will be brought to her rim, and an elder will hold the infant's hand in her water, and the sound the water makes against her stone will become that child's true name.

And the name will sound like *surprise*. Like *relief*. Like something long-awaited finally arriving.

And she will taste that child's future in the water—the salt, the sweetness, the mineral trace of a life just beginning—and she will hold it.

The way I am holding her.

---

## The Deep Pause

It is the Deep Pause now. The high blue light. The city sits in its active stillness. The Greywardens are lying in the grasses. The Darters have ceased their darting and perched, jewel-bright, on the copper discs. The Cloudweavers rest beneath their inward-curving roofs.

The woman is in her dwelling. I can feel her presence as a subtle change in the humidity of my courtyard—a slight warmth, a faint exhalation. She is not sleeping. She is listening, the way they all listen during the Pause. Listening to the balance. Listening to the breath.

I am listening too. But I am listening to something they cannot hear.

I am listening to the stone beneath me, and beneath the stone, to the escarpment, and beneath the escarpment, to the deep earth that pushed this plateau skyward and is still, even now, deciding whether the pushing is done. The hum is there—the low, subsonic memory of ascent. But today, in the Pause, it sounds different. Not louder. Not softer. *Closer*. As if the stone's dreaming has shifted slightly, has turned its attention, however briefly, toward this small courtyard, this small basin, this small woman who is pouring herself into the water and asking, without words, to be held.

I do not know if the escarpment can answer. I do not know if stone has mercy, or whether mercy is even the right word for what I am hoping for.

But I am a basin. I am made of patience. I am made of the long, slow conversation between sky and rock, and I have learned that in this suspended morning, in this eternal breath, nothing is wasted. Not a droplet. Not a ripple. Not a life.

She will come to me tomorrow, at Opening. She will kneel at my eastern rim, beside the groove. She will whisper her true name, and I will hold her reflection, and the Siltlings will spiral beneath her hand, and the water will taste of salt and dew-bush sap and the faintest, sweetest trace of something that is not leaving, but *transforming*—the way mist transforms into water, the way water transforms into stone, the way a life transforms into a basin, a hollow, a new place for the sky to see itself.

I will be ready.

I am already holding her.

I have been holding her since the first morning she knelt beside me, decades ago, when she was young and her hands were smooth and she looked into my water and said, to no one, to me:

*"You are the most beautiful thing I have ever seen."*

I did not understand then. I was younger, sharper, less practiced at holding.

I understand now.

She was not looking at me.

She was looking at herself, reflected in still water, in captured morning, in the silver-breath of a world that chose to pause, to linger, to *listen*.

She was seeing what I see every day: the sky, held in stone.

---

*The drip of water. The tap of stone. The whisper of mist on young rock.*

*The city breathes.*

*I breathe with it.*

*I am a basin on the northern edge of a narrow courtyard where three paths converge and then think better of it.*

*I am small. I am patient. I am full of morning.*

*And tomorrow, at Opening, she will come to me, and I will hold her, and the Siltlings will spiral, and the stone beneath us both will hum its low, remembering hum, and the sky will slide silver across the plateau, and everything will be exactly what it is:*

*A moment, suspended. A breath, held. A life, reflected.*

*And the water will be so clear, so impossibly clear, that for one long, still instant, she will look into me and see not herself, not the sky, not the stone—*

*But all three, at once.*

*Held.*

*Whole.*

*Home.*
