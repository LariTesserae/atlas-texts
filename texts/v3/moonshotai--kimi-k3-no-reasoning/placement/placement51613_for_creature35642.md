I was a cartographer of the immediate, a surveyor of the pause. In this place, the ground didn’t just sit beneath you—it ascended, slowly, inexorably, pushing toward the thin, freezing sky with a violence that took centuries to manifest. To live here was to exist in a state of constant negotiation with a world that hadn't yet decided what it was going to be.

Suspended across the void was one of the main lines—a bridge of carbon-lattice that shimmered with a faint, internal luminescence. Even through the copper-threaded mesh of my suit, I felt the shock—a sharp, stinging arc of static discharge that danced across my knuckles. It was a greeting. A confirmation. *I am here. The line is live.*

I was Kith. I was a custodian of the friction.

My home was a cluster of self-repairing ceramic spheres, tethered to the central conduit by thick, insulated cables. We didn't build upward—we built inward, sprawling across the flat, shielded shelves of the plateau. If the ground shifted, the Nest would sway, its internal dampening systems compensating for the movement, like a bird’s nest rocking in a gale. 

Inside, the air was warm and smelled of ozone and recycled oxygen. The walls were etched with fine, silvery lines—micro-scars from the static discharges that frequently arced between the city-grid and the atmosphere. We didn't hide these scars. We traced them with our fingers. They were beautiful. They were a record of survival, proof that we had stood close enough to the raw power of the mountain to feel its breath.

My work was the maintenance of the immediate. I spent my High Intervals walking the transit conduits, checking the integrity of the carbon-lattice bridges that spanned the growing canyons. I monitored the tectonic sensors, feeling for the vibrations of the crust through the soles of my boots. If I detected a spike in magma pressure—a sudden, high-frequency tremor that felt like a toothache in my jaw—I would signal the grid. A brief, violent illumination that turned the mist into a strobe-lit theater of white and gold.

I was never alone, though I often felt profoundly isolated. I shared the plateau with the herds. The thermal-furred grazers moved in slow, deliberate waves across the high meadows, their breath pluming in the cold. They fed on the engineered lichens, their metabolisms synced to the rhythmic pulses of the city’s power lines. I watched them from my ridge. They were biological sensors. If they grew restless, if they began to migrate toward the higher ridges, I knew that the magnetic levitation tracks were experiencing harmonic interference. I followed them, living in a slow-motion migration that mirrored the shifting of the tectonic plates.

I was a keeper of the transient. I knew that my home was being built beneath me, and that at any moment, the slow-motion violence of the planet’s growth could render me obsolete. Yet, I did not live in fear. I lived in a state of intense, hyper-aware presence. I understood that in a world of rising stone and glowing wire, the only thing that mattered was the integrity of the connection. 

I stood on the ridge, watching a squall line—a mile-long wall of freezing vapor—roll across the plain. It collided with the energy-dampening fields of the city-grid, creating a shower of static discharge that danced across the rocks. It was a cold, clean, terrifying potential. I was a part of it. I was the vibration in the wire, the heat in the vent, the stubborn, dark carpet of moss gripping the stone. I was the pulse between heartbeats.
