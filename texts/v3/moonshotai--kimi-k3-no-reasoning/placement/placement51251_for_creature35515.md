# Where the Water Settles

The first thing is the water, because it is always the first thing here. This is true of the landscape and it is true of me, and I have never been able to separate the two facts, because I am not separate from either.

---

I know where I am before I open my eyes. I know it through my back, my shoulders, the backs of my thighs, the soles of my feet. I am lying on a sleeping platform — woven sedge, thick as a forearm is long, layered over a frame of fitted stone, covered in a living mat of moss that has been growing on this particular platform for longer than I have been alive. The moss is damp. Of course it is damp. Everything is damp. The moss presses its particular texture against my skin — cool, soft, faintly springy — and that texture tells me which platform this is (the third platform in the fourth passage of my cluster's innermost ring, the platform I have slept on for the past two seasons), and it tells me who is near me (three bodies, one on each side and one at my feet, their breathing slow and their skin warm where it touches mine), and it tells me what the night has done (the condensation has been heavy; the moss is saturated; the platform's woven frame is slightly swollen with absorbed water, which means the walls will need checking along the southern passages, where the seep tends to rise first).

I know all of this before I open my eyes, and when I do open them, the visual information is almost an afterthought. Grey light. The underside of a sedge roof, woven and re-woven so many times that the individual stems have blurred into a continuous surface, like the inside of a wave. The curve of a shoulder beside me, grey-green, darker along the spine, rising and falling with a breath so slow it seems geological. The light is what it always is: soft, diffuse, shadowless, the particular quality of illumination that comes from a sun behind a high, even overcast, filtered through air that is thick with moisture. It is not dim. It is not bright. It is the light of the plateau, and it is the only light I have ever known, and I do not experience it as a limitation.

I am one of the people of the weeping stone. I have no name that could be spoken. My name is a frequency — a specific resonance that my chest cavity produces when I thrum, a vibration at forty-three hertz with a slight irregularity in the second pulse, a stutter that I was born with and that makes my signature unmistakable. The community knows me by this stutter. When I press my palm to the ground and thrum softly — *I am here* — the ground carries my stutter to every creature in the cluster, and they know it is me, and they know where I am, and this is enough.

---

I rise from the platform slowly, because there is no reason to rise any other way. The bodies beside me register my movement through the platform's frame — a shift in tension, a change in the vibration signature of the woven sedge — and they adjust, filling the space I leave, their sleep unbroken. This is how it works. We are a single organism in the mornings, a distributed body that shifts and adjusts without waking, and I do not experience my departure from the platform as a separation. I am still touching them. I am always still touching them, even when my skin is not in contact with theirs, because the ground connects us, the water connects us, the plateau connects us, and separation is a concept that has no physical reality here.

I move through the passage. It is narrow — my shoulders brush the sedge walls on both sides — and the floor is flat stone, slick with condensation, and my feet know every stone. Not visually. By touch. The specific texture of each stone, the specific pattern of its fit against its neighbors, the specific way it sits in the peat beneath — this is the map, and I carry it in my feet and my palms and my knees and the broad flat planes of my thighs where the walls brush against me as I pass.

The passage curves. It curves because the water curves, because the channel that runs alongside the passage follows the natural drainage of the land, and the passage was built to follow the channel, and the channel was built to follow the water, and the water follows the land. Nothing here is straight. Straightness is an imposition, a forcing of geometry onto a landscape that has no use for it, and we do not impose. We negotiate. The passage curves, and I curve with it, and my body knows the curve before I reach it, because my body knows every curve in the cluster.

I reach the communal pool at the cluster's center. It is large — perhaps twenty paces across — and it is dark, the water stained nearly black by tannins and peat, and its surface is still. The wind, which is constant everywhere else on the plateau, drops here, caught by the sedge walls that surround the pool on three sides, their woven surfaces absorbing the gusts the way the peat absorbs sound. The pool is a place of stillness within a place of stillness, a quiet at the heart of the quiet, and I enter it the way I enter everything: slowly, without ceremony, letting the water take my weight.

The water is cold. Not painfully cold — I have never experienced water as painful — but cold in the way that the plateau is cold, the ambient coolness of altitude and overcast and perpetual moisture. My skin responds the way it always responds: a brief tightening, a flush of increased blood flow to the surface, and then the slow, comfortable adjustment as my body and the water reach an agreement about temperature. I sink until the water is at my collarbones, until my feet are resting on the pool's bottom (peat, soft, slightly yielding, with the specific granular texture that tells me this pool has been accumulating sediment faster than usual, which means the channel that feeds it has been running heavy, which means the seep rate upstream has increased, which means the condensation season is beginning).

I sit in the water, and I press my palms flat against the pool's bottom, and I listen.

Not with my ears. With my palms, with the soles of my feet, with the skin of my back where it touches the water, with the broad flat surfaces of my thighs where they rest against the peat. I listen with my entire body, because my entire body is an organ of listening, and what I am listening to is the plateau.

## What the Water Says

The water is talking. It is always talking. This is not a metaphor, not a poetic conceit — it is a physical fact. The water moves through the ground and through the rock and through the peat and through the channels and through the pools, and every movement produces vibration, and the vibrations travel through the saturated ground the way sound travels through air, and my body receives them.

What I hear, sitting in the communal pool with my palms on the bottom, is the current state of the plateau, expressed as a complex, overlapping, continuously shifting field of vibration.

The seep is the base note. It is always there: a low, steady, almost subliminal pulse that corresponds to the rate at which water is emerging from the rock faces and entering the ground. Today the seep is strong — stronger than it was yesterday, stronger than it was last week. The pulse is faster, deeper, more insistent. This confirms what the moss on my sleeping platform told me: the condensation season is beginning. The sky is contributing more moisture, the rock is weeping faster, the water table is rising.

The pools are the middle register. Each pool has its own vibrational signature, determined by its size, its depth, its bottom composition, the rate at which water is entering and leaving it. The communal pool I am sitting in has a deep, slow resonance — it is large and deep and its outflow is restricted by a stone weir that we built (that my grandmother's grandmother built, and that we have maintained ever since) to keep the water level high enough for communal gathering. The smaller pools scattered across the meadow have higher, faster signatures, and I can feel them through the ground, each one a distinct note in the chord that the plateau is playing.

The channels are the connective tissue. They carry water between the pools, and their vibrations are the most dynamic — they change with the flow rate, with the accumulation of sediment, with the growth of sedge along their banks. A channel that is running clear has a smooth, continuous vibration. A channel that is partially blocked has a stuttering, irregular vibration. A channel that is about to overflow has a rising, urgent vibration that any creature within a hundred paces can feel through the ground and that triggers, without any conscious decision, a response: someone will go to that channel and clear it, or widen it, or build a small weir to slow the flow. Not because they were told to. Because they felt it, and the feeling is the instruction.

And beneath all of this — beneath the seep and the pools and the channels — there is the deep vibration, the one that comes from the water table itself, the vast slow movement of water through the saturated peat and the fractured rock beneath. This vibration is so low, so slow, that it borders on the imperceptible. I feel it not as a pulse but as a pressure, a constant, gentle insistence against my skin that says: *the water is here, the water is here, the water is here*. It is the most fundamental thing I know. It is the first thing I ever knew. I felt it in the birthing pool, in the first moments of my life, before my eyes had focused, before my lungs had taken their first breath of air. I felt it through my skin, through the thin, permeable, newborn skin that was more membrane than barrier, and it told me where I was, and what I was, and what the world was.

The water is here. The water is here. The water is here.

## The Tending

I leave the communal pool when the light has shifted — not brightened, exactly, but changed in quality, the grey becoming slightly less dense, the diffusion slightly less complete. This is the plateau's version of morning, and it is the time when the work begins.

The work is not assigned. There is no foreman, no schedule, no list of tasks. There is only the tactile network — the constant, low-level exchange of vibration and touch that connects every creature in the cluster — and through the network, the awareness of what needs doing.

Today, what needs doing is the southern wall.

I know this because I felt it during the night, through the sleeping platform, through the ground: a change in the vibration signature of the southern passage, a subtle shift in the way the wall absorbs and transmits vibration. A healthy sedge wall — living, growing, its stems woven tight and its roots anchored in the wet ground — has a specific vibrational quality: dense, slightly elastic, with a faint high-frequency component that comes from the living cells' response to the water flowing through them. A wall that is dying back — a section where the sedge has been damaged, or where the water supply has been interrupted, or where the weave has loosened enough that the living stems are no longer in contact with the ground — loses that high-frequency component. It becomes duller, flatter, more like dead material than living.

The southern wall has a dead section. I felt it last night, and I feel it now, as I move through the passages toward it, trailing my palm along the walls as I go, reading them the way a physician reads a pulse.

The dead section is not large — perhaps a body's length, in the lower portion of the wall, where the sedge stems are oldest and most densely woven. I crouch before it, and I press my palms to the woven surface, and I feel the extent of the damage. The stems are dead — not just dormant, but dead, their cells collapsed, their fibers already beginning to soften in the constant moisture. The weave is still intact — the wall is not in danger of collapsing — but the dead section is a weak point, a place where the wall's structural integrity is compromised, and it will worsen if it is not repaired.

The repair is simple, in concept. The dead stems must be removed, and living stems must be woven in their place, and the living stems must be in contact with the wet ground so that they can root, and the weave must be tight enough that the new stems are held in place while they establish themselves. It is work that my hands know how to do the way my lungs know how to breathe: without thought, without instruction, with a fluency that comes from having done it hundreds of times, from having watched others do it thousands of times before that.

I begin. My fingers — thick, blunt, callused, with the dense, flattened pads that are somewhere between fingertips and hooves — work the dead stems free from the weave. They come away easily, their fibers already weakened, and I set them aside (they will be returned to the peat, eventually; nothing here is wasted). The gap in the weave is a dark, rough-edged hole, and I can feel the difference in the wall's vibration through the exposed edges: the living sections on either side are dense and resonant, the dead section is silent.

I reach for the living sedge that grows at the base of the wall — we encourage sedge to grow along all our walls, a ready supply of building material that replenishes itself — and I select stems. I select by touch: the right diameter, the right flexibility, the right length. I select stems that are alive, that are vigorous, that have the specific springiness that indicates healthy cell structure and good root establishment. I pull them from the ground with a slow, steady motion that brings the roots with them — not all the roots, but enough, enough that the stem will survive transplanting and will re-establish in its new location.

I weave the living stems into the gap. This is the work that my species was shaped for — not by design, not by intention, but by the slow, patient, inexorable pressure of evolution acting on a population of creatures who lived on a plateau where the only abundant building material was a tough, fibrous grass that grew in clumps on wet ground. My hands are the product of that pressure. My fingers are thick because thick fingers are stronger, and strength is what the weaving requires: the ability to force a stiff, resistant stem through a tight weave, to bend it without breaking it, to tuck it and fold it and press it into place with enough force that it stays where it is put. My palms are broad because broad palms distribute force, and the force needed to compress a woven wall, to make it tight and dense and waterproof, is considerable.

I weave, and the work is slow, and the slowness is not a limitation but a quality, a way of being that matches the pace of everything else on the plateau. The sedge grows slowly. The peat accumulates slowly. The water seeps slowly. The erosion proceeds slowly. And I weave slowly, each stem placed with care, each pass of my hands through the weave a deliberate, considered act that is also completely automatic, completely unselfconscious, as natural as the seep itself.

As I work, I thrum. Not a message — not a specific pattern with a specific meaning — but a low, continuous resonance, the stutter in my signature audible (feelable) as a slight irregularity in the vibration. I thrum because thrumming while working is what we do: it is how we maintain contact with the network, how we let the community know where we are and what we are doing, how we stay connected even when we are working alone.

The thrumming travels through the ground, and it is answered. From somewhere in the cluster — the northern passages, I think, near the birthing pools — another creature thrums back. A different frequency, a different signature: this one is smooth, steady, at fifty-one hertz, and I recognize it as the signature of an older creature, one of the keepers of the map, one of the ones who carry the deepest knowledge of the settlement's history. The response is not a message either. It is an acknowledgment: *I hear you. I am here. All is well.*

I continue weaving. The morning light filters through the grey sky and falls on the wet sedge and the wet stone and my wet hands, and the wind moves across the plateau above the walls and drops in the lee of the passage where I work, and the water seeps up through the ground beneath my knees, and the world is exactly as it should be.

## The Others

I am not alone for long. I am never alone for long. The passages are narrow, and the cluster is dense, and the others are always nearby.

The first to join me is a young one — not a newborn, but young enough that their skin is still smooth, still lacking the deep folds and the rust-mottling of age. They approach from the northern passage, and I know they are coming before they arrive: their footsteps produce a vibration that is lighter, quicker, more irregular than an adult's, a vibration that says *young, curious, still learning to move slowly*.

They crouch beside me, and they press their palm to the wall, feeling the repair I am making. Their touch is tentative — not uncertain, exactly, but exploratory, the touch of a creature who is still learning the vocabulary of the wall, still building the library of tactile sensations that will eventually become the map they carry in their body.

I do not instruct them. I do not demonstrate. I simply continue working, and they watch — with their eyes, yes, but primarily with their palm, which is pressed to the wall a hand's breadth from where my hands are working, feeling the vibration of each stem as I force it through the weave, feeling the change in the wall's resonance as the gap fills, feeling the difference between the dead stems I removed and the living stems I am adding.

They will learn. They are already learning. The learning is not a process that happens in discrete steps, with a beginning and an end and a measurable outcome. It is a process that happens the way water seeps into peat: continuously, passively, by the simple fact of contact. The young one's palm is on the wall, and the wall is teaching them, and I am the medium through which the wall's lesson is being transmitted, and this is how every generation learns what every previous generation knew.

After a while — I do not track time in units, but the light has shifted again, and my hands have completed perhaps half the repair — another creature arrives. This one is older, their skin deeply folded, their rust-mottling extensive, their movements slow and deliberate in the way that is particular to the very old: not the slowness of disability but the slowness of completeness, of a creature who has nowhere to hurry to because they have already arrived at everything.

They settle into the passage beside me — not crouching but sitting, their legs folded beneath them, their back against the opposite wall, their palms resting on the wet stone floor. They do not speak. They do not thrum. They simply sit, and their presence is a form of participation: the old ones carry the map most deeply, and their presence at a work site is a way of checking the work against the map, of ensuring that the repair is consistent with the wall's history, with the cluster's structure, with the water's intentions.

The old one sits, and the young one presses their palm to the wall, and I weave, and the three of us are a microcosm of the community: the old, the young, the working, the learning, the being. We do not need to speak. We are touching — through the wall, through the floor, through the ground, through the water that saturates everything — and the touching is the conversation.

## The Pool

The repair is complete. The gap in the wall is filled with living sedge, woven tight, the stems already in contact with the wet ground, already beginning the slow process of rooting. The wall's vibration has changed — I can feel it through my palms, through my knees where they press against the ground, through the skin of my thighs where the passage walls touch them. The dull, flat spot is gone. The wall resonates as a single, continuous, living structure again.

I rise, and the young one rises with me, and the old one remains seated — they will stay here for a while, feeling the repaired wall, updating their map, recording the change in the settlement's structure. This is what the old ones do: they are the memory, and the memory must be maintained as carefully as the walls.

I move through the passages, and the young one follows me, their footsteps close behind mine, their vibrations mixing with mine in the saturated ground. We are heading for the pools — not the communal pool at the cluster's center, but one of the smaller pools on the cluster's eastern edge, a pool that I know well, a pool that is, in the way that specific places are specific, *mine*.

It is not mine in the sense of ownership. Nothing here is owned. But it is the pool where I spend the most time, the pool whose temperature and depth and bottom composition I know most intimately, the pool where I go when I need to be in the water and the communal pool is too crowded, too full of the overlapping vibrations of many bodies. My pool — and I think of it as *my* pool in the way that a particular patch of sunlight might be *my* patch of sunlight, by habit, by affinity, by the simple fact of repeated presence — is a small, deep pool, perhaps three paces across, fed by a seep channel that runs along the base of a boulder the size of a sleeping platform.

The boulder is one of the large, half-sunken ones, one of the landmarks, one of the stones that have sat where they sit for longer than the settlement has existed. It is moss-skinned on its north face, lichen-crusted on its south, and it has a specific vibrational signature — a deep, slow resonance that comes from its mass and its density and the way it is anchored in the peat — that I have known since I was a newborn, since my mother first brought me to this pool and held me against her submerged body and let me feel the boulder's vibration through the water and the ground and her skin.

I enter the pool. The water is colder here than in the communal pool — the seep channel feeds it directly from the rock, and the rock's water is the coldest water on the plateau — but the cold is not unpleasant. It is bracing, clarifying, a cold that sharpens the tactile sense rather than dulling it. I sink until the water is at my chin, until only my face is above the surface, and I let the water support me.

The young one enters beside me. They are smaller, and the water reaches their chin sooner, and they bob slightly, their feet not quite reaching the bottom, their hands resting on the pool's edge for stability. They are learning to be still in the water, and the learning is not easy: stillness requires a specific kind of muscular relaxation, a willingness to let the water take your weight completely, to stop fighting the buoyancy, to trust that you will not sink (you will not sink — our bodies are adapted to this, our density close to the water's, our skin's slight adhesive quality providing just enough grip on the pool's walls and bottom to keep us anchored).

I press my palm to the young one's shoulder — a brief, firm contact that means *relax, you are safe, the water will hold you* — and they respond, their muscles releasing, their bobbing slowing, their body settling into the water's embrace.

We sit in the pool together, and we are still, and the stillness is not empty. The stillness is full: full of the boulder's vibration, full of the seep's pulse, full of the distant thrumming of the cluster, full of the specific, intimate sensation of another creature's skin in contact with mine, full of the water's constant, quiet insistence that it is here, that it is present, that it is the medium in which we live and the substance from which we are made.

## What I Am

I am one of the people of the weeping stone. I am a creature of the plateau, of the pools, of the peat, of the seep. I am shaped by the conditions of this place: the cool, the wet, the high, the sparse. I am what evolution produces when those conditions persist for long enough.

I do not have a word for what I am. I have a frequency — forty-three hertz, with the stutter — and I have a body, and I have a place in the network, and these things together constitute an identity that is as complete and sufficient as any name.

I do not think about the future. I do not think about the past. I do not think about the erosion that is slowly, grain by grain, carrying the plateau away. I do not think about the deep future in which the pools will drain and the peat will dry and the sedges will die and my species will adapt or migrate or die. These things are real, but they are not present, and presence is what I am. Presence is what I do.

I sit in the pool, and the water is cold, and the boulder's vibration is a deep, slow pulse that I feel in my chest, in my bones, in the specific resonance of my thrumming organ, which responds to the boulder's frequency with a faint sympathetic vibration that is not a thrum but a readiness, a potential, the way a string on an instrument vibrates in sympathy with a note played nearby.

I press my palm to the pool's bottom, and I feel the water table, and I feel the seep, and I feel the channels, and I feel the pools, and I feel the cluster, and I feel the creatures, and I feel the plateau, and the plateau feels me.

This is not a mystical experience. It is a physical one. The vibrations are real. The connections are real. The network of touch and resonance that links every creature to every other creature, and every creature to the landscape, and the landscape to the water, and the water to the rock, and the rock to the deep, slow processes of the earth — this network is as real and as tangible as the sedge walls and the fitted stones and the dark pools and the wet ground.

I am a node in the network. I am a point of contact between the water and the land and the community. I am a place where the plateau becomes aware of itself, not through thought, not through language, not through any of the abstract, representational processes that humans use to understand the world, but through the simple, direct, physical fact of being a body in contact with other bodies, a body in contact with the ground, a body in contact with the water.

I am the plateau, touching itself.

## The Stranger's Perspective

If you were standing on the plateau — and you would not be, because the creatures would know you were coming long before you arrived, and they would not avoid you (they do not avoid; they do not fear; they simply do not engage with what is not part of the network) — you would see a low, rounded stone at the edge of a pool, grey-green, moss-patched, motionless. You would see another stone nearby, smaller, smoother. You would see the pool, dark and still, reflecting the grey sky. You would see the meadow, undulating in long, slow swells, dotted with sedge clumps and boulders and more pools, stretching to the haze at the edge of visibility. You would see the walls of the settlement in the middle distance: low, dense, woven, the sedge surfaces mottled with moss and lichen, the stone foundations half-buried in the peat. You would see a landscape that appears empty, that appears silent, that appears to be nothing more than rock and water and sparse vegetation and the constant, low wind.

You would not see me. You would not see the young one beside me. You would not see the old one in the passage, feeling the repaired wall. You would not see the network, the web of vibration and touch that connects every creature in the cluster, that connects the cluster to the landscape, that connects the landscape to the water, that connects the water to the deep, slow, patient processes that have shaped this place and continue to shape it and will continue to shape it long after the last creature has been returned to the peat.

You would see a stone. And the stone would be me. And I would be sitting in the pool, submerged to my chin, my palms pressed to the bottom, my skin drinking the cold water, my chest resonating with the boulder's deep pulse, my entire body a single organ of attention, receiving the world the way the world offers itself: directly, completely, without interpretation, without narrative, without the desperate human need to make sense of things.

I do not need to make sense of things. I need to feel them. And I feel them. And the feeling is sufficient.

## Evening

The light shifts again. The grey deepens, the diffusion increases, the world becomes slightly more underwater than it already is. This is the evening, and the evening is for gathering.

I leave my pool. The young one leaves with me. We move through the passages — the young one ahead of me now, their steps more confident than they were this morning, their body learning the map with the speed that young bodies learn, absorbing the terrain through their feet and their palms and the skin of their knees where they brush against the passage walls.

We reach the communal pool, and the communal pool is full. Not full of water — it is always full of water — but full of bodies. The cluster has gathered, as it gathers every evening, drawn by the thrumming, drawn by the shift in the light, drawn by the deep, instinctive pull toward contact, toward connection, toward the network made flesh.

I enter the pool, and I am immediately in contact with other bodies. A shoulder against my shoulder. A back against my back. A hand finding my hand, the broad palm pressing against mine, the unique pattern of ridges and whorls identifying the creature as one I know well — a creature whose signature is forty-seven hertz, smooth, no stutter, a creature I have sat beside in this pool more times than I can count (I cannot count; counting is not something I do; but the accumulation of shared evenings is a weight in my memory, a density in my map, a thickness in the network of associations that connects this creature to this pool to this light to this water).

We sit, and we touch, and we thrum.

The thrumming begins as a murmur — individual signatures, individual frequencies, a cacophony that is not cacophonous because the frequencies are too low to clash, too deep to produce dissonance. And then, slowly, the way water finds its level, the frequencies converge. The stutter in my signature — forty-three hertz, with the irregular second pulse — merges with the forty-seven of the creature beside me, and the fifty-one of the old one, and the thirty-nine of the young one (whose signature is still developing, still settling into its final frequency), and the dozens of other signatures in the pool, until the thrumming is a single, unified, deep vibration that fills the pool and the ground and the air and our bodies with a resonance that is almost too low to hear and too deep to ignore.

It is not music. It is not ceremony. It is not art. It is simply what happens when we are together and the day is ending and the water is at the correct level and the walls are sound and the community is intact and the plateau is the plateau and everything is, for now, in balance.

We thrum, and the thrumming says: *all is well*. It says: *I am here*. It says: *we are here*. It says: *the water is here, the water is here, the water is here*.

And the water is here. And we are here. And the plateau is here, and the seep is rising, and the condensation season is beginning, and the walls are sound, and the pools are full, and the sedge is growing, and the peat is accumulating, and the erosion is proceeding, and the wind is blowing, and the light is filtering through the grey sky, and the stars will be visible tonight if the cloud cover thins, and the bird will call from the updrafts at the plateau's edge, and the young ones will learn, and the old ones will remember, and the water will rise and fall and rise again, and we will press our palms to the wet ground, and the ground will press back, and this is all the conversation we need.

I am one of the people of the weeping stone, and I am weeping too, in my way — not with grief but with the same slow, patient, persistent seep that brings the water up through the rock and into the pools and into my skin and into the fabric of my life.

I press my palms to the bottom of the pool, and the bottom presses back, and the water holds me, and the network holds me, and the plateau holds me, and I am home, and I have never been anywhere else, and I will never be anywhere else, and this is not a limitation.

This is what the water builds when it is given time.
