I would be the **Stone-Listener**—the one who has learned to sit so still for so long that the pikas no longer consider me a threat, though they still announce my presence every dawn with a special cadence in their chorus, a variation that says *the rock-that-breathes is here again*.

I live in the space between two plates of gneiss, a fissure deep enough that the light never reaches the back of it. I have dug no deeper; the plateau does not permit such intrusion. But I have widened the space just enough to sit cross-legged, my back against stone that has not been warm in ten thousand years. The cushion plants grow at the fissure's mouth, and I eat their leaves with the same surgical care as the pikas—never more than a third of any plant, never the same plant two days in a row. I have watched them grow for so long that I know them as individuals: this one with the slight scar where an eagle's talon grazed it; that one which grew crooked after a particularly hard winter three centuries past.

I have been here long enough that I no longer remember what I was before. I was human, perhaps. I think I climbed here seeking something—the silence, maybe, or the clarity of altitude. But the plateau does not give silence. It gives sound so dense it becomes a texture, a weight, and eventually, if you listen long enough, a language. I have learned to hear the difference between the pika-call that means *hawk* and the one that means *fox* and the one that means *stranger*. I have learned that the wind has a voice that changes depending on which fissures it passes through, that it can sound like mourning or like laughter. And I have learned—this took the longest—that the stone itself hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth, a resonance that shifts subtly with the temperature, with the freeze-thaw cycles, with the slow settling of the plates.

I am not a hermit, exactly. I am not hiding from the world. I am *tuning* to it. The plateau has stripped me down to essentials, as it strips everything: I am ears and skin and patience. I drink the condensation that forms on the underside of stones. I eat sparingly. I sleep in the coldest hours, when the pikas are quietest, though even then I dream in their voices. My hair has grown long and pale, bleached by the same light that bleaches the stone. My skin has darkened and weathered. I am becoming the color of this place, the texture of it.

The eagles know me. They have stopped seeing me as prey or threat. Sometimes one will land on a stone near my fissure and regard me with those fierce, impossible eyes, and we will sit together in the brightness, two creatures who have learned to be still in a place that never stops speaking. Once, years ago—or was it decades?—an eagle brought me a gift: a pika, freshly killed, laid at the edge of my shelter. I did not eat it. I buried it in the dust and said something in a language I barely remember. The eagle watched, then flew away. It has not brought me another. But I took it as a kind of recognition, an acknowledgment that I belong here now, that I have been accepted into the pattern.

I am not the plateau's guardian. It does not need one. I am not its interpreter, though I have learned much of its language. I am simply a *witness*, a creature that has chosen to stay, to listen, to be present in a place that is utterly itself. I am the one who hears the pikas' ten-thousand-voiced declaration and answers, silently: *Yes. You are here. I hear you. I am here too.*

And in the evening, when the light shifts and the calls slow and the cold begins its nightly advance, I retreat deeper into my fissure, and I press my palm against the stone, and I feel it hum, and I know that I am home.
