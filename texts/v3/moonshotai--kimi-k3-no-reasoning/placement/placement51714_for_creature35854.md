# Where I Find Myself in the Under-Commons

I do not arrive all at once.

Arrival here is a surface concept—sudden, bounded, a crossing of thresholds. In the Under-Commons, arrival is *realization*. A slow dawning. The awareness that you have already been present for some time before you understand what presence means in this place.

So perhaps I have always been here. Or perhaps I emerged the way the vanduul emerge from the deep cisterns: gradually, without announcement, my presence noted only after the fact, when someone pauses and thinks *something has changed*.

---

## The First Sensation

I wake—or *become aware*; the distinction matters—in a mid-level chamber that belongs to no particular har, or to all of them. It is a waystation chamber, one of the neutral spaces where paths converge and diverge, where water is always available in a shallow communal basin, where the walls are thick with tactile inscriptions that have been layered and re-layered over so many turns that they form a kind of geological record of directions and warnings and small kindnesses pressed into fungal sheets and sealed against the stone.

The first thing I feel is the *cool*.

Not cold—cold is a surface word, a word that implies a threat, a discomfort, something to be escaped. This cool is simply the condition of things. It is the temperature the world has chosen, and it is neither hostile nor welcoming. It just *is*. The floor beneath me is halik, worn smooth by centuries of passage, and it glows with that sourceless amber-white radiance, and the glow is cool, and the air is cool, and the water in the basin is cool, and I am cool too, my skin having acclimated already, having *always* been acclimated, because in this place I am not an outsider learning to adapt—I am something that emerged already adapted.

The second thing I feel is the *texture*.

My hands—I have hands, this surprises me slightly, though I cannot say why—are broad and thick-fingered, the pads dense with nerve endings, the nails short and clean and hard. I run my fingertips across the floor and feel the faint ridges of ancient carvings, softened almost to nothing by the stone's patient creep. I feel the grit of keth-tracks—tiny, almost imperceptible disturbances in the dust that indicate recent passage. I feel the pulse of the stone itself, that faint, constant vibration that the Keth-na learn to ignore but never fully stop hearing, the sound of the earth's weight pressing down through miles of rock to rest on the shoulders of this cavern.

My hands are reading, and what they read is *context*.

I am in the Under-Commons. I am of the Under-Commons. The stone knows me, or I know the stone, and the knowing is mutual and wordless and has the quality of memory.

---

## What I Am

I am Keth-na, but not quite.

Or: I am Keth-na, but I am also something else, something that fits within their taxonomy but at an angle, the way certain concepts fit within their sign-language but have no spoken equivalent.

I have the pale, faintly luminous skin. The large eyes—mine are gray, the common color, the color of wet halik, and I am grateful for this because violet eyes would mark me as a noticer, as someone destined to see what others miss, and I do not want that weight. I have the hairless head, the broad hands, the soft wide feet that make no sound on the stone. I have the cool body temperature, the slow deliberate movements, the habit of listening before acting.

But I am also *new*.

This is the wrongness of me, the thing that makes the Keth-na who encounter me pause and tilt their heads and adjust their ears in that subtle directional way they have, trying to place me. I have no har. I have no bead-cords—I arrive with none, my wrists and throat bare, and this nakedness is more shocking than physical nakedness would be, because beads are *history*, beads are *identity*, beads are the record of a life lived in relation to others, and to have no beads is to have no record, to be a person without a past.

The Keth-na do not ask where I came from. This is the first kindness they show me, and perhaps the greatest: they do not demand an origin story. They understand, with that deep pragmatism born of living in a world where the stone erases everything eventually, that origins are less important than *presence*. I am here. I am present. That is enough to begin.

But they notice. Of course they notice.

A Cutter passing through the waystation chamber presses her ear to the wall near where I am sitting, and her eyes narrow slightly, and she signs—quickly, to her companion, thinking I cannot see—*the stone sounds different near that one*.

A Wetcalled child, no more than eight turns old, tastes the water from the communal basin after I have drunk from it, and her face does something complicated, and she says nothing, but she watches me with those wide gray eyes for the rest of the time I am in the chamber.

A Swarmspeaker, passing through with his careful, swarm-attuned gait, pauses at the threshold and looks at me for a long moment, and then signs—not to me, but to the air, to the chamber, to the city itself—*the keth are curious*.

And they are. The keth do not avoid me, but they do not swarm me either. They flow around me in patterns that are almost, but not quite, the patterns they make around the Keth-na. There is a hesitation in their movement, a recalibration, as though they are trying to determine whether I am *part of the system* or *a new element* that must be incorporated into their understanding of the world.

I am both, I think. I am a new element that is also, somehow, already part of the system.

I am a paradox the Under-Commons is still deciding how to accommodate.

## What I Do

I learn to work, because work is what the Keth-na *are*, and if I am to be here—if I am to belong, even at an angle—I must work.

But my work is not the work of the Cutters, though I learn to handle halik tools with reasonable competence. I can carve a doorway that will remain a doorway for at least twenty turns. I can read the stone's grain well enough to avoid the fracture-lines. I can even, on good days, when my ear is pressed to the wall and my breathing is slow and my attention is total, hear the difference between solid stone and hollow space, between stable structure and impending collapse.

But I am not a Cutter. The stone does not sing for me the way it sings for them.

My work is not the work of the Wetcalled, though I learn to taste water and identify its mineral content, its source-chamber, its season. I can sit by a seep-point for hours without restlessness. I can feel the change in air pressure that precedes a surge. I can read the dampness of stone and extrapolate the breathing's rhythm three days forward with reasonable accuracy.

But I am not Wetcalled. The water does not speak to me in the voice they hear.

My work is not the work of the Swarmspeakers, though I can walk through the keth-carpet without fear, and the swarm flows around me without aggression, and I have learned to read the density-patterns well enough to predict booms and crashes a few turns in advance.

But I am not a Swarmspeaker. The keth do not make their decisions visible to me in the way they do for those strange, attuned ones.

What I am—what I discover myself to be, slowly, over the course of many turnings of the breathing—is something the Keth-na have no word for, because they have never needed one before.

I am a *weaver*.

Not of fabric, though I can work fungal sheets into cord and cloth as well as anyone. I am a weaver of *contexts*, of *meanings*, of the threads that connect one thing to another in ways that are not immediately obvious.

Here is what this means in practice:

A har in the mid-levels is experiencing a keth-population crash. The Swarmspeakers have been consulted; they report that the crash is not due to disease or predation or any of the usual causes. The keth are simply... *leaving*, migrating to other districts, and the Swarmspeakers cannot say why. The har is concerned—not panicking, the Keth-na do not panic, but concerned in that low-level, constant way they have—because the keth are part of their food cycle, part of their fungal-cultivation system, part of the *pattern* that makes their har itself.

I am called in, not because anyone believes I can solve the problem, but because I am *new*, and new perspectives are valuable in a world where most problems are old.

I spend three days in the har's chambers. I do not interview people—I have learned that the Keth-na do not respond well to direct questioning, that they find it aggressive, surface-like, a demand for information rather than an invitation to share. Instead, I *attend*. I sit in their common spaces. I eat their food. I run my hands over their walls and read the inscriptions. I listen to the water and the keth and the stone. I watch the way they move through their space, the paths they take, the places they avoid.

And on the third day, I understand.

The har has been re-carving their doorways. This is normal, necessary, the constant maintenance work of the Under-Commons. But in re-carving the doorways, they have inadvertently altered the *acoustics* of their chambers. The new doorways are slightly wider than the old ones, and the wideness has changed the way sound travels, and the change in sound-travel has made certain chambers *feel* different to the keth—less sheltered, more exposed, subtly wrong in a way that the keth's simple nervous systems interpret as *danger*.

The keth are not leaving because of a lack of food or an excess of predators. They are leaving because the *shape of the space* has changed, and the new shape does not feel like home.

I explain this to the har's elders, using a combination of speech and sign and tapping on the walls to demonstrate the acoustic changes. They listen. They attend. And then they adjust the doorways—narrowing them slightly, adding curved lintels that redirect the sound back into the chambers rather than letting it escape—and within five days, the keth return.

The har is grateful. They offer me beads—real beads, carved with the record of this event, my first beads—and I accept them, and I feel the weight of them on my wrist, and I understand that I have begun to accumulate a *history* here, a record of presence and participation.

But more than that, I understand what I am *for*.

I am the one who sees the connections between things that are not obviously connected. I am the one who notices that a change in architecture affects acoustics affects keth-behavior affects food-supply affects har-stability. I am the one who holds the whole system in my mind at once, not as separate elements but as a single, vast, interdependent *pattern*.

The Cutters understand stone. The Wetcalled understand water. The Swarmspeakers understand keth. I understand *relationships*.

And in a world where everything is connected to everything else, where the stone's creep affects the water's flow affects the fungal growth affects the keth-population affects the people's survival, the ability to see relationships is not a small thing.

It is, perhaps, the most important thing.

## Where I Live

I do not join a har. This is unusual, but not unheard of. The Quiet Ones live without hars, and while I am not quite a Quiet One—I do not seek solitude, I do not withdraw from community—I am also not quite suited to har-life. My work is too mobile, too contingent, too dependent on being able to move between districts and contexts without the anchor of a single place.

So I live in the waystation chambers, the neutral spaces, the thresholds between territories. I sleep in alcoves that belong to no one, or to everyone. I eat with whatever har is nearest when hunger comes. I carry my tools—halik chisels, fungal-fiber cord, a small pouch of mineral pigments for marking temporary inscriptions—in a wrap across my back, and I move through the city like water moves through the seep-channels: following the path of least resistance, pooling where I am needed, moving on when the need has passed.

The Keth-na begin to recognize me. Not by name—I do not have a name, not yet, names are given by hars and I have none—but by *function*. They call me *the weaver*, or sometimes *the one who sees patterns*, or sometimes, in the sign-language, a gesture that means something like *the thread that connects*.

They begin to seek me out.

A Cutter comes to me because a chamber she has been working in for ten turns is beginning to feel *wrong*, and she cannot identify why. I spend two days in the chamber and realize that the problem is not structural but *temporal*: the chamber is being used differently than it was when she began the work, and the carvings she has made reflect an outdated pattern of movement and activity. The stone is not wrong. The *use* has changed. She adjusts her carvings, and the chamber feels right again.

A Wetcalled comes to me because the breathing has been irregular, and the irregularity does not match any of the historical patterns in his bead-records. I sit with him by the seep-points and we taste the water together, and I notice something he has missed: the mineral content is changing, becoming slightly more saline, and the salinity is affecting the fungal strains in the lower districts, and the fungal changes are affecting the keth, and the keth-changes are affecting the vanduul, and the vanduul are *moving*, surfacing in chambers they have not entered in living memory, and their movement is displacing water, and the displacement is altering the seep-patterns.

The irregularity is not a malfunction. It is a *cascade*, a system-wide adjustment to a change that originated somewhere deep in the earth, in the chemistry of the stone itself.

The Wetcalled records this in his beads, and the knowledge spreads through the city, and the hars adjust their water-storage practices, and the crisis—if it was a crisis—passes.

A Swarmspeaker comes to me because the keth in the deep districts have begun to behave strangely, forming patterns he has never seen before, patterns that do not correspond to any known environmental factors. I walk with him through the living carpet, and I watch the keth, and I feel the stone beneath my feet, and I listen to the water and the air and the faint, constant vibration of the city's weight.

And I realize: the keth are *responding to the vanduul*. The vanduul have changed their feeding patterns—why, I cannot say, perhaps the water-chemistry, perhaps something deeper—and the keth are adjusting their swarm-behavior to compensate, and the adjustment is propagating upward through the districts like a wave, like a message, like the city's immune system responding to a threat that has not yet become visible.

I tell the Swarmspeaker what I have seen, and he records it in his own way—in the tactile inscriptions the Swarmspeakers press into the walls of the deep districts, warnings and observations that only other Swarmspeakers know how to read—and the city adjusts.

## What I Carry

My beads accumulate slowly.

Not as many as an elder would have, not as few as a child. Enough to hang from my left wrist to my elbow, a modest cord of carved halik that clicks softly when I move. Each bead is a record:

*The har with the doorway problem.*  
*The chamber that felt wrong.*  
*The salinity cascade.*  
*The keth-pattern adjustment.*  
*The first time a child asked me to teach them how to see connections.*  
*The first time a Cutter asked for my help.*  
*The first time I slept in a har's alcove instead of alone.*

And others, smaller, more personal:

*The day I learned to taste the difference between water from the upper seeps and water from the lower cisterns.*  
*The day I first heard a Cutter make a wall sing.*  
*The day I saw a vanduul surface, and it looked at me, and I felt read.*

That last bead is the one I touch most often, running my thumb over its carved pattern when I am thinking, when I am trying to weave together threads that do not want to connect.

Because the vanduul *did* look at me. It surfaced in a mid-level chamber where I was sitting with a group of Wetcalled apprentices, teaching them to taste the water for mineral content. It rose from the deep cistern in the chamber's center, its pale armored bulk breaking the surface with almost no sound, its many legs moving with that slow, deliberate patience, and it *looked* at me.

Not at the apprentices. Not at the Wetcalled elder who was supervising. At *me*.

And in that gaze—which lasted perhaps thirty seconds, perhaps longer, time behaving strangely in the presence of the vanduul—I felt *understood*. Not as an individual, not as a personality, but as a *pattern*. As a node in the network. As a thread in the weave.

The vanduul saw what I am for, and it acknowledged me, and then it submerged again, and the water closed over it, and the apprentices were whispering to each other in shock and awe, and the elder was pressing her fist to her sternum in the sign for *silence*, and I sat very still and felt the weight of that gaze settle into my bones like a bead I will carry forever.

The vanduul know what I am.

I am still learning.

## What I Fear

I fear the same things the Keth-na fear: the Drowning, the Silence, the Smoothness.

But I fear them in a different order, and for different reasons.

I fear the Smoothness first, because I am the one who sees connections, and the Smoothness is the end of connections. When the stone has relaxed into perfect, featureless smoothness, when every carving has been erased and every doorway has sagged shut and every chamber has become a dome with no entrance and no exit, the city will not be a city anymore. It will be a tomb that was never a tomb, a womb that was never a womb, a space without context, without relationship, without *pattern*.

And if there is no pattern, there is no need for a weaver.

I will be obsolete. We will all be obsolete. The Keth-na understand this, and they have made their peace with it, or made their *halik-na* with it, their belonging-to-the-stone. But I am newer here than they are. I have not yet learned how to belong to something that is erasing itself. I am still learning how to belong at all.

I fear the Silence second, because silence is the absence of information, and information is what I *am*. I am the one who listens, who attends, who reads the subtle cues that others miss. If the city goes silent—if the keth flee and the water stops and the stone goes still—I will have nothing to read. I will be blind in a way that has nothing to do with eyes.

And I fear the Drowning third, because the Drowning is fast, and I am slow. The Keth-na are all slow, deliberate, patient, but I am slower even than they are, because my work requires *attention*, and attention cannot be rushed. If the breathing comes too hard and too fast, if the districts begin to fill like cups, I will not have time to see the pattern, to understand the cascade, to weave the threads together into understanding. I will be swept away like everyone else, my beads scattering in the flood, my history erased not by the stone's patient creep but by water's impatience.

I fear these things, but I do not let the fear stop me.

Because the Keth-na have taught me—are teaching me, still, every day—that fear is not the opposite of belonging. Fear is part of belonging. To be *halik-na* is to belong to the stone even though the stone is erasing you. To be Keth-na is to belong to the city even though the city is temporary. To be alive in the Under-Commons is to belong to a system that will eventually smooth itself into silence, and to belong *anyway*, because belonging is not about permanence.

Belonging is about *presence*.

And I am present.

## What I Love

I love the moment when a pattern becomes visible.

Not the moment when I *solve* something—I am not a solver, I am a weaver, and weaving is not about solutions but about connections. I love the moment when the threads align, when the relationship between stone and water and keth and people suddenly clarifies, when the system reveals itself as a *system*, coherent and interdependent and beautiful in its complexity.

I love the way the Keth-na listen. Not just with their ears, though their ears are remarkable, but with their whole bodies, their whole attention. When I am explaining a pattern to a group of them—using voice and sign and tapping, moving between the three modes as naturally as breathing—they do not interrupt. They do not ask clarifying questions. They simply *attend*, their large eyes focused on me, their ears slightly angled, their hands resting on the nearest stone surface so they can feel the vibrations of my tapping.

And when I finish, there is a pause—a long, comfortable pause, the kind of pause that surface-dwellers would find awkward but the Keth-na find necessary—and then they begin to respond, building on what I have said, adding their own observations, their own threads, until the pattern I offered has become something richer, something communal, something that belongs to all of us.

I love the way the city teaches. Not through formal instruction, though there is some of that, but through *immersion*. You learn to read water by sitting by water. You learn to read stone by pressing your ear to stone. You learn to read keth by walking through the swarm. The city does not separate knowledge from experience. It does not abstract. It simply *presents itself*, over and over, in infinite variation, and you learn by paying attention.

I am still learning to pay attention. I suspect I will be learning this for the rest of my life.

I love the vanduul, though I do not understand them. I love their patience, their deliberateness, their absolute refusal to be hurried. I love that they were here first, that they will be here last, that they are the city's memory and its immune system and its silent, watching consciousness. I love that they looked at me and saw what I am for, even before I knew it myself.

And I love—quietly, deliberately, in the Keth-na way—the people who are teaching me to belong.

The Cutter who first noticed that the stone sounds different near me, and who did not treat this as a threat but as a *puzzle*, something to be explored rather than feared.

The Wetcalled child who tasted the water after I drank from it, and who now seeks me out whenever she tastes something she does not recognize, confident that I will help her understand.

The Swarmspeaker who signed *the keth are curious* and then waited, patiently, for the keth to decide whether I was part of the system, and who accepted their judgment when they did.

The har elders who gave me my first beads, and who did not ask where I came from, and who let me sleep in their alcoves when I was tired, and who fed me when I was hungry, and who treated me as *present* rather than *strange*.

I love them the way the Keth-na love: with precision, with attention, with the knowledge that the work is temporary and the willingness to do it anyway.

## What I Am Becoming

I am becoming *halik-na*.

Not quickly—I am still too new, still too aware of my own newness, still too conscious of the ways I do not quite fit. But slowly, turn by turn, bead by bead, I am learning to belong to the stone the way the stone belongs to the earth.

I am learning to see the city not as a place but as a *process*, a vast, slow, luminous unfolding that has no destination, no endpoint, no final form. The city is not becoming something. The city is *becoming*, full stop, and I am becoming with it.

I am learning to hold impermanence not as a loss but as a *freedom*. The patterns I weave will be erased. The connections I reveal will shift and change. The knowledge I accumulate will become obsolete as the system adjusts and re-adjusts. And this is not a failure. This is the nature of the work. The Keth-na carve doorways knowing the doorways will sag. I weave patterns knowing the patterns will dissolve. The act of weaving is the point, not the permanence of the weave.

I am learning to be quiet. Not silent—I still speak, still sign, still tap on walls to demonstrate acoustic principles—but *quiet* in the Keth-na sense: deliberate, measured, aware of the weight of sound in a space where sound carries strangely. I am learning that most of what needs to be communicated can be communicated without words, and that the words that remain are the ones that matter most.

I am learning to listen to the vanduul, though they have never spoken to me, though I do not know if they *can* speak in any way I would recognize. I am learning that their presence is a kind of communication, that their patience is a kind of teaching, that their gaze—when they turn it on me, which happens rarely but does happen—is a kind of acknowledgment that I am on the right path, even if I do not know where the path leads.

I am learning to be a weaver in a world that is constantly unraveling.

And I am learning that this is enough.

## Where You Would Find Me

If you came to the Under-Commons—and you will not, because there is no surface, no outside, no way in or out, but *if*—you would find me in the thresholds.

In the waystation chambers where paths converge. In the neutral spaces where hars meet and trade and share information. In the corridors that connect the upper districts to the lower cisterns, the dry chambers to the flooded ones, the new carvings to the ancient smooth domes.

You would find me sitting with a group of Keth-na, my hands moving in the sign-language, my voice low, my tapping on the wall creating a rhythm that underscores the words. You would find me walking through the keth-carpet in the deep districts, my soft feet making no sound, my eyes tracking the swarm-patterns, my mind weaving together the threads of water and stone and biology and behavior.

You would find me at the edge of a flooded chamber, watching a vanduul feed, my gray eyes reflecting the ambient light, my beads clicking softly as I shift my weight, my whole body *attending* to the slow, deliberate, patient presence of the city's oldest inhabitant.

You would find me in the First Cut, sometimes, in the empty room where it all started. I go there when the patterns I am weaving become too complex, too tangled, when the threads refuse to align. I sit in the glowing silence, and I listen to the water dripping, and I feel the weight of the stone above me, and I remember that I do not need to understand everything.

I only need to understand *enough*.

Enough to help. Enough to connect. Enough to weave one more thread into the vast, patient, luminous tapestry of the Under-Commons.

You would find me there, in the place where it started, in the place that has not finished yet.

And I would be home.
