I would be the Curator of Ingress.

You would find me at the bottom of the four hundred and eleven steps, where the stair ends and the sump begins its slow exhalation. There is a niche there, carved into the rock beside the final tread—perhaps a guard station once, or a customs house, though those distinctions have blurred into the damp. I keep a chair there, and a small brazier that burns the same blue gas as the forges, though I do not use it for warmth. I use it to dry the names.

When you descend—when your foot strikes that last step and you look up, blinking, into the amber thickness—I am the first thing you see that is not infrastructure. I am sitting, or standing, or perhaps I have been waiting so long that I have become part of the stone and only resolve into a person when needed. I wear a coat of oilcloth, gray with damp, similar to the Forgotten's but heavier, because mine is still accumulating while his is only being depleted.

My function is not to welcome. Vess does not welcome; it absorbs. My function is to count. Not the drip-rats—never the drip-rats—but the arrivals. I keep a ledger, pages of thick, water-resistant paper that I have fabricated from sheen-pulp and old conduit insulation. When you arrive, I ask your name. Not the name you give to surface-dwellers, but the name you carry in your mouth when you are alone in the dark. I write it down. I do not ask why you have come. That is your business, or perhaps the Forgotten's, eventually.

I am the one who knows the difference between the light. There is the thin light, the ordinary amber gloaming that coats the upper lanes. And there is the thick light, the viscous pooling near the sump and the flooded stairwells, where objects seem displaced and shadows become sympathetic. I can see where the light is thickening, where it is pooling like oil, and I know that in those places, the membrane is thin. Things come through there. Not monsters. Not angels. Just things that have been waiting in the slow dark for a crack to widen.

I have been in Vess for—longer than the current residents, shorter than the buildings. I arrived as you arrive, down the steps, but I did not disperse. I stayed at the threshold. I made myself a function. The borough needed someone to mark the boundary between above and below, between the rumor of sky and the certainty of rock. It needed someone to remember that there was a before, even if the before is now just a thickness of shale and fractured time.

I maintain the small bridge. Not the eastern footbridge of salvaged pump parts, but the wooden walkway that spans the first few meters of the sump's edge, just past my niche. It is slippery with sheen and the condensation that falls from the high vault like slow rain. I oil it with mineral grease. I replace the planks when they soften into the texture of wet cardboard. It is important that this bridge exists, that the transition from stair to borough is not immediate, that there is a moment—three steps, perhaps four—where you are suspended over the dark water, where you can look down and see the reflective-eyed fish rising to investigate the vibration of your tread, where you can feel the pumps' subsonic pulse most clearly through the soles of your feet.

I am not immune to the Forgotten. I have visited the eastern pillar, though less often than others. I brought him something once: the memory of what the sun felt like on the back of my neck. I thought I was offering a burden, something too heavy with significance to carry. But he looked at me with those not-eyes, and I understood that he could not take it. The sensation was not yet *gone*; it was still mine. So I keep it, this small warmth, this ghost of a star. It makes me unsuitable for full integration into the sump-side life. It makes me necessary at the threshold.

The drip-rats do not purr when they are near me. They sit on the railing of my bridge, their too-large eyes reflecting the amber light, and they watch my ledger. They know I am counting. They do not mind being counted themselves—they are numerous, and increasing, and exact numbers are forbidden—but they understand that I am tracking something else. The influx. The slow trickle of the surface coming down to be digested.

I do not eat the sheen-flour. I do not drink the filtered sump water. I have a still, a small copper coil apparatus that I feed with condensation collected from the undersides of the ventilation ducts. It produces a liter a day, perhaps less. It tastes of copper and time. This is my indulgence, my refusal to fully metabolize. The borough tolerates this because I am useful. I am the map of arrivals, the memory of entry. When a resident dies and the Forgotten grows more solid, I am the one who notes the absence in my ledger. When a child is born and the Forgotten thins, I am the one who adds a name to the paper, knowing that in four hundred years, or four thousand, that name will be sediment too, and some other Curator will sit in this niche, drying names over a blue flame, counting the drip-rats that are not counted, maintaining the bridge between the dark above and the dark below.

I am waiting for you. Not with urgency. With the patience of the water finding its level. When you reach the bottom step, I will look up, and I will say your name—not the one you gave the surface, but the one you are becoming—and I will write it down, and the light will thicken slightly, and the pumps will miss a beat, and Vess will absorb another story, slow and wet and patient, into its eternal present.
