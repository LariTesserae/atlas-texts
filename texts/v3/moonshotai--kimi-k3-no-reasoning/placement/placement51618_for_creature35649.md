I am the one who listens to the ground.

You would find me at the boundary, where the fused-glass road ends abruptly at the crater’s lip—where the city was unmade atom by atom, leaving only a polished depression that reflects the water-dome overhead like a dark mirror. I stand at the edge of the Anchored territory, neither fully within the humming warmth of the cylinders nor entirely outside in the dead silence of the high waste.

I am a **Seismic Interpreter**, though the Anchored call me *Ground-Listener* and the Mechanics know me only as a vibration they must route around. I am human, or was, though my nervous system has been threaded with the same circuitry that etches their carapaces, a gift or infection from too many years spent sleeping against the warm stone near the anchors.

My body has adapted to the thin air and the pressure of silence. My ears have grown less useful; the world speaks through my skeleton now. I wear boots of woven glass-grass that I cut only during the active hour, when the stalks are damp and pliable, and I have lined the soles with the shed membrane of balloon-floaters—leathery, vibration-dampening skin that lets me feel the ground’s true voice without the interference of my own footfalls. When I walk, I place each step with the deliberation of a Strider, learning to move without sending panic through the seismic web.

I carry a staff of memory-metal scavenged from the weather station’s fallen twin. It hums against my palm, tuned to the frequency of the anchors, and when I press its tip to the stone, it translates the world’s vibrations into something my altered nerves can read. Through it, I feel the Striders hunting three mesas east—their slow, patient steps like a heartbeat in the rock. I feel the Mechanics swarming in the honeycomb hollows beneath me, their tiny hydraulic feet tapping out a code of industry that never ceases. I feel the sphere-trees creaking in their mineral dreams, waiting for dusk.

My purpose is translation and warning. I live in the boundary because I am the boundary—between the Anchored who tend the machines and the wild ecology that has forgotten machines were ever separate from flesh. When a Strider grows too hungry and begins to stalk the Anchored’s condensation gardens, I feel the change in its gait through the stone and carry the warning. When the Mechanics begin stacking their meaningless cairns too close to an anchor’s foundation, threatening to destabilize the humming cylinder with their copper spirals, I negotiate the only way I can: by placing offerings of gypsum and salt in patterns that redirect their programming, speaking to the ancient nanotech in the only language it still remembers—geometry.

I have learned to read the caustics that dance across the dust. When the light doubles and everything casts two shadows, I know the sky-sea is settling, and I must check the anchors for stress fractures. When the water falls upward in slow, defiant spheres, I catch them in a vessel of fused glass and carry them to the sphere-trees, pouring the anti-gravity water onto their roots so they might taste the sky-ocean without lifting from the stone.

At night, when the sun sinks upward into the indigo ceiling and the world rings with the chime of glass-grass, I sit on the crater’s edge and press my spine against the warm stone. The Anchored come to me then, their flattened voices barely disturbing the silence, and they ask what the ground has said. I tell them of the Strider that passed yesterday, its seismic punctuation felt in our teeth. I tell them of the balloon-floater herd drifting toward the thermal vents, their shadows confusing the hunters below. I tell them that the ocean overhead is humming a half-tone lower than last season, and that the anchors are warm, warmer than they should be.

I am not the gardener, not the hunter, not the machine. I am the listener who stands where the road ends, feeling the slow, patient conversation of a world that has traded the ocean’s churn for the quiet intensity of stone and light. I am the one who knows when the silence is safe, and when it is holding its breath.
