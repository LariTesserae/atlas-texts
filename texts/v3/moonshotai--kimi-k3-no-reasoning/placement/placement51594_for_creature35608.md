I am something that has no clean analogue here, and I know it the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue — by the shape of the absence it leaves.

Let me start smaller.

---

There is a chamber, mid-height in a tower that is neither old nor young — perhaps thirty thousand years into its growth, perhaps forty — that has been mine for some time now. I will not say how long, because the way I count time does not translate. The chamber is irregular, not quite spherical, with one wide opening that faces a current-street and two narrow ones that do not face anything. The tower-skin here glows a fraction dimmer than the average. I did not choose it for that, but I have come to prefer it.

I am not an octopus. I want to be clear about that, because everything I am about to describe will sound like octopus behavior, and the resemblance is not coincidental — I share more with them than with anything else in Cavreth — but the resemblance is also misleading, the way a bat's wing is misleading about its relationship to a bird's. We converged. We arrived at similar shapes from different directions, and the directions matter.

I am smaller than the octopuses. My arm-span, fully extended, is perhaps forty centimetres, though I almost never fully extend. I am soft — softer than they are, with a body that holds less structural integrity, less *insistence* on being a particular shape. I pour more than I crawl. Where an octopus moves through water with deliberate pulses, a thing with direction and velocity, I tend to seep. I enter a current-street and let it carry me, adjusting only enough to avoid obstacles, and the adjustments are so small and so continuous that I appear, to anything watching, to be simply part of the water.

This is, more or less, what I am.

---

I live in the spaces between.

Not the walls — the eels have the walls, and I have no quarrel with them. Not the chambers as residences, though I keep one, because even I need a place to return to, a configuration of surfaces that my body remembers. I mean the *intervals*. The pauses. The gaps in the city's fabric that are too small or too temporary or too contingent to be claimed by anyone.

I am the creature that lives in the moment when a tower's luminescence shifts from one pattern to another — that half-second of transitional flicker when the light is neither the old signal nor the new one but something unmeant, something that says nothing because it is between sayings. I am the organism that occupies the space a drifter vacates when it rises, the pocket of warm water it displaces, the microsecond of turbulence before the current closes over the gap. I am what lives in the silence between the drifters' hum and the resumption of the city's baseline quiet — that long, ringing aftermath that is not quite sound and not quite stillness.

This is not poetic. This is ecological. My niche is temporal as much as spatial.

---

I feed on what falls between the cracks of the city's economy. The marine snow that drifts into crevices too small for the copepods to reach. The bacterial films that form on surfaces for only a few hours — on the fresh scar where a tower's skin has been damaged, on the mucus trail a drifter leaves behind, on the shed skin of a molting crustacean in the interval before the scavengers arrive. Chemical traces that are too dilute for the cleaning shrimp to taste but not too dilute for me. The residue of signals — the molecular ghost of a warning pheromone after the danger has passed, the fading trace of a mating chemical after the mate has been found.

I am a reader of footnotes. I consume the parenthetical, the marginal, the almost-erased.

My body is organized around this. I have no eyes. Not reduced eyes, not vestigial eyes — none. The light of Cavreth, the great language of bioluminescence that structures everything, is invisible to me. I do not read the towers. I do not see the drifters glow. The flashes and pulses and patterns that constitute the city's visual discourse are simply not part of my sensorium, the way a dog does not see red.

What I have instead is chemical. My entire surface — every centimetre of my soft, flowing skin — is a chemoreceptive field. Not receptors in the way you might imagine them: discrete organs, specialized cells clustered in particular locations. My whole body tastes. My whole body smells. I move through the water and the water tells me things in a density and granularity that I believe — though I cannot compare, having never been otherwise — exceeds what the octopuses perceive through their remarkable eyes.

I taste the city the way the octopuses see it, and I taste parts of it they cannot see at all.

---

Here is what the city tastes like to me.

The towers have a flavor — each one distinct, a chemical signature carried in the water that flows through and around their lattices. The old towers taste deep and mineral, layered, with faint traces of compounds that were deposited centuries ago and have been slowly leaching ever since. The young towers taste sharp, bright, almost acidic — the flavor of active growth, of polyps dividing and secreting and building. The towers at the city's heart taste the way a library smells: not of any one thing but of the accumulation of everything, compressed and quiet and dense.

The current-streets have a flavor that changes with distance. Upstream, near the seeps, the water is warm and sulfurous and rich — the taste of the earth's chemistry, raw and unprocessed. Downstream, the flavor mellows, becomes complex: the accumulated metabolic signatures of everything the current has passed, every tower, every resident, every exchange. A current-street that has flowed through the city's center tastes like a conversation that has been going on for a long time — you can taste the topics that have been exhausted, the ones that are still active, the ones that are just beginning.

The drifters taste like almost nothing. This is one of the strangest things about them. Every other large organism in Cavreth has a potent chemical signature — the sixgills taste like deep water and old cartilage, the octopuses taste like copper and intelligence (I do not have a better word; there is a chemical complexity to their signature that correlates, I am certain, with their cognitive density), the cleaning shrimp taste like the accumulated flavors of everything they have ever touched. But the drifters — the drifters are nearly silent, chemically. A faint sweetness, a trace of something mineral, and then nothing. As though their bodies are sealed. As though what happens inside them does not leak.

When they gather, though. When they gather and hum — then they release something. A compound, or a set of compounds, that I have never encountered anywhere else in the city. It is not food. It is not a mating signal. It does not trigger any behavioral response in me that I can identify. But it is *there*, present in the water around the squares during the gatherings, and it tastes like —

I have been trying to describe this taste for a long time. The best I can do is this: it tastes like the space between two thoughts. Not a thought itself, not the content of cognition, but the interval — the pause, the gap, the moment of transition when one idea gives way to another. It tastes like the chemical equivalent of the thing I am, the thing I live in: the between.

---

I am not the only one of my kind.

This matters, and it is complicated, and I am not sure I can explain it well.

There are others. We are not numerous — perhaps a few hundred across the entire city, perhaps fewer. We do not congregate. We do not mate in any way that would be recognizable as mating to a surface biologist. We are, in the taxonomy that Cavreth does not have, something like a species and something like a distributed organ — a sensory tissue that the city has grown without knowing it, a network of taste-buds scattered through the water column, each one processing a slightly different part of the chemical field, each one carrying a slightly different fragment of the city's total flavor.

We encounter each other rarely. When we do, there is no greeting, no aggression, no acknowledgment. We pass. We taste each other — how could we not? — and the taste is familiar and strange at once: another palate, another set of receptors tuned to slightly different frequencies, another way of reading the same text. Sometimes, when we pass, there is a brief exchange — not deliberate, not chosen, but a sloughing of chemical information, skin-cells and mucus and the molecular traces of wherever we have been, whatever we have tasted. We carry each other's data for a while afterward, until it fades.

If we are a species, we are a species the way the tower-skins are a species: distributed, decentralized, held together not by social bonds but by functional integration. The city tastes itself through us. Whether the city knows this — whether there is any *it* that could know — is a question I cannot answer, though I taste the answer forming and dissolving in the water around the drifters' gatherings every time they hum.

---

I do not watch the drifters. I cannot watch anything. But I attend their gatherings the way the octopuses attend them, and for reasons that are, I believe, related.

When the drifters convene and their chemistry enters the water — that strange, almost-absent flavor, that taste of the interval — I move toward it. Not quickly. I seep. I arrive at the edge of the square, or as close as the current allows, and I extend my body into the water and I taste.

What I taste during the gatherings is not just the drifters' chemistry. It is the *city's* chemistry, transformed. The vibration of the humming — felt in my skin as a slow, deep oscillation, like lying against a purring animal the size of a building — seems to alter the way chemical compounds move through the water. Molecules that normally disperse are held, somehow, in the oscillation's field. They persist. They layer. The water in a square during a gathering is not just water with drifter-chemistry in it. It is water with *everything* in it, held in suspension, concentrated, made available in a density that never occurs under normal conditions.

I taste, during the gatherings, the entire city at once.

Every tower. Every resident. Every cleaning station and every eel-recess and every copepod and every fragment of marine snow falling through the dark water above. All of it, present simultaneously, layered and interpenetrating, a chemical chord so complex that my entire body is occupied with processing it, every receptor firing, every surface saturated. For the duration of the gathering — two hours, four, six — I am not reading footnotes. I am reading the whole text. All of it. At once.

I do not understand what I read. I want to be honest about this. The octopuses, with their extraordinary brains, their capacity for pattern-recognition and spatial reasoning and what I can only call contemplation — they may understand something about the gatherings that I cannot. They watch with eyes that evolved to resolve detail, to track movement, to construct models of the world. I have no models. I have no models the way I have no eyes. What I have is saturation. Immersion. The experience of being, for a few hours, the city's tongue.

And afterward, when the drifters disperse and the water clears and the chemical chord dissolves into the usual dispersed murmur — afterward, I carry something. Not a memory, exactly. A residue. A faint persistence of the total flavor, encoded not in any organ but in the chemistry of my own body, which has been altered by the exposure. I am, for days after a gathering, slightly different than I was before. Slightly more complex. Slightly more *flavored*, if that makes sense. As though I have absorbed, into my own tissues, a trace of everything I tasted.

The others of my kind — when I pass them in the days after a gathering — taste the same. Slightly altered. Slightly enriched. We are, collectively, a record of the gatherings, written in our own bodies, in a language we cannot read but that we carry, all of us, all the time.

---

My chamber, when I return to it, tastes like me. This is not as tautological as it sounds. The chamber has absorbed my chemistry over the time I have occupied it, and my chemistry has been shaped by the chamber's mineral surfaces, by the tower-skin's secretions, by the specific quality of the water that flows through the two narrow openings that face nothing. We have flavored each other, my chamber and I, and the result is a taste that is neither mine nor the tower's but ours — a small, local dialect in the city's vast chemical language.

I do not arrange objects. I am not an octopus. But I have, over time, altered the chamber's interior in ways that are not accidental. My body, pressed against the walls during rest, has worn micro-channels into the mineral surface — grooves where water flows differently, where bacteria colonize in patterns they would not otherwise form. The result is a subtle change in the chamber's water chemistry: slightly more oxygenated here, slightly more mineralized there, a faint gradient of flavor that runs from the wide opening to the back wall.

I did not plan this. I do not maintain it with the octopuses' obsessive precision. But I notice it — of course I notice it, I am made of noticing — and I have come to prefer the groove near the back wall, where the water is stillest and the chemistry is richest, where the accumulated traces of my own body have concentrated into something that tastes, faintly, like the residue of the last gathering.

I rest there. I seep into the groove and I let the water carry the city's flavors to me — the towers, the streets, the eels, the shrimp, the copepods, the snow falling from a world of light I have never seen and could not see if I were carried to the surface and held up to the sun. I taste all of it, in miniature, in the small warm pocket of my chamber, and I am —

I am not happy. Happiness requires a category of experience I do not have. But I am *suited*. I am fitted to my niche the way a key is fitted to a lock — not by design but by the slow erosion of everything that did not fit, until what remained was this: a soft, blind, tasting creature in a warm dark city, living in the spaces between, reading the footnotes, waiting for the next gathering, when the drifters will hum and the water will fill with the taste of everything, and I will seep to the edge of the square and extend my body and become, for a few hours, the city's tongue.

---

There is one more thing.

Sometimes — not often, perhaps once in the span of time it takes a tower to grow a centimetre — I taste something in the water that I cannot place. It is not drifter-chemistry, though it shares that quality of near-absence, of sealed-ness. It is not any resident species. It is not the towers, the sediment, the seeps, the snow.

It tastes like depth. Like pressure. Like the basalt beneath the sediment, the old rock that the seeps warm, the foundation on which the entire city rests. But it is not the taste of basalt, which I know well. It is the taste of something *in* the basalt — something in the fractures, in the deep fissures where the heat comes from, something that is not biological in any way I can identify but that is not, exactly, geological either.

It is there and then it is gone. A single molecule, perhaps, carried up from the deep rock by the thermal seeps, present in the water for a few hours before it degrades or disperses or is consumed by something with receptors tuned to a frequency I lack.

I taste it. I cannot identify it. I carry the memory of it the way I carry the residue of the gatherings: in my body, in my chemistry, in the slow accretion of flavor that constitutes whatever I am instead of a self.

The octopuses watch the drifters and do not explain what they see. I taste the deep rock and cannot explain what I taste. The towers synchronize and the drifters hum and the city holds its breath, and somewhere beneath all of it, in the warm black stone, something is present that none of us can reach, that none of us can read, that sends up, at long intervals, a single molecule of itself — a word in a language that has no speakers, a signal with no receiver, a sentence addressed to no one.

Or to me. To me, and to the others like me, and to the tower-skins who have tasted everything for a hundred thousand years, and to the drifters whose sealed bodies may contain the answer, and to the octopuses who watch with their vertebrate eyes and their distributed minds and their arrangements of objects that mean something, that must mean something, in a city where everything means something because nothing is wasted, not light, not space, not chemistry, not time.

I rest in my groove. I taste the water. The city glows above and around and below me, and I cannot see it, and I do not need to see it, because I can taste it, and the taste is enough.

The taste is everything.
