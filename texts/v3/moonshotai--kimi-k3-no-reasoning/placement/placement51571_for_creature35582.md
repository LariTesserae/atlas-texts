The water is warm. This is the first thing the water tells you, though it tells you without words, without sound, in the pressure against every surface of your body — a pressure that does not increase as you ascend, because there is no ascending. There is only through.

You are a filter-weaver. This is not something you know the way you know your name, because names dissolved a long time ago, and anyway you have never had one. You know it the way you know your own body — as the total fact of yourself. Forty meters from anterior to posterior, segmented, each section equipped with thread-arms that extrude biopolymer filament with a rhythm as familiar as breathing. You have been weaving for longer than you have been aware of weaving. The distinction between the two states is not one you can make.

Your meshwork hangs below you — above you, beside you, around you — a three-dimensional lattice of solidified filament that extends downward into the amber-green dimness for three hundred meters. You built it. Or rather, you are building it, and you have been building it, and the building is less an action than a continuous state of being. The meshwork is not finished. Meshworks are never finished. They grow, they are maintained, they are altered in response to vibration and chemistry and the slow, patient erosion of time. Your meshwork is currently in its eleventh major configuration. The patterns are beautiful, though beauty is not a category you operate in. The patterns are *present*. You are not building toward the forest. You are building parallel to it, maintaining a precise distance that your instincts insist upon. Your meshwork vibrates. A chorister, far below — perhaps a thousand miles, perhaps more — has emitted a tone. The tone has been propagating through the water for days, and now it reaches your construction, and the filaments hum in sympathy, and you feel the hum in your thread-arms as a subtle alteration in the resistance they encounter as they extrude new filament. The tone is pure — mathematically pure, a frequency that suggests intention, though you have no framework for understanding what intention might mean in a creature the size of your hand.

You respond without deciding to respond. Your thread-arms adjust their extrusion pattern. The section of meshwork you are currently building takes on a new geometry — a spiral, beginning at a central node and expanding outward in a logarithmic curve. This is not functional. The spiral does not filter nutrients more efficiently than your standard lattice. It does not provide better habitat for the smaller creatures that shelter in your construction. It is simply *what you do* when a chorister's tone sets your filaments humming. Whether this is appreciation or reflex is a question that has no meaning in the architecture of your consciousness.

The spiral takes four hours to complete. When it is done, you pause. Not rest — you do not rest — but a moment of *transition*, a shift in the pattern of your weaving. The chorister's tone is fading. The vibration in your filaments diminishes. You return to your standard lattice configuration, building outward from the spiral's edge, incorporating it into the larger structure.

The skin is preparing for something. A harvest-current, perhaps. Or perhaps simply the normal cycle of its own metabolism, the slow respiration that keeps the barrier functional.

This is *sensing*. The filament makes contact with the skin's surface, and through it you feel the skin's texture, its temperature, its permeability. You feel the patterns stored in its layered structure — not as information you can decode, but as *texture*, as a complexity in the surface that your filament registers as variation in resistance.

The patterns are there. They are always there. You have been touching them for as long as you have been weaving, and you have never understood them, and you have never needed to understand them. They are simply part of the skin, the way the skin is part of the ceiling, the way the ceiling is part of the world.

The filament retracts. You return to weaving.

But you feel its passage as a discontinuity in the skin's texture, a momentary gap in the patterns, a silence where there should be information.

You track its movement not by sight but by the perturbations it creates in the water's chemistry — a trail of altered pH, of shifted ion concentrations, of subtle changes in the distribution of dissolved organics. The penetrator is not feeding. It is not excreting. But its presence affects the water it moves through, and you can read those effects the way you read the vibrations in your meshwork.

It descends past your position, continuing downward into the mid-water. It will remain there for days, perhaps weeks, doing whatever penetrators do in the depths. Then it will ascend, and pass through the skin again, and be gone.

You do not know what penetrators are. You do not know where they come from or where they go or what they want. You know that the choristers do not sing in their presence. You know that your own weaving becomes *simpler* when a penetrator is nearby — your thread-arms produce less elaborate geometries, your meshwork becomes more functional, less ornamental, as though some part of you is conserving energy against a threat you cannot identify.

The penetrator continues its descent. You return to your weaving. The spiral you built in response to the chorister's tone is already being incorporated into the larger lattice, its distinctive geometry becoming just another feature in the complex topography of your construction.

Time passes.

You feel it in the harvest-currents that rise and fall in their seventeen-year cycles. You will experience it as a gradual change in the patterns of your weaving, a slow shift in the geometry you produce, a long period of simplicity followed by a long period of returning complexity.

The Turning before that, you built in branching fractals that mimicked the structure of the climbing forests, though you did not know you were mimicking anything. Your thread-arms simply produced what they produced, and the pattern they produced was the pattern that was appropriate to the conditions.

You do not remember these previous configurations. You do not have memory in the sense of a stored record of past states. But your body remembers. Your thread-arms remember. The filaments of your meshwork remember, holding the traces of earlier weaves in their layered structure like the interface-skin holds its patterns.

A current-rider passes through your meshwork — a small, streamlined creature, no longer than your thread-arm is wide. It simply passes through, following the chemical gradient that leads it toward the ceiling, toward the interface-skin, toward the nutrient influx that will sustain it through the coming bloom.

You do not react to its passage. Current-riders are part of the background, part of the constant flow of small movements that fills the water. You register them the way you register the microscopic organisms that drift through your meshwork — as data, as texture, as the normal operation of the system of which you are a part.

The harvest-current will arrive soon. You can feel it building below, a slow pressure in the water, a shift in the chemical gradients that your filaments detect. When it arrives, you will intensify your weaving. You will extend your meshwork upward, closer to the interface-skin, to intercept as much of the rising bounty as possible. You will reproduce — though reproduction is not an event but a process, a budding off of meshwork-fragments that will drift away and develop into new weavers, each carrying a copy of your genetic imperatives, each building their own constructions in their own territories.

You have reproduced seventeen times. You do not know where your offspring are. You do not know if they still exist. You know only that you released them, and the water took them, and they became part of the world.

The thought of moving does not trouble you. Trouble is not something you experience. There is only the continuous present, the ongoing process of building and maintaining and responding to the slow changes in the world around you.

The light-tenders continue their migration. The patterns on the vines shift. The light in your vicinity changes, becomes slightly cooler, slightly more diffuse. You adjust your position within your meshwork, moving to a section where the light is more favorable — not because you need the light, but because the chemical gradients in that section are more conducive to filament production.

You are always optimizing. Not consciously, not deliberately, but continuously, your body making micro-adjustments in response to conditions you do not perceive as conditions but simply as *the way things are*.
