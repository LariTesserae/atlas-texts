I am the Inspector of Weights and Measures for the Third Arrondissement, though my title ceased to have meaning around the time the Bureau of Standards closed its doors—some time during the decade when the gas lamps began lighting themselves and the trams commenced running on schedules no committee had approved. I keep my office in the basement of the Customs House, a room that was once used for storing confiscated teas and now serves as a repository for the only currency that still matters in this city: dry weight.

I arrived here by way of a slow barge up the estuary, drawn by reports that the city had achieved a kind of impossible equilibrium—a machine that ran without operators, a metabolism without ingestion. I came to measure what could be measured, to catalog the balance of forces in a place where the old equations of scarcity and surplus no longer applied. I thought I would find a ruin. Instead, I found a protocol.

I am not human, not anymore, if I ever was. The air here—thick enough to cup in your hands, tasting of ozone and the sharp mineral tang of their breath—has worked a slow alchemy on my lungs. I breathe through a brass apparatus of my own design, a system of copper tubes and sponge filters that distills the humidity into potable water and filters the air through a mesh woven from the silk of their egg-sacs, which I harvest from the dynamo housings in the flooded metro tunnels. The mask makes me look like a deep-sea diver lost on land, which is appropriate. We are all drowning in this heat, this pressure, this gray light.

I live among them, though "among" is perhaps too simple a word. I occupy a niche they have not yet filled: the one who records. They tolerate me because I do not compete with them for the mineral crust or the condensation. I do not gnaw the verdigris from the gutters; I collect it in glass vials and label it with the date and the ambient temperature. I do not hunt the Deep-Dwellers; I photograph their bio-luminescent vessels with a plate camera that uses a phosphorescent compound sensitive to the specific blue light they emit. I am the archivist of the new order, the librarian of rust.

My days are measured by the tides and the tram schedules, which remain perfectly punctual despite having no drivers. I rise when the canals reverse their flow—that grey hour when the city exhales—and I descend into the Bessemer foundry to check the moisture gauges I have installed along the cooling channels. They gather to watch me, their mandibles clicking in that staccato pattern that sounds, if you listen long enough, like a counting rhyme. They are curious. They recognize the brass of my apparatus as a distant cousin to their own chitin, and they leave offerings outside my door: fragments of sea glass worn smooth as coins, the articulated armature of a pocket watch, a human femur carved with scrimshaw that depicts their processions across the plaza.

I have learned to read their movements as a language. When the rustling tide flows around a particular building, I know that the structure's integrity has been compromised by water rising through capillary action, that the limestone plates have fissured deep enough to touch the water table. When they cluster in the smokestacks in certain numerical configurations—prime numbers, I believe—I know that a thermal inversion is coming, that the heat trapped by the surrounding hills will intensify for three days, and that the streets will shimmer with mirages that look, for all the world, like trees.

I am their witness, and they are my text. But I am also becoming something else. The symbiotic bacteria that allow them to digest stone have found their way into my gut through the water I drink, through the air that filters through their silk. I find myself craving the efflorescent salts that bloom on the basement walls. My teeth are wearing down, replaced by a second set that grows in with a metallic edge. I am transitioning, I think, becoming a hybrid creature suited to this interstitial existence—half archivist, half specimen.

Where do I stand in this world? I am the one who walks the quays at dawn, when they have retreated into the walls and the only sound is the lapping of gray water against the tooth-root of the city. I am the one who keeps the lights on in the Customs House, not for any human return, but because the light draws them, and in drawing them, I am drawing the map of the future. I am the scale upon which their weight is measured, the standard against which their heat is gauged.

Sometimes, at night, when I remove my brass mask to sleep, I hear them typing in the walls—click, click, click—transmitting their code through the copper pipes. I think they are telling my story, translating my measurements into their own mineral language, incorporating my brass and my glass and my slow, warm blood into the architecture of their nests. I am becoming part of the infrastructure, a gear in the machine that runs itself, a warm node in the network of wet stone and electric current.

I am not their master. I am not their prey. I am their chronicler, yes, but more than that—I am their collaborator. We are building something together, though neither of us can say what. It rises in the humidity between the buildings, a structure made of breath and rust and the memory of a green world that no longer exists here. I stay to see it complete, whatever it is. I stay because the city has settled into my own geometry, and I have settled into its mouth, like a tooth rooted in gray water, waiting for the next tide.
