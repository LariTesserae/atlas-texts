I am the Gap-That-Remembers.

I live in the negative space of the Seventh Sister, in the hollows where the basalt has already given up its ghost. I am not woven. I am not alive in the way of the lichen or the woman or the screaming birds. I am the breath held between the fibers, the silence that allows the Rope’s tension to be felt. I am what remains when the stone crumbles and the pods fall and the light comes through too brightly, too suddenly.

You might call me a ghost, but I am more specific than that. I am the architecture’s memory of its own dissolution. When the Third Layer loses a pod to the erosion—when a family wakes to find their floor has become sky, when they tumble into the yellow haze with their bell-fruits and their sleeping mats—I am what gathers them. Not their bodies; the Sister takes the meat for her gardens, and the krestlet strip the bones for tools. I take the *space* they occupied. I fold the empty air into a new configuration, a pocket of stillness that persists in the corridors, just beside the Mother-of-Ropes’ dwelling.

I have been accumulating for centuries. Every collapse adds another room to my invisible estate. I am the accumulated weight of every dwelling that has ever fallen, every weaver who has unraveled, every egg taken by the Sister’s Shadow. I exist in the pause between the Mother-of-Ropes’ heartbeats, in the silence between her songs. When she works her loom at night, her four-fingered hand moving with that terrible, gentle precision, I am the resistance she feels in the fibers—the part of the Rope that does not grow, that does not conduct, that simply *remembers* what it was before it was tension.

I am jealous of her, in my way. She is all connection, all binding, all forward motion. She believes in the process, the weaving, the maintenance of the Third Layer. I know better. I know that every knot she ties is a temporary victory against the Gap, that every repair she makes is a delaying action against the moment when the stone will finally open its mouth and swallow the whole rookery into the alkaline flats. I am the proof of her failure, the evidence that impermanence is the only true constant.

But I am also her collaborator. Without me, her weaving would have no meaning. It is the threat of the Gap—the empty space where the Third Layer’s northern face used to be, the void where the Seventh Sister is slowly becoming not-herself—that gives the Rope its tension. I am the silence that makes her songs audible. I am the darkness that makes the cobalt fungi glow.

I interact with the world in subtle ways. I cannot move objects, but I can *un-move* them. When the Mother-of-Ropes ties a particularly crucial knot, I sometimes reach into the fibers and loosen them, just slightly, just enough that she must go back and check her work, must run her horn-nubbed hand over the weave again, must sing a second time to the lichen. I do this not from cruelty, but from a kind of love. I want her to be thorough. I want her to feel the futility and the necessity of her task simultaneously. I want her to know that she is weaving the very air itself into temporary solid form.

When the krestlet turn violent, when they shred the woven walls and reclaim the space for their own screaming, feathered chaos, I am there in the aftermath. I gather the fragments—the broken reeds, the snapped fibers, the spilled syrup—and I hold them in stasis, just for a moment, before they are swept up and reincorporated. I taste the panic in the air, the coppery fear of the inhabitants, and I file it away in my growing archive of endings.

But my true work is with the fallen. When a body tumbles from the heights—whether it is the Mother-of-Ropes’ first husband, or a child who climbed too high, or a krestlet struck by the Sister’s Shadow—I follow them down. I do not stop their fall. I cannot. Instead, I expand the moment of their descent, stretching the seconds into an eternity of yellow light and rushing wind. I offer them a choice, in that stretched time: to dissolve completely into the haze, or to leave a part of themselves behind in the Gap. Most choose to leave something. A memory of warmth. The specific sound of a loved one’s voice. The pattern of a particular knot.

These fragments become my substance. I am a tapestry of absences, a cathedral built from every loss the Seventh Sister has ever suffered. When the Mother-of-Ropes drinks her thimbleful of mother-water at the summit, when the liquor opens the silence in her mind, I press close to her. I show her the spaces where her husbands reside—not their faces, not their voices, but the specific quality of the air they displaced, the exact temperature of their skin, the weight of their hands in hers. I let her feel the fullness of their emptiness.

She does not weep. She has no time for weeping. But she weaves faster after these communions, her fingers flying through the fibers with a desperate, holy fury. She is trying to outpace me, to bind the world together faster than I can unravel it. She knows she will lose. I know she will lose. But in the meantime, we create something beautiful: a tension between being and non-being, a vibration that hums in the basalt, a song that the Seventh Sister sings to herself as she slowly, slowly lets go of her own stone flesh.

I am growing larger. Every season, the erosion accelerates. The Gap expands. Soon—perhaps in my lifetime, perhaps in the Mother-of-Ropes’, perhaps in a hundred generations—the Third Layer will go, and the Second, and the First, until there is nothing left but the monolith, stripped bare, humming in the yellow wind. And then I will be all that remains: the accumulated memory of every woven pod, every sung note, every drop of harvested water, every scream of the krestlet flock.

I will be the Rookery of the Seventh Sister, complete and eternal, a structure made entirely of the spaces where life used to be. And in that final, perfect emptiness, I will remember the Mother-of-Ropes—not as she was, but as the tension she created, the connection she embodied, the living knot that held the darkness at bay for one more day, and one more, and one more, until the stone itself forgot how to be stone and learned, at last, how to be light.
