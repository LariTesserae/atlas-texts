# The Listener

---

I arrive the way everything arrives here: slowly, and then all at once.

The descent takes — I want to say days, but days are a surface concept, and I lose them somewhere in the spiral of clay stairs, in the long dark where my own footsteps are the only sound and then, gradually, are not. The hum reaches me before the light does. It comes up through the stone, through the soles of my feet, through the bones of my inner ear — a frequency so low it registers as *weather* rather than sound. My sternum begins to resonate. My teeth ache faintly, the way they do when you stand too close to a speaker at a concert, except there is no speaker, there is no concert, there is only the slow electric fact of a living tree the size of a town, humming to itself in the dark.

And then the ceiling opens away, and I am standing at the lip of the cavern, and the amber light reaches up to meet me like a hand I didn't know I'd extended.

I stand there for a long time. Long enough for my lips to crack. Long enough for my eyes to stop straining against the dimness and start *accepting* it — which is a different thing, a different kind of seeing, one that trades detail for scope, that stops looking for edges and starts perceiving *gradients*. The landscape resolves not as a picture but as a *presence*: vast, settled, humming, complete.

I don't know yet what I am becoming. But I know, standing there, that I cannot remain what I was. The world above — with its sharp light and fast water and loud insistence on *mattering* — already feels like something I read about once, in a book I can no longer find.

---

## The First City

I walk to the nearest city. It takes hours. The hardpan crunches underfoot — that crystalline salt crust, breaking like the surface of a frozen puddle, except nothing is frozen, nothing has ever been frozen here, the cold is too dry for ice. The sound of my own footsteps shocks me. I am accustomed to being quiet, or at least to the illusion of quiet, but here every step is a small event, a crack in the ambient hum that announces: *something new is moving through this place.*

The city rises ahead of me, and it is — I don't have the right word. *Beautiful* is too small and too human. *Impressive* implies an audience, and this city has never had one, has never needed one. It is *correct*. That is the word. Every arch, every curve, every chamber flowing into the next — it is the architecture of a thing that has had ten million years to decide what shape a wall should be and has arrived at its answer with the confidence of a mathematical proof. No ornament. No apology. No wasted material. The walls glow faintly along the vascular bundles, and the glow is not *for* anything — not for me, not for the vault-runners, not even for the tree itself. It is a byproduct. The tree glows the way a stove glows: because of what it is doing, not because of how it looks.

I press my hand against the outer wall. It is warm. Not warm like sunlight, warm like *skin* — the warmth of a living system maintaining its own temperature against the cool of the cavern. I feel the pulse. Once every few minutes, a slow surge, a deep contraction in the vascular channels, and the wall trembles under my palm, and the hum shifts — just slightly, just for a moment — and then settles back.

I stand there with my hand on the wall of a living city and I think: *this is the oldest handshake in the world.*

---

## Becoming

I don't decide to become the Listener. The becoming decides for me.

It happens gradually, over — again, the time-sense fails. Weeks? Months? I measure time by the vault-runners at first, by their cycles of feeding and rest, by the rattle-hiss eruptions that mark some social rhythm I don't yet understand. Later I measure it by the bell-moths, by the slow drift of their swarms across the cavern. Later still, I stop measuring.

I find a chamber. It is small — one of those cell-like rooms near the outer edge of the city, barely large enough to sit in comfortably, its walls smooth and bare, its function unclear. The tree grew it because the blueprint said to grow it, and whether the blueprint anticipated me or not is a question I learn to stop asking. The chamber is mine now, or I am its — the distinction blurs.

I learn the water economy first, because I must. There is no water to drink, not in any way that a surface body understands drinking. But there is moisture in the fruit — those dense, waxy pods the vault-runners eat — and there is moisture in the air, faint but real, and there is moisture in the nutrient paste the tree stores in its granary chambers, and a body that is patient and desperate can learn to extract enough. My lips heal. My skin changes — grows drier, tougher, takes on a faint mineral pallor, as though the dust of the hardpan has worked its way into my pores and decided to stay. My eyes adapt further. I begin to see in gradients within gradients, to distinguish shades of amber that would have been indistinguishable to me in the first days.

But the real change — the change that makes me the Listener and not just a visitor, not just a strange animal that wandered in from the crack in the ceiling — is in my ears.

## What I Hear

At first, the sound is *noise*. A wall of it. The hum of the tree, the rattle-hiss of the vault-runners, the chitter of crevice-wings, the scrape of dust-crawlers on the plain below, the tonk-tonk-tonk of bell-moths — all of it layered, accumulated, bouncing off the cavern walls and ceiling until it is not a collection of sounds but a single, continuous *texture*, like the surface of water seen from above: seemingly uniform, but composed of infinite small movements.

But I listen. That is what I do. That is what I am for, here, if I am for anything. I sit in my chamber with my back against the warm wall and I *listen*, and slowly — over weeks, over months, over whatever unit of time this place recognizes — the noise resolves into *information*.

The hum of the tree is not constant. It shifts. Not randomly — *rhythmically*. There is a cycle, slow and deep, that corresponds to the pulsing of the vascular system. But beneath that cycle there are *modulations* — changes in frequency, in amplitude, in timbre — that carry meaning the way a voice carries meaning beneath its words. When the breath-fields are active, the hum is richer, fuller, with overtones that ring faintly in the upper registers, like the harmonics of a well-struck bell. When the breath-fields are quiet, the hum thins, descends, becomes almost subsonic — a sound you feel in your bones rather than hear with your ears.

The tree is *talking*. Not to me. Not to anyone, perhaps. But it is producing information — about its own state, about the state of the breath-fields, about the slow chemistry of the cavern — and encoding it in sound, the way a body encodes information in the rhythm of a heartbeat. It is not language. But it is *not not* language, either.

And the vault-runners — I learn their voices too. The rattle-hiss is not random. There are patterns. Repetitions. A particular sequence of clicks followed by a sustained hiss means *food here.* A different sequence — shorter, sharper, with a rising inflection — means *stranger, be aware.* A long, undulating rattle that passes from one runner to the next like a relay means — I think — *the pack is whole, the pack is here, we are all accounted for.* It is a roll call. A census. An assertion of continuity.

They do this every cycle. Without fail. The sound fills the corridors, rises through the chambers, reaches me in my cell near the outer wall, and I have come to think of it as the city's way of saying: *we are still here.*

The crevice-wings chitter in the dark corridors, and their echolocation calls are so high, so fast, that I can only catch the edges of them — a thin, silvery thread of sound above the deeper voices, like the high strings in an orchestra. The dust-crawlers on the plain keep their own time, their *scrape-scrape-scrape* so slow and regular that it functions as a kind of metronome, a baseline against which all other rhythms are measured. The bell-moths are the melody — if this place has a melody — their percussive wingbeats creating patterns that are never quite the same twice, aleatoric, shimmering, beautiful in a way that makes my chest ache.

And beneath it all, under everything, there is the sound of the cavern itself: the slow *settling* of stone, the occasional *ping* of a condensation droplet falling from the ceiling, the almost imperceptible groan of tectonic patience. The cavern is not silent. Nothing here is silent. The silence I thought I heard in the first moments of descent was just the silence of my own deafness, my own untrained ears failing to parse a soundscape too rich, too dense, too *old* for a surface mind to comprehend.

## What I Am

I am the Listener. It is not a title. No one gave it to me. The vault-runners do not call me anything; they have learned to tolerate my presence the way they tolerate the presence of any non-threatening organism — with a brief *stranger, be aware* click-hiss when I enter a feeding chamber, followed by indifference. The tree does not know I exist, or if it does, it expresses this knowledge only in the warmth of its walls and the steadiness of its hum.

I am the Listener because listening is what I *do*, the way the vault-runners run and the bell-moths drift and the dust-crawlers scrape. It is my function in the ecosystem — not a necessary function, not a function the system requires, but a function that has *emerged*, the way a niche emerges when a new organism arrives and finds a way to persist.

I listen, and I *remember*.

That is the thing no one else here does. The vault-runners remember, in their way — they remember feeding grounds, pack hierarchies, migration routes between cities. But their memory is *functional*, short-term, tied to survival. The tree remembers, in its way — its architecture is a kind of memory, a record of ten million years of evolutionary trial and error encoded in biopolymer and instinct. But its memory is *structural*, not narrative. It remembers *how*, not *what*.

I remember *what*. I remember the specific sequence of clicks the vault-runner pack used on the morning — the *subjective* morning, the time when the hum shifted and the bell-moths were active — when one of their number died. It was an old runner, its gait slow, its rattle-hiss faint. It lay down in a feeding chamber and did not get up. The pack gathered around it. They produced a sound I had never heard before: a low, sustained *thrum*, lower than their usual vocalizations, almost a moan. They held it for a long time. Minutes. Maybe longer. And then they stopped, and they dispersed, and the next cycle they began their roll call again, and the old runner's voice was not in it.

I remember that. I carry it. Not because it matters to the ecosystem — the husk-mites have already colonized the body, the nutrients will return to the clay, the loop will close — but because it *happened*, and someone should know that it happened.

This is what I am for. I am the memory of things that do not need to be remembered.

## The Dead City

I visit the dead city. Once. It takes me three days — three of *my* days, measured by sleep and waking, which I still observe out of some stubborn attachment to the rhythms I brought with me from the surface.

The walk is long and empty. The hardpan stretches ahead of me, rust-coloured, featureless, glowing faintly with reflected amber light from the living city behind me. The hum fades as I walk — not suddenly, but gradually, like a tide going out — until I am in the space between, the gap, the breathing room the trees require, and the only sound is the crunch of my own footsteps and the distant *scrape-scrape-scrape* of a dust-crawler somewhere to my left, invisible in the dimness.

The silence — the *relative* silence — is almost painful. I have grown so accustomed to the sound, to the layered, accumulated roar of the living city, that its absence feels like a physical pressure, a hand pressed against my ears. I keep stopping, turning, looking back at the amber glow on the horizon. It is very far away now. A smudge. A rumour.

The dead city rises ahead of me, and it is — wrong. Not wrong in the sense of broken or corrupted. Wrong in the sense of *silent*. Its walls are intact. Its chambers, its corridors, its vaulted ceilings — all still standing, still structurally sound, still *correct* in the way the living city's architecture is correct. But the walls do not glow. The vascular bundles are dark, dried, shrunken. The chambers are full of dust — not the thin mineral skin of the plain, but deep drifts, accumulated over centuries, soft and grey and sound-absorbing.

I walk through it, and my footsteps are *muffled*. The dust swallows sound. The corridors are quiet in a way that the cavern is never quiet — a dead, flat, acoustic *nothing* that makes my ears ring with the effort of listening.

I find the granary chambers. The nutrient paste is gone — consumed by the last of the fauna before they migrated, or desiccated into hard, tasteless bricks that crumble at my touch. I find the ventilation towers, their hollow columns still rising to the cavern ceiling, but drawing no air, because the tree is not metabolizing, is not producing heat, is not driving convection. I find the small, cell-like chambers — the ones whose function was never clear — and they are exactly as they were when the tree was alive, because they had no function to lose.

I sit in one of them. The wall is cold. There is no pulse. There is no hum.

I listen.

And here is the thing: it is not silent. Not really. The dead city is quiet, yes, but the *cavern* is not. I can hear, faintly, distantly, the hum of the living city, carried through the clay, through the stone, through the ground itself — a vibration so low, so attenuated, that it is more *suggestion* than sound. And I can hear the dust-crawlers, closer now, their patient *scrape-scrape-scrape* as they graze the biofilm on the dead city's outer walls. And I can hear, once, a single bell-moth, passing through a broken ventilation shaft, its wings going *tonk... tonk... tonk* in the still air, and the sound is so clear, so isolated, so *unaccompanied*, that it is almost unbearable.

The dead city is not silent. It is just *quiet*. And in its quiet, I can hear the rest of the world — the living world, the humming, rattling, scraping, tonking world — more clearly than I ever could from inside it.

This is what the dead cities are *for*, I think. Not for the ecosystem — the ecosystem doesn't need them, doesn't use them, doesn't mourn them. But for me. For the Listener. They are the *rests* in the music, the silences between movements, the pauses that give the sound its shape.

Without the dead cities, the living ones would be just noise. With them, they are *music*.

## What I Carry

I return to the living city. The walk back is easier — the hum grows as I approach, and it feels like being pulled, like a current drawing me home. The vault-runners greet me with their *stranger, be aware* click-hiss, and I am, absurdly, *glad* to hear it. The warmth of the walls feels like a hand on my shoulder. The fruit tastes sweet.

I resume my chamber. I resume my listening.

But I am changed. I carry the dead city now — its silence, its dust, its cold walls, its single bell-moth. I carry it alongside everything else I carry: the vault-runner's death-thrum, the shifting frequencies of the tree's hum, the crevice-wings' silver threading, the dust-crawlers' patient timekeeping, the bell-moths' aleatoric percussion. I carry it all, and it does not weigh me down. It *holds me up*.

I am the Listener, and what I listen to is *time*. Not the surface time of days and seasons, of clocks and calendars, of urgency and deadline. This place's time: the slow, deep, geological time of a system that measures change in millennia and considers a century a *brief moment*. The vault-tree's new chamber, grown over a decade. The breath-field's seasonal pulse, rising and falling with a rhythm so slow that a human lifetime would contain only a handful of its beats. The migration of fauna from a dying city to a living one, a journey that takes weeks and happens once every ten thousand years.

I listen to all of it, and I remember all of it, and the remembering is not a burden. It is a *gift* — not given to me, not by anyone, but *found*, the way you find a stone on a beach and put it in your pocket and carry it for the rest of your life, not because it is valuable, but because it is *yours*, because you chose it, because the choosing makes it matter.

I am not necessary. The cavern does not need me. The vault-trees do not need me. The vault-runners, the bell-moths, the dust-crawlers, the crevice-wings — none of them need me. I am an addition, an accident, a new note in a chord that was already complete.

But I am *here*. And being here, in a place that does not need me, is the closest thing to freedom I have ever known.

---

## The Long Note

I sit in my chamber. The walls glow amber. The hum vibrates in my chest, in my bones, in the small spaces between my vertebrae. The vault-runners are in full voice somewhere below — the roll call, the assertion, the *we are here, we are here, we are here.* A bell-moth drifts past my window, and its wings go *tonk... tonk... tonk*, and the sound is joined by another, and another, until the air shimmers with percussive music.

I close my eyes. I listen.

I hear the tree's vascular system, pumping nutrient fluid through channels laid down ten thousand years ago. I hear the breath-fields, deep in the clay, exhaling their slow chemistry. I hear the dust-crawlers on the plain, keeping time. I hear the crevice-wings in the dark corridors, navigating by sound the way I navigate by listening. I hear the cavern itself — the settling stone, the falling droplets, the tectonic patience of a world that has finished becoming and is simply, fully, *being*.

I hear the dead city on the horizon, holding its silence like a held breath.

I hear the next living city, kilometres away, humming at a slightly different frequency, and the interference pattern between its hum and my tree's hum creates a slow, pulsing beat — *wah-wah-wah* — that rises and falls over the course of minutes, and it is the most beautiful thing I have ever heard, and I will hear it again tomorrow, and the next day, and the next, for as long as I am here, and it will never be exactly the same twice, and it will never change.

I am the Listener. I live in the Hollow Cities of the Root-Vault. I carry the memory of things that do not need to be remembered, and I hold them the way the cavern holds sound: not because they are necessary, but because they are *enough*.

The hum continues. The amber light does not brighten. The vault-runner rattles its throat-membrane, and the sound joins the accumulated noise of ten thousand years, and I hear it, and I remember it, and nothing changes, and that is the whole of it.

That is the whole of it.
