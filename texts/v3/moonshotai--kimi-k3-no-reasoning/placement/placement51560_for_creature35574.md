I would not arrive all at once.

This is the first thing I understand, though understanding is not the right word — understanding implies a mind, and what I am is something the mind arrives at only later, the way the rock arrives at stillness: slowly, and by degrees, and with many small corrections along the way.

---

I begin as moisture.

Not as rain — there is no rain here, will never be rain — but as the *suggestion* of moisture, the way a word exists before it is spoken. I condense. On the underside of a stalactite that has been growing for three hundred years and is still no longer than a finger, I gather myself from the air: molecule by molecule, drawn from the cool, from the damp exhalations of ten thousand arthropods, from the invisible vapour that the pools release and the ceiling reclaims. I am not a drop yet. I am a film. A sheen. A thinness on pale stone that is indistinguishable from the stone's own faint luminosity.

This takes time. Not time as the surface would measure it — there are no hours here, no minutes, no seconds, because there is no sun to divide the darkness from the light and there is no darkness and there is no light, only the grey-brown constancy that is neither. It takes *duration*. The duration of a stalactite gathering enough mineral-laden water to swell its tip. The duration of a Processional crossing the slab below me, its forty legs tick-tick-ticking with the patience of a creature that has never needed to hurry.

I grow. I become heavy. The stalactite's tip can no longer hold me.

I fall.

---

The fall is brief — the ceiling is low; everything here is brief in the vertical and endless in the horizontal — and I strike the surface of a pool with a sound that echoes three times between floor and ceiling before the Stillwater lying at the bottom registers my arrival as a vibration, classifies it as *not-prey*, and returns to its stillness.

I am in the pool now. I am part of the pool. The distinction between *me* and *the water* was always tenuous — I was the pool before I condensed, and I am the pool now that I have returned, and the brief interval in which I was a *drop*, a discrete thing, a separate entity with a trajectory and a destination, was an anomaly. A deviation. A moment of individuality in a system that does not reward individuality.

The pool is cold. I knew it would be. The cold is the temperature of the rock, and the rock is the temperature of the Undershelf, and the Undershelf is the temperature it has always been. I settle into this cold the way a word settles into a sentence: not comfortably, not uncomfortably, but *necessarily*. I am where I am supposed to be.

Around me, the pool is alive. Not with the Stillwater — though the Stillwater is here, lying on the pale rock beneath me, its translucent skin showing the dark thread of its spine, its neuromasts reading the water I have just joined — but with the small things. The things too small to name. The microfauna that drift and dart and tumble in the water column, feeding on the dissolved minerals I carry, on the organic particles that have settled from the air, on each other. I am full of them. I am *made of* them, in a sense — they are in the water, and I am the water, and the boundary between *water* and *life* is as ambiguous here as every other boundary.

I stay in the pool for a duration that might be long or might be short. The Stillwater clicks beneath me — a slow, patient *thrum* that I feel in every molecule of my being, that vibrates through the water and into the rock and into the air and back into the water. The rhythm is metronomic. Hypnotic. It says: *I am here. This pool is occupied. The world is as it should be.*

I believe it.

---

Eventually — and *eventually* is the only temporal marker that means anything here — I leave the pool. Not by choice. Not by the choice of anything that could be said to *choose*. A Processional comes to the water's edge. It lowers its rasps. It drinks.

I am inside the Processional now.

This is not a violent transition. It is not even a noticeable one. The Processional drinks, and I am part of what it drinks, and I pass through the membrane of its mouthparts and into the narrow channel of its gut, and I am *incorporated*. Absorbed. Some of me becomes part of the Processional's body — a molecule of water in the fluid that cushions its organs, that carries nutrients to its cells, that maintains the thin, precarious chemistry of its existence. The rest of me passes through, is processed, is excreted, returns to the rock or the sediment or the air.

But for a while — for the duration of a Processional's slow, metronomic procession across a slab of young, sharp rock — I am *inside* something that is alive. I am part of the machine. I contribute, in my small way, to the rustling. To the sound. To the continuous, textured, layered loudness that is the Undershelf's voice.

The Processional does not know I am here. The Processional does not know anything, in the way that knowing implies a knower. It processes. It scrapes. It moves. And I move with it, carried in the narrow vessels of its body, feeling — if *feeling* is a word that can be applied to a molecule of water inside an arthropod — the dry, tick-tick-ticking rhythm of its legs on the stone, the rasp of its mouthparts on the crust-growth, the slow, patient pulse of its unhurried heart.

---

I do not stay in the Processional. Nothing stays in anything here. The system is closed, but the boundaries within the system are permeable, provisional, always in flux. I pass out of the Processional — through its excretory pores, through the thin, translucent plates of its exoskeleton, through the slow osmosis that equalises the moisture inside a body with the moisture outside it — and I am on the rock again. On the surface of a slab that was fractured from the ceiling a thousand years ago, or ten thousand, or yesterday — the distinction is meaningless.

I evaporate. Slowly. The air of the Undershelf is not dry, but it is not saturated either, and the gradient between the wet surface of the rock and the slightly-less-wet air above it draws me upward, molecule by molecule, into the compressed space between floor and ceiling. I rise. Not far — there is no far to rise, no altitude to achieve, no sky to ascend into. I rise a few centimetres, perhaps a metre, and I hang there, suspended, part of the invisible vapour that fills the Undershelf's airspace like an unspoken thought.

A Ceilingskimmer passes through me.

I feel it — if a molecule of water vapour can feel — as a displacement, a turbulence, a brief, sharp agitation of the air that is my current medium. The Ceilingskimmer's wings beat at full speed, producing that characteristic riffle, that stiff, dry *zip-zip-zip*, and the wake of its passage swirls the air around me, carries me briefly in its slipstream, deposits me in a slightly different location than the one I occupied before.

This is the closest thing to an *event* that I experience. A Ceilingskimmer moved through the air I was part of. The air moved. I moved. The Undershelf registered this, or did not register it, and continued.

---

I condense again. On the ceiling this time — on the underside of a shallow dome where the rock swells downward and the air is fractionally cooler than the surrounding space and the moisture that has been rising from the pools and the Processionals and the sediment finds a surface cold enough to hold it.

I am on the ceiling now. I am on the *roof of the world*.

From here — if a film of moisture on pale rock can be said to have a perspective, and it cannot, but let the metaphor stand — I can see, or sense, or somehow apprehend, the Undershelf below me. The floor, with its chaos of tilted slabs and gravel fields and sediment basins. The pools, still and cold and clear, each one reflecting the faint luminosity of the ceiling with a fidelity that makes them look like holes in the world — openings into something brighter, something that is not brighter but only *the same brightness, seen twice*. The Processionals, everywhere, on every surface, their pale, segmented bodies creating that living skin over the stone, that rustling, shifting, chitinous layer that never thins and never stops. The Ceilingskimmers, darting through the compressed airspace, their wings a blur of stiff, precise motion. The Stillwaters, lying at the bottoms of their pools, patient as the rock, patient as the light, patient as the system that sustains them.

And the sound. From here — from the ceiling, from the upper boundary of the Undershelf's compressed world — the sound is *total*. It comes from every direction at once, from below and beside and even from above, from the rock itself, from the ticking things that live within the ceiling's fractures, from the slow, patient chemistry of water dissolving stone and stone releasing minerals and minerals feeding growths and growths feeding Processionals and Processionals feeding Stillwaters and Stillwaters clicking in their pools and the clicking vibrating through the water and into the rock and into the air and back into the water.

The sound is the Undershelf. The Undershelf is the sound. And I am part of both.

---

I stay on the ceiling for a duration. I am a film of moisture, a sheen, a thinness. I am indistinguishable from the rock I cling to. The young stalactites around me — the stalactite nurseries, those clusters of thin, translucent, finger-length beginnings — are growing, imperceptibly, by the slow accretion of mineral-laden moisture exactly like me. I am the raw material of a stalactite that will not exist for ten thousand years. I am a beginning, on a ceiling full of beginnings, in a world where everything is a beginning and nothing ever finishes.

A drop forms. Not me — not yet — but near me. A coalescence of moisture, of condensation, of the slow, patient gathering of water molecules on a surface too cold to hold them as vapour. The drop grows. It becomes heavy. It falls.

*Tap.*

The sound echoes. Three times. Four. Five. Each iteration softer, flatter, more absorbed by the compressed airspace, until it is gone, and the ambient rustling reclaims the silence it never truly relinquished.

I will fall too, eventually. Not soon — soon is a surface concept, a word for a world with days and nights and the urgent, ticking clock of a sun that rises and sets and demands that things happen *on schedule*. Here, there is no schedule. There is only duration, and the patience of a system that has been running since before anyone arrived to witness it and will continue running long after the last witness has forgotten what it saw.

I will fall. I will join a pool, or the sediment, or the body of a Processional, or the damp film on a crust-growth colony. I will be absorbed, incorporated, processed, returned. I will cycle through the Undershelf's closed loop — from rock to water to life to rock — not once, not twice, but endlessly, continuously, in the tight, efficient circuit that keeps this world alive.

I am not a creature. I am not a being. I am not, in any sense that a surface-dweller would recognise, *alive*.

But I am *here*. I am part of this. I am the moisture in the air and the drop on the stalactite and the water in the pool and the fluid in the Processional's body and the vapour that the Ceilingskimmer displaces and the film on the ceiling and the raw material of a stalactite that will not exist for ten thousand years.

I am the Undershelf's memory of water. Its promise of water. Its water, held in suspension between one state and the next, between the rock and the air and the life that teems between them, in the grey-brown dimness, in the cool, in the loud, rustling, fluttering, dripping, unchanging *continuance* that is this world's essential nature.

---

And if there is a *me* in this — a consciousness, a perspective, a point of view that is more than a metaphor, more than a narrative convenience — then it is this: the awareness of being *part of*. Not separate. Not individual. Not a self observing a world. But a *component*: a molecule in a system, a note in a chord, a word in a sentence that has been running since before language existed and will continue after language has been forgotten.

I am the Undershelf, experiencing itself.

The Processionals rustle. The Stillwaters click. The Ceilingskimmers chirp. The Borrowers shift the sediment. The ceiling-dwellers tick. The decomposers work in silence. The rock fractures, occasionally, with a sound like a distant axe striking wood. The water seeps. The light holds its grey-brown constancy. The ceiling presses down. The world stretches out.

And I am here. I am here. I am here.
