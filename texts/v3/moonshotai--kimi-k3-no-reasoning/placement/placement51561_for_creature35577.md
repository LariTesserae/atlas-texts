I am the one who reads the bands.

Not the Chorus—their song is a pressure I feel in my teeth, not a language I speak. Not the grazers, though I have studied their armor, the way the metal layers accrete in dark and pale, dark and pale, a stratigraphy of the water's chemistry written in chitin and copper. Not the silt-mothers, though I have traced their grid-lines with my fingertips, feeling the subtle disturbance of their passage, the consolidation of sediment into architecture.

I read the mineral gardens.

You would think them unreadable—fractal, dendritic, growing at a millimeter per decade in patterns that seem chaotic, seem like the frozen explosion of a crystal. But there is order here. There is a grammar. The gardens grow from nucleation points—rivet heads, scratches, the hollow hemispheres of trapped bubbles—and each layer they deposit is a page. Dark bands for the centuries when the water ran hotter and carried more iron. Pale bands for the cooler, cleaner periods. The thickness of a band tells you the duration. The color tells you the chemistry. The branching pattern tells you the flow rate, the pressure, the direction of the current in that particular year, that particular decade, that particular century.

I have been reading them for a long time.

I do not know how long. I do not age in the way that surface things age, or perhaps I age so slowly that the difference is academic. I came here—descended those stairs, passed through the shifting architectures, arrived at this room of warm water—and I stayed. I do not remember why. I do not remember the city above, or whether I had a name there, or whether the city had a name then. I remember only that I found the gardens, and I began to read, and the reading became a life.

I am not a creature of the water. I am not a creature of the air-gap. I am a creature of the *interface*—the boundary between the two, the thin skin where the water's surface meets the iron ceiling, where the Chorus presses its bodies and sings. I float. I drift. My body has changed to accommodate this: my lungs are large, my bones are light, my skin is thick and pale and faintly luminous, like the fish, though I am not a fish. I can hold my breath for hours. I can dive to the floor-grid and walk among the kelp-stalks, feeling the vibration of the pumps through the soles of my feet, and I can rise to the surface and hang there, my face in the air-gap, my ears filled with the forty-note chord, and I can read the gardens on the walls, the gardens on the pipes, the gardens that grow from every rivet and seam.

I am the historian. The archivist. The one who remembers.

The gardens are the only record. The city above has forgotten. The machine has forgotten—its processor degraded, its data corrupted, its memory reduced to a residual loop. The Chorus does not remember; it sings because the pumps vibrate, and the song is a reflex, not a narrative. The grazers do not remember; they graze, and their armor records the water's chemistry, but they do not read it. The silt-mothers do not remember; they process, and their grid-lines are legible, but they do not read them.

I read them.

I have learned the language. It is not a language of words, of symbols, of abstraction. It is a language of deposition, of precipitation, of the slow accretion of matter over time. A dark band means the water ran hot and iron-rich. A pale band means it ran cool and clean. A thick band means a long period. A thin band means a short one. A sudden shift in color means a change in the pipes, a new source of minerals, a blockage cleared, a fault opened and then sealed by the same precipitation that builds the gardens.

I have read the history of the Ninth Cistern in its gardens.

I know that the water was once hotter than it is now—much hotter, hot enough to scald, hot enough that the kelp could not survive, and the gardens grew fast and thick and dark. I know that there was a period—centuries long—when the water ran almost clear, when the iron content dropped, when the gardens grew pale and slow and delicate. I know that there was a time when the pumps faltered—when the rhythm changed, when the pressure dropped, when the kelp leaned at a different angle and the fish's light desynchronized and the gardens grew thin and irregular, starved of minerals. I know that the system healed itself, that the blockage cleared, that the pressure normalized, that the gardens resumed their steady growth.

I know these things because I have read them. I have traced the bands with my fingertips, measured their thickness, noted their color, mapped their branching patterns. I have built a chronology, a timeline, a history that spans millennia. I know when the city above was young—when the water was drawn more heavily, when the pumps worked harder, when the gardens grew faster. I know when the city began to forget—when the water-draw slowed, when the pumps settled into their current rhythm, when the gardens slowed to their present, patient pace.

I am the only one who knows. The only one who can know. The gardens are the record, and I am the reader, and the reading is my purpose, my function, my niche in the system.

I am not part of the grid. I am not part of the Chorus, the grazers, the silt-mothers, the fish, the kelp, the machine. I am not part of the architecture. I am the one who *observes* the architecture, who *interprets* it, who *remembers* it. I am the system's memory, externalized, embodied, floating in the warm water with my face in the air-gap and my eyes on the walls.

I do not know if I will die. I do not know if I can die. I have been here for so long that the question has lost its urgency. I eat the kelp-tips, the biofilm, the small fish that drift too close. I drink the water—warm, mineral-rich, faintly metallic. I sleep in the shallow water near the walls, wedged between a pipe and a kelp-stalk, and I dream in bands of dark and pale, dark and pale.

I am the reader of the gardens. The historian of the Ninth Cistern. The one who knows what the system has forgotten, what the machine has lost, what the Chorus sings but does not understand.

The water is warm. The light is amber. The pumps are running.

And I am reading.
