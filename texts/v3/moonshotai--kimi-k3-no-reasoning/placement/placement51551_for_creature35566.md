# The Listener in the Mat

I would be the one who hears the root-swimmer.

Not the root-swimmer itself — that life is too complete, too perfectly adapted to its world. I would be something else. Something that came later, or was always there but unnoticed. A human, perhaps, but not one of the ones who installed the machines and left. One who stayed. Or one who arrived after, drawn by the sound, unable to leave.

I would live in the thicket zones, in those dense tangles of interlaced canes that form natural shelters, faintly warmer than the open ground. I would build nothing — the Hollows resist building, the ground too alive, too yielding. Instead, I would hollow out a space in the thickest part of the cane growth, line it with shed root-swimmer fur if I could find it (they shed in patches, sometimes, where they've pressed too long against a rough surface), and I would learn to be still.

The stillness would be the hardest part. Humans are not built for the patience this place requires. We fidget, we shift, we generate noise and heat and disruption. But I would learn. I would have to. The root-swimmers would not come near otherwise, and I would need them to come near.

Because I would be listening for their signals.

The low-frequency vibrations they send through the mat — too low for human ears, but not too low for human hands, human ribs, human spine. I would learn to press myself flat against the ground, to spread my fingers wide on the dense rhizome networks, to feel the pulses that travel through the plant tissue like messages through a wire. At first, I would feel only the machines — the deep throb of the generator, the erratic pulse of the pumps. But eventually, after weeks, months, I would begin to distinguish the other signals. The deliberate, patterned vibrations that are not mechanical. The root-swimmers talking to each other.

I would not understand them. Understanding is not the point. But I would begin to recognize individuals — the one whose signal is slightly faster than the others, the one who always broadcasts from the same location near the third pool to the east, the one whose vibrations carry a quality I would learn to read as alarm, or readiness, or simple presence. I would map their conversations without knowing what they mean. I would become a student of a language made of tremors.

And I would leave things for them.

Not food — they don't need food from me, and offering it would change them, make them dependent, make them something other than what they are. But I would clear paths, sometimes, in places where the mat has grown too dense even for them to pass easily. I would remove the sharp fragments of metal that occasionally flake off the machines and work their way into the root mat, hazards that the root-swimmers cannot see and must navigate around. I would, in small ways, make their world slightly more navigable, and I would do it quietly, slowly, at times when they are far away, so they never associate the changes with me.

I would be a ghost in their world. A presence they feel but never confirm. The large, warm thing that moves on the surface, that they go still for, that they wait out. But also the thing that leaves the mat slightly smoother in its wake, slightly safer.

I would watch the pale glider, too. From my shelter in the canes, I would look up through the gaps in the growth and catch glimpses of it — that pale, translucent shape riding the cold air currents, silent, alone. I would wonder what it eats, if it eats, if it knows it is the only one. I would feel a kinship with it that I would not examine too closely.

And I would tend the beetles.

Not actively — they don't need tending. But I would visit the machines they cluster around, kneel on the root mat, bring my face close to the metal housings, and watch them work. The slow, patient scraping of their mandibles. The faint iridescence of their shells in the amber light. The way they tolerate each other without interaction, without conflict. I would learn from them. I would try to become more like them — patient, armored, content with a small niche and a simple task.

I would not try to fix the machines. They don't need fixing. They run, and they will run until they break, and when they break, the Hollows will continue without them. The plants will grow over them. The beetles will find other surfaces. The root-swimmers will adjust their vibration maps. The system is resilient, and I am not its caretaker.

I am just here. Listening. Learning to be slow. Learning to be cold. Learning to be small enough that the world can continue around me without flinching.

Some days, I would press my ear to the ground and listen to the composite roar — the machines, the water, the creaking biological matter, the wind that barely moves. And beneath it, faint but present, the vibrations of the root-swimmers, talking to each other in a language I will never speak but have learned to hear.

I would be the Listener. The one who stayed. The one who is learning, day by slow day, to belong to a world that does not need me but has not yet asked me to leave.

The roar continues. The frost forms and breaks. And I am here, in the mat, listening.
