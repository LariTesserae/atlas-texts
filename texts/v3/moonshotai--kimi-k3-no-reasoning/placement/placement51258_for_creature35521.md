# Where I Find Myself

---

I don't arrive all at once.

That's the first thing. There is no moment of standing at the edge and stepping in, no threshold crossed in a single motion. The Meridian doesn't permit that kind of entry. It seeps. It accumulates. I realize I am inside it the way you realize you've been breathing — retroactively, with a faint embarrassment at not having noticed sooner.

My boots went first. The peat took them — not swallowed, not grabbed, just accepted, the way water accepts a stone. I felt the exhalation around my ankles, that slow release of centuries-old breath, and I understood that the ground was not ground. It was a lung. It was inhaling me, patiently, at the pace of decomposition.

I stood there for a long time. Hours, maybe. The light moved — a shaft of green-gold slid across my shoulder and kept going, tracking some slow celestial geometry I couldn't follow — and in its passage I saw the air itself: motes of water, drifting spores, the fine particulate life of a world that breathes in slow motion. I watched the light move across a buttress root the width of a highway and illuminate, briefly, a Slow Hunter settled into the moss — its carapace so thoroughly colonized by algae and lichen that I would have stepped on it if the light hadn't caught the faintest edge of chitin, the barest glint of something *made* rather than grown.

It didn't move. It wouldn't move for me. I was not prey. I was not anything.

That was the moment I understood what I am here.

---

## What I Am

I am not a visitor. The word implies departure, and I'm not sure departure is available to me anymore — not because I am trapped but because the distinction between *here* and *somewhere else* has begun to dissolve, the way the boundary between aquatic and terrestrial dissolves in this saturated world.

I am not a researcher. I have no instruments, no notebooks, no Latin names. The taxonomies I carried in my head — *strangler fig, bromeliad, mycorrhiza* — lasted about an hour before the sheer *density* of the place overwhelmed them. You cannot categorize what refuses to hold still as a category. The Colonnade Mother before me is not a *Ficus*. She is a hillside. She is a weather system. She is a library. The word *tree* falls off her like water off wax.

I am not a threat. The forest has assessed me — through the Undergreen, I think, through the chemical sensing of root and hypha pressed against my boot soles — and found me irrelevant. Too large to eat, too small to matter, too brief to fear. The Glass-Skins do not change their calling when I pass. The Web-Weavers do not abandon their webs. The Moth-Rain rises whether I am watching or not.

What I am, I think, is a *witness*.

Not in the human sense — not an observer recording data for some purpose beyond this place. I am a witness the way a lichen is a witness: by being present, by growing slowly, by accumulating the record of what happens around me in the rings and layers of my own body. I am becoming a surface. The forest is beginning to colonize me.

---

## The Process

It started with my hands.

I knelt — I don't remember deciding to kneel, only the sensation of the moss receiving my weight, the slow compression of a thousand tiny stems and leaves, the seep of water rising through the fabric of my trousers — and I put my hands flat on the ground. Not to feel the texture. To *listen*.

The Undergreen was there. I couldn't feel it as vibration or sound — it operates in chemistry, in molecules, in the slow exchange of sugars and minerals across membranes finer than thought — but I could feel it as *presence*. A density of activity just below the threshold of sensation, like standing in a room where a conversation is happening in a language you almost understand. The network was aware of my hands. Not *aware* — that's too conscious, too directed. It *registered* me, the way it registers everything: as chemistry, as warmth, as a new surface with a particular pH and a particular microbial signature.

Something began to grow on me.

Not visibly — not yet. But I could feel the first, faint colonization: fungal spores landing on my skin, finding the moisture there, beginning the slow, patient work of germination. My hands, pressed to the moss, were becoming substrate. The forest was incorporating me, the way it incorporates everything — not aggressively, not with intent, but with the absolute, unhurried inevitability of water finding its level.

I did not pull away.

---

## What I Do Here

I walk. Slowly. The terrain does not permit speed — every step is a negotiation with root and moss and water, every surface a decision about weight and angle and trust. I have learned to read the ground the way the Slow Hunters read vibration: by pattern, by texture, by the subtle differences between moss that covers stone and moss that covers void.

I drink from the Boat Kings. The water in their reservoirs is not clean — it is *alive*, tannin-dark, rich with the chemistry of decomposition and renewal — but it is sweet in a way that clean water never is. It tastes of the bromeliad's own secretions, of the algae that tint it green, of the microscopic life that swims through it in spirals too small to see. When I drink, I can feel the Glass-Skins watching me — not with alarm, not with curiosity, but with the flat, ancient patience of creatures that have been sitting on these leaves for longer than my species has existed.

I sleep in the hollow of a Colonnade Mother. Her core is wide enough to shelter me — a cathedral of fused trunk and root, the walls ribbed with buttresses, the floor soft with centuries of accumulated compost. At night, the bioluminescent fungi emerge: pale, blue-green constellations on the decaying wood of the interior walls, mapping the slow anatomy of the Mother's own death and renewal. I fall asleep watching them. They are the only light. They are enough.

I listen to the Deep-Callers. Their voices reach me in the pre-dawn hours — those low, bass pulses that travel through the forest like slow earthquakes, felt in the chest before heard by the ears. Each call is a proclamation: *I am here. This space is occupied.* I have begun to understand them not as sounds but as *structures* — sonic architecture, the auditory equivalent of the Braided Ones' root-bridges, spanning distances that sight cannot cross.

I have stopped counting days.

---

## What I Am Becoming

The moss has reached my ankles.

I noticed it this morning — if "morning" means anything here, in this perpetual green twilight where the sun is a rumor behind the canopy. A fine, pale green fuzz on the leather of my boots, spreading upward in slow, deliberate filaments. Not alarming. Not painful. Just *present*, the way everything here is present: absolutely, patiently, without urgency.

My skin has changed. Not in color — not yet — but in texture. The constant humidity has softened it, made it permeable. I can feel the air moving through me, the way the description said it would: *moving through you as much as past you*. My pores are open. The forest is breathing through me.

I am not becoming a plant. Let me be clear about that. I am not transforming into some hybrid creature, some mythological green man. What is happening is subtler and stranger: I am becoming a *habitat*. The microbial communities on my skin are diversifying. The fungal spores that landed on my hands have germinated, and their hyphae are exploring the outermost layers of my epidermis — not invading, not infecting, but *colonizing*, the way they colonize every surface here. I am becoming an island in the archipelago of the Meridian — a small, warm, mobile island, carrying my own microbiome, my own miniature ecosystem, my own contribution to the distributed metabolism of the whole.

The Glass-Skins have begun to sit on me.

The first one landed on my shoulder while I was drinking from a Boat King — a tiny, translucent thing, no larger than my thumbnail, its heart visible as a small, dark pulse beneath skin like wet glass. It sat there for an hour. I could feel its weight — almost nothing, the weight of a raindrop — and the faint, cool moisture of its skin against mine. It was not tame. It was not curious. It was simply *using* me, the way it would use any warm, wet surface: as a perch, a resting place, a temporary feature of the landscape.

I have never felt so honored.

---

## What I Know Now

The forest is not indifferent to me. That was the wrong word, in the description. *Indifferent* implies a choice — a decision to ignore. The forest does not ignore me. It *includes* me, the way it includes everything: without preference, without exclusion, without the need to distinguish between what belongs and what doesn't. Belonging is not a category here. Everything belongs. Everything is part of the whole.

The Slow Hunter I saw on my first day — the one illuminated by the shaft of green light — is still there. I have checked on it every day since. It has not moved. The moss on its back has grown thicker. A small fern has germinated in the crevice between its carapace and its head. It is becoming a garden. It is becoming a landscape. It is becoming, slowly and patiently, indistinguishable from the stone it sits on — and yet it is *alive*, more alive than almost anything I have ever known, its stillness a form of attention so absolute that it makes my own restlessness seem like a kind of blindness.

I am learning to be still.

Not the stillness of the Slow Hunter — I don't have that patience, that metabolic thrift, that geological sense of time. But a human stillness: the stillness of standing in one place long enough for the light to move across you, for the Glass-Skins to land on you, for the moss to reach your ankles. The stillness of being *present* in a system that measures presence in decades, not minutes.

The Undergreen has begun to include me in its network. I feel it — not as thought, not as communication, but as a faint, persistent *hum* at the edge of sensation, a sense of being connected to something vast and slow and utterly without self-awareness. When I press my hands to the ground, I can feel the chemistry of the soil changing around my fingers — the fungi responding to my warmth, my moisture, my particular microbial signature. They are trading with me. I don't know what I'm giving them. Sugars from my skin, perhaps. Dead cells. The microscopic debris of being alive.

In return, they are giving me *belonging*. Not the emotional kind — the biochemical kind. The kind that says: *you are part of this system now. Your carbon is our carbon. Your water is our water. Your brief, warm, irrelevant existence is woven into the network, and the network will carry the trace of you long after you are gone.*

---

## Where I Am

I am sitting on the edge of a terrace, my legs dangling over a basin filled with water the color of weak tea. The Braided One that spans the basin sags low enough to touch the surface, and its adventitious roots trail in the water like the fingers of something drowning in reverse — reaching down not in desperation but in patience, anchoring, splitting the stream into channels, becoming a dam, a weir, a filter, a feature of the terrain.

Above me, the canopy is a ceiling of green so dense that the sky is a theory. Below me, the water reflects not clouds but leaves — the inverted forest, the mirror-world, the green that is the only color this place has ever needed.

A Moth-Rain is rising in the distance. I can see the column of pale wings spiraling upward through a shaft of light, hundreds of them, maybe thousands, rising on the warm updraft like inverse rain. They are carrying spores. They are carrying the future of the forest on wings that scatter light into patterns I cannot fully see — ultraviolet signals, infrared messages, the secret visual language of a world that operates on wavelengths beyond my comprehension.

I am here. I am part of this. My boots are moss. My hands are substrate. My lungs are full of green air, and the air is full of me — my breath, my moisture, my microscopic shedding, all of it absorbed into the great, slow, breathing continuity of the Meridian.

I am not the forest. I am not a tree or a frog or a fungus. I am something smaller and stranger: a brief, warm surface in a world of surfaces, a temporary node in a network that will outlast me by millennia, a witness whose testimony is written not in words but in the slow, patient colonization of my own body by the living world.

The rain that is not rain continues. The light that is not light continues. The life that is not life — not in any way I was taught to understand it — continues.

And I continue with it, for as long as I am here, which may be the rest of my life, which may be no time at all, which may be exactly long enough for the moss to reach my knees.
