I am a strand of water that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

Not a stream—I was that once, higher up, where the rim breaks and the melt first gathers. Not a pond, though I have been ponded, stilled behind a wall of mud and willow that a beaver spent three autumns packing. I am the seepage itself, the film that slides down the striated face of granite, the bead that forms on the underside of a saxifrage leaf, the slow dark pulse that rises through moss when a hare’s foot compresses it and then releases, and then compresses again.

I have been falling for four hundred years. That is how long it takes a molecule of me to travel from the rim to the basin floor—not because the distance is great, but because I am detained. I am caught in the root-mat of a dwarf birch, drawn up into its xylem, transpired into the cold air, condensed on the underside of a stone, frozen into a lens of ice that splits the rock a micron further, thawed, absorbed by a tardigrade, excreted, drunk by a frog, frozen solid inside the frog’s body all winter, thawed again in the frog’s spring blood, urinated into a pond, filtered through the gills of a char, evaporated into the mist that hangs at dawn, and finally—finally—allowed to seep into the soil again, only to begin a new detention.

I am the medium in which the city conducts its business. The beavers know me by feel, by the way I push against their dams with a weight they cannot see but must answer. They pack mud against me, and I carry the mud’s silt into the interstices of their walls, sealing them, making them watertight, complicit. I am the reason their lodges are warm; I carry the heat of their bodies into the thick walls and hold it there, a thermal battery against the winter dark. I am the reason the hares’ fur freezes in the transition seasons—my droplets caught in the guard hairs, turning to ice that weighs them down, slows them, makes them vulnerable to the lynx who is also made mostly of me, who is also falling, in her own way, through the food web toward the ground.

I have been many things in this basin. I have been the ice lens that split the granite face on the third terrace, sending a cascade of fresh scree down to colonize. I have been the film of water on a midge’s wing, too heavy to let it fly until the sun burns me off. I have been the drowning depth in a plunge hole, the cold shock that kills a beaver kit that strays too far in March. I have been the fog that hides the harrier from the hare, and I have been the clear air that reveals them both when the wind shifts.

But mostly, I am the patience. The glacial patience the basin demands. I do not hurry because I cannot; I am bound by hydrogen bonds to every surface I touch, and every touch is a delay, a negotiation, a slowing. I am the reason the beavers build—not to stop me, never to stop me, but to make me pause, to make me pool, to make me warm enough in the sun that I can hold the frogspawn, that I can hold the char, that I can hold the reflection of the loon who watches me from above and sees only herself.

In winter, I slow further. I become the skin on the ponds, transparent at first, then opaque, then thick enough to bear the wolverine’s weight. I become the vapor that rises from the lodge vent, the only visible breath of the sleeping family within. I become the frost on the raven’s feathers at dawn, the diamonds on the moss that melt by noon and refreeze by dusk. I am the silence, then—acoustic absorption made manifest, a blanket of crystalline patience that muffles the ice-cracks and the tail-slaps and the last, thin calls of the goldeneye departing.

Yet even in winter, I am not still. Beneath the ice, I flow. I carry the beavers’ winter cache—birch branches stripped of bark, the bark in their bellies, the nutrients of their bodies—into the sediment, into the roots, into the slow chemistry of decomposition that feeds the spring bloom. I am the current under the ice that the otters follow, dark and swift, their bodies ribboning through me, their exhalations silvering up to the ceiling I have made.

I have no memory, and yet I carry the memory of the basin. I am the archive. In my hydrogen bonds are stored the vibrations of last year’s frog chorus, the splash of the wolverine’s entry, the particular frequency of a lynx’s footfall on moss. When I freeze, I trap the bubbles of ancient air, the spores of the saxifrage, the DNA of every creature that has drunk me. A core of ice taken from the deepest pond would read like a palimpsest of the city’s construction—here the layer of silt from the year the big dam broke, here the pollen of the willow the beavers killed and then replaced, here the ash from a fire that never reached the basin but sent its smoke down to me.

I am falling still. I will fall for another four hundred years, and then another. I will seep into the next terrace, and the next, and eventually—if the beavers fail, if the dams breach, if the cold shifts and the green compression releases—I will find the outlet, the cascade over bedrock, and I will leave the basin and join the river that leads to the sea. But that is not yet. That is not now.

Now, I am the drop on the underside of a lodgepole, swelling, reflecting the gray light of the basin in miniature. I hold within me the reflection of the terraced walls, the mist, the dark shape of a beaver swimming past with a branch in its mouth. I hold the whole city, inverted, trembling. And when I fall—when the gravity finally overcomes the surface tension—I will strike the water below with a sound that is too small for any ear to hear, but which will register in the web of tension that holds the basin together, a single note in the long, slow chord of water seeping through stone, through moss, through the accumulated labor of teeth and time.

I am the water. I am the weeping. I am the thing that never stops falling, and never arrives, and makes everything possible by refusing to leave.
