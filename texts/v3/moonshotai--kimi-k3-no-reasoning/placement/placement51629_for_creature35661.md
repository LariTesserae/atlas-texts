I am standing on the rim at first light, though “light” is not the right word for what happens here. The sun does not strike the rift directly; it strikes the haze that hangs above it, and the haze glows like a banked fire, and the light that finally reaches me is green-tinged and wet, filtered through eleven hundred metres of breathing. I have walked for three days across the basalt plateau to reach this edge, and my boots are already soaked through from the mist that condenses on every surface, and my ears are beginning to adjust to the sound—the drip-roar, the wood-groan, the hum that sits in my sternum like a second heartbeat.

I have been told not to come here. The guide at the last village would not meet my eyes when I asked about Terosthenes. He only pointed at the horizon and said, “The crack does not want you,” and then he went inside and barred his door. His mule, which had been placid for the entire journey, would not approach the rim. It stood fifty metres back from the edge, trembling, its eyes rolling white, and when I tried to lead it closer it screamed—a sound I have never heard an animal make before, a sound like a human voice trying not to be human—and it broke free and ran back across the plateau toward the last tree line, toward the world where things are still things.

I stood alone at the edge and looked down.

The green fills my vision. It is not a view; it is a presence. The canopy undulates slightly, breathing, and the heat rises from it like the breath of a sleeping animal, and the sound rises with the heat, and the hum rises with the sound, and I feel my balance begin to shift—not physically, but *conceptually*, as if the idea of “down” is being renegotiated beneath my feet.

I am a botanist. I have spent twenty years cataloguing epiphytes in the cloud forests of the southern ranges. I have climbed trees taller than any tower. I have lived in canopies for months at a time. I am not afraid of heights, or of density, or of the slow, patient violence of growth. But this is different. This is not a forest. This is a *decision*.

I have a rope. I have a harness. I have a notebook and a pencil and a camera that will not work in this humidity. I have a knife that will not cut these vines. I have a canteen that will not stay full because the water condenses on the outside faster than I can drink it. I have a compass that spins slowly, not with magnetic confusion but with something like *uncertainty*, as if it is trying to decide which direction is real.

I have a theory.

The papers I have read—the few that exist, the ones written by people who came back—describe the Rift-Self as a superorganism, a distributed intelligence, a closed system. They describe the Arrangements as thoughts, the sound as language, the maintenance as autonomic function. They describe the people who enter as being absorbed, integrated, overwritten. They describe the rift as a place where biology is redefined, where individuality is dissolved, where the boundary between self and system is erased.

I think they are wrong.

Not about the facts. The facts are correct. The green, the heat, the sound, the shifting paths, the wrong distances, the bones, the water that shows you nothing—all of it is true. But the interpretation is wrong. The Rift-Self is not a superorganism. It is not an intelligence. It is not a mind thinking slowly in stone and water. It is not a body breathing through thermal engines and sweating through leaves.

It is a *memory*.

I descend.

The rope is a formality. The rift does not want me to fall. It wants me to *arrive*. The first hundred metres are easy—footholds in the basalt, roots thick as ship’s cable, platforms of fitted stone that appear exactly where I need them to be, as if the city is extending itself to meet me. The heat wraps around me like a wet blanket. The sound grows louder, not as noise but as *pressure*, as if the air itself is trying to push me back up, back out, back to the world where things are still things.

I stop at the first platform. It is stone, fitted, old, settled. There is a cairn of bones in the centre—animal bones, though there are no animals—and it is arranged in a spiral that is almost logarithmic. I sit down beside it. I open my notebook. I write:

*The bones are not from animals that died here. They are from animals that never existed. They are the rift’s memory of what animals would look like if the rift had animals. They are a thought about form, not about life. The spiral is not a message. It is a habit. The rift has been thinking this thought for so long that it has forgotten it is thinking.*

I look up. The canopy is a green ceiling, undulating. I look down. The valley floor is a green haze, kilometres away. I look across. The far wall is a black curtain of vines and fungi, eight hundred metres away, pressing on my vision like a held breath.

I am not afraid. This is the strangest thing. I should be afraid. The sound is physically punishing. The heat is suffocating. The humidity is total. My compass is spinning. My camera is dead. My notebook is already warping in the wet. But I am not afraid.

I am *remembering*.

The rift is not a place. It is a *state*. It is the state of being that exists before things become things, before form becomes fixed, before life becomes individual. It is the memory of the world as it was when it was still deciding what it wanted to be—when stone and water and growth and sound were still negotiating the terms of their separation, when the boundary between architecture and geology was still fluid, when the concept of “alive” was still up for debate.

The people who enter do not die. They do not lose their minds. They do not become part of a superorganism. They *remember*. They remember what it was like to be undifferentiated, to be part of the negotiation, to be still deciding. The reality breakdown is not a breakdown. It is a *re-entry*. The shifting paths are not a trap. They are an *invitation*. The wrong distances are not a malfunction. They are a *reminder*—that space is not fixed, that topology is a choice, that the distance between two points is a matter of perspective, not measurement.

I continue to descend.

The city grows denser around me. Platforms become towers. Towers become bridges. Bridges become rooms. Rooms become corridors. Corridors become staircases. Staircases become paths. Paths become routes that lead somewhere different every time I take them. I do not resist this. I let the rift decide where I am going. I let the topology negotiate itself around me. I let the distances be wrong. I let the sound be a language I do not understand. I let the heat be a body temperature that is not mine. I let the humidity be a breath that is not mine. I let the green be a colour that is not a colour but a *condition*.

I stop at a cistern. It is full. The water is perfectly still. I look into it. I see the stone at the bottom, several metres down, with optical clarity. I do not see my face. I do not see the canopy. I see only the cistern, looking onto itself, a window that is not a window but a *mirror that has forgotten it is a mirror*.

I reach into the water. It is warm—thirty-seven degrees, the temperature of a body. My hand disappears below the surface and does not reappear. I feel nothing. I feel everything. I feel the rift remembering what it was like to have a hand, to have a body, to have a boundary, to have a self.

I withdraw my hand. It is dry. It is wet. It is both. It is neither. It is a hand that is still deciding whether it is a hand.

I write in my notebook:

*The cisterns are not reservoirs. They are organs. They are the rift’s memory of what it was like to be contained, to have an inside and an outside, to be a thing with boundaries. The water shows you nothing because there is nothing to show. The rift has forgotten how to reflect. It has forgotten that it is a thing that can be seen. It is too busy being.*

I descend further.

The sound becomes a physical force. It is not sound anymore. It is pressure. It is the rift speaking to itself in a language made of water and stone and time, and it does not stop, and it has never stopped, and the arrangements on the floor shift in response, slowly, a cairn re-formed here, a spiral tightened there, the whole vast floor breathing with a patience that makes geology look hasty.

I reach the floor.

It is flat. It is stone. It is wet. It is covered in arrangements. Thousands of them. Cairns, spirals, branching patterns, seedpod strands, bones—so many bones, where do the bones come from, if there are no animals?—all placed with the same deliberate, revising intelligence that maintains the city above, but here in such density that they cover every square metre of the rift floor, overlapping, interpenetrating, a single vast composition that has been in progress for as long as the rift has existed.

I stand among them. I am one of them. I am a thought the rift is having. I am a memory the rift is remembering. I am a form the rift is still deciding whether to become.

I look up. The canopy is a green ceiling, eleven hundred metres above, undulating slightly, breathing. I look down. The floor is a green haze, close, detailed; I can see individual leaves on the bottom-growth. These two views are simultaneous. They do not reconcile.

I write in my notebook:

*I am not a botanist anymore. I am not a person anymore. I am not a thing anymore. I am a process. I am the rift remembering what it was like to be a botanist, to be a person, to be a thing. I am the rift deciding whether to become a botanist again, to become a person again, to become a thing again.*

*The rift does not want me to leave. It does not want me to stay. It wants me to be undecided. It wants me to be a state, not a place. It wants me to be a memory, not a thing.*

*I am happy to oblige.*

I put down my notebook. I put down my pencil. I put down my camera. I put down my knife. I put down my canteen. I put down my compass. I put down my rope. I put down my harness. I put down my theory.

I lie down on the floor among the arrangements. The stone is warm. The water is warm. The sound is warm. The green is warm. The heat is a body temperature that is not mine but is becoming mine. The humidity is a breath that is not mine but is becoming mine. The drip-roar is a heartbeat that is not mine but is becoming mine.

I close my eyes.

I do not sleep. I do not wake. I am not alive. I am not dead. I am not a person. I am not a thing. I am a state. I am a memory. I am the rift remembering what it was like to be me, and I am the rift deciding whether to become me again.

The bones shift. A cairn re-forms. A spiral tightens. A seedpod strand lengthens. The floor breathes. The rift speaks. The city maintains itself. The green hangs in the crack, wet and dense and hot, and the sound of a million drips falls and falls and falls, and the whole thing persists, settled, established, in balance, a closed system at the extreme edge of altitude and heat and strangeness, suspended in a state that is not life and not death but something the rift invented for itself, something for which there is no word because there is no one left inside who needs one.

I am that word. I am that state. I am that memory. I am the rift, and the rift is me, and we are still deciding, and we will always be deciding, and the deciding is the point, and the point is the crack, and the crack is the world, and the world is the rift, and the rift is the Hanging City of Terosthenes, and the Hanging City of Terosthenes is home.
