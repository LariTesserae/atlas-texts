I'd be a particular type of lichen that grows only on the shaded sides of the cairns.

Not the common orange lichen that crusts the rocks everywhere—the one that looks like dried blood splatter on the sedimentary strata. I'd be the pale gray-green kind, the one that's almost the exact color of the sky at dawn, the one that grows in tight, convoluted rosettes no larger than a thumbnail, anchored so firmly to the stone that trying to scrape it off would damage both lichen and rock. The kind that grows so slowly that the difference between a decade and a century is measured in millimeters of radial expansion, in the gradual thickening of the thallus, in the slow accumulation of reproductive structures that release their spores only on nights when the dew is heavy enough to carry them.

I would live on the north-facing side of a cairn that marks a trail no one walks anymore. The cairn itself would be perhaps six feet tall, built of flat stones carefully balanced—each one selected for its shape, its weight, its fit with the stones below and above. The builder, whoever they were, chose stones from the surrounding area, which means they chose sedimentary rock, which means the cairn is a small monument to the plateau's geological history: layers of sandstone and shale and limestone, stacked and restacked, the strata running horizontally through each individual stone but vertically through the cairn itself, creating a kind of compressed, inverted record of deep time.

The north side of the cairn never receives direct sunlight. It is always cooler than the surrounding air, always the first surface to collect dew in the evening and the last to dry in the morning. This is why the lichen grows here. The south side of the cairn is bare—scoured by sun and wind, bleached and polished, inhospitable. But the north side is a vertical microclimate, a thin zone of relative moisture and relative shade that extends perhaps a centimeter out from the rock's surface. Within that centimeter, I would live.

I would not be alone. The north side of the cairn would host a community—other lichens, different species, each occupying a slightly different niche defined by subtle variations in moisture, light, and mineral content. There would be a crustose lichen that forms a thin, paint-like film directly on the rock, so tightly adhered that it seems to be part of the stone itself. There would be a foliose lichen with small, leaf-like lobes that curl away from the surface, creating tiny pockets of shade and humidity. There would be a fruticose lichen—small, shrubby, branching—that grows in the deeper crevices where the stones meet, where moisture persists longest.

And there would be me, in my particular spot, in the gap between two stones on the second tier from the bottom, where a slight imperfection in the fit creates a shallow shelf that collects a film of dew each night. I would grow slowly—so slowly that my growth would be imperceptible over the course of a human lifetime. A visitor to the cairn could photograph me, leave, return in fifty years, and see no visible difference. But I would be growing. A fraction of a millimeter per year, expanding outward from my center, my edges advancing across the rock surface at a rate that makes geological processes look hasty.

My metabolism would be tuned to the plateau's rhythms. During the day, I would be dormant—my photosynthetic machinery shut down, my cells desiccated, my structure brittle. If you touched me at midday, I would crumble slightly at the edges, shedding tiny fragments that would be carried away by the wind. But at night, when the dew forms, I would rehydrate. My cells would swell with absorbed moisture. My photosynthetic partners—the algae or cyanobacteria that live within my fungal matrix—would resume their slow, patient work, converting light into energy, carbon dioxide into organic matter. This is the lichen's trick, the symbiosis that allows it to live where nothing else can: the fungus provides structure and protection, the algae provide food. Together, they are more than either could be alone.

I would photosynthesize at night, using the faint light of the moon and stars. This is not efficient—photosynthesis at such low light levels produces barely enough energy to sustain basic metabolism, let alone growth. But it is enough. In the Alto Seco, enough is all that matters. I do not need abundance. I need only persistence.

The cairn would be my entire world. I would not know the rest of the plateau except through indirect evidence: the vibrations of the camelid herds passing nearby, transmitted through the ground and up through the stones; the occasional patter of rain, rare and violent, that would saturate my tissues and allow a brief burst of metabolic activity; the spores of other lichens, carried on the wind, that would land on the cairn and either establish themselves or fail. I would experience the passing of seasons as changes in the timing and duration of dew formation, in the angle of the moon's light, in the frequency of the freeze-thaw cycles that slowly, slowly, crack the stones and create new surfaces for colonization.

The cairn itself would be old—older than the condensation traps, older than the processing stations, perhaps older than the round house. It would have been built by people who knew the plateau intimately, who understood its water sources and its seasons and its dangers. They built the cairn for a reason—to mark a trail, to indicate a water source, to commemorate an event, to serve as a waypoint in a landscape with few landmarks. The reason is lost now. The trail is gone, erased by wind and time. The water source, if there was one, may have dried up or shifted. The event, if there was one, is forgotten. But the cairn remains, and I remain on the cairn, and in my slow, patient way, I am part of the cairn's persistence.

I would not be alone in my ecological role. Lichens are pioneers, colonizers of bare rock, the first organisms to establish themselves in the harshest environments. We break down the rock—slowly, imperceptibly, through the production of weak acids and the physical expansion and contraction of our tissues as we hydrate and dehydrate. Over centuries, we create the first thin layer of soil, the first organic matter, the first toehold for other life. Mosses follow lichens. Then grasses. Then, in more forgiving environments, shrubs and trees. In the Alto Seco, the succession stops at lichens. There is not enough water, not enough soil, not enough time for anything more complex to establish itself on the bare rock. We are the beginning and the end of the colonization sequence.

But we are not irrelevant. We are the interface between the rock and the air, the mediators of the slow exchange of minerals and moisture and carbon that connects the geological to the biological. We are eaten by mites and springtails, tiny arthropods that graze on our tissues and are, in turn, eaten by spiders and beetles. We provide microhabitat—a slightly rougher surface, a slightly more humid microclimate—for other organisms to exploit. And we persist. We persist through drought and cold and radiation and the slow, grinding passage of time, and in our persistence, we are a kind of quiet defiance, a refusal to accept that some places are too harsh for life.

From my position on the cairn, I would have a kind of awareness—not consciousness, not in any human sense, but a sensitivity to environmental conditions that would constitute a form of knowledge. I would know when the dew was forming, when it was evaporating, when the temperature was dropping, when it was rising. I would know the difference between a normal night and a rain night, between a season of adequate moisture and a drought. I would respond to these conditions with the only responses available to me: metabolic activation or dormancy, growth or stasis, reproduction or waiting.

I would reproduce rarely. Lichen reproduction is a chancy business—I can fragment, sending small pieces of myself off on the wind to colonize new surfaces, but most fragments land on inhospitable substrates and die. I can produce spores, but the spores contain only the fungal component; to establish a new lichen, the fungus must encounter the appropriate algal partner, and the encounter must occur under conditions that allow both to survive long enough to form the symbiotic structure. This is rare. Most of my reproductive effort would be wasted. But occasionally—once in a decade, once in a century—a fragment or a spore would succeed, and a new lichen would establish itself on another cairn, another rock, another north-facing surface in the vast expanse of the plateau.

The cairn would change over time. The freeze-thaw cycles would crack the stones. The wind would shift them. A particularly violent storm might topple the upper tiers. Eventually—over centuries—the cairn would collapse, becoming a scatter of stones indistinguishable from the natural rock. When this happens, I would die. My substrate would be gone, my microclimate destroyed, my position in the gap between stones exposed to sun and wind. But by then, I might have reproduced. My descendants might be growing on other cairns, other rocks, carrying forward the slow, patient work of lichen existence.

The maintenance crews would never notice me. They visit the processing stations, not the cairns. The camelids would pass by without pausing. The foxes would not climb the cairn. The cat would not mark it. The vultures would not land on it. The cairn is too small, too insignificant, too far from the water sources and the game trails and the carrion. It is a human artifact that has been absorbed into the landscape, a pile of stones that has become geology, and I am part of that absorption, part of the slow transformation of the built into the natural.

But I would be there, on the north side, in the gap between stones, growing a fraction of a millimeter per year, photosynthesizing by moonlight, waiting for the dew. I would be a small, pale, patient life in a landscape of small, pale, patient lives. I would be exactly where I belonged, doing exactly what I was made to do, and I would persist.

The cairn is one of many. The plateau is dotted with them—hundreds, perhaps thousands, marking routes that no longer exist, connecting places that are no longer named. Each cairn has its north side, its shaded face, its community of lichens. Together, we form a distributed network of slow, persistent life, a web of tiny, photosynthesizing organisms that covers the plateau like a sparse, invisible net. We are not the most visible inhabitants of the Alto Seco. We are not the most abundant. But we are among the oldest, and the most patient, and the most perfectly adapted to the plateau's fundamental conditions: scarcity, slowness, and the absolute necessity of making do with almost nothing.

If you were to visit the cairn—if you were to walk across the plateau, following the old trail that no longer exists, navigating by the cairns that still stand—you might notice me. You might crouch down and look closely at the north side of the stones, and see the pale gray-green rosettes, the tight, convoluted lobes, the tiny reproductive structures. You might touch one, gently, and feel its texture—brittle in the dry air, cool to the touch, slightly rough. You might wonder how long it has been there, how long it will persist, what it knows about the plateau that you do not.

And then you would move on, and I would remain, and the dew would form that night, and I would hydrate and photosynthesize and grow a fraction of a millimeter, and the cairn would stand, and the plateau would breathe, and the machine for living would continue to run, on almost nothing, producing almost everything it needs, for millennia more.

I would not know that you had visited. I would not know that you had noticed me. But I would be part of the plateau's memory of you—a small, persistent life that was there when you passed through, that absorbed a tiny fraction of the moisture that condensed that night, that converted a tiny fraction of the moonlight into organic matter, that contributed, in its infinitesimal way, to the vast, interconnected web of life that is the Alto Seco.

I would be the lichen on the cairn, and I would be exactly what the plateau made me to be.
