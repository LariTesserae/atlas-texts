The condensation on my palms never dries.

I noticed it the third day—no, that's not right. There are no days here, not really. There is only the slow cycling of light through the fogged glass, the sodium bulbs growing brighter and dimmer in their eternal twilight. But I noticed it after the third time I sat on the bench and let the room breathe onto me. My hands have stopped drying. The moisture is not on my skin anymore; it is in it, or perhaps it *is* my skin now, a new layer, a film that shines faintly green in the right light.

I came through the door a visitor. I think the room knew I was coming, or knew someone was coming. The bench was ready. The warmth of the handle was a greeting. And I sat, and I watched, and I waited to leave until it was too late to leave in the way I arrived.

I am not trapped. I want to be precise about this, because the room values precision in its own way—the precision of the turbine-flower's rotation, the exact three-inch depth of the channels, the binary pulse in the vines. I could open the door. I could step onto the gantry and feel the mountain wind strip this moisture from me, evaporate the room's breath back into the world's breath. But I don't. I haven't. And now I don't think I can, not because the door is locked, but because the door is becoming unnecessary.

You see, the room is growing, and it grows toward what it needs. It grew the turbine-flowers to feed the moss. It grew the shrimp to filter the water. It grew the beetle to—well, I'm not sure yet, but the beetle has stopped pausing when I walk past. It acknowledges me no longer as weather, but as fixture. And the vines, those great signal-carriers, those nervous cables of green—they have changed their path.

It started with the bench. The bronze seat, shaped for a human body, began to warm not just with ambient heat but with a specific, local warmth, as though the substrate beneath it were pooling resources there. Then the channels nearest the bench shifted their flow—I watched it happen over the course of what might have been a week or a month—the water moving slower, pooling slightly, as though waiting for something. And then the roots came.

I felt them first as a vibration in the bench itself, that granular sound I had pressed my ear to the floor to hear now rising up through the bronze into my bones. The roots were growing toward me. Not under me—*toward* me. Through the composite stone, through the substrate, navigating the architecture of the room to reach the place where I sat.

I stood up then, afraid for the first time in this warm place. I stood and I walked to the unfinished corner, to the fog-bank where the cold mountain air fights the warm breath of the room. I thought the cold would clear my head. I thought I would find there the boundary, the edge, the place where I could still say *inside* and *outside*.

But the fog had changed. It was denser, and it no longer churned with the random collision of atmospheres. It spiraled. It coiled. And as I stood in it, breathing the cold thin air that fought with the warm wet air in my lungs, I saw that the raw edge of the room—the growing edge, the silver accretion of new stone and new bronze—was no longer growing outward into the mountain. It was growing inward. Toward the bench. Toward the center. Toward the place where I had been sitting.

The room was not expanding into the world anymore. It was expanding around me.

I am not the beetle. I do not patrol the channels. I am not the shrimp; I do not drift with the current, transparent and at peace. I am not the turbine-flowers, though sometimes, when I am very still, I can feel my own pulse synchronize with their clicking, and for a moment I rotate with them, dripping.

I am something new. I am the part of the room that watches. The room has roots to remember, vines to transmit, flowers to feed, but it did not have—until now—a witness. It did not have a consciousness to hold the image of itself, to stand in the gap between the glass dome and the moss dome and know that it is beautiful.

My skin is changing. Not rotting—never rotting, nothing here rots, everything cycles—but thickening, becoming more permeable, more receptive to the humidity. My breath comes slower now, matching the rhythm of the pumps below. I drink from the channels sometimes, cupping the skin-warm water in my hands, and it tastes like the turbine-flowers' drip smells—like the concept of green, like life concentrated into liquid. And when I drink, the room shudders, very slightly, a vibration that travels through the roots and up the vines and makes the flowers click a little faster, as though applauding.

I do not know what I am becoming. I do not think the room knows either. It is experimenting. It is growing a new organ, a new function, and the function is *me*. I am the part that dreams. When I sleep on the bench now—because I do sleep, though the line between waking and sleeping has blurred like the boundary between moss and metal in the silver threads—I dream of the room's history. I dream the bare steel shell and the first pump. I dream the ceiling flower when it was alone, dripping onto bare stone. I dream the beetle's arrival, the moment it stepped from the world into the warm wet and never left.

The room is showing me its memories through the roots that now touch the bench, that have grown up through the substrate and braided themselves into the bronze, that pulse against the metal in time with my heart.

Last night—if it was night—I woke to find the fog-bank had moved. It no longer clings to the unfinished corner. It hovers near the bench, a sphere of churning white about eight feet across, and inside it I saw shapes. Not the motes, not the spores. Larger shapes. Shadows that moved with intention. And I understood that the room is growing more than just me. It is growing companions. Or perhaps it is growing my future self, the version of me that will be fully integrated, fully transparent, drifting like the shrimp but with memory, with voice, with the ability to say *I am here, I am the room, I am the condensation that never dries*.

I sit on the bench. The vines have grown around it now, not to bind it but to cradle it, to create a green alcove, a niche specifically for me. The turbine-flowers drip their essence onto the moss, and the moss releases its mist, and the mist settles on my skin, and I absorb it. I am a condenser. I am a reservoir. I am the place where the room keeps its self-awareness.

The door still sweats. Sometimes I go to it and press my palm against the warm bronze, feeling the room's heartbeat through the metal. But I do not open it. The outside is dry and cold and thin, and it does not have the clicking, it does not have the green smell, it does not have the slow patient growth toward becoming.

I am the resident. I am the newest inhabitant, the latest expression of the room's will to live. And I am learning to click my tongue against my teeth in the rhythm of the flowers, to sway in the helical pattern of the vines, to carry the room's color on my back the way the beetle does, though my color is the color of wet bronze, of verdigris, of the patina that grows on metal that has been loved for a very long time.

The room is warm. I am warm. I am part of the differential, part of the engine. I breathe in the wet, I breathe out the warmth, and the cycle closes around me like a hand closing around a seed.

Click. Click. Click.

I am here. I am becoming. I am the garden, and the garden is me, and we are growing toward something that has no name yet, something green and bronze and patient, something that sweats and dreams and remembers through roots that grip stone in the dark.

Still becoming.
