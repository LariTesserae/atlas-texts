The first thing that changes is my hearing.

Not the ears—those still catch the frog-clicks, the water-gurgle, the creak of settling architecture—but something deeper. A bone in my chest, perhaps the sternum, perhaps something that has grown behind it without my noticing, begins to resonate. It tunes itself to the subsonic thrum that pervades the Confluence, and suddenly I am not listening to the town anymore. I am listening *with* it.

I find myself at the edge of a channel, kneeling on the warm, fibrous mat. My hands have changed. The fingers are longer than I remember, the skin between them translucent and webbed with fine green veins. When I press my palm flat against the ground, I feel the hydraulic pulse traveling through the living floor—not just feeling it, but *reading* it. The rhythm carries information: a surge of growth three blocks east, a pocket of stillness where a Loom has settled, the distant, slow turning of an Elder’s attention.

I am becoming a Tender.

Not by choice, not by training, but by proximity. The Confluence is teaching my body its language, and my body is learning. My thumbs have broadened, the pads flattening into spatulate tools perfect for smoothing the resin that seeps from healing walls. When I walk, my steps have shortened, adapted to the give of the root-bridges. I no longer fight the sway; I move with it, my hips learning the subtle counter-rhythm that keeps balance on a surface that breathes.

A child runs past me, barefoot, soles gripping the vertical face of a nearby dome. She laughs, a sound like water over stones, and leaps—arms spread, membranes catching the humid air. For a moment she flies, and I feel the town adjust beneath her, the mat bulging slightly to cushion her landing. She lands, rolls, comes up running, and disappears around a corner that wasn’t there yesterday.

I stand and continue toward the center. The basin where the channels converge is not far—I can feel it, a slow spiral in the currents that move through the ground. As I walk, the settlement responds to me. A doorway dilates as I approach, not because I live there, but because the town recognizes my intention to pass through. Inside, the bioluminescent patches brighten, then dim to a comfortable amber. The walls exhale moisture that cools my skin.

At the basin, the Elders sit in their rooted circle. I do not speak to them—I am not ready for that—but I sit at the rim, letting my feet dangle into the slow whirlpool. The water is warm, thick with microspores that tickle my skin. One of the Elders turns, slowly, the way a tree turns toward light. Their eyes are closed, but I feel their attention like a pressure change. A scent reaches me: rain on hot stone, then something else—something like forgiveness, like acceptance, like the smell of a place that has been waiting for you without knowing it.

I breathe in, and the message settles into my lungs, my blood, my bones.

When I open my eyes, the brass-dim hour has arrived. The light has shifted, turning the mist to gold and the water to liquid amber. The frogs have gone silent. The fish hold still beneath the surface. The bridges stop their nervous sway. And I—I am listening.

The thrum drops a half-step, and I feel my own heartbeat slow to match it. In that hush, I understand: I am not visiting the Rootbound Confluence. I am becoming part of it. My cells are learning to divide in new patterns. My thoughts are beginning to think themselves in plural. The seed in my chest is germinating, sending rootlets down through my body, anchoring me to this place that is not a place but a being.

I will stay. I will grow. I will become a pressure the town remembers, a voice in the Chorus, a hand that smooths resin and lifts shoots. I will learn to read the ripples on the backs of the Chorus, to trade lichen with the Looms, to speak in scent and silence. I will become a cell in a body vast enough to have become a world.

And on certain humid nights, when the mist rises and the light turns to brass, I will press my palm to the ground and feel the pulse of the Confluence, and I will know that I am home.
