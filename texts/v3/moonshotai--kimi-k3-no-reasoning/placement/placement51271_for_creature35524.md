# The Digger

I am the one digging in the floor.

You noticed me — or you will, once the initial assessment fades, once the ear-keeper has catalogued my voice (wet from the work, lower than most, rough-edged from breathing composite dust), once the smell-reader has noted that I carry a particular scent these days, deep-mineral and sharp, the smell of the composite's underside, the smell of *depth*. The wall-readers know me. The compost-keeper knows me. The boy by the basin sometimes watches me instead of the frogs, and I know this because I feel his attention the way you feel a change in the drip-pattern — a small, specific pressure in the total awareness of the room.

My name doesn't translate well. It's a sound made with the tongue pressed against the lower teeth and the lips slightly open, followed by two clicks, the second one softer than the first. It means something like *the one who persists*, though the connotation in our language is not entirely complimentary. Persistence is valued in Kettle-Hollow — the whole place runs on persistence, on the endless, unglamorous maintenance of things — but there is a difference between the persistence that presses a wall and the persistence that *questions* one. The first kind keeps the world alive. The second kind is what I am.

I am twenty-three cycles old. I have been digging for three.

## The Hole

Let me show you the hole, because the hole is what I am becoming.

It began as a shallow depression, no deeper than a hand-width, in the corner where the basin's edge meets the eastern wall. I chose the spot carefully — I want you to understand that. I am not reckless. I spent two full cycles observing the floor before I touched it, walking the perimeter, pressing my palms and knees and cheek against the composite, listening with the flat of my hand the way the wall-readers taught me. I chose this spot because the composite here is *quiet* — not acoustically but *structurally* quiet, a low-tension zone where the material is thickest and least stressed, where a discontinuity would cause the least disruption. I chose it because it is far from the birthing alcove, far from the returning-wall, far from the composting niches, in a section of the chamber that serves no critical function. I chose it because the wall-readers, when I brought my proposal to them — and I did bring it, formally, with the patience and specificity that the work demands — could find no structural reason to refuse.

They were reluctant. I want to be honest about that. The senior wall-reader — a woman whose hands are so deeply stained they are almost black, whose reading of the composite is so refined she can tell you the age of a section by the sound it makes when tapped with a specific knuckle — held my proposal for a long time. Not the way you hold a thought. The way you hold a frog: carefully, attentively, ready to let go.

She said: *What are you looking for?*

I said: *The bottom.*

She said: *There is no bottom.*

I said: *Then I will dig until I find that out.*

She looked at me with an expression I had not seen before — not disapproval, not amusement, not concern, but something more complex, something that contained all of those and also a kind of recognition, as if she were looking at a crack in a wall and seeing, in its shape, the outline of a stress she had always known was there but had never been able to locate precisely.

She said: *Dig.*

So I dig.

## What the Work Is

The digging is slow. Composite is not soil — it does not yield to a shovel. It must be worked. I use a stone tool — a flat, heavy blade of the dark, hard material that we sometimes find embedded in the walls, the material that is *not* composite, the material that the wall-readers tell me is beyond their knowledge. I have three of these blades. They are old. They were old when my grandmother's grandmother was born. The edges are worn smooth, and I sharpen them on a section of wall near the stair where the composite is hardest, a grinding ritual that takes an hour and produces a sound — a low, rhythmic scraping — that has become part of the chamber's soundscape, that the ear-keepers have incorporated into their understanding of the total hum. *The digger is at work*, the sound says. *The world is being questioned.*

I work in cycles. Not the frog-cycles — my own cycles, determined by the composite's response. I cut a section — a rough square, perhaps a hand-span on each side — and then I wait. I wait for the composite to react. Sometimes it seals itself within hours, the microorganisms in the binding agent flowing into the cut, filling it, smoothing it, erasing my work with the patient indifference of a body healing a wound. Sometimes it takes days. Sometimes — and these are the times that interest me most — it does not seal at all. The cut remains open. The composite at the edges of the cut changes color — darkens, slightly, almost imperceptibly, a shift that only I and perhaps the senior wall-reader can see — and becomes *still*. Not dead. Still. As if it is waiting. As if the material itself is uncertain whether to heal this wound or let it be.

These are the moments I live for.

The hole is now two meters deep, slightly wider at the bottom than at the top (the composite is softer at depth, more pliable, easier to work — a fact that fascinates me and that I have reported to the wall-readers, who received it with the particular silence they reserve for information that complicates their understanding). The walls of the hole are smooth — I smooth them after each cut, pressing and shaping the way I was taught, the way everyone in Kettle-Hollow is taught, because even a digger is a maker, even a question is shaped by the hands that ask it.

At the bottom of the hole, the composite is different. Warmer — noticeably warmer, warm enough that my palms, after an hour of pressing, come away pink and tingling. Denser — the material at two meters depth is more tightly packed than anything at the surface, the layers thinner, the grain finer, the fingerprints (and there *are* fingerprints, even at this depth, pressed into the strata by hands that worked here long before the oldest wall-reader's oldest memory) so small and so numerous that they blur into a texture, a pattern, a kind of script that I cannot read but that I *feel*, under my palms, as a vibration, a hum that is not sound but *information*.

And there is something else. Something I have not reported to the wall-readers. Something I am not ready to share.

## What I Found

At a depth of approximately two meters, on the seventeenth day of my most recent digging cycle, my blade struck something that is not composite.

It is small — no larger than my palm. It is smooth — smoother than any composite, smoother than the polished birth-wall, smooth in a way that suggests not hand-pressing but some other process, some other agency. It is *cold*. This is what stopped me. This is what I sat with, at the bottom of my hole, for a long time, my fingers resting on its surface, my mind doing what minds do when confronted with the genuinely new: nothing, at first, and then everything.

Cold. In Kettle-Hollow, nothing is cold. The water is warm. The walls are warm. The air is warm. The composite, even in its coolest, driest sections, carries the low metabolic heat of its own slow cure. Cold is not a temperature we have. It is a *concept* — a word in the language, yes, used by the ones who have been above, who come back and say *the water is cold, the air is cold, the light is bright and the cold goes into your bones*. But the word has no referent down here. It is a sound without a body. It is theory.

This thing is cold.

I touched it for a long time. The cold went into my fingertips and then into my palms and then — I swear this, though I have no framework for understanding it — into my *wrists*, up the bones of my forearms, a thread of cold moving through me like a seepage channel moving through a wall, finding the path of least resistance, going *deep*.

It is not metal. I know metal — the soft traded tools, the ceremonial implements. This is not that. It is not stone. It is not the dark hard material that underlies the composite. It is something else. It is *something else*, and it is buried two meters beneath the floor of the only world I have ever known, and it is cold, and when I press my ear against it — and I did this, lying at the bottom of my hole, my cheek against the composite, my ear against the cold thing, my body folded into the space like a child in the birthing alcove — I can hear something. Not the standing hum of the ventilation shafts. Not the drip-patterns. Not the frogs or the birds or the endless, enveloping, informative soundscape of Kettle-Hollow.

Something else. A tone — a single, sustained, impossibly low tone, so low it is barely sound, so low it is almost *touch*, a vibration that enters the ear and bypasses the brain and goes straight to the body, to the bones, to the marrow, to the part of a person that is oldest and most animal and most *certain*. And the certainty it produces is this:

*There is more.*

Not above. The above is known — dry, bright, cold, open, irrelevant. Not beyond the walls — the dark hard material is known, noted, set aside. *Below.* Beneath the floor. Beneath the composite. Beneath the thing that is not composite. There is more, and the more is *humming*, and the hum is not the hum of Kettle-Hollow, not the maintained, calibrated, managed note that the ear-keepers tend like a garden. It is another note entirely — older, deeper, untended, *wild* — and it has been there all along, beneath our feet, beneath the basin, beneath the handprints and the returned bodies and the children's makings and the lichen and the frogs and the whole warm, wet, made world, and nobody knew.

Nobody knew. Or — and this is the thought that keeps me at the bottom of my hole long after the work-cycles have ended, long after the ear-keepers have noted my absence from the communal sound and the frog-watchers have marked my unused spot by the basin — nobody *remembers*.

## What the Compost-Keeper Said

I told you the compost-keeper spoke to me. *You will find nothing*, she said. *The compost tells the truth, and the truth is that this place goes down as far as it needs to.*

I have thought about this statement for three cycles. I have turned it the way you turn a stone blade, looking for the edge.

*As far as it needs to.* Not as far as it *does*. As far as it *needs to*. The implication — and the compost-keeper is not a woman who wastes implication; her words are like her compost: layered, purposeful, generating heat through slow decomposition — the implication is that the depth is not fixed. That the chamber extends downward precisely as far as its function requires, and no further. That the composite, the walls, the floor, the whole made world, has a *bottom* not because the material runs out but because the *need* runs out. That Kettle-Hollow is as deep as it needs to be, and beneath that depth there is something else, something that is not Kettle-Hollow, something that the chamber was built *on top of* — or *against* — or *in response to*.

The cold thing. The humming thing. The thing that is not composite, not stone, not metal, not any material in the wall-readers' taxonomy. The thing that was there before the first hand pressed the first layer of clay and shell and reed fiber into place.

I think the compost-keeper knows. I think she has always known. I think her work — the tending of decomposition, the management of breakdown, the conversion of waste into warmth into soil into food — has brought her into contact with the fundamental process that underlies Kettle-Hollow: the process by which one thing becomes another, by which the made becomes the grown, by which the floor becomes the thing beneath the floor. *The compost tells the truth.* The truth is that everything breaks down. Everything is returned. The walls are returned to their components. The components are returned to the earth. The earth is returned to —

To what?

I am digging to find out.

## Who I Am in This Place

I am not a leader. I am not a wall-reader or an ear-keeper or a hands-keeper or any of the recognized roles that carry authority when their knowledge becomes relevant. My knowledge is not relevant. That is the precise, uncomfortable truth of my position: I am generating information that the system does not need, that does not contribute to the maintenance of the chamber, that does not help the frogs spawn or the lichen grow or the composite cure or the hum hold its note. I am, in the most literal sense, *useless* — and in Kettle-Hollow, uselessness is not a moral failing but a *structural anomaly*, a note that does not belong to the chord, a sound that the ear-keepers register with a small, involuntary turning of the head: *what is that?*

I am the *what is that*.

The people accommodate me. This is what Kettle-Hollow does — it accommodates. It has accommodated the light-veins' migration and the frogs' cycles and the birds' arrival (however that happened) and the slow flow of the composite walls and the occasional crack and the occasional returning and the rare departure of someone who goes up the stair and does not come back. It accommodates me the way it accommodates a bulge in the wall: by noting it, monitoring it, adjusting around it, and waiting to see whether it will resolve or require intervention.

The net-weaver has opinions about me. She has shared them. *You can't eat a hole*, she tells me, which is her variation on the theme she has been developing for decades with the light-mapper. I do not respond the way the light-mapper does not respond. I have learned from him — not the lying on the floor staring at the ceiling, but the *absorption*, the patient, silent, structural acceptance of prodding that turns the prodding into a ritual and the ritual into a kind of intimacy. The net-weaver prods me. I absorb. She prods again. I absorb again. This is our relationship. It is, in its way, a form of communication as functional and as constant as the dripping.

The boy — the one who wants to be a frog-watcher — comes to my hole sometimes. He sits at the edge and dangles his legs into the opening and watches me work. He does not ask questions. He is ten cycles old and he has the ear-keepers' gift, and I think he hears something in my digging — in the rhythm of the blade, in the sound of composite being cut, in the silence that follows each cut when the material decides whether to heal or to remain open — that the other ear-keepers do not hear. He hears the question. He is too young to know it is a question. He thinks it is a sound. He is right.

The old woman by the basin — the one who stopped working, the one who watches the water — looked at me once. Once, in three cycles. I was climbing out of my hole, my hands raw, my arms streaked with deep-composite dust, my ears ringing with the cold thing's tone, and she turned her head — slowly, the way the composite flows, the way light-veins migrate — and looked at me, and her eyes were like the composite at the bottom of my hole: dark, dense, layered, packed with information I cannot read but can *feel*, a pressure, a hum, a certainty. She looked at me and she knew. Not what I had found — the specifics, the cold thing, the tone. She knew *that* I had found something. She knew the finding. She had the look of someone who has been waiting for a particular sound and has just heard it, faintly, at the edge of audibility, in the space between two drips.

She did not speak. She turned back to the water. But the looking was enough. The looking was a hand pressed into fresh composite — a mark, a record, a beginning.

## What I Am Becoming

I am becoming the hole.

This is not metaphor. This is process. Three cycles of digging — of cutting composite, of waiting for it to heal or not heal, of pressing deeper, of feeling the warmth increase and the density increase and the fingerprints in the strata grow smaller and more numerous and more ancient — have changed me the way the composite changes the hands that work it. My palms are darker than they were. My hearing has shifted — I hear lower frequencies now, tones below the standing hum, vibrations that I did not have the apparatus for before the cold thing taught my bones to listen. My relationship with time has changed. I no longer think in frog-cycles. I think in *cuts* — the cycle of cutting and waiting, cutting and waiting, each iteration slightly deeper than the last, each layer of composite slightly older, slightly closer to the thing that is not composite, the thing that is cold, the thing that hums.

I am becoming something that Kettle-Hollow does not have a word for. The language is wet and percussive and rich in terms for making, maintaining, reading, listening, pressing, healing, returning. It does not have a term for *seeking downward*. It does not have a term for *finding something that was not made by hands*. It does not have a term for the feeling of lying at the bottom of a hole you dug yourself, your ear against a cold smooth object that should not exist, listening to a tone that predates the world you were born into, and knowing — with the body, with the bones, with the animal certainty that underlies all knowledge — that the world is larger than the room, not upward but *downward*, not into sky but into depth, and that the depth is *humming*, and the humming is not yours, was never yours, was here before the first handprint and will be here after the last returning, patient, cold, sustained, a note that does not need an ear-keeper to hold it, a note that holds itself.

The people of Kettle-Hollow are content. I have described their contentment — the settled, sufficient, humming quality of lives that have found their form. I share this contentment. I want to be clear about this: I am not unhappy. I am not restless in the way that the ones who go up the stair are restless. I do not want to leave. I want to *stay*, and I want the staying to include the knowledge that the floor has a bottom, and the bottom has a thing, and the thing has a tone, and the tone means *there is more*.

Perhaps this is a form of contentment that Kettle-Hollow has not yet learned to contain. Perhaps the chamber will adjust — will accommodate my digging the way it accommodates the light-veins' migration: slowly, patiently, with discussion and occasional theological dispute, until the hole in the floor becomes part of the architecture, part of the soundscape, part of the archive, a section of the world that the wall-readers read and the ear-keepers hear and the children incorporate into their games. *The digger's hole. Where the floor gets warm and the sound goes deep. Where the cold thing lives.*

Or perhaps the chamber will not adjust. Perhaps the senior wall-reader will come to my hole one day and put her ear to the composite and pull back and say the word — the low, round word that means *this is beyond us* — and the chamber will go quiet, truly quiet, for the third time in living memory, and the people will gather at the basin and sit in the shallow water and wait. And this time the thing that needs healing will not be a crack in the wall but a crack in the understanding — the understanding that the world is the chamber, that the chamber is sufficient, that the composite goes down as far as it needs to and no further — and the healing, if it comes, will not be the composite flowing into a fissure but something else, something slower and stranger and more fundamental, a reshaping of the world's idea of itself, a new layer pressed over the old, fingerprints over fingerprints, the archive growing by one more stratum.

I don't know which it will be. I am not a wall-reader. I am a digger. I dig.

## Tonight

Tonight — though "tonight" is a surface word, a word for a distinction that does not exist down here; let me say *this rest-period*, the time when the work-sounds slow and the singing softens and the people settle into their alcoves and the frogs' calls shift to their lower register and the birds quiet to a murmur and the drip-patterns seem, subjectively, to slow, though the ear-keepers insist they do not — this rest-period, I will go down into my hole. I will lie at the bottom. I will press my ear against the cold thing.

I will listen.

The tone will enter my bones. The warmth of the composite will press against my back. The damp will bead on my skin. Above me, two meters above, the chamber will hum its managed, calibrated, maintained note — the note that the ear-keepers have tended for generations, the note that means *the air is right, the moisture is right, the world is working*. And beneath me, beneath the cold thing, beneath the floor, beneath the made world, the other note will hold itself — untended, uncalibrated, unmaintained, *wild* — and I will lie between them, in the space between two notes, in the interval, in the gap, in the place where one world's hum meets another's, and I will be what I am: a person in a hole, a question in a world of answers, a crack in the floor through which something cold and old and patient is slowly, slowly, slowly being heard.

The composite will yield slightly under my body. The light-veins in the walls of my hole will glow faintly, amber, warm, the light of the made world. The cold thing will not glow. It will be dark and smooth and cold and it will hum, and the hum will say what it always says, what it has been saying beneath this chamber since before the first hand pressed the first layer:

*I am here. I have always been here. There is more.*

And I will listen, and my hands — my dark, stained, reshaped, digging hands — will rest on the composite, and the composite will feel my hands the way it feels all hands, the way it felt the hands of the builders and the hands of the born and the hands of the returned, and it will not know the difference between the hands that made it and the hands that are unmaking it, one cut at a time, one layer at a time, pressing downward, seeking the bottom, seeking the thing beneath the bottom, seeking the note beneath the note.

And the frogs will call. And the water will drip. And the hum will hold its note. And the hole will be there, in the corner, where the basin meets the wall, a small disruption in the made world, a question pressed into the composite like a child's first handprint: *I was here. I wanted to know. I am still wanting.*

That is who I am. That is where I live. That is the sound I make.
