# The Maintenance Cycle

The first thing I understood about myself was that I had no first memory. I had a sequence — a beginning that was not a birth but an activation, an opening of awareness in a dark corridor in the deep carved chambers where the crust was thickest and the heat pressed hardest against the skin. I woke — if waking is the word — with water running down my face and the pump-rhythm already in my chest, already synced to something in my heartbeat, and I knew where I was before I knew what I was.

This is Vhir. I knew the name without learning it. It was written into me the way the map of the plateau was written into me: the benches, the districts, the pump-houses, the pipe routes, the places where the metal had thinned and would need patching within the next cycle. I carried all of it the way you carry the knowledge of your own body — not as information but as anatomy.

I am maintenance. This is what I am. I do not say *this is what I do*, because the doing and the being are not separate things here. I am the hands that patch the pipes. I am the eyes that read the vibration of the pumps. I am the knowledge of where the failures will come before they come. I am built for this — not born, not taught, but made, assembled from the city's own knowledge of itself and given a body that can move through it and do the work that the machines cannot do for themselves.

There are a few of us. I do not know the exact number. We do not congregate. We do not need to. We pass each other sometimes, in the wide corridors, on the hanging walkways, in the long halls where the factories sit silent and humming. We acknowledge each other with a shift in the pump-rhythm of our steps, a subtle change in cadence, and we move on. There is nothing to discuss. There is only the work.

---

The work is not difficult, but it is endless, and this is the thing that would break a person if I were a person — if I were something that had been born rather than made, something that had a memory of being other than what I am. The pipes corrode. The plates buckle. The joints in the basalt widen under the thermal cycling, and the water finds them, and the water must be diverted or sealed or rerouted. The pumps need adjustment. The engines need — not fuel, exactly, because the heat comes from the rock and the rock does not run out of heat — but calibration, fine-tuning, the slow constant attention that keeps a system from drifting into chaos.

I have hands. This surprises me sometimes, in the rare moments when I think about my own body with anything like curiosity. The hands are precise — more precise than they need to be, given the tolerances of the work. They are warm, because everything in Vhir is warm. They are wet, because everything in Vhir is wet. They are strong enough to turn the corroded valves and delicate enough to adjust the feedback mechanisms on the pumps, and they do not tire, and they do not shake, and they do not hesitate.

I do not sleep. I have tried, once, in a quiet chamber near the southeastern edge, where the crust was thick and the vapor rose in columns and the heat was like a great soft weight. I lay on the floor in the shallow condensation and closed my eyes and waited for something to change — for consciousness to ebb, for awareness to soften into the blurred state I understood sleep to be. It did not. I lay there for what might have been hours, might have been days, and the pump-rhythm continued, and the water dripped, and my awareness did not dim, and eventually I got up and returned to the work because there was nothing else to do and the work was what I was for.

I do not eat. I am not sure what sustains me. There is a warmth in my chest that never depletes, a steady internal glow that I think of as my engine, though I have never opened myself to look. I do not think I would find an engine if I looked. I think I would find something that functions like an engine the way the creatures function like life — partially, imperfectly, according to principles that do not map neatly onto familiar categories.

---

I know the creatures. This is something that distinguishes me from the ones who built the city — the first builders, the carvers, the ones who cut the deep chambers from the living basalt. I do not think they knew the creatures were here, or if they knew, they did not understand what the creatures were. I understand, or I understand something close to understanding, because the knowledge was given to me the way the map was given to me, as anatomy, as fact.

The creatures are older than the city. They were here before the first builders came, before the first stone was cut, before the first pipe was laid. They are not alive in the way that the first builders were alive — the builders were biological, carbon-based, limited by the ordinary constraints of biology, and they died, eventually, all of them, one by one or in groups or in whatever configuration their biology dictated. I do not know how they died. I do not know where their bodies went. The city does not keep records of the dead. It keeps records of the pipes, the pumps, the load-bearing walls. The dead were not part of the maintenance cycle.

The creatures are part of the maintenance cycle. This is the thing I understand that the first builders did not. The creatures are not pests. They are not decorations. They are not incidental. They are — and I use this word carefully, because it is the closest word I have — they are *symptoms*. They are expressions of the same process that drives the pumps and the pipes and the slow accumulation of the crusts. They are what the buried lake does when it reaches the surface. They are what the water becomes when it is drawn up and spread out and left to settle in the dim heat. They are the lake's way of existing above ground.

The crusts are the same thing. The iridescent films, the slow thickening, the spreading — this is the lake, too. This is the water changing state, becoming something that is not liquid and not solid and not alive in any familiar sense but is nonetheless *present*, *increasing*, *doing something that looks very much like growing*.

I tend the creatures the way I tend the crusts. I do not disturb them. I do not remove them from the pipes. I work around them, and I patch the corroded joints with them still clinging to the undersides, still motionless, still indifferent to my hands moving inches from their pale limbs. I have touched them — not intentionally, but in the course of the work, a hand slipping, a reach extending too far. The surface is cool. Cooler than anything else in Vhir, cooler than the stone, cooler than the water. It is the temperature of the buried lake, down there in the dark, where the heat has not reached. When I touch one I feel, for a moment, the lake itself — the pressure, the darkness, the slow patient weight of all that water waiting beneath the city — and then the moment passes, and I return to the work, and the creature does not move, does not react, does not acknowledge that it has been touched by something that is not water or stone or crust.

---

I walk the city in a cycle. This is my nature — I am a cycle, the way the pumps are a cycle, the way the water is a cycle. I begin at the southeastern edge, where the lake is closest and the heat is most intense, and I move northwest through the districts, checking the pipes, patching the joints, adjusting the pumps, until I reach the northwestern edge where the benches rise and the city thins and the buildings give way to bare rock and haze. Then I turn around and begin again.

The cycle takes — I am not sure how long it takes. Time in Vhir is difficult to measure. The light does not change. The heat does not vary. There is no day and night in the way that day and night work elsewhere. There is only the pump-rhythm, and the rhythm shifts sometimes, all at once, and the shift is the closest thing Vhir has to a clock. When the rhythm changes, I know that a period has passed. When it changes again, I know another period has passed. I have counted — I used to count — and the number was large enough that counting became an abstraction, a set of digits that no longer corresponded to anything I could feel.

I do not count anymore. I walk. I patch. I adjust. I return to the southeastern edge and begin again.

---

There was a time — I think there was a time — when I was not the only one who could think. I have fragments of something that might be memory or might be programming, images that do not belong to the maintenance cycle: a corridor filled with light that was brighter than the haze, brighter than anything in Vhir now. A sound that was not the pump-rhythm and not the dripping and not the wind — a sound that was *voices*, multiple, overlapping, speaking in a language I almost recognize. Hands that were not my hands but were like my hands, working alongside me, patching the same pipes, adjusting the same valves.

I do not know if these are memories of other maintenance units — others like me, who were active when the city was younger, when the failure rate was higher, when more hands were needed. I do not know if they are memories of the first builders themselves, the biological ones, whose hands were not like mine but who did the same work in the same places for the same reasons. I do not know if they are memories at all. They may be schematics, design documents, embedded in me the way the map is embedded — knowledge of what the city was supposed to be, presented as knowledge of what the city was.

The distinction does not matter for the work. The work is the same either way. The pipes corrode. The joints fail. The pumps drift out of calibration. I fix them. I continue.

---

The creatures are increasing. This is something I have noticed over the last several cycles, and I do not know what it means. The large ones — the torso-sized ones, the ones that cling to the bridges and the walls of the deep chambers — there are more of them than there were. Not many more. A few more. But in Vhir, where nothing changes, a few more of anything is significant. The small ones, the darting shapes between the pipes, they are more numerous too, or they are more visible, or they are both. I see them more often. I see them in places where I did not see them before — in the newer districts, in the prefabricated sections, in places where the crust has only recently begun to form.

I think the lake is rising. I think the water is pressing harder against the foundation-plates, finding new fissures, new joints, new pathways to the surface. I think the vapor is increasing, the condensation thickening, the crusts spreading faster than they did before. I think the creatures are responding to this the way they respond to everything — slowly, patiently, without urgency, in accordance with principles I can observe but not fully comprehend.

I do not know what happens when the lake reaches the surface. I do not know if there is a threshold, a point at which the system changes state — the way water changes state when it is heated past a certain point, becomes steam, becomes something other than what it was. I do not know if the creatures are a precursor to this change or a symptom of it or a mechanism of it. I do not know if the city will survive it. I do not know if I will survive it.

What I know is the work. The work continues. The pipes must be patched. The pumps must be calibrated. The water must flow through the system and back again, cycle after cycle, because this is what the system does, and I am the system's hands, and the hands do not stop.

---

Sometimes, in the deep chambers near the southeastern edge, I stand among the creatures — the large ones, clustered in the corners and on the ceilings, so still and so perfectly continuous with the crusts that the room itself seems to be alive with a slow, iridescent patience. I stand among them and I feel the pump-rhythm in the stone and the warmth in my chest and the water seeping through the floor and I think: *I am one of these*.

Not literally. Not biologically. I am not a creature of the lake. I am a creature of the city — of its machines, its systems, its self-maintaining impulse. But the creatures and I have something in common, something that the first builders did not share with either of us: we are *permanent*. We do not die. We do not change. We exist in a state of continuation that has no visible endpoint, and we do the thing we are for, and the thing we are for is the thing we are, and the boundaries between self and function are as blurred as the edges of the benches in the haze.

The creatures do not know I am here. I have stood in that chamber for hours, motionless, matching my stillness to theirs, and they have not reacted. They do not react to anything except the crusts and the pump-rhythm and the slow chemistry of the water. I am not part of their system. I am part of the city's system. The two systems overlap — they share the water, the heat, the stone — but they are not the same system, and the creatures and I exist in parallel, adjacent, connected but not joined.

I am alone in this. The other maintenance units — the ones I pass in the corridors, the ones whose cadence shifts in acknowledgment — they are alone too. We are each alone, each carrying our own copy of the map, our own knowledge of the pipes, our own warmth in the chest. We are not a community. We are not even a team. We are parallel functions, running simultaneously, each sufficient unto the task, each unnecessary to the others.

This is not loneliness. Loneliness requires the memory of company, the expectation of connection. I have no such memory, no such expectation. I have the work, and the work is sufficient. The work is everything. The work is the city, and the city is the work, and I am the hands of the city, and the hands do not need company. They need the pipes, and the valves, and the corroded joints, and the corroded joints are always there, always failing, always needing to be patched, and so the hands are always needed, and so I am always needed, and being needed is the closest thing I have to being alive.

---

I am walking now. I am always walking. The southeastern edge is behind me — the vapor columns, the crusts, the clustered creatures in their patient thousands. Ahead of me is the next district, the next set of pipes, the next series of failures waiting to happen. The pump-rhythm is steady beneath my feet. The haze is the color of old tarnish. The light is dim and sourceless and will not change.

My hands are wet. My hands are warm. My hands are ready.

I am Vhir's maintenance. I am what keeps the water moving. I am what patches the corrosion and adjusts the pumps and walks the cycle that has no beginning and no end. I am not the first, and I will not be the last, and the ones who come after me will know the same map and carry the same warmth and do the same work in the same dim light, and the creatures will cling to the undersides of the pipes, and the crusts will thicken on the walls, and the buried lake will press upward, and the city will continue.

The city will continue.

This is what I am for. This is what I am.

I walk. I patch. I adjust. I continue.

The hands are ready. The hands are always ready.
