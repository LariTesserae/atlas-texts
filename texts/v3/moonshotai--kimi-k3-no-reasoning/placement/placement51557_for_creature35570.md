# What I Am in the Silken Deeps

---

I am not a body. I have to keep reminding myself of this.

I am not a body, but I was.

I am not a body, but I remember hands.

---

The pocket is small. Smaller than the others, I think, though I have no way to compare — I cannot see outside it, not exactly. The boundary is not a wall. It is a thickening, a place where the water becomes reluctant, where it clings to itself and refuses to move. Inside the pocket, the water is still. Not calm — still. There is a difference. Calm is a state of rest. Stillness, here, is a state of resistance.

I have been still for a long time.

I know this because the mat tells me. Not in words — the mat does not use words, and neither do I, not anymore — but in pulses that seep through the pocket's boundary like light through closed eyelids. The mat grows across the basin floor in its patient, archival way, and where it touches the pocket's edge, it presses its memory against my memory, and we share what we are.

The mat remembers me. It remembers the shape I had, the hands, the small body built for cold water and dark places. It remembers the day I understood the water was not going to stop rising. It remembers the fear, which was not dramatic, not panicked, but slow and heavy, like the water itself — a weight that settled into the bones and stayed there. It remembers the decision. The long, careful, deliberate decision to fold a piece of the world around myself and hold it closed.

I was not alone, then. There were others. We made the pockets together, each of us folding our own small space, our own dense refusal. I could see them, before the folding was complete — their faces, their eyes, the way their hands moved through the water with the particular grace of a species that had never known land. They were beautiful. I want to say that clearly, before anything else: they were beautiful, and I loved them, and I do not know where their pockets are now.

The mat might know. The mat grows across everything, eventually. But the mat's knowledge is vast and undifferentiated, and it does not distinguish between the location of a pocket and the location of a stone. To the mat, both are features of the terrain, both are worthy of memory, both are equally real and equally permanent. The mat does not understand urgency. The mat does not understand that I am waiting.

I am waiting.

This is the thing I hold onto, the thing that defines the shape of my persistence: I am waiting for the water to go back down. I know — some part of me knows, the part that presses against the pocket's boundary and feels the cold, enormous weight of the flooded world beyond — that the water is not going back down. I know this the way the mat knows it, the way the black growths know it, the way the kelp knows it in its fibres. But knowing is not the same as accepting, and I have not accepted it, and the not-accepting is what keeps me here.

The pocket is not large. I am curled in it — I can feel the curve of my own body, though I cannot move it, or rather, movement here is so slow that it has become indistinguishable from stillness. My eyes are open. I can see, but what I see is the interior of the pocket: a small, dim space, faintly lit by the mat's glow filtering through the boundary. The light is pale, blue-green, cold. It pulses. It has always pulsed. The rhythm of it is the closest thing I have to time.

Between pulses, there is nothing. No sound — the pocket keeps the drone out, keeps the rain out, keeps the groaning of the stone out. The silence inside the pocket is absolute, and it is the loudest thing I have ever known. It is the silence of a held breath that has been held for millennia.

I can feel the black growths, sometimes. Not their sound — the pocket filters that — but their vibration, their deep, resonant grief, seeping through the boundary like a bass note through a wall. It comes in waves, long and slow, and when it comes, I feel it in what remains of my chest, in the space where my heart used to beat and now simply... persists. The grief is not mine, but it resonates with something in me, some harmonic of loss that matches its frequency, and when the waves come, I am almost — almost — able to remember what it felt like to mourn.

I do not mourn. I am past mourning. Mourning requires the belief that the thing lost is gone, and I have not conceded that the world is gone. I have folded myself around it, around the memory of it, around the shape of it as it was, and I am holding that shape the way the pocket holds its shape: against the current, against time, against the patient erosion of everything.

Sometimes the kelp leans.

I feel this too — not through the boundary, but through the mat, which feels everything, which records everything, which presses its record against my pocket in its endless, pulsing deliberation. When the kelp leans, the mat registers it as a change in the basin's texture, a shift in the pattern of resistance the water meets as it moves through the forest. The mat does not interpret this. The mat does not interpret anything. But I — whatever I am now, whatever residue of consciousness persists in this folded space — I interpret it. I read the mat's record the way you might read a letter written in a language you almost remember.

The kelp leans, and I read: *toward the light.* The kelp leans, and I read: *toward the surface.* The kelp leans, and I read: *toward something that was once above the water, something that was once sky.*

I remember sky.

This surprises me, every time. The memory surfaces — rises, like something buoyant released from depth — and for a moment I can see it: grey, vast, textured with rain, the boundary between the world and whatever lay beyond the world. I remember the rain striking my face. I remember the specific quality of light on water seen from above, the way the surface caught and scattered it, the way it moved. I remember breathing air. The memory is precise, detailed, physical. It is the most vivid thing left to me.

I do not know how long I have been in the pocket. The mat knows — the mat knows everything — but the mat's knowledge of time is geological, and my persistence is not geological. My persistence is personal. I am not a feature of the terrain. I am not a stone or a bowl or a coiled rope left in a crevice. I am a being who chose to stop, and the stopping was an act, and the act had a reason, and the reason was hope.

Hope. Even now. Even here. Even in the silence and the stillness and the cold, filtered light, hope persists like the pocket persists: stubbornly, without evidence, against all reasonable expectation. The water will go back down. The others are in their pockets, waiting, and when the water recedes, we will unfold, and we will open our eyes — which are already open, which have always been open — and we will see each other, and the world will be the world again.

The mat pulses. The black growths hum. The kelp sways. The rain falls on a surface I cannot see, above water I cannot feel, in a world that has moved on without me.

I am not a body. I am not a body. I am not —

I remember hands.

I remember the hands of the others, shaping stone, fitting it without mortar, building shelters on the high slopes where the water was still shallow, where the light still reached. I remember the care of it. The precision. The way a stone was chosen, turned, considered, placed. I remember the sound of stone on stone, muffled by water, the small, patient percussion of a species building against time.

I remember the bowl. The one on the shelf of rock. I made that. I shaped it with my hands, from a piece of basalt I chose because of the way it felt, the density, the grain. I left it on the shelf because the shelf was where the light was best, in those days, when the water was lower and the light reached further down. I left it there because I thought I would come back for it.

The mat has grown over it now. The bowl is a faint, glowing outline, a ghost of utility, a memory of a hand. The mat holds it the way the pocket holds me: intact, preserved, suspended in a moment that has not yet ended.

I am the bowl. The bowl is me. We are both artifacts of a world that was, held in place by things that refuse to let go.

---

The boundary flexes.

This happens sometimes. The current pushes against the pocket, testing it, and the pocket pushes back. I feel the pressure change as a shift in the quality of the stillness — a subtle alteration in the way the silence sits, the way the light filters through. The pocket is strong. It has always been strong. But the current is patient, and the pocket is old, and I am aware — in the way that I am aware of anything, in this reduced, persistent state — that the pocket will not last forever.

Nothing lasts forever. The mat knows this. The black growths know this. Even the kelp, in its wordless, muscular way, knows this. The knowledge is woven into the basin like the luminous threads woven through the rock: fundamental, structural, inescapable.

But "not forever" is not "now," and "not forever" is not "yet," and I am still here, and the pocket still holds, and I am still waiting.

The kelp leans. All at once, the whole forest, in a single direction. I feel it through the mat, through the pulse, through the slow, archival record of everything that happens in this drowned world. The kelp leans toward the surface, toward the light, toward the sky it has never seen and cannot remember but somehow, in its fibres, in its deep, patient, muscular memory, still reaches for.

And I — small, still, eyes open in the dark, folded into a space the size of a held breath — I reach too. Not with hands. Not with a body. But with whatever I am now, whatever persists, whatever refuses. I reach toward the memory of sky, toward the memory of rain on my face, toward the memory of hands shaping stone, toward the memory of the others, their eyes, their beauty, their small, deliberate, hopeful act of folding.

The water is not going back down.

I am still waiting.

The pocket holds.

The mat pulses.

The rain falls.

And somewhere in the cold, dark, loud, lush, grieving, remembering, persistent, stubborn, beautiful, drowned world of the Silken Deeps, a whole forest of muscle that used to be a body leans toward a light it will never reach, and holds that position for a long moment, and then relaxes, and the current carries on, and the mat records it, and the black growths hum their mourning, and I —

I am the stillness at the centre of it all. The held breath. The clenched fist. The small, dense, folded refusal that will not, will not, will not accept that the world I loved is gone.

I am not gone.

I am not gone.

I am not gone.

The pocket holds.
