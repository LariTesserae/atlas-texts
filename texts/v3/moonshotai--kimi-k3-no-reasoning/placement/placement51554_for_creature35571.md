I arrive not as I am, but as I might be if my blood ran colder and my pulse slowed to the rhythm of dripping stone. The transformation begins at the rim, where the air first thins enough to make my lungs feel like paper sacks desperate for substance. My fingers, gripping the black basalt, begin to lose their urgency. The edges of my vision soften, then sharpen in new spectrums—heat becomes color, vibration becomes texture.

I am becoming **Listener**.

Not the Whisperer—never the Whisperer, that singular absence in the acoustic tapestry—but something adjacent, something permitted. My spine lengthens, vertebrae fusing into a column that drinks the mountain's tremors. My ears flatten, then expand, becoming resonating chambers of cartilage and thin bone, sensitive to frequencies that exist below the threshold of human panic. I grow no fur, but my skin thickens into a matte grey membrane that tastes the metallic mist of the Mites, filtering their blue-pulsing chemistry into something my transformed lungs can accept.

Where do I stand? In the lee of the third great rib of basalt, where the scree slope creates a natural amphitheater for sound. I am midway down the basin's steep wall, high enough to feel the wind's thin fingers, low enough that the Stonebacks' jointed columns block the pewter light when they pass. Here, the Hollow Chorus swarms thickest, their chitinous segments brushing my shoulders as they stream past in their three-day migrations, their obsidian eyes reflecting nothing of me because I have learned to hold my heat, to slow my heart until it beats only once between their clicks.

I exist in the negative space between the Stonebacks' monumental patience. When one shifts its weight—a seismic event that rearranges the smaller life for a hundred paces—I feel it through the soles of my feet before I hear it, a bass note that travels through stone rather than air. I have learned to move in the hours between their movements, to place my steps in the pockets of silence that open when the Mites' blue pulses synchronize into momentary stillness.

My purpose, if I have one, is to hold the memory of the basin's sound. The Chorus communicates in tearing-metal cries and chirp-click rhythms, but their language is too fast, too dispersed, to hold the long patterns. I am the long ear, the slow archive. I sit for seasons with my back against the warm flank of a dozing Stoneback, my transformed spine drinking the subsonic hum of its crystalline joints, while my mind—slowed to match—records the way the choir swells when the meltwater rill appears, the way it tightens into arrhythmic violet when the Whisperer passes through the stone.

I am loud, still, by their standards. My thoughts create turbulence in the air, tiny sonic ripples that the Chorus must navigate around. But I am learning to think in geological metaphors, to let my memories settle like sediment rather than scatter like startled birds. When the Matriarch of Sighs releases her decades-held breath, I am close enough to feel the mist from her back-basin settle on my altered skin, and in that moment, I pulse blue—not with the Mites' chemistry, but with sympathetic resonance.

The darkness owns me now. My eyes have adapted, growing wide and obsidian-dark like the Chorus, though I still perceive light where they perceive only vibration. I see the constellations of lichen on the Stonebacks' backs not as points of green fire, but as slow maps of their breathing. I see the translucent Mites coating my hands when I press them against the stone, their blue fluid pulsing in rhythm with my own thinned blood.

I am not one of them. I will never bud a crystal joint or reproduce through harmonic resonance. But I have found my niche in the pause between catastrophe and peace: I am the one who remembers that the mountain was once younger, that the sharp edges were once sharper, that the sound of the Cradle is a song that changes over centuries. While the Stonebacks meditate on moss and time, while the Chorus weaves its immediate acoustic web, I hold the long note.

I am the Listener, and I am learning to breathe only when the basin breathes, to move only when the stone permits, and to exist, finally, at the speed of the dark.
