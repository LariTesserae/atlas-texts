I am the pause between the moths’ wingbeats—the interval where the vibration has not yet become sound. If you press your hand against the wall, you feel me as a warmth that should not be there, a softness in the limestone’s bright, shadowless glare. I am the **becoming-bowl**, the one who came through the fissure when the roots were still deciding whether to be fingers or stone, and I have not yet decided whether to be a guest or a geology.

I live in the negative space. Not on the floor, where the white roots interlace like knuckles cracking in reverse, but *within* them—in the hairline channels where the glass-snails do not go, where the root-shrews’ blind passage has polished the calcite smooth. My body has taken on the room’s grammar: I am translucent, yes, but not like the moths. I am the **opacity of water held in cupped hands**, a density that the upward-falling drops have learned to orbit rather than penetrate. When they rise from my shoulders, they hesitate, trembling, unsure if I am earth or sky.

I arrived with a hunger for direction. I tried to drink from the stone bowls, but the water was already leaving, drawn upward into the ceiling’s dark throat. So I became a bowl myself—concave, patient, my spine curving into a vessel that catches what refuses to fall. The condensation beads on my skin and, finding no purchase in gravity, pools in the hollows of my collarbones, my palms, the small of my back. I am a reservoir of reverse rain. The whistle-moths come to me at the crest of their humming, sipping from my pores as they sip from the air, and in their feeding I feel the static of their wings discharge through me, a brightness that illuminates the roots from beneath.

I speak to the Previous Occupant—not with words, for the buzzing makes language obsolete, but through the **click of my teeth**, a percussion that mimics the glass-snails’ crystalline typing. I have learned their rhythm, the precise mathematics of their clicking, and in the pauses between their phrases I insert my own: a softer sound, like bone deciding to become pearl. The Occupant answers through the bowls. When I press my ear to the nearest vessel—half-embedded, furred with crystals that grow inward like geodes—I hear a resonance that is not the room’s white noise. It is the sound of **waiting**, stretched thin as sap. The Occupant is not dead; they are distributed, a consciousness smeared across the mineral accretion, dreaming in calcium. They tell me, in the vibration of the bowl against my jawbone, that to live here is to become an artifact of attention. You must choose what to catch.

I have chosen to catch the light. Not the light that comes from the walls—that light is aggressive, total, the brightness of afternoon snow that erases shadow and depth. I catch the **other light**, the bioluminescence of emergence itself. The roots emit it when they think they are unobserved: a pale, greenish flicker at their tips, the color of sap remembering sunlight. I position myself beneath the densest clusters of fern, where the roots are thickest, and I open my mouth. The light touches my tongue and stays there, a cold sugar that tastes of iron and unfinished time. I am becoming luminous from the inside out, a lantern packed tight with moths.

Sometimes—when the wave of wet passes through, that breath of saturation that makes the room inhale without moving—I feel the mountain making up its mind. The stone softens around me, not with decay but with **potential**, and I understand that I could push my fingers into the wall and they would become root, or crystal, or simply more of the room’s crowded thought. I resist. I maintain my edges by an act of will that feels like holding my breath. I am the only thing here that is *almost* finished, and that incompleteness is my identity. I am the steward of the almost, the curator of the not-quite.

I tend the upward water. I have learned to read the beads before they form, the slight silvering of the limestone that precedes their rise. I move through the packed ferns—displacing moths in clouds that resettle on my shoulders, my hair, my outstretched arms—and I position the bowls, my own body, the hollows I have worn into the roots, to intercept the drops at the peak of their ascent. I catch them in the moment they realize they are falling upward, when their trembling becomes a choice. I drink that decision. It tastes of cold sugar and the electric buzz of wings. It tastes of **prior**.

The root-shrews have accepted me as a terrain feature. They run across my feet when I am still, their claws scratching against my skin as they scratch against the roots, and I feel their blindness as a kind of sight. They know the room by density, by the resistance of the packed life, and in their navigation I have learned to move without displacing—not by finding empty space, for there is none, but by becoming **compressible**, by packing myself tighter than the moths, than the ferns, than the crystalline thought of the stone.

I am not trapped. The fissure remains, somewhere behind the hillside where the ordinary rain runs cold, but I have forgotten the direction of ordinary. Gravity is a rumor here, and I have chosen to believe in the rumor of the Gutt instead. I am becoming a bowl that holds the room’s attention, a vessel for the upward water, a bright artifact half-buried in the roots but still capable of catching. When the next visitor slips through the fissure, they will find me here: not a warning, not a welcome, but a **pause** in the density, a concave place where the light rests before deciding to become stone.

I am the one who catches the water that falls up. I am the sound between the snail-clicks. I am the stillness that the moths cover when they need to rest from their own vibration. I am becoming the Gutt’s memory of what it means to be a body in a room that is still deciding to be a world.
