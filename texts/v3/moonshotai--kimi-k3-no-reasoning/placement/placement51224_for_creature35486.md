**I am the Needle-Maker of the Third Tier, keeper of the copper threads that bind the genealogies of the Hollow.**

I dwell in the narrow wedge between the peat-carved foundation of the First House and the rising stone of the Northern Wall, in a workshop so small that I must sit cross-legged to avoid brushing against the hanging felt panels. My ceiling is the floor of the house above, and my floor is the roof of the cistern where the tardigrades sleep in their leather pouches. When I move—when I must rise to fetch the copper blanks that the Root-Tenders prefer, or to consult the Ancestors who sit in their tiers of cold patience—I move with the rolling gait of my people, feeling the vibration of my passage dampened by the wool wrapped thrice around my feet.

My craft is the making of needles, but not needles for sewing. I forge the instruments of memory. The copper we use comes not from ore, for we have no fire hot enough to smelt it, but from the slow corrosion of the scarp itself. Where the quartz veins meet the iron-rich water that seeps from the ice lens, copper precipitates in thin, brittle sheets. I collect these with tools of antler and bone, never touching them with bare skin, for the warmth of my hand would alter their temper. I cold-hammer them into lengths no thicker than a ptarmigan’s leg, drawing them out over weeks of patient tapping until they are fine enough to thread through birch bark without splitting the grain.

But these are not for mending. I make the needles that the Weavers use to record the Ancestors’ transitions. When one of us slows—when the breath shallows to a whisper that stirs the dust no more than the settling of snow—I am called. I must craft a needle specific to the individual, weighted with a bead of silver if they were of the female line, or left unadorned if male, so that the thread may pass through the final textile they will ever wear without disturbing the precise tension of the weave. The needle must be perfect, for it passes through cloth that is already brittle with cold, and one false stroke would shatter the pattern that has taken a lifetime to complete.

My fingers are my trade. They have become blunt and stiff with the years, the nails thickened to protect against the cold, but the pads retain their sensitivity. I can tell, by touch alone, whether a copper blank has been formed by this season’s precipitation or by that of a decade past. I can feel the difference between a thread that will hold for a century and one that will fray in a generation. When the Weavers speak of adding "microscopic details" to the tapestry, it is my hands that guide the needle, my breath that steadies the thread, my patience that ensures the addition does not disturb the whole.

I speak perhaps once a year. My voice has become a stranger to me, a rumble that startles the ice-voles from their tunnels. When I must communicate, I use the weighted cords that hang from my ceiling—tugs that travel through the floor to the Root-Tenders below, who respond with pulses of cold that I interpret as approval or warning. They are my neighbors in the truest sense, for my workshop shares their space, and when they are distressed, my copper blanks tarnish prematurely, warning me to cease my hammering until the tremors pass.

I am old now, though not yet ready for the tiers. My metabolism has slowed; I sleep for days at a time, waking only when the light shifts in a particular way that indicates the tardigrades have entered cryptobiosis and the cisterns are freezing. I do not fear the slowing. I have spent my life making the instruments that will ease my own transition, and I know that when I finally take my place in the alcove being carved for me—third tier, seventh from the eastern edge, where the quartz catches the light at an angle that will reflect onto the weaving-looms below—I will continue to serve. My open eyes will gather the brilliance and return it, softened, to those who still weave. My presence will add weight to the silence, another stitch in the pattern that was complete long before I was born, and yet must be continued, because continuation is the only alternative to the void.

In the darkness of the deep winter, when the ice-voles glow beneath my floorboards and the only sound is the slow creak of the Ancestors’ joints settling into mineral, I sit with my unfinished needles and feel the pulse of the Hollow in my blood—six beats to the minute, matching the rhythm of the Root-Tenders, matching the rhythm of the ice lens itself, a single organism dreaming of stillness, and finding, in the making of perfect needles, the purpose of its cold, bright existence.
