You wake to the sound of water thinking.

Not the sharp, individual drip of rain on stone, but the **composite voice**—the layered gluck-gluck-gluck of a siphon negotiating its load, the slow sucking pop of clay releasing a buried bubble, the distant thud of a water-tilter somewhere in the maze, counting time in wet, mechanical syllables. You are not in a bed. You are in a **hammock of woven reeds**, suspended between the cedar poles of a hexagonal pod whose walls weep condensation in slow, deliberate beads.

You are not a visitor. You are a **function**.

You are a **Silt-Listener**, a pod-clan’s first responder to the language of the Settling. Your name is not spoken, but *felt*—a specific vibration in the water, a frequency of stillness that your clan recognizes as your attention. You are young, but your ears are old. Your feet, broad and splayed, have never known a floor that did not shift, and so they have become a kind of sensory organ, reading the subtle tremors that precede a slump, the minute changes in pressure that signal a new channel forming.

Your day begins before the grey light filters through the thatch. You do not rise; you *emerge*. You swing from your hammock, your bare feet finding the packed clay floor, which is cool, slick, and faintly trembling with the basin’s slow, internal respiration. The air smells of wet ash, cold stone, and the sharp, metallic tang of the newly exposed minerals—a scent you have learned to parse like a text.

You step out into the corridor, a narrow, waterlogged canyon of touching roofs and dripping eaves. The light is diffuse, sourceless, a flat grey that seems to come from the water itself. The town is a single, continuous organism around you: a warren of hexagonal pods, their reed walls daubed with shell-grey mud, their thatched roofs merging into a dripping canopy. There are no streets, only veins—channels of packed clay and stepping stones slick with algae, where water moves with slow, purposeful intent.

You move with the rolling, deliberate gait of your people, your balance so finely tuned to the unstable ground that you do not think about it. Your toes splay slightly on a submerged stone, feeling the vibration of a distant trip-hammer. Your hands are quick, grey, and calloused, your fingers ending in the slightly spatulate pads that allow you to grip slick reed and wet clay.

You are on your way to the **First Reading**.

You arrive at the pod of the eldest, a structure half-sunk into the mire, its floor perpetually a few inches above a water table that refuses to drop. The eldest—a figure of indeterminate age, their slate-grey skin a map of fine lines, their dark hair braided into tight, functional cords—does not greet you. They are already listening, their head cocked, their eyes closed.

You join them. You kneel on the slick clay floor, your knees sinking slightly into the damp surface. You place your palms flat against the ground. You close your eyes.

You are not meditating. You are **diagnosing**.

You feel the slow, steady pulse of the main siphon, a deep, resonant thrum that speaks of stable pressure. You feel the faint, erratic flutter of a secondary channel, a new stream that has appeared overnight, seeking a path. You feel the subtle, sickly *drag* in the clay to the north, a sign that a mud-cliff is considering a slump. You taste the air: the sharp, ozone tang of fresh mineral, the faint, sour note of organic decay from a drowned cedar root.

The eldest speaks, their voice a low, liquid murmur, full of clicks and glottal stops. They are not asking a question. They are *confirming* a reading.

"The north face is restless," they say. "It will want to move before the next high water."

You nod. You have felt it. "The new stream is clean," you reply. "It will want to be tapped. It is already cutting a path toward the old willow copse."

The eldest’s eyes open, their slate-blue irises catching the grey light. "Then we will ask it to wait," they say. "We will offer it a new path. We will weave a weir from the white willow and show it where it is needed."

This is the work. This is the **partnership**.

You spend the morning not building, but *negotiating*. You and a small team of Silt-Born move through the town’s veins, your waxed reed and fish-skin clothing shedding water, your bare feet finding purchase on slick stone. You carry no tools of metal, only implements of stone, bone, and the dense, black heartwood of drowned cedar. You arrive at the new stream, a brackish, slow-moving channel that is already beginning to undercut a low bank of pale clay.

You do not dam it. You **invite** it.

You drive thin cedar probes into the mud upstream, tasting the water that rises. You read the sediment, the mineral content, the speed of the flow. You identify a natural low point in the terrain, a place where the water *wants* to go, a place where it can be used to power a new water-tilter for shaping wood. You begin to weave a new path for it, using living willow branches, their roots already drinking from the saturated soil, to create a flexible, self-repairing channel. You pack the banks with clay and crushed shells, creating a sluice that will guide the water with gentle, persistent pressure.

You are not commanding the water. You are *suggesting* a course of action. You are a midwife, not a master.

As you work, a **mud-diver** breaks the surface nearby, its sleek, otter-like body a flash of dark fur. It watches you with intelligent, unafraid eyes. You pause, your hand resting on a willow branch. The mud-diver is not a pet; it is a colleague. You have learned to read its behavior. A sudden, panicked exodus of mud-divers from a colony is a sure sign of an impending slump. Their absence from a favorite fishing spot can signal a change in water chemistry, a new vein of salt leaching from the bedrock.

This one is calm. It dives again, its prehensile tail flicking a spray of water. It is a good omen. The land is stable here.

By midday, the new channel is established. The water, once erratic, now flows with a quiet, purposeful hum, turning a hollowed log that will soon power a trip-hammer. The sound is a new note in the town’s composite voice, a small, satisfied *thud-thud-thud* that speaks of work being done, of energy being harnessed.

You return to your pod as the grey light begins to fade, though there is no sunset, only a slow, subtle darkening of the water’s reflective surface. You eat a stew of glasswort, salt-tolerant tuber, and smoked fish, seasoned with the sharp, mineral tang of the salt that still leaches from the bedrock. You eat with your clan, your pod-mates, a silent, efficient communion. Conversation is minimal, a series of low clicks and murmurs that blend with the ambient drip and gluck.

After the meal, you climb into your hammock. You do not sleep as others sleep. You **drift**. You remain half-aware of the sounds around you: the slow drip from the roofline, the distant thud of the new water-tilter, the faint, reassuring hum of the town’s hydraulic heart. You are a node in the network, a sensory organ in the body of the Settling. You are not a resident of the Tumbledown Reservoirs.

You are a **function of its being**.
