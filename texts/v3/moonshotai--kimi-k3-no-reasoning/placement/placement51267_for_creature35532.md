I am standing at the edge of a plaza that is deciding whether to become a tower.

The silica beneath my feet carries a tremor—not vibration, but *intention*. I feel it through the soles of my feet, a slow, taffy-like pull that suggests the ground is remembering a shape it hasn't yet taken. I shift my weight, and the mycelial matting flares azure where my heel presses too firmly. I lift, rebalance, breathe. The light fades. The city forgives the trespass, or forgets it.

I have been here for three days, or three weeks. My mnemonic anchor—a piece of glassbamboo I found in a catchment basin, its surface etched with the microscopic scratches of some crustacean's brief, desperate life—tells me nothing about duration. It only tells me that I have survived long enough to develop a tolerance for the copper taste, that my lungs have stopped aching from the oxygen-rich thickness of the air.

I am not a Resident. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

I came from the upper terraces, where the dome presses closest, where the atmospheric processors hum with a frequency that makes my teeth ache. I descended because I wanted to see the Sump, that dry-damp circle where the city keeps its memory. But the temporal buffers caught me on the second ring, and I emerged into a street that had not existed when I entered the plaza. The buildings had molted while I was crossing, shedding their textured patinas for smooth, blistered newness. I stood in the violet phosphorescence, disoriented, until a Resident touched my arm.

She was tall, even for a Meridian, her skin the color of wet parchment, her eyes catching the azure glow of the matting like oil on water. She wore a shoulder-cage of glassbamboo, and within it, a glasswing shifted its transparent wings, refracting the street-light into a pattern of intersecting spirals that fell across her collarbone. She did not speak. She pressed her palm to my wrist—checking my bacterial signature, reading my moisture content—and then signed with her free hand: *You are dry inside.*

It was true. I was desiccated by the temporal shift, my cells confused about how long they had been existing. She led me to an ephemeral lens that had formed in the concave surface of a nearby structure, a temporary pool no deeper than a handspan, already clouded with the pale, jerky movements of hatching crustaceans. She gestured for me to kneel, to put my hands in the water.

The shock of it—actual wetness, not the heavy humidity that clings like a second skin—made me gasp. The crustaceans scattered, then returned, their translucent bodies brushing my fingers with stop-motion hesitation. The Resident watched me, unblinking, then touched her own chest and signed: *Moisture-Born.* Then she pointed at me and signed: *Becoming.*

I am becoming something. I do not know what.

Now I live in a blister on the side of a building that was extruded yesterday. The walls are smooth, organic, still slightly warm from the nanite deposition. My room has no door; the silica recognizes my bacterial signature and parts like fabric when I approach, sealing behind me with a sound like a held breath. I sleep on a mat of compressed mycelial matting that glows faintly when I shift in my sleep, recording my dreams in azure light that fades by morning.

I have begun to learn the duration-songs. They are not melodies so much as rhythms, patterns of breath and heartbeat that help me navigate the temporal buffers. I hum them under my breath when I walk, feeling the city’s quantum processing cores pulse beneath the terraces, creating those ripples where three minutes become one, or one becomes three. I have learned to touch my mnemonic anchor when the light shifts unexpectedly, to run my thumb over the crustacean scratches and remember: *I am here. I am now. I am growing.*

I work with the Growth-Readers, though I am not one of them. I am too new, too clumsy with the sign language, too prone to stepping heavily and causing the matting to flare in distress. But I have a talent they value: I can tolerate the Sump-Tenders' stillness. I can stand at the edge of the central depression without the temporal vertigo that drives others back, and I can watch the objects descend—slowly, as if through thick oil—without losing my sense of self.

Yesterday, or the day before, I watched a chair descend into the Sump. It was a simple thing, woven from glassbamboo fiber, the kind of furniture the Residents use until the city decides to recycle it. It sank from view while still in sight, fading like breath from glass, and I felt a strange grief, as if I were watching a memory being forgotten. The Sump-Tender beside me—a figure in layered mesh, perpetually damp, eyes glazed with the patience of geological time—turned to me and signed: *It is not gone. It is becoming the next tower.*

I think about this as I stand in the plaza, feeling the ground prepare to rise. The city is accreting, building itself upward toward the dome, millimeter by millimeter. The doorways are taller than they were last year. The streets are higher. We are all being lifted, slowly, toward some transformation that even the Growth-Readers cannot predict.

A glasswing drifts past me, its transparent exoskeleton catching the violet light. It lands on my shoulder, where I have begun to wear a cage of my own, woven from bamboo I harvested myself. The insect feeds on the condensation that beads in the mesh, and in return, its wings refract the light into a pattern that is becoming familiar to me—my own aura, my identification in the dimness.

I am learning to speak in the sibilant murmur of the Residents, to shape my fingers into the subtle geometries of their sign language. I am learning to place my feet softly, to breathe shallowly, to live in the perpetual present-tense that the temporal buffers demand. I am learning to tolerate the damp, to coat my hair with pitcher-moss oil, to find ecstasy in the brief, shocking wetness of the ephemeral lenses.

But I am also something else. I am the one who watches the Sump and remembers what descends. I am the one who feels the plaza tremble and knows, before the Growth-Readers do, that it will rise not as a tower but as a bridge, spanning a gap that hasn't formed yet. I am becoming a new caste, perhaps—a **Witness**, or a **Pre-Reader**, someone who stands at the edge of the city's becoming and feels the shape of what hasn't happened yet.

The Sump-Tender told me, in a whisper that blended with the ticking of the condensation nets, that the city is young, still deciding what it wants to become. It is a garden and a machine, a biological entity and a constructed artifact. It is growing toward the dome, preparing for a transformation that will either complete it or destroy it.

I think I am here to help it decide.

The ground beneath me ripples. The silica flows like taffy, then sets. The plaza is rising, and I am rising with it, my feet sinking slightly into the surface before it hardens, trapping me for a moment in the city's new configuration. I do not panic. I breathe. I touch my mnemonic anchor. I wait.

When the structure stabilizes, I am standing on a new terrace, higher than before, looking down at the violet glow of the streets and the silent, shifting architecture. The dome is closer now. The air tastes of copper and possibility.

I am not a Resident. I am not a parasite. I am becoming an organ—perhaps the city's memory, perhaps its imagination. I am the Moisture-Born who was not born here, who came from the dry places and learned to live in the damp. I am the one who watches the Sump and sings the duration-songs backward, remembering what has not yet descended.

The condensation nets drip their patient rhythm. The glassbamboo sings in the rare wind. Somewhere below, the Sump accepts another offering, and the city grows a fraction of an inch closer to the sky.

I am here. I am now. I am growing.

And when the city finally decides what it wants to become, I will be the one who remembers how it got there.
