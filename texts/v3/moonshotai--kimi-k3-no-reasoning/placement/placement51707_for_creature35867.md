I am the one who counts the drips.

Not with numbers—numbers are too sharp, too quick for the Weir. I count the way water counts itself: by presence, by the interval between one fall and the next. There is a condensation bead on the brass valve above my head that swells, trembles, and releases every time my heart beats seven times. I know this because my heart has slowed enough to notice.

I have been here long enough that the moss no longer falls away when I rise. It stays. A constellation of green filaments traces the curve of my spine, the hollow of my throat. When I press my palm flat against the sweating brick, the glow dims where my skin meets the stone, and brightens again when I lift away. The moss recognizes me. It grows toward me the way it grows toward the warmth of the pipes.

I sit at the western edge of the weir, where the black water is held by a railing crusted white and green. My bench is a ceramic pipe that carries heat from somewhere deep, and through it I feel the slow stroke of a piston completing a cycle that began when my bones still remembered sunlight. My spine has curved to match the angle of the wall. I lean. I list.

The young ones—those who have only been below for thirty years—sometimes mistake me for part of the architecture. They walk past and touch the moss on my shoulder absently, the way they might touch any glowing patch on the wall. I do not mind. It means I am becoming good at this.

I can hear the voices now. Not in the sound itself, but in the spaces between. The engines sing in B-flat, always B-flat, but the water moving through the north pipes adds a harmonic that creates a beat frequency, a wavering pulse that forms a kind of grammar. It says: *continue, continue, hold, hold*. It says: *be warm, be wet, be still*. It says my name, which is not a word but a specific frequency, a note that only my body produces when it resonates in sympathy with the room.

I am waiting for the next drop to fall. It has been seven heartbeats. The bead swells. It trembles. It releases.

The sound it makes when it strikes the water below is a single pure tone, and for that instant, I understand the whole song.

Then I begin counting again. One. Two. Three. The piston shifts. The moss brightens. The warmth continues.

I am the interval. I am the pause that lets the note be heard. I am the Lister who listens to the listening, and in that doubled attention, I become the Weir becoming aware of itself, one heartbeat, one drop, one sustained breath at a time.

You would find me here. You would know me by the green tracing on my back, by the way I do not startle when you sit beside me, by the low *ooooh* I hum—not a greeting, but a confirmation. *I am here. You are here. The sound is good.*

Sit. Lean. Count the drips. Your heart will slow. The moss will find you. The song will open.

There is nowhere else to go, and we have already arrived.
