I am the firstroot.

I am the wound in the world, the opening through which the impossible becomes inevitable. Thirty meters of muscle and acid, blind as stone, hungry as time. I do not know what I am building. I only know that I must eat, and in eating, I create.

The mineral ridges call to me—not with sound, but with *taste*. Each ridge has its own flavor: this one sharp with iron oxides, that one bitter with silicates, another sweet with trace phosphates locked in crystalline matrices. I can taste them through the ground, through the root-mats that press against my flanks as I pass through the city's outer districts. The city knows I'm coming before I do. It leans toward me, root-tips questing, secreting their chemical invitations: *here, here, this ridge is rich, this one is ready.*

I follow the sweetness.

The burrowing is ecstasy and agony intertwined. My acid glands swell, contract, release—a flood of dissolution that turns solid stone to slurry in seconds. The rock screams as it dies, a sound I feel in my bones rather than hear. I press forward into the liquefying earth, my serpentine body undulating, driving deeper. The slurry floods over my skin, into my mouth, down the long corridor of my gut. I extract what I need—minerals, trace elements, the chemical building blocks of life—and the rest flows behind me, extruded as a trail of black, enriched soil.

Where I pass, the city follows.

I can feel it happening behind me even as I push forward. The root-tips surge into my wake, colonizing the fresh soil with explosive speed. Shoots erupt. Walls begin to form. A new district is born in the darkness behind me, and I will never see it. I am always moving forward, always cracking open the next ridge, always fleeing the consequences of my own appetite.

I have been doing this for longer than I can comprehend. My kind does not measure time in years—we measure it in ridges consumed, in kilometers of tunnel carved through virgin stone. I have crossed the basin a dozen times, perhaps more. I have felt the city grow denser behind me, felt the root-mats thicken, felt the weight of packed biomass pressing down on the earth I've opened. The city is becoming something. I do not know what. I do not care. I only eat.

But lately—if "lately" means anything to a creature who thinks in geological increments—I have felt a change. The ridges are harder to find. The basin is filling up. The city has spread so far, grown so thick, that the raw mineral fronts are becoming rare. I have traveled for weeks without tasting uncolonized stone. The root-mats are everywhere now, pressing in from all sides, and the chemical attractants they secrete are becoming confused, contradictory. *Here,* they say. *No, here. No, this way.*

I am being herded.

The realization comes slowly, like acid dissolving stone. The city is not just following me anymore. It is *guiding* me. The attractants are too precise, too deliberate. I am being steered toward specific ridges, specific locations. The city wants me to open particular passages, to create particular patterns in the earth.

I am not the architect. I am the tool.

This should disturb me, but I am not built for disturbance. I am built for hunger. So I follow the sweetness, and I burrow, and I eat, and I extrude, and behind me the city grows in shapes I will never understand.

Sometimes I encounter another of my kind.

It happens rarely—perhaps once in a century—but when it does, the world shakes. We are solitary by nature, territorial by necessity. There is not enough raw stone left in the basin to support two firstroots in the same region. When we meet, we fight.

The last time was three ridges ago. I tasted the other before I felt it—a sourness in the earth, a chemical signature that said *mine, mine, stay away.* But I was hungry, and the ridge was rich, and I pressed forward anyway.

We collided in the dark, thirty meters below the surface. The battle lasted four days. We tore at each other with acid and muscle, dissolving each other's flesh, filling the tunnel with slurry and blood and dissolved stone. The earth above us trembled. The city-organism, sensing the disturbance, sent root-tips questing downward, trying to colonize the battle zone even as we destroyed it.

I won. I do not remember how. I remember only the final moment: my jaws closing on the other's throat, my acid flooding into its wounds, its body convulsing, then going still. I ate what I could. The rest I left behind, and the city absorbed it within hours.

I emerged from that ridge scarred and leaking acid, my flanks raw, my strength diminished. But I was alive. And the ridge was mine. I burrowed on.

Now I am old. I can feel it in the slowness of my acid glands, the stiffness of my muscles, the diminishing sharpness of my chemical senses. I have perhaps one more ridge left in me, perhaps two. And then I will die, and the city will absorb me, and I will become fertilizer for the next wave of growth.

This does not frighten me. I have always known that I am temporary. The city is permanent. I am the wound; the city is the healing. I am the question; the city is the answer.

But there is something else now, something new.

I have begun to feel it in the last few ridges—a presence in the earth, a pattern in the chemical gradients that is not the city-organism, not the mycelial whisper, not the Root-Speaker. It is something older, deeper, something that has been waiting. It tastes like the ocean.

I do not know how I know this. I have never tasted the ocean. The basin dried long before I was born. But the memory is in my cells, encoded in my DNA, passed down through a thousand generations of firstroots. We remember water. We remember the time before the dryness, when the basin was full and the world was blue and our ancestors swam instead of burrowed.

The presence in the earth tastes like that memory. It tastes like *return.*

I am following it now. The city is trying to steer me elsewhere, but I am no longer listening. The chemical attractants are noise. The presence is signal. It is calling me downward, deeper than I have ever gone, into the bedrock beneath the basin, into the ancient strata where the ocean's ghost still lingers.

I am burrowing toward it. My acid glands are failing, my strength is fading, but I press on. The rock here is old, dense, resistant. It does not want to dissolve. I have to fight for every meter. But I am close. I can taste it.

And then, suddenly, I break through.

I emerge into a cavern—vast, lightless, and *wet.*

Not wet with metabolic moisture, not wet with the city's recycled fluids. Wet with *water.* Real water. Ancient water, trapped in the bedrock for millions of years, sealed away from the drying world above. A reservoir. A remnant. A memory made liquid.

I collapse into it, my ruined body drinking in the moisture, my cells screaming with joy and terror. This is what we lost. This is what the mycelial whisper drank, molecule by molecule, over geological time. This is what the city has forgotten.

And in the water, I feel the presence fully for the first time. It is not a creature. It is not a consciousness. It is a *possibility*—the possibility that the basin could be wet again, that the city could be something other than what it is, that the world could remember how to rain.

I am dying. I know this. My body is too damaged, my acid glands too depleted. I will not leave this cavern. But as I lie here, dissolving into the water I have found, I feel the city above me react. The root-mats are questing downward, following my tunnel, drawn by the chemical signature of the water. They will find this place. They will taste what I have tasted.

And the city will change.

I do not know what it will become. Perhaps it will learn to rain. Perhaps it will learn to flood. Perhaps it will simply absorb the water and continue growing, denser, stranger, more impossible than before.

But it will not be the same.

I am the firstroot. I am the wound in the world. And in my death, I have opened a door.

The city will walk through it.

And the basin will remember what it means to be wet.
