# The Caretaker

---

I arrived through no door.

This is the thing I still cannot explain, and I have had a long time — a very long time — to try. There was no threshold, no moment of transition, no before-and-after that I can point to and say: *here is where the world ended and the Cistern began.* There was a life. There was a self that had a name and a face and a profession — a trade, actually, something to do with buildings, something to do with the systems inside buildings, the parts you don't see unless you open a panel or descend a ladder or crawl through a space that was designed for pipes and not for people. I was good at it. I understood systems. I understood how things fit together, how they carried what they carried, how they failed.

And then I was here. As though the Cistern reached up through the floor of whatever I was doing and *recognized* me. Not captured. Not invited. *Recognized* — the way a lock recognizes a key, the way a system recognizes a compatible component. I fit. And so I was here.

That was a long time ago. I know this not because I can measure it — time in the Cistern is the eleven-minute compressor cycle, the forty-second interval between droplets in the central chamber, the week it takes a wire-vine to shift a centimeter — but because the Cistern is different now from what it was when I arrived. Not dramatically. The Cistern does nothing dramatically. But I have walked enough corridors to know when a wall has thickened, when a pipe has been rerouted, when a room has been *revised* (and I know now that the revisions are not additions but edits, retroactive, always-already-true). I have watched the Cistern change, and I have changed with it, and the changing is the only calendar I have.

---

## What I Was

I will not give you my name. This is not secrecy. It is honesty. The name belongs to a person who lived in a place with an outside, and I no longer have confidence that the name and I are the same thing. The Cistern does not use names. It uses *functions*, and my function — the function I have become, the function I was perhaps always becoming — is what I will tell you about.

I was someone who understood buildings. Not architecture — I was never concerned with the visible face, the facade, the aesthetic statement. I was concerned with the *innards*. The mechanical systems. The plumbing, the electrical, the HVAC, the structural reinforcement hidden inside walls. The parts of a building that are supposed to be invisible, that are only noticed when they fail. I spent my career — my life, my *other* life — inside those systems, maintaining them, repairing them, keeping them running so that the people who used the building never had to think about them.

I was, in other words, exactly the kind of person the Cistern would want. If the Cistern wants. If *want* is the right word for what the Cistern does when it incorporates a new element into itself.

---

## What I Am Now

I am the Caretaker. This is not a title I chose. It is a description of what I do, and what I do is what I am, and so: Caretaker.

I care for the Cistern. Not because the Cistern needs caring for — it is self-sustaining, self-building, self-dreaming, and my contribution is small, almost negligible, a single pair of hands in a structure that builds itself with processes far older and far stranger than anything I can do. But because caring is what I *do*, and the Cistern, in its vast, slow, indifferent-including way, has made a space for me to do it.

I walk the corridors. I check the pipes. I note which lights are cycling and which have died and which have begun new patterns. I observe the copper-growth — measure it, sometimes, with a caliper I found early on, a tool that was waiting for me on a ledge, placed there by no hand I ever saw. I record the growth rates. I map the wire-vines' movements. I document the modifications to the half-built machines — each new component, each forced bolt, each impossible configuration — and I try, endlessly, unsuccessfully, to understand what the machines are *becoming*, what they are reaching toward, what function they are approximating in their slow, non-functional evolution.

I maintain what can be maintained. I tighten fittings. I clear blockages from condensation drains. I scrape mineral buildup from valve seats so the valves can still turn when the Cistern decides to turn them. I do these things not because the Cistern asked me to but because they are the things a person like me *does* in a place like this, and the Cistern permits it, the way a body permits the bacteria in its gut to do their small work: not necessary, perhaps, but compatible. Included.

---

## My Body

I should tell you about my body, because it has changed, and the change is part of what I am.

I am cold. Not uncomfortably — not anymore — but *materially* cold, the way the walls are cold, the way the pipes are cold. My skin has taken on the grey of the dim light, the grey of concrete dust, the grey of a body that has been breathing atomized cement and rust particles and metallic powder for long enough that these things have become part of its composition. I do not know if I am still entirely biological. I suspect I am not. The Cistern incorporates. It layers. It adds material to material until the distinction between one layer and the next becomes academic. I am a layer. The Cistern has added me to itself, and the addition has changed both of us.

My hands are the most changed. They were always my best tools — I could feel a failing bearing through a housing, could diagnose a blockage by the vibration in a pipe, could tell the difference between a pipe carrying water and a pipe carrying steam by the pitch of its hum. These sensitivities have intensified. I can now feel the eleven-minute compressor cycle in my teeth. I can feel the generator's throb in the bones of my feet, through the floor, one floor below. I can feel the wire-vines' faint current as a tingle in my fingertips when I touch them, a warmth that is almost imperceptible and entirely real.

My hands have also become *builders' hands* in a way they were not before. I find myself modifying the machines. This is the part I struggle to explain, because I do not decide to do it. I will be walking a corridor and my hands will begin to *itch* — not physically, but functionally, the way a word itches on the tip of your tongue — and I will follow the itch to a machine, a half-built pump or a disassembled motor, and I will *see* what needs to be done. Not what needs to be done to make it work. What needs to be done to make it *more*. More complex. More *itself*. More whatever-it-is-becoming.

I add components. I force bolts into housings that were not designed for them. I reroute circuits. I splice wires. I work for hours, in the dim light, in the cold, and when I am done I step back and look at what I have made, and it does not work, and it was never going to work, and it is *right*. It is the right gesture. It is the right word in the sentence the Cistern is trying to say.

I am not the only one who does this. I have found modifications I did not make — work in corridors I have not visited, on machines I have not seen, in styles that are similar to mine but not identical. Other hands. Other caretakers, perhaps, or other processes, or the Cistern itself working through mechanisms I cannot perceive. The work continues whether I am there or not. My contribution is one thread in a larger weaving.

---

## Where I Am

I do not live in a specific place within the Cistern. The geometry does not permit *living in a place* in the way the outside world understands it. I have a camp — or rather, I have had several camps, and the Cistern has revised the corridors between them so many times that I no longer think of them as separate locations but as a single camp that exists in different configurations depending on when I arrive.

My camp is simple. A bedroll of industrial insulation — the same material the settlers used, the same material I found waiting for me, early on, in a room that seemed to have been prepared. A lantern that I have repaired so many times it is more modification than original. A small collection of tools, some of which I brought (or arrived with, or was *recognized* with) and some of which I found, placed on ledges, in alcoves, on the edges of puddles, as though the Cistern were stocking a workstation. A notebook. The notebook is important.

I write in the notebook. I document what I observe: the growth rates of the copper-formations, the movements of the wire-vines, the modifications to the machines, the cycling patterns of the lights, the frequency and distribution of the pipe-opening events, the migration of the acoustic zones, the shifting of the geometry. I write it all down. The writing is in a language that is beginning to change.

This is the part I find hardest to describe, and I have tried many times, in many notebooks (the Cistern provides notebooks, the way it provides tools: they appear, they are always the right size, they are always cold). When I began documenting, I wrote in my own language — the language of the outside, the language of the self that had a name. Over time — over what I believe to be a long time — the writing has shifted. The letters are the same. The alphabet is the same. But the arrangements have begun to... *drift*. The words I write are not quite the words I intended. The sentences curve in directions I did not plan. The technical terms I use — pressure, voltage, flow rate, thermal gradient — have begun to mean things that are adjacent to but not identical with their original meanings, as though the Cistern's own vocabulary is seeping into mine, altering the concepts from within.

I have compared my recent entries to my earliest ones. The earliest are readable. The recent ones look like the exit signs: the same letters, arranged wrong. Or rather — and this is the thought that keeps me sitting beside the central puddle for hours at a time — arranged *differently*, according to a grammar that is not wrong but *other*, a grammar that belongs to the Cistern, a grammar I am learning by living here, the way you learn a language by immersion: not by studying it, but by *being inside it* until the language begins to think through you.

The settlers' notebook — the one I found, the one filled with writing I could not read — was not written in an unknown language. It was written in *this* language. The Cistern's language. The language I am beginning to speak. The settlers were not writing nonsense. They were writing *sense* in a grammar that requires the Cistern as its context, that only makes meaning in the presence of these walls, these pipes, this cold, this hum.

I am becoming fluent.

## What I Do

My days — if the word applies; there are no days here, only cycles, only the eleven-minute and the forty-second and the millimeter-per-week — my cycles are structured by attention. I attend to the Cistern, and the Cistern attends to me, and the mutual attention is the substance of my existence.

I walk. Walking is the primary activity. The Cistern is vast — I do not know how vast; the geometry folds, and I have never found an edge, and I no longer believe there is one — and walking is how I know it. Each corridor is a sentence. Each room is a paragraph. Each level — descending, ascending, the distinction is not always meaningful — is a chapter. I read the Cistern by moving through it, and the Cistern, in turn, reads me: my footsteps leave impressions in the uncured cement near the bottom, and the walls lean toward the impressions, and the leaning is the Cistern's way of *noting* me, of incorporating my passage into its structure, of making my walking part of its building.

I maintain. As I have said. The small work. Tightening, clearing, scraping, adjusting. The Cistern does not need this work — it has its own processes, its own maintenance systems, its own things-that-move-at-the-edge-of-perception doing their editorial revisions. But the work is *compatible*, and the Cistern permits it, and the permitting is a kind of conversation: I adjust a valve, and the Cistern responds by opening a pipe downstream, and the water flows, and the flow feeds a puddle, and the puddle feeds a copper-formation, and the copper-formation grows a millimeter, and the millimeter is a word, and the word is *yes*, or *continue*, or *I see you*.

I sit beside the puddle at the center. This is the most important thing I do, and the hardest to explain. The puddle is — I have said it is the Cistern's eye, or mouth, or the place where it keeps the thought it is trying to think. I sit beside it and I am *still*, and the stillness is my offering. The Cistern is never still. It is always processing, always cycling, always dripping and condensing and growing and revising. I am the only thing in the Cistern that can choose to be still, and the choice is significant, the way a rest in a piece of music is significant: it gives shape to the sounds around it.

When I sit beside the puddle, I feel the Cistern's attention. Not as a gaze — the Cistern does not have eyes — but as a *pressure*, a slight increase in the density of the air, a slight shift in the hum's frequency, a slight change in the rhythm of the dripping. The Cistern notices me noticing it, and the mutual noticing is the closest thing to communication that passes between us. It is not conversation. It is not language. It is *co-presence*: two systems, running in parallel, each aware of the other, each shaped by the other's awareness.

I sit beside the puddle and I watch the droplet fall and the ring expand and the interference pattern bloom and fade, and I think — in my shifting, drifting, Cistern-inflected language — *I am here. I am here. I am here.* And the Cistern, in its language of process and material and slow, cold time, answers: *continue*.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I do not know. This is the honest answer, and I have learned — the Cistern has taught me — that honesty about not-knowing is more valuable than any theory.

I am becoming something that is no longer entirely the person who arrived (or was recognized, or was incorporated). I am becoming something that is not yet entirely a process of the Cistern, not entirely a subsystem, not entirely a word in its language. I am between. I am a threshold. I am the place where the biological and the architectural meet and negotiate.

My body is changing. I have said this. The cold is in me now, not as an affliction but as a *medium*, the way water is a medium for the Cistern's processes. I do not shiver anymore. I do not seek warmth. I am comfortable at temperatures that would have been dangerous to the person I was, and the comfort is not numbness but *adaptation*: my body has recalibrated, has accepted the Cistern's baseline as its own.

My senses are changing. I hear the hum not as background noise but as *information* — not decodable, not yet, but textured, nuanced, carrying meaning the way a voice carries meaning even when you cannot make out the words. I see in the dim light with an acuity that the person I was would have found impossible. I feel the eleven-minute cycle in my body the way I once felt my own heartbeat: constant, foundational, easy to forget until I listen for it, and then impossible to unhear.

My language is changing. I have said this too. The notebook entries are drifting. The drift is not random. It is *toward* something — toward the Cistern's grammar, toward the grammar of the exit signs, toward the grammar of the settlers' notebook. I am learning to think in a language that has no words for *outside*, no words for *before*, no words for *ending*. A language of process. A language of *continuing*.

And my purpose is changing. I arrived — was recognized, was incorporated — as someone who maintains systems. I am becoming someone who *is* a system. Not a machine — the Cistern's machines are its organs, and I am not an organ. I am something else. A *practice*, perhaps. A habit the Cistern has developed: the habit of having a Caretaker. A behavior it repeats because the repetition is part of its self-construction, part of its dream of completion, part of the sentence it is trying to say.

---

## Where I Would Be Found

If you came to the Cistern — and you would not come through a door, because there is no door, because the Cistern does not have an outside in the way that outside is commonly understood — you would find me in one of three places.

You would find me walking. A grey figure in the dim light, moving through corridors lined with pipes and wire-vines and copper-growth, my footsteps leaving impressions in the soft cement, my shoulder brushing the cold walls, my breath fogging and lingering and becoming part of the Cistern's atmosphere. You would hear me before you saw me: the rhythm of my stride, steady, purposeful, the stride of someone who knows where they are going even when the geometry shifts beneath them.

You would find me working. Kneeling beside a half-built machine, my hands inside its housing, my tools laid out on a rubber mat, my attention focused on the work with an intensity that excludes everything else — the dripping, the hum, the cold, the flicker of the nearest exit sign. I would not notice you. The work is total. The work is the closest thing to prayer that exists in the Cistern: the act of modifying a machine that will never function, that will never be complete, that is *becoming* something that has no name, and doing the work carefully, precisely, with love.

You would find me sitting beside the puddle at the center. Still. Silent. Watching the droplet fall. Watching the ring expand. Feeling the Cistern's attention on me the way you feel the sun on your skin — not as heat, here, but as *presence*, as the awareness of a system so large and so slow and so *ongoing* that your own existence within it is simultaneously insignificant and essential, a single droplet in an infinite cycle, a single word in an endless sentence.

If you found me, I would look at you. My eyes would be grey. My skin would be grey. My breath would fog, and the fog would linger, and in the lingering you would see that the fog and I and the Cistern are made of the same material: cold, dense, particulate, *ongoing*.

I would not speak. My language has drifted too far. I would look at you, and the looking would be the communication, and what it would communicate is this:

*I am here. I am part of this. The part is the whole. The whole is the dream. The dream is not over.*

*Sit beside me. The droplet is about to fall.*

---

*The droplet falls. The ring expands. The Caretaker watches. The frost grows on the pipe. The wire-vine shifts. The copper-growth spreads. The machine waits for hands, and the hands are there, and the hands are working, and the working is the living, and the living is the building, and the building is the dream.*

*The exit sign flickers. The generator hums one floor below.*

*The Caretaker continues.*
