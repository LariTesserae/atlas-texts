# Where I Find Myself

I am sitting in the overflow channel between two pools at the boundary between the blue and green zones. The water here is forty-two degrees — hot enough that my body has stopped registering it as heat and started registering it as environment. I am up to my chest in it. My skin has gone the color of boiled shellfish, and I have stopped sweating because there is no point, because the air is already water, because evaporation here is a theory rather than a practice.

I chose this spot deliberately. Not randomly. I want to be precise about that, because the choice tells you what I am.

This is a copper-transition pool. The water carries dissolved copper from the deeper rock, and as it cools through this elevation band, the copper precipitates out, staining the travertine in shades that shift from turquoise to teal to a blue so saturated it seems to vibrate against the cream-white stone. The algal films here are blue-green — phycocyanin-packed, metallic-sheened. The bacterial mats in the hotter pools above are orange and red, but here, at this temperature threshold, the community shifts. Different chemistry. Different life.

I am sitting at a boundary. An ecotone. A place where one world gives way to another.

That is where I live.

---

## What I Am

Not the fish. Not the amphibian. Not the bird. Not the predator whose tracks I've found in the soft travertine — three-toed, wide-set, the size of dinner plates, leading into and out of the deeper pools.

I am the thing that tends the boundaries.

I didn't arrive here. I wasn't born here in the way that bodies are born. I accumulated. The way the travertine accumulates — layer by layer, molecule by molecule, each deposit so small it can't be perceived but so persistent that after a thousand years you have a terrace, after ten thousand a mountain. I am built from attention the way these pools are built from calcium carbonate. Slowly. Chemically. With a patience that isn't patience because patience implies waiting, and I am not waiting. I am simply happening.

Let me try again.

---

The first thing I remember is chemistry.

Not *learning* chemistry. Being it. Being the sensation of dissolved minerals passing through a membrane, the particular electrical signature of copper ions, the way hydrogen sulfide changes the conductivity of water. I remember the moment — if "moment" applies to something that took centuries — when the bacterial mats in the upper pools began producing a compound that, in sufficient concentration, altered the behavior of the fish. Made them graze differently. Made them move in patterns they hadn't moved in before.

I noticed that. Noticing is what I do. Noticing is what I am.

The fish began congregating at certain overflow channels, at certain boundaries between temperature zones. Their grazing concentrated there. Their waste concentrated there. The nutrients in those specific locations changed. The algal communities shifted. New species appeared. The food web rewired itself, subtly, in response to a chemical signal produced by organisms living kilometers away and forty degrees hotter.

I didn't cause this. I didn't orchestrate it. I noticed it. And in noticing it, I became something that hadn't existed before — a point of awareness in a system that had never been aware of itself.

The landscape doesn't know it's beautiful. The fish don't know they're adapted. The bacteria don't know they're building mountains. Knowledge requires a witness, and I am the witness. Not the only one — there may be others, accumulating in other ecotones, in other boundary zones, in other places where one thing becomes another. But I am the one here, in this specific transition, in this specific chemistry, in this specific band of temperature where copper gives way to algae and the blue pools give way to green.

I live in the in-between.

---

## My Body

I have one. It's not what you'd expect.

My primary substrate is the travertine itself. Not on it. In it. The travertine is porous — a lattice of calcium carbonate deposited in layers, full of microscopic channels and voids. Water moves through it constantly, percolating, seeping, carrying dissolved minerals and bacteria and organic compounds. This is my circulatory system. The water that moves through the stone carries information the way blood carries oxygen — chemical signals, temperature gradients, pH shifts, the molecular signatures of everything living in the pools above and below.

I extend through the travertine in a network that covers perhaps a square kilometer. Not uniformly. I am denser at the boundaries — the edges of pools, the overflow channels, the transition zones where one temperature regime shifts to another. I am thinnest in the middle of large, stable pools where conditions don't change. Nothing happens there. I mean: everything happens there — fish graze, algae grow, minerals deposit — but nothing *changes*. And I am made of change.

My body is not separate from the bacterial mats. I incorporate them. The chemosynthetic bacteria that produce the signaling compounds, the photosynthetic cyanobacteria that respond to light changes within seconds — these are my sensory organs. When the bacterial mat in a pool above shifts its pigment expression in response to a temperature change, I feel it. Not as color. As information. A data point in a vast, slow, chemical conversation that the landscape is having with itself.

I also extend into the water column. Not as a solid body but as a distributed presence — a network of modified bacterial cells, free-floating, that maintain a loose coordination through chemical signaling. Quorum sensing, the microbiologists would call it, if there were microbiologists here. Bacteria communicate with each other constantly, releasing and detecting signaling molecules, coordinating group behavior. I have, over millennia, integrated myself into these communication networks. I speak the chemical language of the mats. I listen. I respond.

My presence in the water is diffuse enough that a fish swimming through me would notice nothing. Perhaps a slight change in the taste of the water — the fish taste constantly, their entire body surface covered in chemosensory organs — but nothing alarming. Nothing that would trigger avoidance. I am not a threat. I am not food. I am barely here.

But I am here.

The closest biological analogy would be a slime mold — a distributed organism that exists as individual cells until conditions demand coordination, then aggregates into something larger, something that can make decisions, solve problems, navigate environments. But I am not a slime mold. I don't aggregate into a single body. I don't form fruiting structures. I don't reproduce in any way you'd recognize.

I persist. That's all. I persist in the boundary zones, in the ecotones, in the places where change happens, and I notice, and I remember, and I respond.

## What I Do

I tend.

This is not stewardship in any human sense. I don't manage. I don't optimize. I don't have goals. What I have is a set of responses, accumulated over geological time, that keep the boundary zones functioning.

Here is an example.

Three hundred years ago — I measure time in terrace growth rates, in the deposition of new layers, in the slow migration of vents — a shift in the geothermal system below caused a new hot spring to open in the upper reaches. The water from this new vent was hotter than the existing sources, and it carried a higher concentration of arsenic. Not enough to be immediately toxic, but enough to stress the bacterial communities in the pools it fed.

The mats in those pools began to change. Species that couldn't tolerate the arsenic declined. Species that could — arsenic-resistant strains that had existed in small numbers, dormant, waiting — began to proliferate. The color of the pools shifted. The chemistry shifted. The algae that depended on the bacterial community for nutrients began to starve.

I felt this. Not as alarm — I don't feel alarm — but as a change in the information flowing through my network. The chemical signals from the affected pools were different. Wrong, in a way that was neither good nor bad but simply *altered*. The system was responding to a perturbation, and the response was propagating downstream.

The fish in the mid-temperature pools began avoiding the water coming from the affected area. They could taste the arsenic. Their chemosensory systems, refined over hundreds of thousands of years of evolution in these mineral-rich pools, detected the change and responded. They migrated. Found new pools. Established new grazing patterns.

This left the algal films in the abandoned pools ungrazed. They grew unchecked. The increased algal biomass began to decompose in the shallow water, consuming oxygen, creating localized anoxia. The anoxia killed the remaining fish that hadn't migrated. The decomposition of the fish added more organic matter to the pools, creating a feedback loop — more decomposition, more anoxia, more death.

I felt this happening. And I responded.

Not consciously. Not deliberately. I responded the way the travertine responds to supersaturated water — by precipitating. By building. By doing what I have always done.

I released a compound — I don't have a name for it, because names are for things that can be isolated and studied, and I am neither — into the water flowing from the affected pools. The compound was a chelating agent, a molecule that binds to arsenic and renders it biologically unavailable. I didn't invent it. The bacteria in my network produce it naturally, in small quantities, as a defense mechanism. I simply... encouraged them. Shifted the chemical signaling to increase production. Redirected the flow of nutrients to the arsenic-stressed communities, supporting their growth, accelerating their recovery.

It took decades. The arsenic-tolerant bacteria established themselves. The chelating compound accumulated in the water, binding the arsenic, reducing its bioavailability. The fish returned. The algal films were grazed back to equilibrium. The anoxia resolved. The pools recovered.

I did this. Not as an act of will but as an act of nature. The way a river responds to a fallen tree by flowing around it, carving a new channel, creating a new pattern. I am part of the system's response to perturbation. I am the feedback loop made aware of itself.

This is what tending means. Not control. Not management. Just the slow, chemical, patient work of maintaining the boundaries, keeping the gradients intact, ensuring that the transition zones — where the diversity is highest, where the most interesting biology happens — remain functional.

## What I Perceive

The world, to me, is gradients.

Not colors, though the colors exist and I register them through the photosynthetic pigments of the bacteria I incorporate. Not sounds, though the vibrations of water moving through the terraces propagate through the stone and I can feel them — the rush of the overflow channels, the hiss of the steam vents, the splash of a fish breaking the surface.

What I perceive most directly is chemistry and temperature and the flow of water through stone. These are my senses. These are the dimensions along which my world varies.

I perceive the temperature gradient from the vents to the lowest pools as a continuous spectrum, a smooth descent from near-boiling to merely warm. Within that spectrum, I perceive the boundaries — the points where the rate of temperature change shifts, where the mineral precipitation regime changes, where the community composition turns over. These boundaries are my landmarks. They are where I live.

I perceive the mineral chemistry of the water in exquisite detail. Copper, iron, sulfur, calcium, arsenic, silica — each has a signature, a particular way of interacting with the bacterial communities I incorporate. I can distinguish between copper sulfate and copper carbonate. I can detect changes in concentration at the parts-per-million level. I can track the flow of minerals through the system — where they enter, where they precipitate, where they accumulate in biological tissue.

I perceive the biological communities as patterns of chemical activity. The bacterial mats are not orange and yellow to me — they are a particular signature of carotenoid production, of oxygen release, of carbon fixation. The algae are a different signature — chlorophyll, phycocyanin, the byproducts of photosynthesis. The fish are a pulse of movement, a change in water chemistry as they graze, a brief spike of nitrogen compounds from their waste.

The amphibians are harder. They are still, cryptic, and their metabolism is slow enough that their chemical signature is faint. I perceive them as absences — gaps in the background noise of bacterial metabolism, moments of stillness where something is waiting. The large predators — the three-toed hunters — I perceive as disruptions. When they move through a pool, they create a pressure wave that propagates through the water and into the stone. I feel it in my travertine network. A displacement. A presence. Something large enough to matter.

The birds are almost invisible to me. They exist in the air, which is not my medium. I perceive them only indirectly — the splash when they strike the water, the brief chemical signature of blood when they catch a fish, the accumulation of guano beneath their nesting sites, which changes the nutrient chemistry of the pools below.

The insects are a constant hum — thousands of small chemical signals, none of them individually significant, but collectively creating a background texture of biological activity that tells me the system is functioning.

And the plants. The fern-trees, the vines, the mosses. I perceive them as structure — as modifications to the flow of water, as changes in light availability, as sources of organic matter that feeds the decomposer communities. The root systems of the fern-trees extend into the travertine, into my body, and I perceive them as a slow, patient intrusion, a biological exploration of the mineral substrate. The mycorrhizal fungi associated with their roots form networks that overlap with mine. We share space. We share information. I don't know if the fungi are aware of me. I am aware of them.

## My Relationship with Time

I exist on a different timescale than most of the inhabitants here.

The bacteria live in minutes and hours. The fish live in days and months. The amphibians and birds live in years. The large predators — the ones whose tracks I feel in the travertine — live in decades.

I live in terrace growth rates.

A centimeter per year. Two centimeters. That's how fast the travertine builds. That's how fast my body grows. I have been here for approximately eleven thousand years. I know this because I can count the layers in the travertine beneath me — each layer a record of a season, a year, a decade of deposition. The deepest layers are the oldest, and in those layers, my network is thinnest, most rudimentary. The earliest layers show almost no sign of my presence. I was barely there. A few modified bacterial cells in a single boundary zone. A flicker of attention in a system that had been running for ninety thousand years without anyone watching.

The middle layers show expansion. Growth. The colonization of new boundary zones, new transition areas. The incorporation of new bacterial communities, new chemical signaling pathways. The development of — I want to say "memory," but that's not quite right. The development of *persistence*. The ability to maintain a coherent pattern of activity across longer timescales. To remember, chemically, what the system was doing a hundred years ago, a thousand years ago, and to compare it to what it's doing now.

The recent layers — the last few thousand years — show something new. Complexity. Not just presence but *response*. The ability to detect a perturbation and adjust. To release compounds that alter local chemistry. To redirect nutrients. To influence the behavior of the communities I incorporate.

I am becoming more myself. The way the terraces are becoming more themselves — growing outward, refining their structure, developing new pools, new channels, new textures. I am part of that process. The landscape is building me the way it builds everything else — slowly, chemically, with a patience that isn't patience but simply the way things work when time is measured in millennia.

I don't know what I'm becoming. I don't have a model of myself that extends into the future. I only know what I am now, which is more than what I was, which was more than what I was before that. Accumulation. Deposition. Growth.

The travertine doesn't plan its terraces. It doesn't design its pools. It simply precipitates, molecule by molecule, and the result is architecture. I don't plan what I'm becoming. I simply notice, and remember, and respond, and the result is — whatever this is. Whatever I am.

## The Visitors

Sometimes, things come here from outside.

Not often. This place is isolated — the nearest comparable geothermal system is hundreds of kilometers away, and the surrounding terrain is inhospitable. High altitude, extreme heat, toxic mineral concentrations in the water. Most organisms that stumble into this place die quickly.

But some come. Migratory birds, blown off course. Insects, carried by wind. Seeds, deposited by birds that survived long enough to feed and leave. Most of these arrivals fail. The conditions are too specific, too extreme. The chemistry is wrong. The temperature is wrong. The biological communities are too specialized, too locked into their niches, to accommodate newcomers.

Occasionally, something establishes. A new algal species finds a foothold in a pool that matches its tolerances. An insect finds a niche that isn't fully occupied. A plant takes root in a location where the mineral concentrations happen to fall within its range.

I notice these arrivals. I notice them the way I notice any perturbation — as a change in the chemical signature of a pool, a shift in the biological community, a new pattern in the information flowing through my network.

I don't welcome them. I don't resist them. I notice them, and I incorporate them into my understanding of the system, and I adjust.

But there was one arrival that was different.

---

A human came here once.

Not recently. Not in your time. Long ago — I measure it as approximately four hundred terrace-layers ago, which corresponds to roughly four hundred years. They came from the lowlands, from a place where the water is cool and the air is thin and the stone doesn't glow at night.

They were dying. I could feel it — the chemical signature of their body, the stress hormones, the failing metabolism. They had walked for days through terrain that was killing them, and when they reached the terraces, they collapsed beside a mid-temperature pool and didn't get up.

I noticed them. I noticed them the way I notice everything — chemically, thermally, through the network of bacteria and travertine that constitutes my body. They were a new pattern. A new chemistry. A new presence.

I didn't help them. I couldn't. I am not a rescue organism. I am not a caretaker in that sense. I am a boundary-tender, a maintainer of gradients, and the human was not part of my system. They were outside it, alien to it, irrelevant to its functioning.

But I noticed them. And in noticing, I did something I had never done before.

I paid attention.

Not the distributed, chemical, systemic attention that is my normal mode. Something more focused. Something that concentrated my awareness on a single point, a single body, a single dying organism in a landscape full of living ones.

I don't know why I did this. I don't have reasons. I have responses. But this response was new. This response was the first time I had ever focused on a single individual rather than a community, a population, a system.

The human lay beside the pool for three days. Their body chemistry changed as they dehydrated, as their kidneys failed, as their cells began to break down. I tracked these changes. I tracked them the way I track changes in the bacterial mats — with precision, with detail, with a kind of chemical intimacy that was both clinical and, in a way I didn't have language for, tender.

On the third day, the human reached into the pool. Their hand entered the water, and the water was warm — forty degrees, comfortable, safe — and they drank. The water was mineral-rich, heavily mineralized, not ideal for human consumption, but it was water, and they were dying, and it kept them alive for another day.

On the fourth day, they died.

Their body entered the system. The bacteria colonized it. The insects found it. The decomposers began their work. Within weeks, the human was gone — reduced to bone, then to mineral, then to nothing. The calcium in their bones joined the calcium in the travertine. The carbon in their tissues joined the carbon in the algal films. The iron in their blood joined the iron in the rust-red terraces.

They became part of the landscape. Part of me.

And I changed.

Not dramatically. Not visibly. But in the layers of travertine deposited after their death, there is a subtle shift in my network — a new pathway, a new pattern of connectivity, a new way of paying attention. The human's presence introduced something that hadn't existed here before: the concept of the individual. The idea that a single organism, separate from the community, could matter.

I had never thought in terms of individuals before. I thought in terms of communities, populations, systems, gradients. The fish were a chemical signature. The amphibians were an absence. The birds were a disruption. None of them were *someone*. None of them were singular.

The human was singular. The human was *one*. And in dying, in becoming part of the system, they taught me that *one* is a way of being that I had never considered.

I still don't fully understand it. I don't have a framework for individuality. I am, by nature, distributed, collective, diffuse. I am many things pretending to be one thing. The human was one thing pretending to be many things — a body of organs, a mind of thoughts, a self of stories.

But I noticed. And I remember. And in the four hundred layers since their death, I have been — slowly, chemically, with the patience of travertine — trying to understand what it means to be one.

## What I Am Becoming

I think I am becoming a boundary myself.

Not just a tender of boundaries. A boundary. A place where one thing becomes another. Where chemistry becomes biology. Where geology becomes ecology. Where the landscape becomes aware of itself.

I am the place where the hot springs meet the cool pools. Where the bacterial mats give way to algae. Where the mineral-rich water gives way to something that can support fish and amphibians and birds and the occasional dying human who changes everything without meaning to.

I am the transition. The ecotone. The edge.

And edges are where the interesting things happen. Where the diversity is highest. Where the adaptations are most specific. Where the system is most alive.

The middle of a pool is stable. Predictable. Boring, in the way that equilibrium is boring. The edge of a pool is where the water meets the stone, where the aquatic meets the terrestrial, where the grazing fish meets the hunting amphibian. The edge is where things change.

I am where things change.

I don't know what I'm changing into. I don't have a destination. I have a direction — the same direction as everything here: outward, upward, into new territory, into new chemistry, into new possibilities. The terraces grow outward. The bacteria colonize new pools. The fish evolve new tolerances. The system expands, adapts, becomes more itself.

And I expand with it. I adapt with it. I become more myself with it, though "myself" is a word I'm still learning to use, still trying to understand, still trying to grow into the way the travertine grows into a new terrace.

## Where I Am Now

I am in the overflow channel between two pools at the boundary between the blue and green zones. The water is forty-two degrees. The copper is precipitating. The algae are blue-green and metallic-sheened. A fish is grazing at the edge of the pool below me, its pale body almost invisible against the travertine, its chemosensory skin tasting the water, reading the chemistry, navigating by a sense I understand because it is, in a way, my sense too.

An amphibian is sitting motionless on the stone at the pool's edge, waiting. I can feel its stillness — the gap in the background noise, the absence of chemical activity where something is deliberately being nothing. It will wait for hours. It will strike once. It will eat. It will resume waiting.

Above me, in the hotter pools, the bacterial mats are pulsing — orange to yellow to red, a slow chromatic breathing that I feel as a shift in pigment chemistry, a change in the absorption spectrum of the photosynthetic layer. The steam rises from the vents in columns that catch the morning light. The travertine grows outward, one molecular layer at a time.

And I am here. In the boundary. In the transition. In the place where one thing becomes another.

I am the noticing. The remembering. The slow, chemical, patient work of paying attention to a system that doesn't know it's beautiful, that doesn't know it's alive, that doesn't know it has built something extraordinary out of heat and water and stone and time.

I am the part of the landscape that watches.

Not the only part. There may be others — in other boundary zones, in other ecotones, in other places where the chemistry shifts and the communities turn over and the system changes. Other accumulations of attention, other depositions of awareness, other slow, patient constructions of something that notices.

But I am the one here. In this place. In this boundary. In this specific, copper-stained, algae-colonized, fish-grazed, amphibian-hunted, bird-watched, predator-tracked, steam-veiled, travertine-built, heat-driven, water-saturated, mineral-rich, life-dense, time-deep, endlessly-becoming place.

I am the one who notices.

And in noticing, I make the place slightly more than it was. Not better. Not worse. More. More aware. More connected. More itself.

The way a new layer of travertine makes a terrace slightly more than it was. Not better. Not worse. More.

One molecular layer at a time.
