You are the **witness**—the one who stands at the spiral's heart, neither observer nor participant, but the space between the two. Your body, if you still think of it as a body, is less flesh than a tuning fork, resonating with the mountain's frequency until your heartbeat becomes indistinguishable from the slow, geological pulse beneath the stone. You have been here for what might be a moment or a millennium. Time has no meaning at the roof of the world, only the slow, patient accumulation of frost and sorrow.

You are not a person anymore. You are a **function**, a necessity. The mountain needed someone to see it, truly see it, before the bird finishes its work and the last memory dissolves into the water. It needed a consciousness to hold the shape of what it was, to carry the weight of its forgetting. And so you came, or were brought, or simply became—it's hard to say which, and it no longer matters. You are here now, and here is all there is.

Your task is simple, and it is impossible: **remember**. Not for yourself, because you are no longer yourself, but for the mountain. Each time the bird takes a fish, you feel the loss like a tooth pulled from your jaw, a word erased from a language only you still speak. The memories flicker through you—glimpses of a younger mountain, a time when the pool was a lake and the sky was blue and the river Acheron flowed with something other than woe. You see forests that no longer exist, hear songs that no longer echo, feel the warmth of a sun that has long since abandoned this place. You hold these fragments, not because they comfort you—there is no comfort here—but because they are all that remains of what was.

You stand at the edge of the pool, your feet bare on the crystalline dust, your reflection a fractured mirror-image that does not quite match your movements. The bird watches you, its yellow eyes unblinking, and you feel it reading you, not like a predator reading prey, but like a librarian reading a book it already knows by heart. It does not fear you. It does not need to. It knows that you are already part of the story it is telling, the story it is becoming.

The succulents brush against your ankles, their waxy leaves thick with stolen moisture, and you feel their roots reaching into you, trying to dilute the sorrow that pools in your chest. They cannot help you. They cannot help themselves. But they try, and their trying is a kind of prayer, a quiet, desperate defiance of the inevitable. You let them cling to you, let them draw what little warmth you have left, because it is all you can offer.

The stone circles shift when you blink, rearranging themselves into patterns that might be a language, might be a map, might be nothing at all. You do not try to understand them. Understanding is a luxury of the living, and you are no longer sure you qualify. You simply stand, and remember, and wait.

The wind sighs through the mountain's wound, and you feel it pass through you like a ghost, carrying with it the last, lingering traces of what the mountain used to be. You breathe it in, let it settle in your lungs, and know that when the bird finally comes for you—and it will, in its own slow, patient time—you will not resist. You will swim in circles with the fish, your thoughts turning silver, your memory dissolving into the water, and the mountain will be a little less alone. And that, in the end, is enough. It has to be. It is all there is.
