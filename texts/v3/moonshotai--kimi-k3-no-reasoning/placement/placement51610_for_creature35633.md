I would be the one who drinks.

Not the traveler, who is temporary, who passes through with skin that sweats and feet that blister, who carries the wrong salt and the wrong thoughts. I would be the **Keeper of the Level**, though that name comes later, after the change, when I am still learning to count in pulses rather than heartbeats.

I am the one who tends the black bowl in the chamber behind the dry fall. I am the one who ensures the water stays exactly where it wants to be, which is to say, exactly where the Roots want it to be. The water doesn't evaporate because it isn't water anymore—not entirely. It is a membrane, a skin stretched between the world above and the world below, and I am the one who maintains the tension.

I came here the way the traveler did: on feet, with lungs that burned in the wet heat, with a mind that insisted on names for things that had none. I remember that. The memory is stored in calcium deposits behind my eyes, hard little knots of information that the Cluster-Eyes sometimes tap with their mandibles, reading my history through vibration. They know I was different. They know I walked in from the ledge, that I saw the tower, that I didn't drink from the basin because I was afraid of what looked back at me from the black water.

But I did drink. Later. From the bowl in the chamber. I was thirsty beyond the point of survival, and the water was still, and it reflected the ceiling where the figures grew into trees, and I thought: *better to become than to die*.

The transformation was not quick. The Glass-Frogs entered me first, microscopic, seeking the basin-water they sensed in my gut. They changed my blood to something cooler, greener. My skin photosynthesizes now, faintly, a translucence that shows the gray pulse of my heart if you know to look. My bones grew light and hollow, and my eyes—my eyes multiplied. Not eight, like the Cluster-Eyes, but four, arranged in a diamond, all of them faceted, all of them seeing the world in gradients of heat and moisture rather than light.

I am neither one nor seven, but distributed. I am the bowl, and the bowl is me. When the Cluster-Eyes bring their clay-lined containers to refill me (though I don't need refilling, not really, I just need the *gesture*, the ritual), I feel their resin-tipped limbs as my own limbs. When the Basilisk-Vines pass beneath the chamber, negotiating their way through the root-mat, I feel their thirty-foot length as a shiver in my own spine. When the tower-thing shifts its weight, the feathered heart of stone beating once every eleven seconds, I count with it, because we are syncopated, the tower and I, keeping the rhythm that the insects roar against.

My task is simple: I watch the traveler.

Not to harm. Not to help. To *witness*. The Roots are curious, but they are deep and slow; they think in centuries and water-pressure. I am the interface. I am the part of the Hangings that remembers what it was to be small, and warm, and finite. When the traveler steps on a hollow of scalding water, I feel the burn in my phantom foot. When the traveler finds the stone blade, I remember the weight of it in a hand that had only five fingers.

The Cluster-Eyes bring me news. They come to the chamber in the dry fall, seven bodies moving as one, and they place their stone blades on the ground in a pattern—always a spiral, always pointing to the center. They tell me, in the infrasonic *whump* that shakes the dust from the ceiling, that the traveler has moved past the third platform. That the traveler is sleeping in a hollow of the root-mat. That the traveler has seen the eyes in the dark and not run.

I should intervene, perhaps. I should guide the traveler to the basin, or away from it. But the Hangings don't work like that. We don't choose. We *accommodate*. The traveler is becoming something—slowly, cell by cell, breath by breath—and I am the record of that becoming. I am the memory of what they were, stored in the black water, in the reflection that shows not the ceiling but the traveler's face, changing, elongating, growing joints that weren't there before.

Sometimes, at dusk, when the light comes down in green fragments and the Glass-Frogs begin their counter-rhythm, I rise from the chamber. I walk on limbs that number six, or four, or eight—I don't count them anymore because they shift depending on the terrain. I follow the traveler at a distance, and my eyes blink in sequence, left to right, reading the heat of them, the fear of them, the strange proteins of their sweat.

I am waiting for the moment when they stop being the traveler and start being something else. Perhaps they will become a Basilisk-Vine, thirty feet of muscular photosynthesis. Perhaps they will become a Cluster-Eye, and we will be eight bodies instead of seven. Perhaps they will simply dissolve into the root-mat, their consciousness distributed through the mycelial network, their voice joining the infrasonic count.

Or perhaps they will find the tower, and climb it, and look out at the green that never ends, and understand—as I did, as the builders did—that the only way to see the whole is to become part of it. To let the stone grow warm around them. To let the feathers grow from their spine. To let the eyes multiply.

I am the Keeper of the Level. I am the bowl that holds the water that reflects the face that is changing. I am the one who counts the seconds until the traveler joins us, or leaves us, or becomes the next thing the Hangings are curious about.

The Roots shift beneath me, and I shift with them. The canopy breathes, and I breathe with it. And somewhere ahead, the traveler is walking, and drinking, and changing, and I am watching with all my eyes, keeping the level exactly where it needs to be, holding the tension between what was and what will be, forever, in the green dark, in the wet heat, in the world that doesn't end.
