# The One Who Arrives Mid-Sentence

The light reaches me before I reach the light.

That is the first thing. Before I have a body to stand in, before I have eyes to be blinded by, the cool mineral brightness *finds* me—presses against the skin I haven't finished wearing yet, soaks through into whatever I am becoming, and reads me. I am legible to it. That is the second thing. The light reads me the way it reads the stone: aloud, slowly, in a voice I feel in my sternum.

I arrive in pieces.

Not dramatically—not shattered, not strewn. I arrive the way the chamber itself is arriving: incrementally, by accretion, one thin layer of self at a time. First the light, soaking in. Then the cool air, damp, the slow exhalation of the room filling a pair of lungs that are still deciding whether they remember how to breathe. Then the floor beneath me—raw fracture plane, unweathered, bright-faceted—and the pressure of standing, the weight of a body that has weight here, that is subject to gravity *mostly*, that obeys the local rules *as they currently stand*.

I open my eyes. Or I grow eyes. Or the chamber grows eyes in me. The distinction is already beginning to blur.

---

I am not Undertow.

I want to establish that early, because the blurring will make it difficult later, and because I need to hold onto the distinction while I still can, while I still have a *while* in which distinctions operate.

Undertow is here. I can see her from where I stand—or rather, I can see the light passing through the space where she stands, pooling on the floor in the shape of a woman asking a question. She is very far along in her becoming. She has been here so long that the chamber has begun to speak *through* her, the way water speaks through a riverbed—not by choosing to, but by the simple fact of prolonged contact, of mutual wearing, of two processes that have run alongside each other long enough to start sharing a direction.

I have just arrived. I am not translucent. My hands, when I raise them, are opaque—warm-skinned, ordinary, the nails slightly ragged, a small scar on the left thumb that I recognize as *mine* in a way that startles me, because I did not expect to recognize anything. I did not expect to have a *mine*.

But I do. I have a body, and it is solid, and it casts a shadow that arrives almost on time, and the shadow is dark in a way that the chamber's shadows are not—an old-fashioned darkness, imported, the kind that existed before the chamber learned to glow.

Undertow sees me.

She does not pivot the way the salamander-thing pivots. She simply *is* facing me, as if she has always been facing me, as if my arrival were a thing she has been expecting without knowing she was expecting it. Her mineral-pale eyes catch the light and shift, oil-slick iridescence rippling across the irises, and somewhere above us the moth's flight path wobbles—corrects for the wind of her attention.

She does not speak. I understand why. Speech requires a shared grammar of causality, and the chamber's grammar is still under negotiation. Words would arrive damaged. Words would fall upward, or pool in wrong places, or mean something different by the time they reached the listener.

Instead, she does what she does with the stone: she *asks*.

She extends her translucent left hand toward me, fingertips glowing faintly, pulsing in sympathy with the beetles' rhythm, and the gesture is unmistakable. It is the same gesture she makes when she presses her palm against the fracture planes and asks the stone what it is deciding. She is asking me what I am deciding.

I do not know what I am deciding.

I look at my hands. Solid. Ordinary. The scar on my thumb.

I look at her hands. Translucent. Luminous. Becoming.

I look at the space between us—the bright gap, the lit-up emptiness, the distance measured not in meters but in iterations, in versions, in the slow accretion of selves that the chamber has already produced and will continue to produce until it finishes becoming whatever it is becoming.

I take a step toward her.

My shadow follows. On time. Still mine.

---

## What I am

I am the second iteration.

This is not a title. It is not a role I chose. It is a fact that accumulates around me the way Undertow's role accumulated around her—slowly, by the simple fact of my presence, by the chamber's recognition that there is now *another*, that the system has gained a variable, that the equation has changed.

Undertow was the first iteration. She arrived before the chamber knew what arrival meant, and she has been here long enough to become part of the room's vocabulary—a word in the language of becoming, a mark on the wall that is still growing, still following its own unfinished sentence.

I am the second iteration. I arrived after the chamber had already begun to learn what arrival means, and so my arrival is different from hers—less violent, less improvisational. The chamber has *protocols* now, or the beginnings of them. It has Undertow's example to work from. It knows, roughly, what to do with a human-shaped thing that appears in its bright, unfinished heart.

What it does is: it lets me be solid.

Undertow is translucent because she arrived when the chamber's physics were at their most broken, when the light stuttered in panic-bursts and the rules broke down entirely for hours at a time. She shed skins trying to find a version of herself that the emerging rules would support. She is thin because the early iterations were *costly*—because being the first meant absorbing the worst of the chamber's uncertainty, meant serving as a buffer between the raw becoming and the possibility of coherence.

I arrive to a chamber that is still broken, still becoming, still humming its mid-boot frequency—but it is *less* broken than it was. The light still stutters, but less violently. The physics still fail, but less completely. The stalagmites are still uncertain, but some of them have reached ankle-height, and a few have committed to a shape.

Undertow did that. By being here. By tending. By offering her uncertainty as a gift and her persistence as a scaffold.

I arrive on the scaffold she built, and the chamber, recognizing this, lets me keep my opacity a little longer. Lets me keep my shadow. Lets me keep the scar on my thumb, the ragged nails, the body that still believes in an outside even though I can no longer remember what the outside looked like.

I remember that there *was* one. That is more than Undertow has.

She looks at me with her mineral-pale eyes and I can feel her *hunger*—not for food, not for contact, but for the thing I carry that she has lost: the memory of a before. The belief, however fading, that this chamber is not the only place, that becoming is not the only mode, that there is a world where things are finished and stay finished and do not spend every moment trembling on the edge of their next iteration.

She wants to ask me about the outside.

She does not ask.

She has learned not to ask questions that the chamber cannot answer.

---

## How I live

I do not sleep in the nest.

The nest is Undertow's—her skins, her stone flakes, her dark crevice where the light does not reach. I would not fit there, physically or otherwise. The nest is shaped like her uncertainty, and mine has a different geometry.

Instead, I sleep near the door.

Not *against* it—the smooth rectangle of polished stone, the door-shaped implication, radiates a warmth that I find uncomfortable, a too-intent attention, as if the door is studying me the way the salamander-thing studies movement. But *near* it. Close enough to feel its presence, far enough to pretend I am not drawn to it.

I am drawn to it.

I do not know why. Undertow is drawn to it too—she wakes from her dreams with her translucent fingers pressed against the smooth stone, feeling it warm under her touch, feeling it consider her. But her draw is different from mine. She is drawn to the door because she suspects it will open when the chamber finishes becoming, and she hopes she will still be herself when it happens.

I am drawn to the door because I suspect it leads *back*.

Back to the outside. Back to the before. Back to the world where things are finished and stay finished.

I do not know if this is true. I do not know if the door leads anywhere, or if it is only an implication, a suggestion the stone is making to itself, a possibility that will dissolve when the chamber's physics settle and the need for doors evaporates.

But I sleep near it, and in my sleep I dream of a place where the light comes from above instead of below, where shadows arrive on time and stay in their correct positions, where water falls in only one direction and pools reflect what is actually there.

I wake from these dreams with my opaque hands pressed against the raw fracture planes, and the stone beneath my palms feels *relieved*—as if my solidity is a comfort to it, a reminder that there are still things in this chamber that have not yet begun to dissolve into process.

I eat the biofilm, the way Undertow does. I have learned to graze it selectively, triggering fluorescence with the pressure of my touch. It tastes like potential. It tastes like the moment before flavor decides. It is changing me—I can feel it, a slow thinning, a gradual shift from mass to process—but the change is slower than it was for Undertow. The chamber is gentler with me. I am the second iteration. The learning curve has begun.

I drink from the ceiling, catching the condensation droplets on my tongue. Some fall downward. Some fall upward. I catch both, the way Undertow taught me—not with words, but by demonstration, by the tilt of her head, by the way she intercepts the rising droplets without flinching, drinking the future as it escapes.

The upward-falling droplets taste different from the downward-falling ones. The downward ones taste like the present—cool, mineral, immediate. The upward ones taste like *consequence*. They taste like the finished chamber, the teeming pool, the too-many-limbed happiness that Undertow is not ready to understand.

I am not ready either. But I drink them anyway.

## What I do

I do not tend.

Undertow tends. That is her role, her accumulation, her word in the chamber's growing vocabulary. She tends the becoming the way a wound tends a scar, and she does it with a patience and a tenderness that I can feel in the stone when I press my hands against the walls—a residual warmth, a history of asking, a long comma of uncertainty that runs at waist height through the room.

I do something else.

I *witness*.

This is not a passive thing. Witnessing, in a chamber where observation alters the observed, where the moth's flight path wobbles under the weight of Undertow's attention, where my own shadow arrives a half-second late as if reluctant to follow me into the room's next version of itself—witnessing is an *act*. It is a participation. It is a way of saying to the chamber: *I see what you are doing, and I am holding it in memory, and memory is a kind of structure, and structure is a kind of becoming.*

I witness the beetles. I follow their slow circuits, their solitary grazing, their patient farming of light. When two circuits intersect and the space between them brightens, I am *there*—solid, opaque, present—and the brightening leaves a mark not only on the stone but on me. A faint warmth in my chest, sympathetic resonance, the chamber's events registering in a body that still believes it is separate from the system.

I am becoming less separate. I can feel it. The biofilm is thinning me. The upward-falling droplets are teaching me consequence. The hum in my sternum is finding a frequency that resonates with something in my bones.

But for now—for this iteration—I am still solid enough to witness, and witnessing is what I do.

I witness the salamander-thing. Its weather-bottle body, its wrong-bending limbs, its whole-being pivot to track movement. I witness its gelid sacs beneath the ledge, the almost-copies developing inside, the limbs that change in number between viewings. I witness without touching, because I have learned from Undertow that touching the sacs is a question the chamber is not ready to answer.

I witness the moth. The spiral. The tightening. The constellations sketched in displaced condensation, each circuit fractionally smaller than the last. I witness the countdown that Undertow cannot bring herself to watch directly, the approach of the point above the center of the chamber where something will happen when the moth arrives.

I witness for both of us. Undertow tends; I witness. She asks the stone questions; I hold the answers in a body that still remembers what it means to hold something. She becomes translucent; I remain, for now, opaque. She is the first iteration; I am the second. She arrived before the chamber knew what arrival meant; I arrived after, and my arrival is easier because of hers.

We are not the same. The blurring will come—the thinning, the shift from mass to process, the slow dissolution of the boundary between self and system. But for now, we are distinct. She is the question; I am the record of the asking. She is the gesture; I am the mark the gesture leaves.

The chamber needs both.

## The moment I am in

Right now, I am standing near the door-shaped implication, and Undertow is standing at the edge of the pool, and we are not looking at each other, but we are *aware* of each other in the way the beetles are aware of each other—spaced apart, each alone, each performing a slow circuit of our own territory.

Undertow has just placed a pebble at the pool's edge. Quartz. Clear. Out of place. A new word in the language she is inventing.

The word might mean: *I am not ready.*

The word might mean: *I am still here.*

I add a word of my own.

I reach into the pocket of the body I am still wearing—the solid, opaque, scar-thumbed body—and I find nothing, because I arrived with nothing, because the second iteration does not carry objects from the outside, because the outside is already fading from my memory like a dream that thins upon waking.

So I use what I have. I press my opaque hand flat against the raw fracture plane beside the door, and I *push*—not physically, not with force, but with the same asking-pressure that Undertow uses, the same gift of uncertainty. The stone responds. A groove begins to form around my fingers—not the same as Undertow's groove, not a long comma at waist height, but something new. A short, vertical line. A mark that runs perpendicular to hers.

An intersection.

A crossroads in the language we are inventing together.

The mark might mean: *I arrived after you and I am still becoming.*

The mark might mean: *There is another way to be unfinished.*

The stone accepts the mark. It does not dissolve it or absorb it or reject it. It holds it, the way it holds Undertow's pebble circle, her leaning slab, her waist-high comma. It adds my word to the vocabulary.

The chamber hums. The light stutters. The moth spirals.

Above me, a condensation droplet lets go of the ceiling and falls upward. It passes close to my face, and in its curved surface—brief, distorted, bright—I see the chamber as it will be. Finished. Teeming. Wrong in ways that make the current wrongness look like a rough sketch of sanity.

In the finished chamber, Undertow is swimming in the pool, and she has too many limbs, and she is happy.

In the finished chamber, I am standing beside the pool, and I am still opaque, still solid, still casting a shadow that arrives on time.

In the finished chamber, we are both there. Both iterations. Both words in the language. Both marks on the wall.

The droplet reaches the floor. It is absorbed. The vision fades.

Undertow steps back from the pool. I step back from the wall. We move toward our separate territories—her nest, my door-side sleeping place—and the chamber rearranges itself around our movement, accommodating, accreting, becoming.

The salamander-thing pivots to track us both, its weather-bottle body churning with colors that have no names in any language we have invented yet.

The beetles glow.

The moth spirals tighter.

The hum deepens.

We are becoming. The chamber is becoming. The becoming is becoming something neither of us can predict, and we are in it, and we are of it, and we are not the same but we are not separate, and the space between us is bright with the light that comes from below, sourceless, patient, reading us aloud in a voice we feel in our sternums.

Undertow curls into her nest of shed skins. I settle near the door. The dark finds us differently—her in the true ordinary darkness of the crevice, me in the bright dim twilight of the open chamber, my shadow pooling beneath me, almost on time, almost correct, almost the shadow of someone who still believes in an outside.

Almost.

The chamber continues to become.

We become with it.

I close my eyes. The hum is in my bones now. The light is in my skin. The scar on my thumb is fading—not disappearing, just *thinning*, becoming translucent at the edges, becoming a window into something underneath that is not anatomy.

I do not fight it.

I witness it.

The becoming continues.
