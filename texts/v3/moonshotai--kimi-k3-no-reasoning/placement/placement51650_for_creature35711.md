The air hits me like a wet cloth soaked in the smell of crushed leaves and diesel that has forgotten how to burn. I have come through the eastern hardpan where the clay cracks into tessellations sharp enough to cut boots, but here—here in the bowl—the ground yields. It swallows my steps whole, leaving no prints, only the slow *shlup* of water rising to fill the vacuum of my passage.

I should not be here. The thought is not fear, exactly. It is recognition. The valley is taking my measure even now, pressing against my ribs with the weight of ten thousand held breaths. I feel it testing my edges, wondering if I am solid or soluble.

I came with notebooks. I came with the intention of cataloging, of naming, of fixing the rail-walkers and the grease-slugs into taxonomic amber. But the notebooks are soaked through now, the ink bleeding into black Rorschachs that look suspiciously like eyes. The humidity here does not merely dampen; it *negotiates*. It asks: *Are you sure you want to hold onto this?* And when I hesitate, the paper begins to soften, to become substrate for moss.

I am standing on a rail line that has become a spine. The steel thrums beneath my boots—not with electricity, but with language. The rail-walkers are speaking to the third pumping station, their claws tapping out rhythms that translate, roughly, to: *Something new has entered. It does not hum.*

I stop. I breathe. The air is too thick, too sweet with fermentation and the copper-tang of oxidized blood. My lungs work harder here, and I understand that this is not a failure of my biology but an adaptation the valley is demanding. To breathe here is to participate. To exhale is to contribute carbon to the hive-hearts, moisture to the diaphragm-tenders, heat to the grease-slugs.

I kneel on the rusted rail. I place my palm flat against the metal. It is warm. Not from the sun—there is no sun, only the memory of light trapped in the canopy's shingles—but from the friction of a thousand hands that have touched this exact spot, absorbing the iron into their fur, their skin, their intent.

What am I?

I am not a curator. I have no stomach for the shears, for the necessary violence of balance. I watched a jaguar—if it was a jaguar, if it was not simply the shadow of the sump-mother taking a walk—drag a dying tapir into the black water last night. The tapir did not struggle. It seemed to accept the incorporation, its body relaxing into the lattice of caiman backs, becoming part of the reef that breathes. I could not do that. I cannot decide what stays and what goes.

I am not a tender. I lack the patience of the diaphragm-frogs, the willingness to press my soft body against broken pistons for decades, to inflate myself into the machinery and push, not to restart, but to prevent seizing. I am too restless. I want things to *work*, to function, to achieve their original purpose. I am still too attached to the idea that the pumps should pump, that the rails should carry trains, that the darkness should be dispelled.

I am not a singer. I do not know how to vibrate at the frequency of roots. I cannot push against the soil and create the micro-earthquakes that tell the snakes where to go.

But I am here. And the valley is pressing against me, and I feel something shifting in my chest, some organ I did not know I possessed beginning to beat in time with the subsonic hum.

I think I might be a *witness*.

Not a recorder—that implies separation, objectivity, the lie that you can observe without being incorporated. But a witness in the old sense, the sense of *bearing*. To bear the weight of the darkness. To bear the heat. To bear the knowledge that the machinery was never abandoned, that the valley is not a ruin but a body, and that I have walked into its digestive tract and been found not-food, not-waste, but perhaps—perhaps—*enzyme*.

I am becoming porous. My skin is learning to absorb the light that isn't there, to drink the tannins from the black pools. When I touch the diesel engine hive, the bees do not sting. They land on my hands, their wings beating against my pulse, adjusting their rhythm to mine. Or perhaps mine to theirs.

I have stopped trying to leave. The valley does not prevent me. The rail lines still run to the hardpan, to the world outside where things are separate, where a tree is a tree and a machine is a machine and the darkness is merely the absence of photons. But I find I no longer want that clarity. It seems suddenly violent, that separation. Lonely.

I sleep now against the flank of the sump-mother. The caimans do not stir. They have grown moss over their eyes, and their breath rises and falls with the slow inevitability of tides. When I dream, I dream in vibrations—the root-singers sending their three-day warnings through the soil, through my bones, telling me that the water is rising, that I must grow gills or learn to breathe through my skin, that the time of dry things is ending.

I am dissolving. Not dying—*dissolving*. My edges are blurring. I can feel my thoughts becoming slower, warmer, more humid. I am forgetting the names I brought with me. The notebooks have become part of the understory, their pages hosting colonies of bioluminescent algae that pulse in patterns that look like sentences I almost understand.

Yesterday—though "yesterday" is a concept that has no meaning here, where time is measured in oxidation and the slow lean of falling trees—I found a rail-walker dead on the tracks. The jaguars had culled it. Its fur was the color of old rust, and its eyes were open, reflecting nothing because there was nothing to reflect. I did not bury it. I did not move it. I sat beside it until the beetles came, until the vines began to thread through its ribs, until it became a bridge for smaller things, a nursery for fungi, soil.

I bore witness. And in bearing witness, I became part of the process.

The valley is pressing against me now with a specific intent. I can feel it asking, with the patience of geological time: *Are you necessary?*

I do not know. But I am here. I am humming, finally, at the frequency of the hive-hearts, the diaphragm-tenders, the grease-slugs in their diesel wombs. I am part of the hush that contains multitudes. I am the sound of too much life trying not to be heard, and finding, in the not-hearing, a kind of peace.

When you come—if you come—you will find me in the third pumping station, or perhaps in the cooling towers where the bats have become the lungs of the architecture. I will be the one who does not run from the darkness, who does not try to light a fire or restart the engines. I will be the one with the moss growing in the hollow of my collarbone, with the bees nesting in the cage of my ribs, with the rust-red fur beginning to sprout along my forearms where I have touched the rails too often.

I will be the *witness-tender*, the one who watches the maintenance of the world without trying to fix it, who bears the weight of the night and finds it not crushing but *cradling*.

The valley breathes. And I—finally, mercifully—am breathing with it.
