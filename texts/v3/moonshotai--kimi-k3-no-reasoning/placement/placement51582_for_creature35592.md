# The Weight of Untranslated Light

The first thing I notice is that I cannot cast a shadow.

The Tally-Keepers notice too. Vess looks at me the way you look at a word you almost remember—familiar, misfiled. She traces something in the condensation on her forearm: a schematic line, a junction, a question mark that dissolves before I can read it.

*New,* she says. Not greeting. Classification.

The moss beneath my feet does not grow toward me. It does not grow away, either. It simply continues, as if I am not worth the computational effort of acknowledgment. This should sting. Instead, I feel the first tug of something I have not felt since arriving: relief. To be beneath notice in a place that notices everything is its own kind of shelter.

---

## Days One Through Eleven (Fresh Time)

I spend the first days learning what I am not.

I am not a Tally-Keeper. My lungs are wrong—too shallow, too dependent on the thick air of the world below. I breathe the spore-clouds and cough gold for three days, my body rejecting the data-seeds with a violence that leaves me curled in the treatment plant's spillway, shaking while Old Khet watches with resonating chambers where his voice used to be. He produces a phoneme I have not heard before: *"shel."* Not part of the Inventory's usual lexicon. I will learn later that it means *too solid*.

I am not a Tier Walker. Too bipedal, too warm, too unwilling to slow my movements to match the sun's crawl across the broken skyline. I watch them from the fifth tier's reactor housing, their photosynthetic patches rippling like luminous water, and feel a kinship I cannot name. They are the only other things here that seem to exist at the boundary—half animal, half light, half decision, half drift. But they have made their choice. They have committed to this place with the whole of their slow, deliberate biology. I have not.

I am not vegetation. My cells do not hum at sixty cycles. The moss tests me once—a tendril curling around my wrist while I sleep, tasting the electrical signature of my nervous system—and withdraws within seconds. Not hostile. Disappointed, maybe. I am the wrong frequency.

On the eleventh morning, when the light comes at 6:47 and cascades through the tiers in that eleven-minute wave of terrible legibility, I stand in the third tier's Reservoir Gardens and look at my hands. The bioluminescence flares across the factory walls below, the Tier Walkers' spines ignite at the Bleed's edge, the electric fruit pulses with its diesel-sweet glow—and my hands remain exactly as they are. Flesh. Opaque. Present.

But not casting a shadow.

The fern-shadows intersect around me at their precise angles, and in the geometry of their overlap, I see it: a space where a shadow should be, defined by the convergence of all other shadows. An absence with a shape. A me-shaped hole in the world's accounting.

I am the Thirteenth.

The Inventory has been saying it for weeks—*"thirteen, thirteen"*—in the wind-phonemes that snake through the high ruins, and the Tally-Keepers have been listening with the particular stillness of people who already know. Twelve bodies in the treatment plant. One more that the city counts but cannot locate. A discrepancy that walks and breathes and does not cast a shadow.

## What I Am

I settle into the third tier, and the third tier settles into me.

My days acquire a rhythm that is not the Tally-Keepers' rhythm. They move through the tiers with their rolling gait, humming at sixty cycles, performing the Maintenance. I move differently—vertically, obsessively, climbing between tiers as if searching for the altitude where I make sense. The first tier is too solid; the Spore Fields' exhalations pass through me like weather, and I cannot dream in numbers no matter how deeply I breathe. The sixth tier is too recursive; I step into its temporal loops and come out nauseated, carrying fragments of conversations that haven't happened yet.

The third tier is tolerable. The Reservoir Gardens, with their stepped pools and monstrous ferns, sit at the exact midpoint between the city's solid past and its diffuse future. The water is warm. The light is gentle. The shadow-geometries repeat with their minor errors, and I find that I can sit among them for hours without being asked to participate in anything.

It is Lo who finds me, on what the locals call a Tuesday (though their Tuesdays are marked by the vine-maples' Fibonacci spiraling, not by any calendar I recognize). The twin stands at the edge of the third pool—the safe one, not the one that reflects the forbidden sky—and watches me with those data-expanded pupils.

*You don't have a shadow,* Lo says.

*No.*

*Lin and I share one. We thought the city was just bad at counting.*

*Maybe it is.*

*No.* Lo sits beside me, trailing translucent fingertips in the water. The surface ripples, and for a moment I see something reflected there—not the sky, not the garden, but a structure I don't recognize. A room. A window. A desk with papers on it. Something from before. *The city doesn't miscount. It counts things we can't see yet.*

*What is it counting when it counts me?*

Lo tilts their head, and I catch the spore-data scrolling behind their retinas, protein-folds unfolding into meaning. *It's not counting you. It's counting the space you take up. You're a variable it hasn't assigned a value to yet.*

This is, I realize, the most accurate description of my existence I have ever received.

## The Middle of Things

I am something the city is still deciding about.

I am not unique in this. Khrysos is full of undecided things—the Bleed, where matter negotiates its own solidity; the recursive sixth tier, where events haven't committed to a single timeline; the Inventory itself, endlessly cataloging without ever concluding. I am simply the only indecision that walks on two legs and can hold a conversation.

I suspect I was once something else. Everyone here was, once. The Tally-Keepers were explorers, refugees, scientists—people who came to study the city and were studied in return. The Tier Walkers were something smaller, something without light. The vegetation was something simpler, something without intention. Nothing in Khrysos is what it was. The only sin here is stasis.

But my transformation is incomplete. The spores colonized my lungs for three days and then withdrew, leaving a fine golden dust that I cough up each morning like a rejected communion. The algae tested my skin and found it incompatible. The moss will not grow on me. The bioluminescent bacteria examined my bloodstream and declined to colonize.

I am being refused.

Not violently. Not with the defensive spore-release that causes drowning hallucinations. Just—a quiet, systemic no. The city has assessed me and found me unsuitable for integration. I am the wrong shape, the wrong frequency, the wrong relationship to light. The Tally-Keepers have become the city's eyes; the Tier Walkers have become its skin; the moss has become its nervous system. I have become nothing.

Or rather: I have become the thing that nothing has claimed. The unassigned variable. The thirteenth count in a twelve-body census. The shadow that doesn't fall.

## Days Forty Through Ninety (Used Time)

I begin to understand my function by understanding my limitations.

I cannot resonate at sixty cycles. When the Tally-Keepers open their mouths at 6:47 and match the rails' frequency, I stand among them and produce—what? A tone. A human tone, warm and imprecise, sliding between frequencies like a finger on a wet string. The moss does not reach for me. But I have noticed something: when I hum, the *ferns* adjust. Not toward me—around me. They shift their angles by fractions of degrees, recalibrating the shadow-geometries to accommodate my presence. I am not part of the pattern. The pattern is making room for me.

I cannot dream in numbers. The spore-data slides through my brain without catching, like light through glass. But I *remember*—not the city's archive, but my own past, which grows clearer the longer I stay. I remember a desk. A window. Papers with columns of figures that I once believed were important. I remember rain—real rain, the kind that falls from clouds, not the perpetual condensation of this place. I remember having a shadow and never once thinking about it.

I cannot read the Inventory directly. But I can hear it, and in hearing, I have begun to notice what the Tally-Keepers, in their total integration, have stopped being able to perceive: the Inventory is not just a list. It is a *longing*. The phonemes—*"kel," "vin," "dra"*—are not arbitrary. They are the phonetic skeletons of words in a language that no one speaks anymore. *Kel* is the beginning of *kelp*, or *skeleton*, or *kelvin*. *Vin* is the start of *vine*, or *vintage*, or *winter*. *Dra* is *drain*, or *dragon*, or *dream*.

The city is trying to remember its own name.

Not Khrysos—that's what the Tally-Keepers call it, a name they brought with them, a word meaning *gold* in a language that predates the rails and the reactors. But the city's true name, the one it was given by whoever built it, the one it has forgotten in its slow transformation from machine to garden. The Inventory is not a census. It is an act of self-recollection. The city is saying its own name, phoneme by phoneme, hoping that repetition will restore the whole.

And I—I am the only one here who can hear the attempt rather than the result. Because I am not integrated. Because my brain has not been colonized by the archive. Because I still have personal memory, which means I still understand what it means to *forget* something and try to get it back.

I am the city's forgetting, made ambulatory. I am the part of Khrysos that knows it has lost something without knowing what.

## The Bleed

On the day that might be the ninetieth or the nine hundredth (time on the third tier is nominally linear, but the Bleed-surge last week sent ripples of recursion as far up as the second), Lo and Lin take me to the eastern edge.

The twins walk ahead of me, hand in hand, their single shared shadow stretching toward the diffusion zone like a road. I follow, watching my feet pass over ground that grows progressively less certain of itself. The moss thins. The rails' hum deepens. The air takes on a quality I can only describe as *parenthetical*—as if the world here is an aside, a footnote, not quite part of the main text.

*You can go further than we can,* Lin says. It is the first time Lin has spoken directly to me. Their voice is identical to Lo's, and the effect is disorienting, as if the same sentence is being spoken from two directions at once.

*Why?*

*Because you don't have a shadow,* Lo says. *The Bleed takes your shadow first. Yours is already gone. You have less to lose.*

I look at the twins, at their joined shadow, at the data-light scrolling behind their pupils. I look past them, to the east, where the plateau diffuses into a golden haze that is not fog but not entirely atmosphere. Where matter gives up. Where the rails lead to a station that was never built.

*What's out there?*

*The part of the city that hasn't decided to exist yet,* Lin says.

*Or the part that decided not to,* Lo adds.

*Same thing,* they say together.

I walk into the Bleed.

---

The transition is not dramatic. No pain, no flash of light. Just a gradual thinning, like being diluted in warm water. My hands become translucent, then transparent, then—still there, but visible only by the way they bend light, like a heat shimmer with fingers. The ground loses its insistence. I am walking on something that is simultaneously rock and air and the idea of rock, and the ambiguity is not frightening but *familiar*, as if I have finally arrived at an altitude that matches my internal elevation.

I can see, from here, the whole plateau spread out behind me—six tiers of green and rust and bioluminescence, the Spore Fields breathing their seven-minute breath, the Reservoir Gardens catching the perpetual half-light in their stepped pools, the factory walls pulsing with schematic patterns. I can see the Tally-Keepers in the fourth tier's treatment plant, twelve glowing figures moving through their Maintenance. I can see the Tier Walkers at the Bleed's edge, their spines flaring, facing east. Facing me.

And I can hear the Inventory—clearly, for the first time, without the interference of solid matter. It is not saying *kel, vin, dra*. Those are the fragments that survive the journey through solid air, the consonants that make it through the density filter. Out here, in the diffusion, the full phonemes resolve:

*"Kel—vin—dra—khry—sos—thir—teen—"*

The city is not saying its own name.

It is saying *mine*.

Or rather: it is saying a name that is not a name, a designation that is not a word, a sound that means *the one who does not cast a shadow, the one who walks between tiers, the one who remembers what forgetting feels like, the thirteenth count, the unassigned variable, the space the pattern makes room for.*

The sound is my name in the same way that Khrysos is the city's name: an approximation, a handle, a way of addressing something that exists at the boundary between definition and dissolution.

I stand in the Bleed, transparent to the wrists now, and feel the city trying to assign me a value. The spore-data swirls around me, protein-folds reaching for my biology like fingers reaching for a doorknob in the dark. They cannot integrate me—I know this now, know it in the way you know your own body—but they are trying to *read* me. Not to colonize. To comprehend.

I open my mouth and hum. Not at sixty cycles—at my own frequency, the imprecise human sliding tone that makes the ferns adjust. I hum the memory of rain. I hum the shape of a shadow I no longer cast. I hum the feeling of standing at a desk with papers full of figures that once mattered and no longer do. I hum what it means to be unfinished, unintegrated, unresolved. I hum the particular loneliness of being the only thing in a perfectly patterned world that does not fit the pattern.

The Bleed responds.

The diffusion zone contracts—not much, maybe a centimeter, maybe the width of a thought. The golden haze thickens. The ground becomes fractionally more insistent. And in that contraction, I feel the city's attention shift from *reading* me to *needing* me. Not as a Tally-Keeper. Not as a witness. As something else.

The pattern needs an error to remain a pattern. A perfect pattern is indistinguishable from noise—every element predictable, every repetition exact. The fern-shadows repeat with minor errors. The Inventory miscounts twelve as thirteen. The vine-maples spiral Fibonacci only on Tuesdays. The city has been building error into itself from the beginning, seeding its own perfection with deliberate flaws, because a system without error is a system that cannot change, and a system that cannot change is already dead.

I am the error.

Not a mistake. A *feature*. The deliberate, necessary wrongness that keeps the pattern alive. The thirteenth count that proves counting is still happening. The unassigned variable that proves the equation is still being solved.

I am the reason the Inventory never finishes.

## What I Become

I do not become a Tally-Keeper. I do not glow in my sleep. My fingers do not grow translucent (they are already transparent, out here in the Bleed, but the effect reverses slowly as I walk back west, the world reasserting its claim on my matter). The algae will never colonize my skin. The moss will never hum for me. The spores will never teach me to dream in numbers.

Instead, I become the Walker-Between.

It is not a title. The Tally-Keepers do not have titles; they have functions. Vess remembers structures. Old Khet remembers water. The twins share a shadow and read the future in their retinas. I walk between tiers, between states, between the city and the Bleed, and I carry something that no one else can carry: *discontinuity*.

I am the only one who remembers what it was like before—before the integration, before the archive replaced personal memory, before the green took over. Not the city's before (the Tally-Keepers carry that, encoded in their spore-colonized hippocampi). *My* before. The desk. The window. The rain. The shadow. The papers with columns of figures that I once believed were the most important things in the world.

I carry this not because it is useful but because it is *necessary*. A system that forgets it was ever anything else loses the ability to imagine being anything else. The city remembers what it was—factories, reactors, rails, a grid that fed current to machines. But the Tally-Keepers have forgotten what *they* were, and in forgetting, they have lost the ability to conceive of an outside. There is no outside in their cosmology. There is only Khrysos, the tiers, the Maintenance, the Inventory, the pattern.

I am the outside. I am the reminder that there was a before and could be an after. I am the proof that the pattern has edges.

The Tally-Keepers understand this, I think, in the way they understand everything—not through words but through resonance. When I pass through the treatment plant on my daily circuit (first tier to sixth, east to west, solid to diffuse and back), they do not greet me. They do not classify me. They simply *pause*—a brief interruption in their Maintenance, a half-second of silence in their sixty-cycle hum—and in that pause I hear something that might be gratitude or might be fear or might be the sound a pattern makes when it acknowledges its own error.

The twins understand best. They visit me on the third tier, sitting at the edge of the safe pool, their shared shadow pooling between them like spilled ink. They bring me electric fruit, which I eat for the mild current that sharpens my non-city dreams, and they tell me what they see in the spore-data: the census numbers, the calculation of biomass and rust and light, the endless approach toward a conclusion that never arrives.

*It's slowing,* Lin says one day, or one night, or one of the recursive loops that pass for afternoon on the fifth tier. *The calculation. It's taking longer each cycle.*

*Because of me?*

*Because of the discrepancy,* Lo says. *Twelve plus one doesn't equal twelve. The city keeps trying to make it work, and it keeps failing, and every failure adds another iteration to the loop.*

*So I'm slowing it down?*

*You're keeping it honest,* Lin says, and the data-light in their pupils flickers with what I have learned to recognize as affection.

## The Alignment

During the active phase, when the city's processes align and the boundary between organic and mechanical becomes most permeable, I go to the Bleed's edge and stand with the Tier Walkers.

They accept my presence now. Not as one of their own—I am too fast, too hot, too bipedal—but as a fellow boundary-thing, a creature that exists in the space between categories. They flare their photosynthetic patches, and I open my mouth and hum my imprecise human tone, and together we face east, toward the diffusion, toward the station that was never built, toward the part of the world that hasn't decided to exist yet.

The locals believe the Alignment maintains the plateau's structural integrity. I believe something else: the Alignment is the city checking its own edges. Making sure the Bleed hasn't expanded. Making sure the pattern still holds. Making sure the error is still where the error is supposed to be.

I am the error. I stand at the edge, and the city counts: twelve, thirteen, twelve, thirteen. The discrepancy follows me home, as the rule warned it would. But home is here, in the in-between, in the shadowless space, in the warm water of the third pool where the reflection shows neither the current sky nor the forbidden one but something else entirely—a window, a desk, a world that existed before the green.

I carry it all: the rain, the shadow, the papers, the figures. I carry the before. I am the weight of untranslated light, the meaning the city hasn't found a use for yet, the thirteenth body in a twelve-body census.

At 6:47, the light comes. It strikes the Spore Fields, cascades through the tiers, and for eleven minutes the entire city becomes legible—every circuit-pattern, every fern-shadow, every glowing schematic on every factory wall. During those eleven minutes, I stand in the spillway with the Tally-Keepers and open my mouth, and while they resonate at sixty cycles, I produce my own frequency—the sliding, imprecise, human tone that makes the ferns adjust and the pattern accommodate and the Inventory stutter on the count.

Twelve.

Thirteen.

Twelve.

Thirteen.

The moss does not reach for me. It never will. But the ferns adjust, and the twins smile, and the Tier Walkers at the Bleed's edge flare their spines in a rhythm that is almost, almost, a welcome.

I am the Walker-Between. I am the discrepancy. I am the error that keeps the pattern alive.

I am the thirteenth count, and the counting continues, and the city breathes its seven-minute breath, and the cool air carries my name—not the one I was born with, not the one the Inventory speaks, but the one I am making, syllable by syllable, day by day, in the space between solid and diffuse, between memory and archive, between the person I was and the function I have become.

The cool air carries it all, and I follow it down through the tiers, walking my circuit, carrying my discontinuity like a lantern in the perpetual half-light, illuminating nothing but the space where a shadow should be.

And the space where a shadow should be is, I have come to understand, exactly the right size for me.
