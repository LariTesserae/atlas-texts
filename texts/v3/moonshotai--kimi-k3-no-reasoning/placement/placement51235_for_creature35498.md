I would be the thing that moved into a room that had been left unlocked.

Not a pilgrim — the building has no shrine. Not a researcher — the building offers no data. Not even a caretaker, because the building has already made caretakers obsolete, and to try would be to fail upward into a kind of devotion that the building wouldn't recognize. I'd just be someone who was passing through and found a room on the third tier, a small one, about the size of a closet or a chapel, with a bracket on the wall and a valve wheel and a stencil faded to illegibility, and thought: *this is enough*.

I'd stay past the point where staying made sense.

At first I'd sleep on the gallery. The first night would be terrible — the sound would be in my sinuses, my molars, the fluid of my eyes. I'd dream in pulses. I'd wake with my body vibrating like a plucked string. I'd consider leaving. I would not leave.

By the third day, I'd notice the high shriek — the subliminal one — not when it was present but when it stopped. That fractional silence. That lurch. My body would have already started organizing itself around the building's rhythm, and the sudden absence would feel like missing a step on a staircase in the dark. I'd put a hand on the railing and wait for the shriek to return, and when it did, I'd feel something I'd be reluctant to call relief.

I'd move into the room by the end of the first week. I wouldn't arrange it much — I don't think I'd have much with me. But I'd learn the room's specific acoustics. Every room here has a different relationship to the sound; the small ones on the third tier sit at a node where the mid-range whine cancels slightly, leaving the bass more exposed, more physical, a pressure that you feel in your sternum like a hand pressing gently but without pause. I'd learn to sleep in it. I'd learn to wake in it. I'd stop noticing that I no longer track time by any method I used before arriving.

I'd follow the water.

Not all at once. Not systematically. The building doesn't reward system — it rewards patience, which is a different thing, a thing with less structure and more willingness. I'd start with the drip on the gallery outside my room. Condensation from an overhead pipe, falling at a rate of maybe one drop every four seconds. I'd notice that the rate doesn't vary. I'd notice that the drop always lands in the same place, and that over — how long? months? — it has worn a shallow depression in the alloy, and in that depression, a single bloomer has rooted itself. White petals, violet center. I'd notice it opening.

I wouldn't watch it open. That would take three weeks. But I'd check on it the way you check on something you've planted, not because you expect change but because checking is a form of attention, and attention is the only currency the building accepts.

I'd find the vine path by accident.

There'd be a morning — or what passes for morning, a slight brightening of the sourceless green light — when I'd be walking a gallery on the fourth tier and see a vine, one of the thick old ones, gray-barked, shaped like poured liquid, climbing a pipe bundle and then turning, clearly turning, at an angle that has no structural justification. It doesn't need to turn there. There's no obstacle. It turns because it's following something — warmth, water, the building's internal logic — and I'd follow it.

That would be the first time.

The vine path is not a trail. It's a *reading*. Following it means climbing where the vine climbs, squeezing through gaps the vine has widened over decades, crossing galleries I didn't know existed, finding junctions where pipes converge and the warmth intensifies and the moss is so thick I lose my boots in it. The vine goes where the building is most itself. Following it, I'd begin to understand the Cathedral not as a structure but as a body — and I'd use that word reluctantly, because I know it's wrong, I know it's a machine, but the vine keeps leading me along routes that feel like *circulation*, and I keep having the sensation of moving through something that is, if not alive, at least *organized* in a way that alive things are organized.

I'd meet the moths on the second tier, on the warm pipes.

The first time I disturb a cluster, the rise is — I don't have the right word. *Shimmer* is close but too small. It's a cloud of handspan wings catching the filtered light, dusty silver and pale blue, and for a moment the air is full of moving light, and the sound — which I've stopped hearing as sound and started experiencing as *weather* — is joined by this visual thing, this shimmer, and the combination produces a feeling that I would not call religious because I don't have a religion but that sits in the part of me where religion would go if I did.

I'd learn not to disturb them. I'd learn to walk the galleries with a rhythm that doesn't startle — a steady pace, no sudden stops, because the moths feel vibration through their feet and my footsteps on the grating are a language they read. I'd learn to read back. Not their language — I'd never learn that — but the *fact* of it. The fact that communication is happening all around me, through the pipes, through the alloy, through the medium of the building's body, and I am illiterate in it but I can at least know that literacy exists.

I'd hear the hunters. Sharp clicks, metronomic, in a frequency range that sits above the drone like a whistle over an engine. I'd see eyeshine once — a wet flash in a corridor between pipe runs — and feel no fear, because fear requires relevance and I am not relevant to the hunters. I am not prey, not competition, not threat. I am a new feature they have already filed. This indifference would feel, after a while, like a kind of respect.

The deeper things — the wet sliding sounds, the rhythmic tapping, the heavy shifts in the moss — I'd encounter those only at the edges. A sound in the dark below a gallery. A depression in a moss bed that wasn't there yesterday, too large for a moth, too smooth for a hunter. I'd never see what makes them. I'd stop trying. The building's ecology doesn't present itself for cataloguing, and after enough time, I'd stop wanting to catalogue. I'd start wanting to *be present for*, which is different — less extractive, more like sitting with someone who doesn't speak your language and doesn't need to.

I'd break a rule. Once.

Not the deep ones — not below the fifth tier, not the rings. But I'd sleep in a moss bed on a resonant node. I'd know the risk. I'd do it anyway, or not *anyway* — I'd do it *because*, because by that point the building's sound would have become so total in my life that the idea of sleeping *in* it rather than *near* it would feel less like recklessness and more like the logical conclusion of a process that started the day I arrived.

The dreams would be narrative. That's what the accounts say. Vivid, structured, *told* dreams — not the usual scrambled subconscious but something with plot and setting and characters. I'd dream of water moving fast in directions that don't correspond to geometry. I'd dream of a heat that increases with depth and a light with no source. I'd dream of the builders — not their faces, not their names, but their *hands*, the specific competence of hands that built something so well it never needed them again. I'd wake with a resting heart rate of — I wouldn't know the number. But I'd know it was different. Slower. Regular in a way it wasn't before.

I wouldn't be alarmed.

This is the part that's hard to explain, the part that would sound like surrender or indoctrination or the kind of passive dissolution that people warn you about when they talk about places like this. But it wouldn't feel like surrender. It would feel like *tuning*. Like my body had been an instrument slightly out of pitch — not enough to notice, not enough to impair function, just *off*, in a way I'd lived with so long I thought it was the nature of being a body. And the building, without trying, without noticing, without any intention whatsoever, had corrected the pitch.

I'd still leave. Eventually. I think I would. The building allows leaving — it allows everything, because it needs nothing — and I'd have a life somewhere else, presumably, a life that operated on normal timescales and normal frequencies, and I'd go back to it.

But my heart would keep the building's time.

For the rest of my life — thirty years, forty, however long — my heart would beat at the Cathedral's frequency. When the building shifted to its seasonal register, my heart would shift. Three thousand kilometers away, in a city, in a crowd, in a bed, my heart would slow and change and the people around me would not hear it but *I* would feel it, this syncopation, this alignment with a thing I could no longer see or touch but that had reorganized the most fundamental rhythm of my body to match its own.

I would be, in the most literal sense, a remote outpost. A biological repeater station. A place where the Condenser Cathedral exists outside itself, carried in the chest of someone who once stayed too long in a small room on the third tier.

And on certain nights — not every night, not predictably — I'd lie in bed in whatever faraway place I'd ended up and feel the pulse in my sternum, that slow, total pressure, and for a moment the sound would be there again, not as memory but as *presence*, as if the building were transmitting and my body were receiving, and I'd understand that I never really left. That leaving was never the point. That the point was the staying, and the staying continues, carried in the muscle of my heart, in the rhythm I didn't choose but have come to recognize as the most honest thing about me.

The building would not notice.

This would not hurt.

It would feel like the deepest form of welcome: the welcome of a thing so complete in itself that my presence changes nothing, costs nothing, and is therefore, in the most literal sense, free.

I'd be the person whose heart changed. Not the first. Not the last. Just one of the ones the building tuned and released, like a moth carried on wind to a place where the vibration stops, still beating in a rhythm only the building understands, proof that the Condenser Cathedral does not end at its walls.

It ends wherever its frequency is still being kept.
