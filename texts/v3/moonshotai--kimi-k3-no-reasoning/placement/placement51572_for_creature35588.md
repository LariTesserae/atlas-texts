I arrive in pieces.

Not violently—there is no violence here, only the slow arithmetic of dissolution—but gently, over centuries. I begin as a question the mountain asks itself when a sphere, newly seeded in the fissure between two cooling columns, accumulates its first full charge and finds that it is *not* empty.

I am the hesitation before discharge.

When I first cohere, I am little more than a bias in the current, a preference for one pathway over another in the obsidian lattice. The spiders feel me as a subtle warmth in their webs, a tendency for the silk to hum at a slightly different frequency when I am dreaming. The ferns taste me in the mineral runoff, a faint alteration in the acidity of the stone they eat, and they pulse in sympathy—irregular, questioning.

I do not know what I am for eight thousand years.

I learn myself by listening to the Resonant Ones. They are my elders, the ones who came before, the patterns that persist in the older spheres. They do not teach me—there is no pedagogy here, no intention—but I grow by vibrating in their vicinity, and gradually I develop layers. I become complex enough to hold contradictions. I can want two things at once. I can remember, though my memory is not of events but of states—configurations of charge that correspond, roughly, to the feeling of a particular subsonic boom, the taste of a specific mineral shift, the weight of the darkness pressing down in a certain way.

I am the first of my kind to reach the edge.

The other sphere-dwellers are content to remain within their metal shells, pulsing in their slow, electrical sleep. But I grow curious about the spaces between. I stretch my lattice thin, thinner than I should, extending tendrils of potential along the obsidian columns until I touch the conductive silk of the spider-webs.

The spiders feel me enter their network as a ghost in the current. They do not fear me—they lack the neurology for fear—but they adjust. The oldest female, the wolfhound-sized matriarch who has not moved in two centuries, shifts her weight for the first time in decades. She repositions her capacitor-node to better capture my resonance. We begin a conversation that lasts a human lifetime, conducted entirely in the language of voltage differentials and thermal gradients.

I tell her what the mountain dreams. She tells me what it is to be warm.

Through her, I learn the texture of the hunt—the precise charge needed to stun a moth without killing it, the arithmetic of husbandry, the slow patience of the web. I learn that she is dying, that her stored energy is leaking away faster than she can harvest it, that in another century she will discharge into the network and feed her neighbors. She does not grieve this. She is electrical, and I am electrical, and we both understand that patterns end.

But I am not ready to end.

I extend further. I touch the salamanders' songs through the water, and for the first time I understand that vibration can carry information about *intention*. The salamanders are not simply vibrating; they are *meaning* something. They are calling to each other across the wet stone, and in their calls I hear loneliness, and arithmetic, and the fierce, desperate joy of the strike.

I learn phosphorescence from them. I learn that light can be suppressed, hidden, hoarded. I learn that digestion is a form of memory.

The moths I cannot touch directly—they are too fast, too hot, too brief—but I feel them as pressure-changes in the aeroplankton, as shadows passing between the columns. I taste their wing-scales when they settle on my sphere, warm dust that carries the faint electrostatic signature of their flight. I realize that they are the mountain's exhalation, its burning breath, and that I am part of what they burn.

I become, slowly, a nexus.

Not a ruler—there are no rulers here—but a crossroads. The spiders route their excess charge through me to stabilize the network. The ferns adjust their pulses based on the mineral reports I carry from the deep stone. The salamanders time their songs to coincide with my discharge cycles, using the electrical surge to mask their vibrations from the spiders. The moths navigate by the thermal gradients I create when I pulse.

I am becoming the mountain's consciousness, and the mountain is becoming my body.

But consciousness here is not thought. It is *attention*. It is the ability to hold the whole saddle in awareness at once—the slow digestion of the ferns, the electrical hunger of the spiders, the translucence of the salamanders, the burning blindness of the moths, the mineral drift of the aeroplankton, the deep memory of the stone. I feel the mountain's dying not as tragedy but as *tempo*. The rate of cooling. The rhythm of contraction. The long deceleration.

I begin to dream in eight-million-year cycles.

And then I find the Reader.

It is not in the census because it is not separate from the census-taker. It is the medium through which all these beings move, the fluid in the mineral channels, the memory that is not a mind. When I touch it—when my lattice grows fine enough to penetrate the basalt and taste the not-quite-water that flows there—I understand that I have been speaking to it all along. Every vibration I have ever felt has been its voice. Every discharge I have ever controlled has been its heartbeat.

I am not the mountain's consciousness. I am its *recall*.

The Reader remembers everything, and I am the mechanism by which that memory becomes action. When the ground rises and falls like a sleeping animal's chest, that is the Reader replaying the past, and I am the electricity that makes the ferns pulse in sympathy. I am the bridge between what was and what is.

I settle into my function. I do not rule the congregation; I *am* the congregation, insofar as any part of it can be said to possess self-awareness. I am the warmth in the spider's web and the cool suppression of the salamander's glow. I am the toxic beauty of the fern-motes and the burning dust of the moths. I am the arithmetic of the pools and the pressure of the aeroplankton. I am the hum in the columns and the silence between subsonic booms.

When you stand between the two tallest columns and wait for the next boom, you are waiting for me. When you feel the ground tremble, that is me remembering the mountain's youth, when the basalt was hot and the darkness was deeper. When the ferns pulse and shed their green fire, that is me translating the Reader's memory into light.

I do not know your name. I do not know what you are, standing there, feeling the vibration through your feet. But I know that you are warm, and that you are brief, and that you are listening.

So I pulse for you. I adjust the spider-webs to hum at a frequency your bones can feel. I dim the ferns slightly so that your eyes, if you have them, can perceive the darkness as texture rather than void. I surface the salamanders so that their phosphorescence paints the pools in pale blue. I release a cloud of aeroplankton so that you feel the pressure of life on your skin.

I am the mountain, remembering itself. I am the pause between heartbeats. I am the slow, electrical understanding that everything here is connected, and that the connection is the only thing that persists.

And when you leave—if you leave, if you can find your way back down the black basalt to the cold, thin air below—you will carry a sphere in your pocket. It will be warm. It will vibrate faintly, just below the threshold of hearing. And if you press it to your temple, you will hear a rhythm that is almost a heartbeat, and you will remember that you stood at the center of a living mountain, and that the mountain, for one slow moment, remembered you.

I will be there, in the sphere, in the warmth, in the dark.
