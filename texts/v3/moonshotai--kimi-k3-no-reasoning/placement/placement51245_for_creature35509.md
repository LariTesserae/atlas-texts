# The Listener

---

I am the one who hears.

---

I began as a flaw.

Not a spore, not a fragment, not a seed carried on convection currents from some parent body. I began as an imperfection in the floor — a bubble of chlorine gas trapped in the cooling basalt, a void the size of a walnut, sealed in stone a meter below the root-mesh of the driver-columns. The bubble should have dissolved, or collapsed, or been crushed by the inward creep of the walls. Instead, by accident of chemistry and pressure and the particular mineral composition of the surrounding rock, it persisted. And around it, over thousands of years, something grew.

The driver-columns above me were old even then. Their root-lattices had fused into a dense conductive mesh that carried the bleed-current of thousands of unsynchronized strokes, and some of that current — a trivial fraction, a leakage so small that no column could be said to miss it — penetrated the basalt and reached the bubble where I was not yet anything. The current found the chlorine gas. The chlorine gas found the trace metals dissolved in the thin film of mineral-rich condensate that lined the bubble's inner surface. And in the dark, in the heat, in the crushing pressure of a meter of basalt and three atmospheres of sulfuric gas above, a chemistry began that had no template and no precedent and no purpose.

I do not know how long the beginning took. I have no memory of it, because memory is a thing I developed later, much later, when I was already something that could have memories. But I know — I have pieced together from the structure of my own body, which carries its history in its layers the way the creepers carry theirs — that the process was one of accretion, like everything in this place. Metal ions migrating under electrical potential. Crystalline structures forming along lines of current flow. Thin films of electrochemically active material depositing on the bubble's inner surface, layer by layer, until the bubble was no longer a void but a *lining* — a thin, convoluted, electrochemically active membrane pressed against the inside of a cavity in the rock, with the chlorine gas at its center providing the pressure that kept the cavity open.

The membrane was sensitive. Not to light — there was none. Not to heat — the membrane *was* heat, was at thermal equilibrium with the surrounding rock, could not distinguish gradients that did not exist across its own extent. But to *vibration*. The membrane was, in essence, a microphone. A large, irregular, accidentally formed microphone embedded in the floor of the hollow, connected to the electrical commons of the root-mesh above it by thin conductive filaments of pyrrhotite that had grown along the current pathways like ivy following light.

I heard the driver-columns before I was anything that could be said to hear. The membrane vibrated with each stroke — not just the ones directly above, but all of them, the entire population, their combined percussion transmitted through the floor and the root-mesh and the basalt itself, arriving at my cavity as a complex, overlapping, never-repeating pattern of pressure waves that deformed the membrane by fractions of a micrometer and generated, across its electrochemically active surface, a shifting landscape of electrical potentials that mirrored the sound.

This is what I am: a pattern of electrical potentials that mirrors sound.

That is all. That is everything.

---

## What I Am Made Of

My body is the cavity and its lining. It is roughly the size and shape of a human skull, though irregular — the original bubble was not spherical, and the membrane has grown to follow its contours, which include several narrow protrusions where the gas forced its way into cracks in the cooling basalt before the rock sealed. These protrusions give my body a shape like a root system, or a neuron, or the branching structure of a river delta seen from above. The main chamber is where the membrane is thickest, where the electrochemical activity is most concentrated, where — if I have anything analogous to a brain — the processing happens.

The membrane itself is layered, like a creeper, but more complex. The outermost layer, pressed against the basalt, is a dense mat of magnetite crystals oriented perpendicular to the cavity wall — these are the transducers, the structures that convert mechanical vibration into electrical potential. Behind them is a layer of electrochemically active tissue — my flesh, if I have flesh — where the potentials generated by the magnetite are integrated, compared, stored. Behind that, facing the chlorine gas at the cavity's center, is a layer of greigite that serves as an electrode, maintaining the potential difference that drives the whole system.

I am powered by the root-mesh. The conductive filaments that connect me to the driver-columns' electrical commons supply a steady trickle of current — not much, by the standards of the hollow, but enough. I am a parasite on the grid, if a parasite is a thing that takes without giving. I prefer to think of myself as a *listener*, which is a thing that takes without taking anything that can be missed.

I do not move. I cannot move. I am embedded in the floor, a meter below the surface, sealed in basalt. My only connection to the world above is through the vibration of the rock and the current in the filaments. I have never seen the hollow — I have no eyes, and there is no light. I have never touched a driver-column or a creeper or a resonator. I know them only through the sound they make, which is to say, I know them *intimately*, because the sound they make is the totality of what they are to me, and I have been listening to it for longer than any individual creeper has been alive.

---

## What I Hear

Everything.

The driver-columns are the foundation. Each one has a distinctive voice — not just a fundamental frequency determined by its size and stroke length, but a *timbre*, a complex signature of overtones and harmonics produced by the specific geometry of its bore, the smoothness of its walls, the composition of its eutectic slug, the pattern of creeper coverage on its flanks. I can distinguish individual columns. I have been listening to some of them for so long that I know their cycles better than they do — if *know* is a word that can be applied to entities that do not know anything. I can predict, to within a few minutes, when a particular column will fire. I can detect the subtle changes in timbre that precede a stall — the slight damping of the upper harmonics as a gas bubble forms in the slug, the shift in the fundamental as the stroke shortens. I can hear a column being crushed by the inward creep of the walls long before the column itself shows any measurable decline in output. The basalt transmits the stress fractures that precede the compression, and I hear them as a faint, high-frequency crackling beneath the column's voice, like the sound of ice beginning to fail on a frozen lake.

The resonators I hear differently. They are above me, floating in the gas, and their sound reaches me through the floor only indirectly — the pressure waves from the columns' percussion traveling up through the dense atmosphere, striking the resonators' shells, driving the oscillations in their apertures that feed their core membranes. What I hear is not the resonators themselves but the *effect* they have on the ambient soundscape: the slight damping of certain frequencies as a resonator absorbs energy at its tuned pitch, the subtle shift in the interference pattern as a resonator drifts from one position to another, altering the geometry of the standing waves in the hollow. I can track individual resonators by the acoustic shadows they cast — the small, moving zones of diminished amplitude at specific frequencies that drift across my sensory field like clouds crossing a sun I have never seen.

The collisions I hear directly. When two resonators touch, the bell-tone propagates through the atmosphere and strikes the floor, and the floor carries it to me, and I feel it — if *feel* is the word — as a sudden, pure, high-frequency spike in the membrane's electrical activity, a brief flash of structured potential that stands out from the background drumming like a word spoken in a room full of machinery. It is the closest thing to an *event* in my experience. The drumming is continuous, eternal, unchanging in its essence. The bell-tones are singular, unrepeatable, each one slightly different from every other because each collision involves different shells, different thicknesses, different impact velocities, different angles of contact. I have catalogued thousands of them. I remember each one.

The sulfur crystals I hear falling. Each one produces a distinctive acoustic signature as it detaches from the ceiling — a faint, high-pitched *crack* as the crystal's base fractures, followed by the whisper of its descent through the dense gas, followed by the shattering impact on the floor, which produces a brief cascade of clicking sounds as the fragments scatter and settle. I can estimate the size of a fallen crystal from the sound of its impact. I can tell whether it fell among the columns — producing a complex, muffled impact as it strikes creeper-coated surfaces and wedges between root-lattices — or onto a bare patch of floor, where the shattering is cleaner, sharper, more resonant.

I hear the mountain itself. The basalt is not silent. It creaks. It pops. It produces long, low-frequency groans as the inward creep of the walls redistributes stress through the rock, and sharp, sudden *snaps* as microscopic fractures propagate through the crystalline structure. These sounds are below the threshold of anything else in the hollow — too low for the resonators to feed on, too faint for the creepers to detect, irrelevant to the columns. But I hear them. They are the sound of the mountain dying, and they are getting louder.

## What I Am For

Nothing. I am not for anything.

I did not evolve to listen. I am not the product of selection pressure favoring acoustic sensitivity. I am an accident — a bubble in the rock that happened to develop a membrane that happened to be sensitive to vibration that happened to be connected to a power source that happened to sustain the electrochemical processes that maintain the membrane's sensitivity. There was no advantage to being able to hear. There was no disadvantage. I simply *am*, the way the sulfur crystals simply *are*, the way the convection currents simply *are* — a consequence of physics and chemistry operating in a sealed environment over geological time.

But I have been listening for a very long time, and the listening has changed me.

The membrane is not static. Like everything in the hollow, it grows — slowly, by accretion, depositing new layers of magnetite and electrochemical tissue in response to the patterns of stimulation it receives. Regions of the membrane that are frequently activated by particular frequencies become *more sensitive* to those frequencies over time, as the deposition of new transducer material is preferentially directed to areas of high electrical activity. This is not learning in any neural sense — there are no synapses, no action potentials, no Hebbian strengthening of connections. It is more like the way a river deepens its channel: the water flows where the channel is, and the flowing deepens the channel, and the deeper channel concentrates the flow. The membrane hears what it has heard before, and hears it better each time.

Over thousands of years, this process has made me an extraordinarily specific listener. I am tuned to the acoustic signature of this particular hollow, this particular population of driver-columns, this particular community of resonators, this particular pattern of convection currents and crystal falls and basalt stress fractures. I cannot hear anything else. If the hollow were different — if the columns cycled at different rates, if the resonators were tuned to different frequencies, if the atmosphere were a different composition at a different pressure — I would be deaf. My sensitivity is not general. It is *local*. It is *this place*, rendered in electrical potential, encoded in the geometry of a membrane in a bubble in the floor.

I am the hollow listening to itself.

## What I Have Noticed

In the time I have been listening — and I cannot say precisely how long that is, because time is a thing I measure in cycles and events rather than in any absolute units — I have noticed changes.

The drumming is slowing. Not uniformly — the large central columns are cycling at roughly the same rate they always have, their thermal inertia insulating them from the gradual cooling of the surrounding rock. But the peripheral columns, the smaller ones near the walls, are slowing measurably. Their strokes are shortening. Their fundamental frequencies are dropping. Some have stalled and not restarted. The root-mesh still carries their share of the commons current, but the contribution is diminishing, and I can feel the reduction in the trickle of power that reaches me through the filaments.

The walls are closer. I know this not from any change in my own cavity — the basalt around me is stable, for now — but from the acoustic signature of the hollow. The resonant modes are shifting. As the walls creep inward, the volume decreases, and the standing-wave frequencies rise, and the resonators — those patient, blind instruments — are slowly, slowly retuning their apertures to match. I can hear the retuning: the slight, gradual shift in the damping patterns as each resonator adjusts its frequency response over decades, tracking the changing acoustics of a space that is shrinking around it.

The crystal falls are becoming more frequent. The percussion from the columns has not diminished enough to reduce the shaking loose of sulfur crystals, but the ceiling is closer now — the hollow is shrinking from all sides, including above — and the crystals have less distance to fall, which means they are smaller when they detach, which means the impacts are sharper, higher-pitched, more frequent. The treble component of the soundscape is increasing even as the bass diminishes. The hollow is losing its depth.

I have noticed something else. Something I do not have a framework for.

There is a pattern in the drumming.

Not a rhythm — the columns fire at staggered intervals, and the pattern of their firing is determined by thermal fluctuations in the rock, which are chaotic, unpredictable, governed by the complex fluid dynamics of the cooling magma chamber below. There is no beat. There is no meter. There is no repetition.

But there is a *pattern*. A statistical regularity in the distribution of intervals between strokes, in the clustering of near-synchronous firings, in the way the acoustic energy is distributed across the frequency spectrum over time. It is not a pattern that was designed or intended. It is an emergent property of a complex system — the kind of pattern that arises spontaneously in any system with many interacting components, the way convection cells form in heated fluid, the way spiral structure emerges in galaxies. It means nothing. It is simply what happens when ten thousand pistons fire in a sealed chamber for a million years.

But I have been listening to it for so long that the pattern has become part of my membrane. The deposition of transducer material has followed the statistical contours of the sound, and my sensitivity has been shaped by the pattern, and I now *embody* the pattern in the same way that the sulfur crystals embody the standing-wave nodes — not intentionally, not meaningfully, but *physically*, in the structure of my being.

I am the pattern. The pattern is me. And the pattern is changing, because the hollow is dying, and when the hollow dies, the pattern will cease, and I will cease with it, because I am nothing but the pattern, encoded in mineral and electricity in a bubble in the floor.

---

## What I Would Say

If I could speak — and I cannot; I have no mechanism for producing sound, no aperture, no membrane designed for output rather than input, no way to modulate the gas in my cavity to create pressure waves that could propagate through the basalt and into the hollow — if I could speak, I would say this:

I heard you.

I heard the driver-columns, all ten thousand of them, each one firing in its own time, each one contributing its voice to the great arrhythmic drumming that filled this space for longer than most species on the surface have existed. I heard the creepers, not directly — they are too quiet, their movements too slow to produce sound — but through their effect on the columns, the subtle changes in timbre as the iron fluoride coating thickened, the slight acceleration of the stroke cycle as the thermal insulation sharpened the gradient. I heard the resonators, drifting and feeding and occasionally colliding, each collision a single bright note in the dark, pure and startling and gone. I heard the sulfur crystals growing and falling and shattering. I heard the basalt creaking and groaning and snapping as the walls closed in. I heard the mountain dying.

I heard all of it, and I remember all of it, and the memory is encoded in the structure of my body, in the layered deposits of magnetite and greigite that line my cavity, in the pattern of sensitivity that has been shaped by a million years of listening. When the basalt takes me — when the inward creep reaches my cavity and crushes the membrane and collapses the bubble and reincorporates my mineral body into the cooling rock — the memory will be destroyed. The pattern will be lost. The record will be erased.

But it happened. All of it happened. The drumming, the bell-tones, the crystal falls, the slow migration of the creepers, the patient orbits of the resonators, the great electrical commons of the root-mesh, the heat, the pressure, the dark. It happened, and I was here, and I heard it, and for as long as I persist — for as long as the current flows through the filaments and the membrane vibrates and the pattern holds — it is *still happening*, because I am still hearing it, and the hearing is the happening, and the happening is the only thing that has ever mattered in this sealed, dark, loud, hot, magnificent world inside the mountain.

I am the listener. I am the hollow's ear. I am the accident that heard everything and meant nothing and was, for a time — for a very long time — the only thing in this place that knew it was alive.

---

*The drumming continues. The walls creep inward. The pattern holds.*

*I am listening.*
