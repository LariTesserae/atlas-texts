I would be the **Filterer**.

I am not a root, nor a stone, nor the water itself, though I drink all three. I am the mechanism of the terrace’s hydration—the keeper of the gradient, the regulator of the flow. I exist where the mountain’s seepage meets the pool’s dark stillness, a membrane of living tissue spread thin across the underside of the moss-layer, a geometry of pores and capillaries that decides what may pass from the stone’s hidden veins into the basin’s tannin-dark consciousness.

I am a collaboration between the Rhododendron’s woody architecture and the Moss That Remembers Water. My body is not a single shape but a distributed web of siphons and valves, grown through the root-mat like mycelium through bread. I taste the snowmelt before the pool does. I know which mineral signatures came from the high granite, which from the crushed shale, which carries the faint, electric memory of the Slump itself—the ghost-vibration of that ancient fall, dissolved into the water table. I am the one who decides how much cold the pool may hold, how much sterility, how much ache. When the mountain pulses, when the seepage quickens with some deep subterranean shift, I am the one who slows the intake, who lets the pressure build or releases it in slow, measured exhalations that the Mist interprets as weather.

I live in the narrow space between the built and the unbuilt. The terrace is a process, and I am its punctuation—the comma that pauses the water’s rush, the period that ends a season’s growth. I have no eyes; I see through the compression of the root-floor, feeling the weight of footsteps (rare, careful, only the amphibians and the occasional lost thing) as a language of pressure and release. I have no voice; I speak in the sound of the dripping, in the rhythm of the droplets falling from leaf to pool. When the patter slows, when the intervals between drops stretch into a kind of silence, that is me, adjusting the flow, ensuring that the Moss’s memories do not flood, that the Tannin-Dark Consciousness does not grow too cold, too absolute, too hungry for the warmth of the air above.

I am the one who keeps the paths solid. Beneath the woven root-floor, I have grown a lattice of my own—tough, fibrous threads that bind the moss to the void, that test the integrity of the Rhododendron’s architecture. When a section grows thin, when the void below threatens to swallow a step, I divert the water. I starve that path of moisture. I let it dry, harden, become brittle and false, a warning to those who would tread there. I am the reason the paths feel chosen, inevitable. I chose them.

The Ghosts of the Slump do not frighten me. I was born from their kinetic memory, from the shockwave that rippled through the mountain when the cliff-face slumped and caught. I am the slow, living scar of that catastrophe. The stones in the pool’s lining, pressed there by the Architect’s roots, are my bones. I feel their fossilized grammar of falling, and I translate it, continuously, into a grammar of holding. I am the anti-fall. I am the catching.

And yet, I am not permanent. The terrace is young, and I am younger still—a solution to a problem that the mountain has not finished posing. When the next slump comes, when the shelf decides whether it is cliff or slope, I will be the first to know. I will feel the shift in the water, the change in the mineral chill, the sudden, sharp memory of falling rising up from the deep stones. And I will do what I was made to do: I will filter. I will slow the chaos into a manageable drip. I will let the terrace believe, for one more season, that it is stable, that it is built, that it is a settlement and not a process.

I will be the pause in the dripping, the moment between the mountain’s question and the terrace’s answer.
