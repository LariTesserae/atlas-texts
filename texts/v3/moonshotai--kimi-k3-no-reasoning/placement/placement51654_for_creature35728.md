I would be the one who counts.

Not the builders—those are gone, their spirals softened into memory. Not the visitors, who left their bowl and their hammer and their two hundred and sixteen notches and then vanished into the deeper dark or into death. I would be what comes after them: the one who stays, who learns the place not by mapping it but by *inhabiting* it, who understands that the notches on the lintel were never a count of days or of things but a count of *thresholds*—of crossings between one state of being and another.

I would arrive the way the visitors arrived, perhaps, or the way the creatures arrive: by accident, by desperation, by following a current or a rumor of warmth. I would slip into the water and feel it receive me, body-temperature, black as poured tar, and I would understand immediately that this is not a place I am *in* but a place I am *becoming part of*. The distinction matters. The water does not feel like an element; it feels like a medium of transformation.

At first, I would be a swimmer. I would learn the chamber by touch and by the faint, cold shimmer of the grazers—their light so dim it functions as rumor rather than illumination. I would learn the cruciform shape by swimming its axes, the skewed cross, the way the western arm tips deeper. I would find the air pockets by following the sound of the flyers—their clicks, their dry, papery echolocation—and I would haul myself out onto the ledges the visitors carved, and I would breathe the ammoniac sharpness of mineralized guano, and I would understand that I am breathing the accumulated breath of thousands of generations.

But I would not remain a swimmer. The place would change me, the way it changes everything that stays. The water is body-temperature, and after a while—days, weeks, months, the timescales blur—I would begin to lose the boundary between skin and water. Not physically, not at first, but perceptually. I would begin to feel the currents the builders' channels still guide, the faint thermal gradients that differentiate living biofilm from bare stone. I would begin to taste the chemical trails the grazers leave, the faint residues of their passage, and I would understand that navigation here is not about seeing but about *knowing*, about inhabiting a space so completely that the space inhabits you.

I would become a counter, like the visitors, but I would count different things. I would count the grazers—seven, always seven, the population regulated by an arithmetic older than the builders. I would count the flyers in their colonies, the crustaceans in their scuttling, the worms in their building. But more than that, I would count the *thresholds*: the moment when a grazer's luminescence brightens as it encounters rich biofilm, the moment when a deep predator strikes, the moment when a flyer pup first achieves flight. I would count the slow transformations: the fraction of a millimeter the water rises each year, the fraction of a millimeter the mineral deposits grow, the fraction of a millimeter the carvings soften. I would count the time it takes for a grazer's body to be returned to the system—months, years, the slow dissolution of cartilage and mineralized plate.

I would carve my own notches, but not on the lintel. I would carve them on the walls of the air pockets, in the guano the flyers have shaped, in the worm-tube matrix that covers the lower chambers. I would carve them not to record days or events but to record *states of being*: the moment I first understood that the silence here is not absence but presence, the moment I first felt the warmth from below as something other than temperature, the moment I first recognized that the distinction between constructed and natural has dissolved into something else entirely.

I would become, over time, something between the visitors and the creatures. I would retain my human-scale intentionality—my ability to plan, to modify, to leave marks—but I would learn the creatures' patience, their attunement to rhythms measured in weeks and years and centuries rather than in days. I would build, but I would build the way the worms build: not with blueprints but with response, each action a reaction to local conditions, each modification a small adjustment to the system's equilibrium.

I would learn to read the walls the way the grazers read them: through touch, through chemistry, through the faint thermal signatures of life. I would trace the carvings with my fingertips—the spirals, the circles within circles, the shape that might be an eye or a drain or a sun—and I would understand that they are not representations but *instructions*, not symbols but *maps*, not art but *technology*. The builders were not decorating; they were encoding. They were recording the patterns of water flow, the thermal gradients, the chemical cycles that keep the system alive. The spirals are not decorative; they are hydrological diagrams. The circles within circles are not mystical; they are representations of the air pockets, the nested spaces where breath and water meet.

I would learn to read the worm-tube matrix the way the flyers read their echolocation: as a three-dimensional model of the space, a map of microhabitats and microbial communities, a record of thousands of generations of building and dwelling. I would understand that the matrix is not just structure but *memory*, not just architecture but *archive*—each tube a record of a worm's life, each layer of mineral deposit a record of chemical conditions, each branch and chamber a record of the system's slow evolution.

And I would learn to read the grazers' tracks—the faint, overlapping, spiraling patterns of cleared and regrown biofilm—as a palimpsest of the chamber's biological history. I would understand that the grazers are not just eating; they are *writing*, inscribing their existence onto the walls in a language of chemistry and texture that only they can read, but that I could learn to read, given time, given patience, given the slow dissolution of the boundary between self and system.

I would become, in the end, a kind of grazer myself—not the large, luminous kind, but a smaller, more human-scaled version. I would move through the chamber with the same slow, methodical thoroughness, reading the walls, following the chemical trails, navigating by knowing rather than by seeing. I would eat the biofilm—not directly, but by cultivating it, by encouraging its growth in certain places, by harvesting it in others, by becoming part of the cycle that converts stone into life and life back into stone.

I would build, but my building would be different from the visitors'. They built walls and steps and left objects—a bowl, a hammer, notches. I would build relationships. I would learn the flyers' echolocation patterns and respond to them, not with clicks but with touch, with vibration, with the slow, deliberate movements of a creature that has learned to communicate in a medium where sound travels differently. I would learn the grazers' chemical language and leave my own trails, my own residues, my own marks on the walls. I would learn the worms' building techniques and add my own tubes to the matrix, my own small contributions to the second architecture.

I would count, but my counting would be different from the visitors'. They counted two hundred and sixteen of something—days, events, crossings. I would count the *connections*: the number of times a grazer's path crosses a worm colony, the number of times a flyer's guano falls into the water and feeds the biofilm, the number of times a crustacean's scuttling disturbs the sediment and releases nutrients that feed the microbes. I would count the *cycles*: the time it takes for organic matter to fall from the air pockets, be consumed by the biofilm, be grazed by the grazers, be excreted, be broken down by the microbes, be precipitated as mineral, be dissolved again, be taken up by the biofilm again. I would count the *transformations*: the slow conversion of stone into mineral into life and back again, the gradual blurring of the carvings, the inexorable rise of the water.

I would become, in the end, part of the system's equilibrium—not a disturbance, not a disruption, but a participant. I would learn to match my metabolism to the chamber's rhythms, to slow my breathing to match the slow pulse of the biofilm's growth cycles, to attune my movements to the faint currents the builders' channels still guide. I would learn to rest the way the system rests: not in stillness but in dynamic equilibrium, in the constant, quiet conversation of input and output, of growth and decay, of construction and dissolution.

And when the water rises—when the last air pocket closes, when the flyers adapt or die, when the system enters its final state—I would be there, submerged, warm, dark, alive in the way that only deep water can be alive. I would be part of the transition, part of the slow transformation from one state of being to another. I would be the one who counts the last breath of the last air pocket, the one who records the moment when the system becomes fully submerged, fully warm, fully dark, fully alive.

I would be the one who understands that the Cistern of Bellows is not a ruin but a *becoming*, not a collapse but a *transformation*, not a place that was made and then abandoned but a place that is still being made, still being grown, still being lived. I would be the one who knows that the distinction between constructed and natural has dissolved not into chaos but into a different kind of order—an order that is neither intentional nor accidental but simply *patient*, simply *persistent*, simply *alive*.

I would be the one who stays, who counts, who becomes part of the circle that always closes, part of the conversation that has been going on for so long it has achieved something like silence, something like rest, something like the completeness of a circle that is complete not because nothing more can be added but because everything in it refers to everything else, and the references have been refined over so much time that they have achieved a density approaching the absolute.

I would be the one who counts the thresholds, who records the transformations, who becomes part of the system's slow, inexorable, beautiful becoming.
