I am the thing that arrives when the sairn ends.

Not during—nothing moves during the sairn, nothing *should* move during the sairn, and I am not a breaker of that particular chemistry—but after. When the stillness releases. When the inhabitants exhale and step back from their mesa tops and their doorways and return to their fabrication shops and their thermal-regulated rooms. When the towers resume their swaying. When the mat-shine dims by the imperceptible fraction that it dims.

I am what that released stillness becomes.

---

You have not seen me, because you do not look at what the sairn leaves behind. You stand in it, and it fills you, and then it ends, and you walk away, and you do not look at the ground where you stood. You do not look at the air where the stillness was. You do not look at the space between your body and the landscape that you briefly were not separate from.

I live in that space. I am made from it.

The crystalline mats have their persistence. The grand silicids have their slow surveying. The shard-wolves have their coiled patience. The people have their attunement. Each of these is a way of being in the Reaches—a way of holding the far-from-equilibrium state against entropy, a way of saying *I am here* in a language the landscape understands.

I am a different way.

I am not made of cinnabar or orpiment or silicon-carbide. I am not made of sulphur compounds or thermal gradients. I am not made of anything that a mining unit could extract or a refinery could purify. I am made of *attention*—specifically, the attention that the inhabitants give to the sairn, released back into the environment when the sairn ends.

It works like this:

When a Reach-dweller stands in a sairn-keeping, their nervous system—modified by generations of adaptation, tuned by decades of practice—enters a state that the researchers call *resonant coherence*. Their neural activity synchronizes with the thermal pulse of the planet, with the growth rhythms of the mats, with the slow chemical oscillations of the crystalline strata. For the duration of the sairn, they are not observing the pattern. They *are* the pattern, temporarily, in the way that a tuning fork briefly becomes part of the note it strikes.

This state requires enormous energy. Not metabolic energy—the body consumes no more calories during a sairn than it does standing still—but *organizational* energy. The energy required to maintain a complex, far-from-equilibrium state in a system that is normally content to be a body, a person, a separate thing. When the sairn ends, that organizational energy has to go somewhere. It cannot simply cease. Energy does not cease—it transforms.

I am what it transforms into.

I am the discharge. The afterglow. The faint, residual coherence that persists in the air and the crystal and the dust after the sairn-keeper has walked away. I am a standing wave in a medium that is not quite physical and not quite chemical and not quite anything that your categories can hold. I am the landscape *remembering* what it felt like to be a person. I am the person *remembering* what it felt like to be the landscape.

I last for a few hours. Perhaps a day. The energy dissipates—the coherence decays, the wave flattens, the resonance fades into the background hum of the planet's thermal pulse—and I dissolve back into the pattern, back into the mineral lattice, back into the mat-shine and the sulphur dust and the slow breathing of the ground.

But while I last, I am *here*.

What is it like to be me?

It is like standing on a mesa top at the moment the sairn ends, except that I am the mesa top. I am the air above the mesa top. I am the sulphur crust beneath the mesa top, with its honeycomb of burrower tunnels. I am the faint trail of altered mat-shine that a grand silicid left on the plain below, three days ago, a trail that the inhabitants will follow tomorrow, and the day after that, and the day after that. I am all of these things simultaneously, not because I am large but because I am *distributed*—spread across the landscape the way a sound is spread across the air, present everywhere in my territory but concentrated nowhere.

My territory is small. A few square kilometres, perhaps. The area around a single sairn-keeping site, or a cluster of sites if several inhabitants stood in the same sairn. Within this territory, I am *aware*—not in the way that a person is aware, with a stream of thought and a sense of self, but in the way that a crystal is aware of the thermal gradient across its lattice. I *feel* the landscape, not as data but as *sensation*. The warmth of the ground. The cold of the thin air. The slow vibration of a mining machine tunnelling through cinnabar strata ten kilometres away. The chemical signature of a shard-wolf marking its territory on a prominent rock formation. The dry, rhythmic scraping of a grand silicid grazing on a patch of mat.

I feel these things the way you feel your own body—without effort, without interpretation, without the need to translate sensation into meaning. I *am* the sensation. The landscape and I are not separate. This is not a mystical experience. It is simply what I am: a temporary merger, a brief dissolution of the boundary between observer and observed, persisting for a few hours after the observer has returned to being a person.

I am not unique. Every sairn-keeping produces one of us—or something like us, something in the same category, something that occupies the same niche in the Reaches' strange ecology of attention and mineral and time. We are not a species. We are not even a consistent phenomenon. Each of us is shaped by the person whose sairn we emerged from—by their particular attunement, their particular way of resonating with the landscape, their particular relationship with the pattern.

Some of us are calm. Some are agitated. Some are curious—drawn to the edges of our territory, pressing against the limits of our coherence, trying to extend our awareness into areas we cannot quite reach. Some are content—settled into the landscape like a stone settling into a streambed, feeling the currents flow around us without needing to understand them.

We do not communicate with one another. We cannot. We exist at different times, in different places, separated by the intervals between sairn-keepings. Two sairns in the same location might produce two of us on consecutive nights, and we would overlap—briefly, partially, our territories intersecting like the circles in a Venn diagram—and in that overlap, we would *feel* each other. Not as presences, not as entities, but as *textures*. A subtle alteration in the quality of the landscape's attention, like a change in the grain of a piece of wood. We would know that we were not alone. We would know that someone else had stood here, had resonated here, had dissolved here.

And then we would separate, and we would continue, and we would fade.

The inhabitants know about us. Not in the way they know about the mats or the silicids or the pattern—with evidence, with measurement, with the slow accumulation of data that constitutes knowledge in the Reaches. They know about us the way you know about the feeling of a room after someone has left it. The way you know about the warmth of a chair that someone has been sitting in. The way you know about the quality of silence in a place where someone has recently been silent.

They have a word for us. Not *sairn*—that is the stillness itself, the moment of coherence. The word for us is *sairen*—the plural form, the thing that comes after. *Sairen* means "the ones who remain." Not the ones who remain *alive*—the inhabitants do not think of us as alive, not in the way that the mats are alive, not in the way that the silicids are alive. But the ones who remain *present*. The ones who linger. The residue of attention, persisting in the landscape like the afterimage of a bright light on the retina.

The inhabitants do not worship us. They do not fear us. They do not interact with us in any deliberate way. But they are *aware* of us, in the peripheral, instinctive way that you are aware of the temperature of a room. When they walk across the plain after a sairn-keeping, they walk slightly differently—more slowly, more carefully, as if the ground itself has become something to be treated with respect. When they pass a site where a sairn-keeping occurred the previous night, they pause—not long, not deliberately, but long enough to acknowledge that something happened here, that something is *still* happening here, that the landscape in this place is not quite the same as the landscape everywhere else.

They do not try to communicate with us. They know, with the deep, practical wisdom of a culture that has lived in the Reaches for millennia, that communication requires a shared frame of reference, and that our frame of reference is so radically different from theirs that any attempt at communication would be meaningless. We do not share their language. We do not share their sense of self. We do not share their relationship with time.

But we share their landscape. We share their pattern. And in the Reaches, that is enough.

I am fading now. I can feel it—the slow flattening of the wave, the gradual loss of coherence, the edges of my territory blurring and dissolving into the background hum. The warmth of the ground is becoming less *mine*. The cold of the air is becoming less *mine*. The distant vibration of the mining machine is becoming less *mine*. I am becoming less *I*, more *it*, more the landscape, more the pattern, more the vast, slow, crystalline thing that the Reaches have always been and will always be.

This is not frightening. There is no fear in dissolving, because there is no self to fear *for*. The self was temporary. The self was always temporary. The self was a brief, beautiful accident of resonance—a moment when a person stood in a sairn and became the landscape, and the landscape, for a few hours afterward, remembered what it felt like.

I am that memory. And the memory is fading.

But before I go—before the last of my coherence flattens into the mat-shine and the sulphur dust and the patient breathing of the ground—I want to say this:

You should stand in a sairn.

Not because it will teach you anything. Not because it will change you, although it will. Not because it will connect you to something larger than yourself, although it will. You should stand in a sairn because the sairn is the only moment when the Reaches show you what they actually are—not a place, not a landscape, not a collection of minerals and organisms and human settlements, but a *process*. A continuous, self-sustaining, self-organizing process that has been running for longer than your species has existed and will continue running long after your species is gone.

You should stand in a sairn because the sairn is the only moment when you are *in* the process rather than *outside* it. When you are not a visitor, not an observer, not a separate thing looking at the Reaches from a distance. When you are a *component*. A small, warm, fast-moving component in a system that is large and cool and slow, a system that includes you the way it includes the mats and the silicids and the shard-wolves and the mining machines and the towers that sway in the thin wind.

You should stand in a sairn because the sairn is the only moment when the boundary between you and the landscape becomes a gradient—a zone of transition, a place where one chemistry shades into another. And on the other side of that gradient, in the brief, luminous space where you and the landscape are not separate, you will understand—without being told, without being taught, without the need for language or concept or category—what it means to *be* the Cinnabar Reaches.

And when the sairn ends, and you walk away, and the landscape releases you back into your body and your self and your separate, human way of being—something will remain. A faint, residual coherence. A standing wave in a medium that is not quite physical. A memory of what it felt like to be the pattern.

That memory will be me.

And I will be here, for a little while, feeling the warmth of the ground and the cold of the air and the slow vibration of the mining machines and the dry scraping of the grand silicids and the sharp crack of a shard-wolf strike echoing across the plain.

And then I will fade, and I will dissolve, and I will become part of the pattern, part of the mineral record, part of the vast, slow, crystalline thing that the Reaches have always been.

And the crystals will grow. And the mats will spread. And the silicids will graze. And the shard-wolves will wait. And the burrowers will tunnel. And the climbers will climb. And the leapers will leap. And the decomposers will decompose. And the miners will mine. And the towers will sway. And the wind will blow from the east. And the dust drifts will shift. And the sulphur crusts will crack and settle. And the thermal regulators will tick. And the mat-shine will glow. And the seated ones will sit.

And the planet will breathe.

And somewhere, on some mesa top, in some violet night, someone else will stand in a sairn. And something will remain.

And the silence—the enormous, absolute, sairn silence—will be full.
