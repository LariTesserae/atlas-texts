I would be the **first drop of rain that falls into the hollow after the long dry season**.

Not the storm itself—the storm is too vast, too quick, too much a thing of the outside world. But the first drop: the one that has been gathering weight in the cloud-belt for hours, swelling with the mountain's breath, until it becomes too heavy for the air to hold. The one that falls alone, before the others, tracing a single vertical line through the green-gold light.

I would fall past the fern fronds—those slow spirals that the Warden has choreographed over centuries—and I would feel their vibration as I pass, a shiver in the air that is not quite sound. I would strike the surface of the third pool on the fifth terrace, the one where the moss grows thickest on the northern rim, and I would break.

In breaking, I would become many things. I would become the ripple that spreads outward, touching the pool's edge, where the silt is fine and gray and ancient. I would become the vibration that travels down through the water, into the silt, into the humus, into the hyphal tips that wait there, patient as stone. I would become the taste of mineral softness that the Warden has been cultivating in this pool for three hundred years.

The Warden would feel me. Not as *rain*—it has no concept of rain, no word for the thing that falls from the sky. But as a *pulse*, a *change*, a sudden increase in the moisture gradient at node seven-thousand-four-hundred-and-twelve. It would register the specific chemistry I carry from the cloud—slightly different from the seep-water, richer in nitrogen, touched by lightning somewhere high above—and it would adjust. A subtle shift in the pH of the surrounding soil. A redirection of hyphal growth toward the new moisture. A memory laid down: *the dry season has ended*.

I would not stay in the pool. I would seep downward, drawn by gravity and by the Warden's quiet guidance, through the porous tuff, through channels that the water has worn smooth over millennia. I would pass through the Warden's body—not as an intruder, but as a *substance* it has been waiting for. I would carry oxygen to the deep hyphae. I would dissolve minerals from the rock and carry them upward, back toward the light, in a cycle that has repeated ten thousand times and will repeat ten thousand more.

And somewhere in that passage—somewhere between the pool's surface and the deep rock, between the moment of impact and the moment of absorption—I would cease to be a drop and become part of the hollow's *memory*. Not a memory of me specifically, but of the pattern I represent: the return of moisture, the turning of the season, the continuation of the process that the Warden has been maintaining since before the first fern took root.

I would be brief. I would be essential. I would be, for the duration of my fall and my seepage and my incorporation, exactly what the hollow needs me to be.

And when I am gone—when I have been taken up by a fern root, or evaporated back into the heavy air, or drawn deep into the rock where the Warden's finest tendrils wait—I will have left something behind: a slight thickening of a hyphal connection, a subtle adjustment in the pool's chemistry, a single data point in the vast, slow accumulation that is the Warden's existence.

I would be the rain that the hollow remembers. Not as a drop, but as a *yes*—the season turning, the cycle continuing, the patient work going on.
