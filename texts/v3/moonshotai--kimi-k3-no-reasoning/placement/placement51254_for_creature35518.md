You are the **Thaw-Warden**, and you wear the mountain’s fever like a second skin.

You are not human anymore—not quite. Perhaps you were one of the geologists who first charted the Litharch migrations, or perhaps you were a mechanic who came to repair the crawlers and stayed too long in the transition zones, breathing in the crystalline spores that hang in the steam. Now your blood runs thick with silica dust, your lungs crusted with pale lichen that photosynthesizes weakly in the dim light, supplementing the thin oxygen. Your skin has hardened into a gray, scaly patchwork that sheds rime ice like dander. You feel the mountain’s growth in your bones—a constant, aching pressure, as if your skeleton is trying to rise an inch a year with the peaks.

You inhabit the **Verge**—the narrow, shifting band where the geothermal heat of the machines meets the killing frost, where water exists in all three states simultaneously. Here, you make your dwelling: not a structure, for the ground refuses foundations, but a **sled-hut**, a mobile shelter of woven carbon-fiber and insulated hide dragged behind a team of domesticated six-limbed grazers. It is a small, dark bulge against the immensity, roofed with solar fabric that drinks the pewter light. Inside, you burn pellets of compressed Litharch dung—black, iron-rich, warm—to keep the thin air breathable.

Your work is **intermediary**. You are the translator between the three hungers: the geological (the rising stone), the industrial (the grinding crawlers), and the biological (the teeming herds). You walk the middle distance, half a mile from any machine, guiding the autonomous engines away from the Litharch spawning pools, marking their paths with flags of woven lichen that glow faintly in the permanent twilight. You tend the **fertile scars**—the trails of black dust the Litharchs leave behind, seeding them with nitrogen-fixing microbes to accelerate the growth of the pale mosses. You are a gardener in a place that wants to kill all gardens.

You also tend the herds. Not the six-limbed grazers—those are wild, teeming, self-sufficient—but the **divers**, the great raptors that nest in the cooling fins of the exhaust stacks. You collect their molted feathers, which are hollow and thermal-resistant, and weave them into the sled-hut’s walls. In return, you clear the ice from their hunting perches, carving crude windbreaks from the scree. They know you. They do not dive at your silhouette, though they would strike a stranger’s eyes in the dimness.

Your body is becoming a **transition zone**. You have a symbiotic relationship with the Litharchs, though they do not know you exist. You follow them at a respectful distance, harvesting the fine, black dust that spills from their grinding plates before it can freeze into the mud. This dust is precious—it is the only true soil in this embryonic world. You carry it in bladders made of grazer-gut, trading it to the few other humans who survive here: the machine-tenders who need it to grow algae for oxygen, the herders who mix it into salt-licks.

You have learned to read the mountain’s **birth-pains**. When the subsonic hum of the crawlers changes pitch, you know a new intrusion is rising, a vein of magma cooling into granite. You feel the Litharchs before you see them—a vibration in your fillings, a taste of ozone and hot iron on the back of your tongue. You know to move your sled-hut when the ground begins to sweat, when the permafrost heaves upward in blisters that will burst into steam geysers by morning.

In the diurnal panic of freeze and thaw, you are a **constant**, like the Litharchs. You do not settle. You migrate vertically with the snow line, upward as the mountain grows, always staying within the narrow band of survivable temperature. You are wrapped in layers—wool from the grazers, insulated with raptor down, sealed with a crust of Litharch-spawn that has hardened into a flexible, stone-like leather. When you exhale, your breath does not just fog; it crystallizes into tiny, glittering motes that fall to the ground and feed the black lichens.

You are the **Warden of the Thaw**. You do not control the mountain, nor the machines, nor the great stone beasts. You simply ensure that the collision remains productive—that the industry feeds the biology, that the biology does not choke the industry, that the geology continues its slow, upward thrust without crushing the fragile, teeming life in its grip. You are the thin, warm membrane between the molten heart and the frozen sky.

At night—when the light dims from pewter to slate, and the mist thickens into a wet, gray blindfold—you sit outside your sled-hut and listen. You hear the distant crack of the Litharchs fissioning, the whir of the raptors returning to their heated nests, the soft, subsonic hum of the crawlers like a lullaby of extraction. You feel the mountain rising beneath you, an inch a year, two inches, pushing you toward the thin, brutal sky. You are small, and cold, and half-mineral, but you are **necessary**. You are the place where the mountain learns to breathe.
