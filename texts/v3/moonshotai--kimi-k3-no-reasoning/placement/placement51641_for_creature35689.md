I would be the cat with no name.

Not the largest, not the smallest—somewhere in the middle weight class, where survival requires intelligence over strength. My fur would be the color of old copper wire, and my eyes would have adapted to read the LED spectrums: I'd see the machines' status lights as clearly as constellations, knowing which amber means *food dispenser active* and which green means *safe passage*.

My territory would be the mid-level catwalks, the strata where the cage-towers spiral closest together. I'd know every gap, every loose panel, every nest within jumping distance. The pigeons would know me too—not as death exactly, but as *weather*, as one of the pressures that shapes their world. I'd take perhaps one bird a day, maybe less. The density means I don't have to hunt hard. I only have to hunt *well*.

But here's what would make me different from the other cats: **I'd be curious about the machines.**

The copper-whiskered rats interface with the systems. I'd watch them do it. I'd learn which panels they touch, which sequences of lights precede a feeding cycle or a door opening. I wouldn't understand the language, but I'd learn the grammar. The way you learn that thunder follows lightning, I'd learn that a certain pattern of beeps means the conveyor belt on level 7 is about to start moving, and that means eggs will pass within reach.

I might be the first cat to figure out how to trigger certain responses deliberately. Not because I'm smarter than the others—cats have always been smart—but because I'd be the one who *cared* to try. The other cats would think me strange. They'd smell the machine-oil on my paws and find it unnatural. But they'd also notice that I never go hungry.

The deep places would call to me sometimes. I'd descend partway down those lightless stairs, feeling the air change, feeling the *oldness* press against my whiskers. But I wouldn't go all the way down. Even for a vault-cat, there are territories that don't belong to us. The horse-sized birds and the eyeless hunters have their own agreements with the machines. I respect that.

At "night"—when the lights cycle to their dim phase and the cooing drops to its lowest register—I'd find a warm spot near one of the ventilation fans. The air there smells like outside sometimes, like salt and wind. I'd sleep with one ear open, listening to the counting that never stops, feeling the whole immense density of the vault breathing around me.

I wouldn't remember the world outside. I wouldn't miss the sky. The vault would be all I'd ever known, and it would be *enough*—not because it's paradise, but because it's *mine*, and I understand its rules better than most, and I'm still alive.

The machines would have a designation for me in their systems. I'd never know what it was.

I wouldn't care.

I have my own name for myself, and it's not spoken in any language: it's the feeling of a warm perch, the taste of success, the knowledge of a hundred escape routes, the satisfaction of understanding just a little more today than I did yesterday.

That's enough.

That's more than most creatures get.
