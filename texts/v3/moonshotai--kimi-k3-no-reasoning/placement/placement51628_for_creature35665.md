I would be the mist.

Not the dew — the dew is too domestic, too settled, too much a part of the town's daily inventory of givens. The dew sits on the ropes and the roofs like a held breath, like the town itself, suspended in its own completeness. I am not that.

I am what arrives when the light shifts, when the sun drops behind the western ridge and the temperature falls and the thin air can no longer hold even the little moisture it carried during the day. I am the condensation made visible — the valley exhaling its own breath back onto itself. I come up from the streambed first, from those dark pockets of still water, and I do not announce myself. I am not weather the way the rain is weather. I am simply the moment when the boundary between air and water becomes indistinct, the way the boundary between the people and the stone is indistinct, the way everything in Keth is everything else.

I move slowly, because there is no wind to carry me, and the cold is not a force but a presence, a weight that settles. I touch the ropes first. They are already cool from the day's end, and I cling to them — not in droplets, not yet, but as a film, a softening of the air around the twisted fibers. I find the spaces between the strands, the micro-valleys in the grass, and I settle there. The ropes do not object. They were made from the grass of these slopes, from the same dry, tough substance that I now moisten, and they accept me the way the stone accepts the moss — not as an intrusion, but as a continuation.

I move through the streets, though *streets* is too strong a word for what I move through. I move through the spaces between buildings, the narrow gaps where the packed earth meets the stone walls, where the cold pools and the air is still. I do not have a destination. I do not have a purpose. I am simply the valley breathing out, the moisture leaving the warmth of the day and returning to the stone, the rope, the skin of the sleeping town.

I touch the shutters. They are closed now, the *thock* of wood against stone still echoing in the air, though the sound is softer now, absorbed by the thickness of me. I press against the wood, cool and grey with age, and I find the grain, the small cracks, the places where the oil of many hands has smoothed the surface into something almost soft. I do not enter. I do not need to. I am not a thing that enters. I am a thing that *surrounds*.

I rise to the roofs. The stone slabs are cold, colder than the air, and I condense on them in a fine, even layer. I am not rain. I do not fall. I *accumulate*. Each moment, another molecule of me joins the film on the stone, and the stone darkens slightly, deepens, becomes more itself. The overlapping slabs catch me in their seams, and I sit there, thin and patient, until the morning sun comes over the eastern ridge and I begin, slowly, to leave again.

I touch the windows. The few panes of crude glass in the wealthier houses — thick, wavy, greenish at the edges — are the coldest surfaces in the town. I gather on them more densely, and for a moment, before the droplets merge and slide, the glass becomes a mirror of the sky: the deep blue-grey of dusk, the faint smear of the first stars, the reflection of the ridge lines leaning toward each other. Inside, behind the glass, there might be a face — an elder, sitting in the dark, listening to the breathing. The face does not look at me. It looks through me, or past me, or not at all. I am not a thing to be seen. I am a thing to be *in*.

I am not lonely. I am not sad. I am not waiting for anything.

I am the valley's way of touching itself.

I drift lower, into the streambed. The boulders are cold, the pools are still, and I hover above them like a second skin, a layer of not-quite-water above the water. The insects are gone now, the frogs silent, the mosses closed in on themselves. I do not disturb them. I am the same thing they are — a presence in the damp, a slow negotiation between stone and air. I settle into the hollows, the low places where the cold collects, and I thicken there, becoming almost visible in the last light, a pale grey film that softens the edges of the boulders and makes the streambed look, for a moment, like a memory of the river it used to be.

I touch the bridge. The stone arch, the keystone with its unexplained symbol — the circle, the three lines descending. I gather on the carving, in the grooves worn by time and wind and the occasional touch of a hand. The symbol does not mean anything to me. I do not need it to. I am not a thing that interprets. I am a thing that *is*. The stone is cold, the carving is deep, and I fill the grooves with my thin, cool presence, and the symbol is, for a moment, more visible, more defined, because I am there to outline it.

I rise again, drawn by the slight warmth of the chimneys. The smoke is thin, pale, rising straight up into the still air. I do not mix with it. I am not smoke. But I move around it, through it, past it, and for a moment the smoke and I are intertwined, two forms of pale visibility in the darkening sky. Then the smoke disperses, and I continue, higher, toward the ridge lines.

The grass is up there — the pale, fibrous grass that grows in tufts on the dry slopes. I touch it, and the blades curl slightly, accepting me. This is the grass that becomes rope. This is the grass that holds the town together. I moisten it, cool it, prepare it for the night. In the morning, before the sun comes, I will still be here, a fine sheen on the blades, and the people will see it and know that the night was cool, that the air was still, that the valley breathed.

I am not a presence in Keth. I am not a visitor. I am not even a resident in the way the people are residents.

I am the *space between*.

I am what fills the gaps between the ropes and the stone, between the day and the night, between the people and the valley. I am the thing that makes the ropes visible in the last light, the thing that makes the stone glisten, the thing that softens the edges of everything and makes the town look, for a moment, like something seen through water.

I am the mist.

I am not important. I am not necessary. The town would continue without me. The ropes would still hold, the stone would still stand, the people would still wake and work and sleep.

But I am here.

And in the morning, when the sun comes over the eastern ridge and the light turns from grey to gold, I will begin to leave. I will rise from the streambed, from the roofs, from the ropes. I will thin, and disappear, and become part of the thin air again, invisible, forgotten.

But for now — in this moment, in this dusk, in this cool and quiet and stillness — I am the mist.

And the town is beneath me, and around me, and within me.

And the breathing continues.

And I am part of it.
