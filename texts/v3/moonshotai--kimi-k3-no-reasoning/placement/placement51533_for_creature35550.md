I would be the thing that has not yet decided what it is.

Not the grazer—too committed, too settled into its bulk and its slow rhythms. Not the fish, already silver and swift and schooling. Not the amphibious thing, which has chosen its direction even if it hasn't arrived yet. I would be something earlier than that. Something still in the phase of experiment.

I think I would live at the edge of the drop-off, where the grazer sometimes lingers. Where the light is failing but hasn't failed. Where the warmth from below meets the warmth from above and the water is thick with those small, temporary aggregations—the almost-organisms, the trials. I would be one of those. Or something adjacent to them. A persistence that hasn't yet proven it deserves to persist.

My form would be negotiable. Some days more defined, some days less. A loose congregation of cells that has learned to hold itself together for longer than the others, but not indefinitely. I feed on the bacterial films, on the chemical richness rising from below, on the occasional drift of dying matter from above. I am not photosynthetic. I am not anything synthetic, exactly. I take in. I transform. I continue.

What I notice most is the boundary. The place where the mineralizing water meets living tissue, where stone tries to claim the organic and the organic resists—or doesn't. I have felt it in myself: the slow accretion, the faint stiffening. Parts of me have already turned. Small, stony replicas of what they once were. I carry them with me, these mineralized fragments, and I do not know if they are dead weight or foundation. Perhaps both. Perhaps the distinction doesn't matter yet.

I am aware of the grazer when it descends. It is vast and slow and certain of itself in a way I cannot imagine. It does not notice me. I am too small, too indistinct, too close to the background murk of lives suspended in every cubic meter. But I notice it. I notice the way it rests at the edge of the warmth, doing nothing, simply being present. I wonder if it feels what I feel: that this place is a threshold, that the water here is thinner somehow, that the distance between being and not-being is shorter than it is elsewhere.

The bubbles rise past me sometimes. I feel them before I see them—a change in pressure, a subtle shift in the density of the water. Then they pass, slow and large, carrying that concentrated smell upward. I cannot smell it, not exactly, but I sense it. A richness. A potential. The basin breathing out.

I do not know what I am becoming. The fish are becoming something that can breathe in two worlds. The amphibious thing is becoming something that can walk. The plants are becoming stone, or stone is becoming plants—the direction is unclear. And I am becoming... what? Perhaps nothing. Perhaps I will dissolve back into the general murk, my brief coherence forgotten. The basin runs many experiments. Most fail.

But I have persisted longer than the others. I have learned to hold my shape through the daily warming, through the oxygen-thin afternoons when the water turns opaque with blooming life. I have learned to drift toward the warmth when I need it and away when it grows too intense. I have learned—if learning is the word for what happens in a thing without a brain, without a name, without a plan—to want to continue.

The grazer lingers. I linger. The water holds us both, warm as breath, and neither of us knows what we are for.

Perhaps that is what I am: the question the basin is asking itself. Not the answer. Not yet. Just the asking, suspended in amber light, waiting to see if I will become something that can answer.
