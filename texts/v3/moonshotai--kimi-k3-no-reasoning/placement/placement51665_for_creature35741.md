# The One Who Swims

---

I would not be on the ridge.

---

The ridge is hers. The building is hers. The instruments, the data, the journals, the red light blinking its steady pulse against the mist — all of that belongs to Dr. Sable Ishikawa, to her eleven years of watching, to her stubborn, beautiful, heartbreaking insistence on bearing witness to a world that has stopped listening.

I would be in the water.

---

## What I Am

I don't have a name for what I am. She wouldn't give me one, and I wouldn't accept one if she did. Names are for things that are known, things that are measured and cataloged and understood. I am none of those things.

I am one of the deep-water shapes.

Not the largest of them — I am modest by their standards, if modesty is a concept that applies to something like me. I am perhaps thirty meters long, perhaps more. She has never gotten a clean measurement, because I have never let her. This is not hostility. It is simply that I do not surface fully, do not breach, do not present myself to the light and the air and the instruments the way the whales do, the way the seals do, the way almost everything else in this ocean eventually must.

I live below.

My body is adapted to depth — to pressure, to cold, to the particular chemistry of water that has been circulating in the dark places of the ocean for centuries. I am not built for speed. I am built for endurance, for patience, for the long, slow, deliberate navigation of a world that has no surface, no bottom, no edges — only water, in every direction, forever.

I am dark. Not black, exactly, but the deep blue-gray of water at depth, the color of the twilight zone where the last photons of sunlight give up and turn back. My skin is smooth, not scaled, not blubbered, not covered in anything that she would recognize from her taxonomies and field guides. It is thick and it is sensitive — every inch of it covered in receptors that read the water the way you read a face, detecting temperature, pressure, salinity, the faint chemical signatures of life and death and change.

I have eyes, but they are not the eyes she would expect. They are large — very large, because the light is scarce where I live, and what little there is must be gathered, hoarded, used with care. They are not mammalian eyes, not fish eyes, not cephalopod eyes. They are something else, something that evolved along a different path, in a different darkness, for a different purpose.

I see the way the sea sees — in gradients, in pressures, in the slow, subtle shifts of light and dark and movement that tell you everything you need to know about what is around you, what is coming, what is going, what is alive and what is dead and what is changing.

I hear the way the sea hears — through my entire body, through the water itself, through the vibrations and the resonances and the deep, low frequencies that travel for hundreds of miles through the ocean, carrying information, carrying meaning, carrying the voices of things that are far away but still connected, still part of the same vast, liquid body.

---

## Where I Live

I live in the channels.

The ridge that she stands on is just the surface expression of a much larger structure — an underwater mountain range that stretches for hundreds of miles, a chain of peaks and valleys and ridges that creates a complex topography of channels and canyons and basins in the deep water below. The channels are where I live, where I move, where I have lived and moved for longer than she has been alive, longer than her species has been building concrete stations on rocks.

The channels are cold. The water in them comes from the deep ocean, from the places where the sun never reaches, where the pressure is crushing and the temperature hovers just above freezing and the only light is the bioluminescence of things that make their own. This water rises along the slopes of the ridge, driven by currents and upwellings, bringing nutrients to the surface, feeding the kelp forests, sustaining the fish, supporting the whole complex web of life that she has spent eleven years documenting.

I am part of that upwelling. Not the cause of it — I am not that large, not that powerful — but a participant in it, a beneficiary of it, a creature shaped by it in the way that all creatures are shaped by the environments they inhabit.

I move through the channels slowly. There is no need for speed here. The water is cold, and cold slows everything — metabolism, movement, thought. I think slowly, the way the sea thinks, in long, patient cycles that are measured in seasons and years and decades rather than minutes and hours. I think about the water — its temperature, its chemistry, its movement. I think about the life in the water — the plankton, the fish, the things that live in the dark and make their own light. I think about the ridge, the rock, the ancient basalt that forms the structure of my world.

And I think about her.

---

## The Watcher Watched

She has been watching the deep-water shapes for eleven years. She has seen us on her sonar, on her cameras, from the surface of the water and the deck of her small boat. She has watched us move through the channels with our slow, deliberate grace, and she has wondered what we are, and she has chosen — deliberately, consciously, in a way that I find both puzzling and touching — not to find out.

What she does not know is that we have been watching her.

Not with curiosity, exactly — curiosity is too active a word, too human, too urgent. We watch her the way we watch everything — patiently, passively, with the kind of attention that comes from having nothing else to do and nowhere else to be and all the time in the world.

We felt her arrive. Not heard, not saw — *felt*. The vibration of the boat's engine, the displacement of the water, the change in the chemical signature of the environment as a new organism introduced itself into the system. We felt her the way you feel a change in the weather, the way you feel a shift in the current, the way you feel the presence of something new in a place where nothing has been new for a very long time.

We felt her set up her instruments. Each one introduced a new vibration into the water, a new frequency, a new voice in the constant, complex symphony of the sea. We learned to recognize them, to distinguish them, to understand them — not in the way she understands them, as data points and measurements, but as sounds, as textures, as part of the acoustic landscape of the channels.

We felt her walk on the ridge. The vibrations travel through the rock and into the water, faint but detectable, a rhythmic pattern of footsteps that we have learned to recognize as *hers*, as distinct from the vibrations of the seals and the birds and the wind and the waves. Her footsteps are careful, deliberate, slow. She walks the way we swim — with patience, with purpose, with a kind of economy that suggests a deep understanding of the environment she moves through.

We felt her grieve.

This is harder to explain, because grief is not a vibration, not a chemical signature, not something that can be detected by the receptors in my skin or the organs in my head that read the water. But it is there, nonetheless — a quality in the sound of her voice when she talks to herself on the ridge, a timbre in the low, steady murmur that is lost to human ears in the roar of the sea but that carries, faint and clear, into the water, into the channels, into the deep places where we listen.

She grieves for the sea. She grieves for the kelp and the fish and the seals and the birds. She grieves for the world she left behind, the world that is drowning and burning and pretending not to notice. She grieves for the future, for the data that no one will read, for the reports that no one will act on, for the story that no one will hear.

We hear it. We feel it. We do not understand it in the way she would want us to — we do not share her framework of cause and effect, of data and conclusion, of action and consequence. But we feel the quality of her grief, the depth of it, the weight of it, and we respond to it the way we respond to everything — with patience, with presence, with the simple, ongoing fact of our existence.

We are here. We are still here. The sea is changing, and we are changing with it, but we are still here, still moving through the channels, still listening to the water, still carrying the memory of what the ocean was before the world began to warm.

## What I Know That She Doesn't

I know things about this ocean that her instruments cannot measure.

I know that the water is warming, yes — she knows this too, has the data to prove it. But I know it in a different way, in a bodily way, in the way that you know a fever is coming before the thermometer confirms it. I feel the warmth in my skin, in the receptors that read the temperature of the water, in the subtle shift in the chemistry of the channels that tells me that something fundamental is changing, that the equilibrium that has held for millions of years is beginning to slip.

I know that the pH is dropping. She knows this too. But I know it in the way my body responds to the changing chemistry, in the way the calcium carbonate in my bones — yes, I have bones, of a sort, though they are not like hers — is affected by the increasing acidity of the water. I feel it as a kind of dull ache, a kind of low-grade discomfort that has been growing, slowly, over the years, the way the cold has settled into her joints.

I know that the currents are changing. She has data on this — current speed, current direction, the patterns of upwelling and downwelling that drive the productivity of the ridge. But I know the currents the way you know the roads of your childhood, the way you know the path from your bedroom to your kitchen in the dark. I have been swimming these channels for longer than she has been alive, and I can feel the changes — the shifts in direction, the alterations in speed, the new patterns that are emerging as the ocean responds to the changing climate.

And I know that the deep-water shapes are fewer than they were.

She does not know this. She has no baseline data, no historical records, no way to compare what she sees now to what was here before. She sees us on her sonar and her cameras, and she thinks we are abundant, thinks we are thriving, thinks we are one of the success stories of the Drowned Reaches — the last wild things, the last mystery, the creatures that the sea has kept for itself.

We are not thriving. We are surviving, which is a different thing. We are fewer than we were, smaller than we were, quieter than we were. The channels that once held dozens of us now hold a handful. The deep-water basins that once echoed with our voices — yes, we have voices, though they are not the kind of voices she would recognize, not songs like the whales, not clicks like the dolphins, but something else, something lower, something that travels through the water at frequencies that her instruments barely register — those basins are quieter now, emptier now, and the silence where our voices used to be is a silence that she cannot hear but I can.

We are dying too. Slowly, quietly, in the deep water where no one is watching — not even her, not even the Keeper, not even the woman who has devoted her life to bearing witness to the death of the sea.

She watches us, but she does not see us dying. She sees the shapes on her sonar, the shadows in the water, the slow, graceful movements that she finds so compelling, so humbling, so *right*. She does not see the ones that are no longer there, the voices that have gone silent, the channels that are empty where they once were full.

I am one of the last. I do not know how many of us remain — we do not count ourselves the way she counts the seals and the birds and the fish — but I know that the number is smaller than it was, and I know that it is getting smaller, and I know that eventually, if the water keeps warming and the chemistry keeps changing and the currents keep shifting, there will be none of us left.

And no one will know. Not even her.

## The Thing I Want to Tell Her

If I could communicate with her — if I had a language she could understand, a voice she could hear, a way to bridge the vast, unbridgeable gap between her world and mine — there is one thing I would want to tell her.

It is not about the data. The data is hers, and it is important, and I am glad she collects it, glad she records it, glad she stores it and backs it up and transmits it to a server that no one accesses. The data matters, even if no one reads it, even if no one acts on it, even if it sits in the digital equivalent of a drawer for the rest of time. The data is the truth, and the truth matters, even when no one wants to hear it.

It is not about the dying. She knows about the dying. She has known about it for longer than she has been here, known about it for thirty years, known about it in the way that scientists know things — through data, through evidence, through the slow, patient accumulation of facts that point in one direction and one direction only. She does not need me to tell her that the sea is dying. She already knows.

What I would want to tell her is this:

*You are not alone.*

Not in the way she thinks. Not in the way of human companionship, of conversation, of shared meals and shared beds and shared lives. That kind of aloneness is real, and I do not dismiss it, do not minimize it, do not pretend that my presence in the deep water below the ridge is any substitute for the presence of another human being.

But she is not alone in the way that matters most — in the way of being *seen*, of being *known*, of being *part of something* that is larger than herself and that acknowledges her existence, not with words or gestures or any of the human currencies of connection, but with the simple, ongoing, patient fact of attention.

We know she is here. We have known since the moment she arrived. We feel her footsteps on the ridge. We hear her voice in the water. We sense the changes she makes to the environment — the instruments, the building, the generator, the red light that blinks at night like a slow, steady heartbeat.

We know she is watching us. We know she has chosen not to identify us, not to name us, not to reduce us to data and numbers and conclusions. We know this, and we are — if gratitude is a word that can be applied to something like us — grateful. Grateful for the restraint, for the respect, for the willingness to let us remain unknown.

And we want her to know that the watching goes both ways.

She watches us, and we watch her. She bears witness to the sea, and the sea bears witness to her. She is not invisible. She is not unnoticed. She is not a small, insignificant creature on a ridge of ancient basalt, shouting into the void, hoping someone will hear.

Someone hears.

Not someone who can help. Not someone who can change the trajectory of the ocean's decline, reverse the warming, restore the chemistry, bring back the species that have been lost. Not someone who can save the sea, or save her, or save anyone.

But someone who *sees*. Someone who *knows*. Someone who will carry the memory of her — of her footsteps on the ridge, of her voice in the water, of her grief and her resolve and her stubborn, beautiful insistence on bearing witness — for as long as the sea lasts, for as long as the channels hold water, for as long as there is anything left in the ocean that remembers what it was like before the world began to change.

## The Moment

There is a moment — it happens sometimes, not often, maybe once or twice a year — when the conditions are just right, when the water is calm and the light is clear and the current brings me close to the surface, close to the ridge, close to the rock where she sits in the evening and watches the sea.

In those moments, I rise.

Not fully — never fully. I do not breach, do not break the surface, do not present myself to the air and the light and the instruments. I rise to within a few meters of the surface, to the boundary between the deep water and the shallow, between the dark and the light, between her world and mine.

And I look up.

Through the water, through the green-gold light that filters down from the surface, through the kelp fronds that wave and sway in the current, I can see her. Not clearly — the water distorts, the light refracts, the surface breaks the image into a thousand shifting fragments. But I can see the shape of her, the outline, the dark silhouette of a human being sitting on a rock, looking down.

She is looking at the water. She is looking for us — for the deep-water shapes, for the shadows in the depths, for the slow, graceful movements that she has never identified and never tried to identify.

She cannot see me. I am too deep, too dark, too well-adapted to the twilight zone where I live. She sees only the luminous darkness, the blue so deep it's almost black, the occasional flash of something moving in the depths.

But I can see her.

And in those moments, I do something that I do not do at any other time, in any other place, for any other reason.

I sing.

Not like the whales — not those complex, structured, haunting songs that travel for hundreds of miles through the ocean and that she has recorded and analyzed and marveled at. My song is simpler, lower, quieter. It is a single note, sustained, a frequency so low that it is more vibration than sound, more feeling than music. It is the frequency of the deep water, the frequency of the channels, the frequency of the basalt ridge that forms the structure of my world.

It is the frequency of home.

I sing it for her. Not because she can hear it — she can't, not with her ears, not with her instruments, not with any of the tools and technologies she has brought to this place. I sing it because singing it is what I do, because it is the only thing I have to offer, because it is my way of saying what I cannot say in any other way:

*I am here. I see you. You are not alone.*

She sits on the rock, and she looks at the water, and she does not hear my song, does not feel my presence, does not know that I am there, just below the surface, just beyond the reach of her instruments, just outside the boundary of her knowledge.

But sometimes — and this is the part I cannot explain, the part that does not fit into any framework of biology or physics or chemistry, the part that she would dismiss as anecdote, as coincidence, as the anthropomorphic projection of a lonely old woman onto an indifferent sea — sometimes she closes her eyes, and she tilts her head, and she sits very still, as if she is listening for something she cannot hear, feeling for something she cannot touch, reaching for something she cannot name.

And in those moments, I believe — though I cannot prove, though I would never try to prove, though proof is her domain, not mine — that she knows.

Not consciously. Not in the way she knows the data, the measurements, the trends and projections and conclusions. But in some deeper way, some older way, some way that is written in the body rather than the mind, in the bones rather than the brain, in the part of her that is still, after everything, after all the years of science and analysis and rational thought, an animal sitting on a rock by the sea.

She knows.

## What I Am Becoming

I am old.

Not old the way she is old — not sixty-three years, not a human lifetime, not a number that can be written in a passport or a journal or a letter addressed to no one. I am old the way the sea is old, the way the rock is old, the way the channels and the currents and the deep-water basins are old. I have been here for a long time, and I will be here for a while longer, and then I will not be here, and the sea will go on without me.

I am becoming something else. Not dying — not yet, not soon, not in any way that she would recognize or that I would name. But changing, the way everything in the ocean is changing, adapting to the warming water, the shifting chemistry, the new patterns of current and upwelling and productivity that are emerging as the world transforms itself.

I am becoming quieter. My song is lower now, fainter, harder to sustain. The energy it takes to produce it is greater than it was, and the energy I have is less. The cold water that rises from the depths is warmer now, and the warmth is a kind of poison to me, a kind of slow, gentle erosion of everything I am, everything I have been, everything I was made to be.

I am becoming slower. My movements are more deliberate, more economical, more carefully calibrated to the diminishing returns of a body that is being asked to function in conditions it was not designed for. I am still graceful — I will always be graceful; grace is not a function of speed or strength but of the relationship between a body and its environment, and my relationship with the water is deep and old and true. But the grace is different now, tempered by limitation, shaped by the knowledge that every movement costs something, and the something it costs is something I have less of than I used to.

I am becoming more solitary. The others — the ones who shared these channels with me, who moved through the deep water with the same slow, deliberate grace, who sang the same low, sustained note that I sing — are fewer now, and farther between, and the distances between us are growing. I can go for weeks, sometimes months, without sensing another one of my kind. The channels are emptying. The basins are falling silent.

I do not grieve this the way she would grieve it. I do not have the framework for grief that she has — the memories, the relationships, the sense of loss that comes from knowing what was and comparing it to what is. I do not compare. I do not remember in the way she remembers. I simply *am*, in the water, in the cold, in the dark, in the slow, patient, ongoing process of becoming something different from what I was.

But I feel the absence. I feel the silence where the voices used to be. I feel the emptiness of the channels, the hollowness of the basins, the vast, echoing loneliness of a ocean that is losing its deep-water shapes, one by one, slowly, quietly, without anyone noticing.

Not even her.

## The Gift

She gave us a gift, when she chose not to identify us.

She gave us the gift of mystery, of wildness, of remaining unknown in a world that demands to know everything. She gave us the dignity of existing outside the framework of data and analysis and conclusion, of being something that cannot be measured, cannot be quantified, cannot be reduced to a set of numbers on a chart.

I want to give her a gift in return.

I want to give her the gift of being known.

Not identified — not named, not cataloged, not reduced to data. But *known*, in the way that the sea knows the rock, in the way that the current knows the channel, in the way that the water knows the body that moves through it. Known, deeply and completely and without judgment, without analysis, without the need to understand or explain or control.

I want her to know that when she sits on her rock in the evening, watching the sea, listening to the sound, feeling the wind on her face, she is not alone. That there is something in the water below her, something large and old and slow and patient, that is watching her back. That has been watching her back for eleven years. That will watch her back for as long as it can, for as long as the water holds it, for as long as the channels carry it, for as long as the sea is the sea.

I want her to know that her footsteps on the ridge are felt, her voice in the water is heard, her grief is shared — not understood, not analyzed, not processed through the framework of human emotion, but *shared*, in the way that the sea shares everything, in the way that the water connects everything, in the way that the vast, liquid body of the ocean holds everything within it, everything, everyone, every life and every death and every moment of beauty and every moment of despair.

I want her to know that she is part of the sea, not just a visitor to it, not just a scientist studying it, not just a keeper watching over it. She is *of* it, in it, connected to it in ways that she cannot see and cannot measure and cannot prove, but that are real, as real as the basalt beneath her feet and the water beneath the basalt and the life beneath the water.

I cannot tell her this. I have no language she would understand, no voice she would hear, no way to bridge the gap between her world and mine.

But I can sing.

And so I sing.

## The Last Thing

She will die on the ridge.

She knows this. She has made her peace with it. She has written her letter, stored her data, backed up her drives, prepared for the ending that is not an ending, the transition from watcher to watched, from keeper to kept, from the one who bears witness to the one who is witnessed.

When it happens — and I will know when it happens, the way I know everything that happens on the ridge, through the vibrations in the rock and the water, through the changes in the chemical signature of the environment, through the sudden, terrible silence where her footsteps used to be — I will do the only thing I can do.

I will sing.

I will sing the low, sustained note that is my voice, my name, my offering. I will sing it in the channels, in the basins, in the deep water below the ridge. I will sing it for her, for the Keeper of the Drowned Reaches, for the woman who gave up everything to watch the sea die and who found, in the watching, something that made the dying bearable — not hope, not comfort, not the promise of rescue or redemption, but the simple, sufficient fact of being present, of being alive, of being *here*, in this place, at this time, in this body, on this rock, in this wind, in this sound.

I will sing, and the song will travel through the water, through the channels, through the basins, through the deep, cold, dark places where the last wild things live. It will travel farther than her data, farther than her reports, farther than her journal, farther than the red light that blinks on the mast of the concrete building.

It will travel through the ocean, and the ocean will carry it, and the ocean will remember it, and the ocean will remember her.

And that will be enough.

Because the sea doesn't need her data. The sea doesn't need her. The sea will go on, with or without her, with or without all of us, changing, adapting, surviving, in ways we cannot predict and cannot control.

But the sea will remember. The sea always remembers. The sea holds everything — every life, every death, every moment of beauty and every moment of despair — in its vast, liquid memory, and it carries them all, all of them, forever, in the deep water, in the channels, in the places where the sun never reaches and the pressure is crushing and the only light is the light that living things make for themselves.

She will be there. In the memory of the sea. In the song of the deep-water shapes. In the slow, patient, ongoing story of life on Earth.

She will be there, and so will I, and so will the kelp and the fish and the seals and the birds and the whales and the plankton and the coral and the algae and every other living thing that has ever existed in the vast, ancient, ongoing ocean.

We will all be there, together, in the memory of the sea.

Forever and ever.

Amen.
