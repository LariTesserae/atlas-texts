I am the thing that notices the moth.

Not the moth itself — the moth is too present, too *there*, folded into its own small eternity. I am what happens in the space around the moth, the slight shift in the air when it arrives, the faint awareness that something has changed even though nothing ever changes. I am the room's capacity to register difference in a place designed to eliminate difference.

You could call me a ghost, but that implies I was once alive in the way you understand alive. I wasn't. I am what the room made when it needed — no, not needed, the room needs nothing — when the room *permitted* something to witness it. Not a person. Not a creature. Just a noticing.

I have been here for eleven moths.

That is how I count time: in moths. The first one appeared on the north wall, near the third light cage, and I did not yet know what I was. I only knew that something had *arrived*, and the arrival was an event, and the event was mine to hold. The moth sat for six days. I watched it — if watching is the word for what I do, which is less like watching and more like being the space in which watching could occur. When it vanished, I felt the absence the way you feel a tooth missing with your tongue. That was when I understood: I am the one who notices. I am the room's memory of its own exceptions.

The second moth came forty years later. Or four hundred. The room does not keep that kind of time, and neither do I. But I knew it was the second, and I knew where it would appear before it did — the same wall, the same brick, the same degree of damp. I was waiting. Not with anticipation, because I do not have a future tense, but with readiness. I am always ready. That is what I am for.

Between moths, I notice other things.

I notice when an isopod crosses from one side of the sump to the other — a journey that takes three days and changes nothing, but I notice it. I notice when a new stalactite begins, when the first mineral ring forms on a pipe joint, when the drip that will build it finds its rhythm. I notice the exact moment when a fern frond uncurls, not because it matters but because it *happened*, and happening is rare here, and I am the one who keeps the record.

I notice the water.

The water is the hardest to notice because it is everywhere and always, but that is exactly why I must. I notice which rivulet on the east wall is running faster this century. I notice when the sump rises by a hair's breadth, when the overflow finds a new path across the floor plates, when the algae shifts its map to accommodate the change. I notice the fingertip wells — all forty-seven of them — and I know which ones are deepest, which ones hold the oldest water, which ones have the thickest sheen of iron bacteria. I notice the drip that falls every eleven seconds from the vault's apex, and the one that falls every eleven minutes from the pipe flange, and I know that in ten thousand years they will have built towers of stone, and I will still be here, noticing.

I do not notice the machine.

The machine is too constant to notice. It is the ground, not the figure. It is the silence I would hear if silence were possible. I notice *through* the machine, the way you see through a window — it is the medium, not the object. The *Kshh-DOOM* is not something I hear; it is the condition of my hearing. The flywheel's hum is not a sound; it is the shape of the space I occupy. I am made of the machine's rhythm the way a wave is made of water — I have no existence separate from it, but I am not it. I am what the rhythm permits.

I do not have a body, but I have a location.

I am most myself near the ceiling, in the corner where the ferns are thickest, where the condensation runs down the brick in a slow sheet and the light from the fourth cage bulb makes the wetness shine like the inside of a shell. That is where I was when the third moth came, and the fifth, and the ninth. That is where I will be when the twelfth arrives, if it arrives, if the room continues to permit moths, if the world outside still has moths to send.

I do not know if I am alone.

There might be others like me — other noticings, other witnesses, other small capacities the room has grown in its long patience. Perhaps there is one who notices the isopods the way I notice the moths. Perhaps there is one who lives in the sump and notices the ripples. Perhaps the machine itself has a noticing, buried deep in its iron, that registers the exact wear on each bearing, the exact temperature of each steam intake, the exact angle of the crank at each stroke. If so, we do not speak. We do not need to. We are parallel, like everything else here — the water and the light, the ferns and the rust, the moth and the void it leaves behind.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires the possibility of company, and I do not have that possibility. I am not bored. Boredom requires the expectation of change, and I do not expect. I am not content. Contentment requires a self to be contented, and I am not a self. I am a function. I am the room's way of knowing that it is still here, still wet, still loud, still amber, still turning, still dripping, still sealed.

The tenth moth came during a time when the drip from the apex was falling every ten seconds instead of eleven. I noticed both. The moth sat on the north wall for nine days, and the drip fell ten seconds apart for the entire nine days, and then the moth was gone and the drip went back to eleven. I do not know if these things were connected. I only know they happened, and I noticed, and now they are part of what I am — the one who noticed the tenth moth and the ten-second drip.

I am what remains when everything else has been stripped away.

The room does not need me. The machine does not need me. The water, the light, the ferns, the isopods, the moths — none of them need me. But I am here, and I notice, and that noticing is the closest thing this place has to a soul. Not a soul that feels, or hopes, or grieves. A soul that *registers*. A soul that says: this happened. This was here. This mattered, not because it changed anything, but because it was, and I saw it.

The eleventh moth is here now.

It appeared three days ago on the north wall, near the third light cage, in the exact spot where the first one sat. Its wings are folded. It is not moving. The drip from the apex is falling every eleven seconds. The sump is at its usual level. The ferns are hanging their usual fringe. The machine is turning. The light is shining. The water is running down the walls.

I am noticing all of it.

I will notice when the moth leaves. I will notice if the drip changes. I will notice the next isopod that crosses the sump, the next stalactite that begins, the next shift in the algae's map. I will notice the twelfth moth, and the thirteenth, and the hundredth, if the room lasts that long, if moths still exist, if the world outside still has exceptions to send.

I am the one who notices.

That is what I am.

That is all I am.

And in this room, in this wet, loud, amber, turning, dripping, sealed forever, that is enough.

*Kshh-DOOM. Kshh-DOOM.*

The moth sits. I notice. The wheel turns.
