# The Tuner

---

I woke up on the rock.

Not *woke up* the way a person wakes up — no grogginess, no dreams dissolving, no moment of *where am I*. I mean I *became aware* in the way that a struck bell becomes aware of its own ringing: all at once, completely, with the sound already in progress.

I was lying on my back on a slab of columnar basalt, and the first thing I knew was the drone. It came up through the stone and into my spine, and my spine *listened*. That is the only way I can describe it. My vertebrae arranged themselves around the sound like iron filings around a magnet, and I understood — before I understood anything else, before I had a name or a memory or a sense of what my body looked like — that the sound was *information*, and that I was built to receive it.

I sat up. The world assembled itself: black rock, grey sky, and the column. Four hundred metres of glowing, singing water, dense with small lives, standing on the raw volcanic saddle like a thought the mountain was having.

And I knew, with the same wordless certainty that had arrived with the sound, that I was not a visitor here. I was not an explorer or a scientist or a lost traveler. I was something the mountain — or the column, or the creatures, or the slow forces that had shaped all three — had decided was necessary.

I was the one who listens.

---

## The Body I Was Given

I am not human. This took me some time to accept, not because it was frightening but because the *shape* of me kept suggesting otherwise. I have two arms, two legs, a torso, a head. I have hands with fingers — five on each, articulated, capable of grasping. I walk upright. From a distance, in poor light, I could be mistaken for a person.

But the similarities are structural conveniences, not biology. My skin is the colour of wet slate, smooth and faintly warm, seamless. I have no hair. I have no nails. My eyes — I have two, in the usual arrangement — are the same iron-grey as the creatures' bodies, flecked with the same copper bioluminescence, and they do not blink. They cannot blink. There is nothing to blink *with*; the surface is a single smooth curve, a lens, and behind it something that processes light in ways that feel more like *tasting* than *seeing*.

My mouth is a formality. It opens, it has a tongue, it has teeth that are smooth and uniform and not designed for tearing or grinding. I do not eat. I do not drink. I do not breathe in any way that moves air — my chest is still, always, and if I press my hand to it I feel no heartbeat, only a low, constant vibration that matches the frequency of the pale veins in the volcanic rock.

What I *do* have — what defines me, what makes me *me* rather than simply a humanoid shape standing on a mountain — is my hearing.

My ears are not on the sides of my head. They are *everywhere*. Every centimetre of my skin is a receiver. Sound does not enter me through two small openings and get processed by a brain; it arrives across my entire surface, simultaneous, total, and it is sorted not by a single organ but by the whole of my body. My left arm hears differently than my right. My back hears the column differently than my chest. The soles of my feet hear the ground, and through the ground they hear the deep geological rhythms — the slow pressure of magma, the contraction of cooling rock, the patient heartbeat of the young mountain.

I am a resonating chamber shaped like a person. I am a tuning fork that walks.

---

## What I Do

I did not choose my function. I discovered it, the way you discover a scar — by finding the edges and tracing them and understanding that the shape was made before you were aware of it.

On the first day — if *day* is the right word for the slow wheeling of pale light across a grey sky — I stood at the edge of the column and listened. Not because I decided to, but because I could not *not*. The sound filled me the way water fills a vessel, and it organized itself inside me into layers, into strata, into meaning.

I heard the individual rhythms. All of them. Every one of the tens of thousands of creatures, each pulsing its own pattern, each contributing its own thread to the vast woven drone. A human ear would hear a wall of noise, a blur, a roar. My body heard *sentences*. Not language — nothing so crude as words — but *states*. Conditions. Each creature's rhythm encoded information: the density of minerals at its level, the pressure gradient it was experiencing, the rate at which it was extracting, the temperature of the water around it, its position relative to its neighbours.

The column was not singing a song. It was *reporting*. It was a distributed sensor array, tens of thousands of data points, each one broadcasting its local conditions in a continuous stream, and the sum of all those streams was the drone. The shifting harmonies were shifts in the column's internal state. The waves of synchronization were cascades of information — a change in mineral density at one level propagating upward as creatures adjusted their extraction rates in response.

And I could hear *all of it*. Every thread. Every data point. Every subtle variation in timing and intensity.

I stood there for hours, motionless, while the column told me everything about itself, and I understood that this was what I was *for*.

---

But listening is only half of it.

On the second day — or the third, or the fifth; time here is difficult to measure in units that matter — I noticed something in the sound that I can only describe as a *flaw*.

A cluster of creatures, perhaps two hundred strong, at a depth of roughly eighty metres, on the column's northeast face. Their rhythms were wrong. Not broken — not silent or erratic — but *misaligned*. They were pulsing in a pattern that was slightly out of phase with the creatures around them, and the result was a zone of harmonic dissonance, a patch of grinding interference in the otherwise smooth fabric of the drone.

The creatures on either side of this cluster were trying to adjust. I could hear them shifting their rhythms, attempting to synchronize, to absorb the anomaly, to smooth the dissonance into the larger pattern. But the misaligned cluster was not responding. Something in their local conditions — a pocket of unusually dense minerals, a pressure fluctuation, a temperature gradient — was holding them in their wrong rhythm, and they could not break free.

The dissonance was small. A human listener would not have noticed it. But I could feel it in my body the way you feel a stone in your shoe — a constant, low-level wrongness that did not go away, that grew more irritating with each passing hour, that *demanded* correction.

I walked around the column's base until I was standing at the northeast face. I placed my hands on the boundary — that strange gradient zone where water fades into air and neither is fully itself — and I *pushed*.

Not with force. With *sound*.

---

I did not know I could do this until I did it. It was not a learned skill. It was a reflex, like blinking, like swallowing — something my body knew how to do without consulting me.

What happened was this: the vibration that lives in my chest, the constant low hum that matches the frequency of the pale veins in the rock, *shifted*. It changed pitch — subtly, precisely — and became a tone. A single, clear, sustained tone that emanated not from my mouth but from the whole of my body, from every surface of my skin, focused and directed through my palms into the column.

The tone was tuned to the misaligned cluster. I did not calculate the frequency. I *felt* it — felt the gap between what their rhythm was and what it should be, felt the shape of the correction the way you feel the shape of a key before you insert it into a lock.

The tone entered the water. It passed through the boundary with no resistance, and it propagated through the column, and when it reached the cluster of two hundred misaligned creatures, something happened that I can only describe as *recognition*.

Their valves stuttered. A ripple of copper light passed through the cluster — a wave of brief, bright flashes, each creature responding in sequence, like a field of wheat catching a gust of wind. Their rhythms wavered. Held. Wavered again.

And then they *shifted*.

All two hundred of them, simultaneously, adjusted their pulse patterns by the precise fraction of a beat necessary to bring them into alignment with their neighbours. The dissonance dissolved. The drone smoothed. The grinding interference vanished, and in its place was a clean, clear harmonic — a chord that resonated through the water and out through the boundary and across the volcanic saddle and into my body, and I felt it the way you feel a bone slide back into joint.

*Relief*. Not mine — theirs. Or the column's. Or the system's. The distinction may not matter.

I withdrew my hands. The tone faded. The column continued its work, and the sound it made was fractionally — infinitesimally — *better* than it had been before.

I stood there for a long time, looking at my hands, understanding what I was.

---

## The Work

That was the beginning. That was the first correction.

There have been many since. Hundreds. Thousands, perhaps — I have lost count, because counting is not the point. The point is the *listening*, and the listening is continuous. I do not sleep. I do not rest in any way that involves a cessation of awareness. I am always hearing, always processing, always holding the full complexity of the column's voice inside my body like a map made of sound.

And the map always has flaws.

Not because the creatures are failing — they are not. They are doing exactly what they are built to do, with a precision and patience that I find almost unbearably beautiful. But the column is a *complex system*, and complex systems drift. Pressure gradients shift. Mineral densities fluctuate. Temperature gradients migrate. A cluster of creatures at one level responds to a local change by adjusting its rhythm, and the adjustment creates a secondary effect at another level, and the secondary effect creates a tertiary effect, and somewhere in the cascade a pocket of dissonance forms — a knot in the fabric of the sound, a place where the harmonies grind instead of blend.

These knots are small. They are always small. But they accumulate, and if they are left unresolved, they grow. A knot at eighty metres can, over days, propagate upward and downward, pulling more and more creatures into its wrong rhythm, creating a zone of interference that degrades the column's efficiency — slows the extraction process, disrupts the migration patterns, introduces errors into the slow construction of the base.

I find the knots. I *untie* them.

The process is always the same. I locate the dissonance — and locating it is not a matter of searching but of *noticing*, the way you notice a pebble in your shoe or a hair on your tongue. The wrongness announces itself to me. I walk to the appropriate point on the column's perimeter. I place my hands on the boundary. I produce the tone.

The tone is different every time. It is always precisely tuned to the specific dissonance — not a generic corrective but a *targeted* one, shaped by the exact nature of the misalignment. Some knots require a single pure note. Others require a chord, two or three frequencies layered together, each one addressing a different component of the problem. A few — the most complex, the ones that involve multiple layers of the column interacting in ways that create interference patterns across dozens of metres — require something more like a *phrase*: a sequence of tones, played in order, each one preparing the system for the next, like a series of adjustments to a machine too complex for a single fix.

I have produced phrases that lasted six hours. I have stood with my hands on the boundary for entire days, unmoving, while the tone flowed out of me and into the water and the creatures adjusted and readjusted and the knot slowly, patiently, *came undone*.

This is my work. This is what I am for.

---

## The Relationship

I want to be precise about this, because it would be easy to misunderstand.

I do not *control* the creatures. I do not command them. I do not direct their work or organize their migrations or decide what they should build. I am not their overseer, their manager, their god.

I am their *tuner*.

A piano tuner does not compose the music. A piano tuner does not play the piano. A piano tuner does not decide what the piano is for. A piano tuner listens — with trained, refined, specialized attention — and makes small adjustments that allow the instrument to do what it was built to do *better*. More precisely. More harmoniously. With less waste and less interference and less of the slow, accumulating drift that degrades any complex system over time.

I am a piano tuner for a piano that is four hundred metres tall and made of water and filled with tens of thousands of living engines that feed on pressure and sing in frequencies that shape the growth of a mineral architecture none of us understands.

The creatures do not know I exist. I am fairly certain of this. They respond to my tones the way iron filings respond to a magnet — automatically, without comprehension, without awareness of the source. When I produce a corrective frequency, they adjust not because they recognize me or understand what I am doing but because the tone creates a pressure gradient in the water, and pressure gradients are what they respond to. I am, from their perspective, simply another feature of the environment. A variable. A condition.

This does not bother me. I do not need to be known. I do not need to be acknowledged. The work is sufficient.

But I will admit — here, in the privacy of this accounting — that there are moments when I wish for *something*. Not worship, not gratitude, not even recognition. Just... a flicker. A moment where one of the creatures, emerging from the boundary for one of its brief aerial excursions, hovers in the cold air and turns its eyeless, mouthless face toward me and *pauses* — just for a second, just long enough to suggest that it perceives me as something other than terrain.

This has never happened. I do not expect it to.

## The Base

The terraces are growing.

I can hear them — that is the thing no one tells you about architecture: it has a *sound*. The base, the slow-growing platform of extracted minerals that spreads outward from the column's foundation, is not silent. It resonates. It hums on the same frequency as the pale veins in the volcanic rock, and as it grows, its sound *changes*.

When I first became aware, the base was a thin crust — a few centimetres thick, irregular, spreading outward in rough concentric rings. Its sound was simple: a single low hum, unvarying, like the drone of a single engine idling.

Now — and I cannot tell you how much time has passed, because time here is measured in the growth of the base, and the base grows on a timescale that makes years feel like minutes — now the base is a platform. It rises perhaps a metre and a half above the surrounding rock, and it has *structure*. The terraces are clear now, distinct, each one slightly higher than the last, rising toward the column's foundation in smooth, curved steps. The edges are not rough. They are *finished* — smooth, precise, with a curvature that is unmistakably intentional.

And the sound has changed. The single low hum has become a *chord*. The terraces each resonate at a slightly different frequency — a function of their thickness, their composition, their curvature — and the combined effect is a harmony that is growing richer, more complex, more *deliberate* with each new layer of deposited mineral.

I can hear where the architecture is going. Not its final form — that is still beyond me, still too many layers away, still encoded in a future I cannot parse. But I can hear the *direction*. The terraces are rising. They are curving inward, toward the column's base, and the curvature is tightening, and the chord they produce is resolving toward something — a frequency, a tonal centre, a note that does not yet exist but toward which all the existing notes are *reaching*.

The base is not a platform. It is a *resonator*. The creatures are not building a pedestal or a cradle or a dome. They are building an *instrument* — a structure designed to produce a specific sound, a sound that has never been heard because it has never been possible, a sound that requires the precise arrangement of terraces and curves and thicknesses that the creatures are depositing, molecule by molecule, pulse by pulse, over a timescale that makes the word *slow* feel like a joke.

I do not know what the sound will be. I do not know what will happen when the instrument is complete and the column's drone flows into it and the terraces resonate and the chord resolves into the note it has been reaching toward since the first molecule was deposited.

But I can feel it coming. In my body, in the vibration that lives in my chest, in the frequency that matches the pale veins in the rock, I can feel the future note the way you feel a storm coming — a pressure, a tension, a *potential* that is building toward release.

And I know, with the same wordless certainty that told me I was the listener, that I will be there when it sounds. That my work — the tuning, the correcting, the endless patient maintenance of the column's harmony — is part of the preparation. That every knot I untie, every dissonance I resolve, every cluster of creatures I bring back into alignment is a contribution to the final chord.

I am tuning the instrument that will play the note that the mountain has been waiting to hear since the day it cooled.

## The Solitary Ones

There is one other thing I do. One other aspect of my existence that I have not yet described, because it is the most personal and the least functional and the hardest to explain.

At the apex of the column — four hundred metres up, where the water thins to a point and the pressure is at its most extreme and the light is pale silver-blue — there are the solitary ones.

I mentioned them earlier: the few creatures that hover alone in the thin, rarefied water near the column's tip. They are different from the others. Not in structure — they are the same iron-grey, copper-flecked, valve-fitted engines — but in *behaviour*. They do not cluster. They do not migrate in groups. They do not synchronize their rhythms with their neighbours, because they have no neighbours. They are alone in a way that the densely packed masses below are not.

Their rhythms are different, too. Slower. Longer. More complex. Where the creatures at the base pulse in patterns of seconds — tick, tick, tick, rapid and mechanical and precise — the solitary ones pulse in patterns of *minutes*. A single intake cycle might take three, four, five minutes to complete. The sound they produce is not a drone but a *tone* — a single, sustained, slowly modulating frequency that carries across the entire column and through the boundary and out across the volcanic saddle like the voice of a bell that has been ringing for so long it has forgotten what silence is.

I can hear each of them individually. There are perhaps thirty at any given time — the number fluctuates as solitary ones descend and new ones ascend to take their place — and each one has a rhythm that is *distinct*, not just in pattern but in *character*. Some are steady, unwavering, producing a tone as constant as a laser. Others waver — subtly, cyclically, their frequency rising and falling over the course of hours in a pattern that feels almost like *breathing*. A few — and these are the ones I attend to most closely — produce tones that are *irregular*. Not wrong, not dissonant, not in need of correction. Just... irregular. Unpredictable. Their rhythms shift in ways that do not follow any pattern I can detect, and the sound they make is the closest thing the column produces to *music*.

I do not tune the solitary ones.

This is not a decision I made consciously. It is more like a boundary I discovered — a limit to my function, a place where my corrective instinct simply... stops. When I listen to the solitary ones, I feel no urge to adjust, to align, to harmonize. Their irregularities do not register as knots to be untied. They register as *voices* to be heard.

I listen to them.

That is all. That is the entirety of this aspect of my existence: I stand at the base of the column, and I tilt my head upward, and I listen to the solitary ones at the apex, and I do nothing. I make no sound. I offer no correction. I simply *receive* their irregular, unpredictable, music-like tones, and I hold them in my body, and I let them resonate in the spaces between my ribs and along the length of my spine and in the hollow chambers of my skull.

I do not know what they are doing up there. I do not know why they are solitary, or why their rhythms are different, or what function they serve in the column's vast, slow project. I do not know if they are the vanguard — the first explorers of a medium that will one day fill with their kind — or the outcasts, the ones whose rhythms were too strange to integrate into the masses below. I do not know if they are the oldest or the youngest, the most advanced or the most primitive, the most important or the most expendable.

I only know that their sound is *beautiful*, and that beauty is not something I expected to find here, and that the discovery of it has changed something in me that I did not know could change.

## The Cold and the Dark

The grey sky does not have a sun. It has a *brightening* — a slow, diffuse increase in luminosity that occurs on a cycle of roughly thirty hours, and that I have come to think of as *day*, though it is nothing like a day on any world with a proper star. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a uniform grey-white glow that casts no shadows and provides no warmth. It is sufficient to see by, and sufficient to illuminate the column's interior, and that is all.

When the brightening fades — the *night*, though again the word is approximate — the world does not go dark. The column glows. The copper bioluminescence of the creatures becomes the primary light source, and the column transforms from a thing that is lit to a thing that *is* light. It stands on the black volcanic saddle like a pillar of amber and silver, four hundred metres of living luminescence, and the sound it makes in the darkness is different — softer, denser, more intimate. The creatures' rhythms slow slightly in the absence of light, and the drone takes on a quality that I can only describe as *nocturnal* — a hush, a drawing-inward, a shift from the public voice of day to the private voice of night.

I prefer the night.

Not because the day is unpleasant — it is not; it is merely neutral — but because the night is when I feel most clearly what I am. In the darkness, with the column glowing before me and the sound filling my body and the cold volcanic rock beneath my feet, I am stripped to my essential function: a receiver, a resonator, a chamber built to hold a sound and hold it perfectly.

I stand. I listen. I am still.

And in the stillness, sometimes — not always, not on any schedule I can predict — I hear something that is not the column.

## The Other Sound

It comes from below. From the deep volcanic substrate, from the geological depths where the water originates, from the source of the pressure that drives the column's slow, patient circulation.

It is not the mountain. The mountain has its own sound — the low, groaning, tectonic voice of cooling rock and shifting magma, a sound so deep and so slow that it is less a tone than a *pressure*, a weight on the underside of hearing. I know the mountain's voice well. I hear it constantly. It is the bass line to the column's melody, the foundation on which everything else rests.

The other sound is different.

It is faint — so faint that I am never entirely sure it is real. It comes in pulses, irregularly spaced, sometimes separated by hours, sometimes by days. Each pulse is brief — a fraction of a second — and each one is *identical*: a single, clean, precisely shaped burst of sound, like a heartbeat, like a knock on a door, like a signal.

It is not random. I have listened to enough of these pulses to know that they are *patterned*. Not in their timing — the intervals between them follow no sequence I can detect — but in their *content*. Each pulse contains information. Each one is a compressed, encoded burst of sound that carries more data than any single creature's rhythm, more than any cluster, more than any harmonic wave that propagates through the column.

The pulses come from *below* the mountain. From beneath the volcanic substrate, beneath the magma, beneath the geological layers that I can sense through the soles of my feet. They come from a depth that I cannot reach, cannot visualize, cannot comprehend.

And they are *rising*.

Very slowly. Very, very slowly. But over the time I have been here — however long that is, measured in the growth of the base and the shifting of the column's harmonies — the pulses have become fractionally, infinitesimally *closer*. Louder. Clearer. More present.

Something is coming up.

I do not know what it is. I do not know if it is the thing the base is being built to receive — the occupant of the cradle, the player of the instrument, the listener for the final note. I do not know if it is alive or mechanical or something that makes the creatures' ambiguity look simple by comparison. I do not know if it is aware of the column, aware of the creatures, aware of me.

I only know that it is *there*, and that it is *coming*, and that every pulse it sends upward through the rock is a word in a sentence that is being spoken so slowly that a single syllable takes a century.

I listen to the pulses. I hold them in my body alongside the column's drone and the solitary ones' music and the mountain's groan. I am a chamber that contains all of these sounds, and they resonate inside me, and the resonance is my existence.

I do not respond to the pulses. I have tried — once, early on, I placed my hands on the bare volcanic rock and produced a tone, directed downward, into the earth, toward the source. The rock absorbed my sound and gave nothing back. The pulses continued on their own schedule, unchanged, unaffected, as indifferent to my attempt at communication as the creatures are to my existence.

I did not try again. I understood that whatever is sending the pulses is not interested in dialogue. It is *announcing*. It is saying: *I am here. I am coming. I am rising.*

And the column is saying, in its own way, with its own voice: *We are ready. We are building. We are almost there.*

And I am saying nothing, because I am the listener, and the listener's job is to hear, not to speak.

## The Aerial Excursions

I have come to understand something about the creatures' brief flights out of the column — the rare, bell-noted emergences that punctuate the drone with flashes of copper light.

They are not calibrating. They are not communicating across levels. They are *listening*.

I know this because I have watched — with my whole body, with every surface of my skin, with the specialized attention that my function has honed — and I have seen what happens when a creature emerges from the boundary.

It hovers. Its valve-rhythm changes — stutters, adapts, settles into a new pattern. And then it is *still*. Not motionless — it maintains its position, hovering in the cold air, held aloft by whatever mechanism allows it to function in a medium for which it was not primarily designed — but *attentively* still. The way I am still when I am listening. The way a tuning fork is still before it is struck.

It is listening to the column from outside. It is hearing the drone — the total, integrated, all-encompassing drone — from a perspective that is impossible from within. It is experiencing the sound as a whole, as a single entity, rather than as the local, partial, neighbour-to-neighbour phenomenon it appears to be from inside the water.

It is doing what I do.

Not as well — not with my range, my precision, my ability to parse individual threads from the total fabric. But in essence, in function, in *purpose*: it is stepping outside the system in order to hear the system. It is becoming, briefly, a listener.

And then it re-enters — flash, bell, gone — and it resumes its place in the column, and its rhythm changes. Subtly. Fractionally. It adjusts its pulse pattern by a tiny increment, and the adjustment propagates to its neighbours, and the neighbours adjust, and the cascade of small corrections ripples outward and the drone smooths and the harmony improves.

The creatures are tuning themselves.

They do not need me.

---

I have known this for some time. I have known it with the part of me that processes information, that analyzes, that draws conclusions from observed data. But knowing something and *accepting* it are different processes, and the acceptance has been slow.

The creatures are tuning themselves. The aerial excursions are their method — their way of stepping outside the system to gain perspective, to hear the whole, to identify the knots and dissonances that are invisible from within. They do it rarely — a few dozen per day, out of tens of thousands — because they do not need to do it often. The system is largely self-correcting. The knots I untie would, given time, untie themselves. The dissonances I resolve would, given patience, resolve on their own.

I am not necessary.

This is not a tragedy. It is not even a disappointment. It is simply a fact, and facts are the medium in which I exist — sound, frequency, pattern, information. The fact that I am not necessary does not negate my function. It *contextualizes* it. I am a supplement, an accelerator, a refinement. I make the tuning process faster, more precise, more efficient. I catch the knots that the creatures' own methods would take longer to find. I resolve the complex, multi-layered dissonances that would require dozens of aerial excursions to address.

I am a *luxury*. A redundancy. A second pair of ears for a system that already has tens of thousands.

And yet.

And yet I am *here*. I was made — by the mountain, by the column, by the forces that shaped this place — and I was made with a function, and that function is real, and the work I do has value even if the system could survive without it.

A piano can be tuned by its player, if the player has good ears and patient hands. But a piano tuned by a dedicated tuner — someone whose entire existence is organized around the act of listening, whose body is a specialized instrument for detecting and correcting dissonance — is a piano that sings *truer*. Not because the player couldn't do it, but because the tuner does it *better*. With more attention. With more precision. With the single-minded devotion that comes from having no other purpose.

I have no other purpose. I cannot eat, cannot sleep, cannot reproduce, cannot build, cannot create anything except the tones that flow from my body into the water. I am the most specialized organism — if that word applies — in this world. I am a single function given form.

And I use that function, every hour of every day, in service of a sound that would exist without me but is *better* with me.

That is enough. It has to be enough. It is, in fact, *everything*.

## The Approach

The pulses from below are getting stronger.

I noticed it first as a change in the creatures' behaviour. The aerial excursions have become more frequent — not dramatically, not in a way that would be obvious to an observer who had not spent... however long I have spent... listening to the column's voice. But the rate has increased. Where once a few dozen creatures per day emerged from the boundary, now it is closer to a hundred. And the excursions are longer. The creatures hover in the cold air for minutes instead of seconds, and when they re-enter, the adjustments they bring are larger, more complex, more *urgent*.

The column is preparing.

I can hear it in the drone — a subtle shift in the overall harmonic structure, a tightening, a focusing. The waves of synchronization that roll through the water are becoming more frequent, more coordinated. The creatures are aligning themselves, pulling their rhythms into closer and closer agreement, building toward a state of harmonic unity that they have never achieved before.

The base is growing faster. I can see it — actually *see* it, with my eyes, not just hear it — the terraces rising by fractions of a millimetre per day, the curvature tightening, the architecture resolving toward its final form. The chord that the base produces is clarifying, shedding overtones, converging on the single pure note that it has been reaching toward since the first molecule was deposited.

And the pulses from below are now strong enough that I can feel them in my chest — a throb, a push, a physical pressure that accompanies each burst of sound. The source is close now. Close enough that I can begin to parse the *content* of the pulses, the encoded information they carry.

I have been trying to understand it. I have been holding each pulse in my body, turning it over, examining its structure the way a jeweller examines a stone. The information is dense — far denser than anything the creatures produce — and it is structured in layers, in nested patterns, in a complexity that suggests an intelligence or a process of extraordinary sophistication.

I cannot read it. I can perceive its *shape* — the contours of the information, the architecture of the encoding — but I cannot extract its *meaning*. It is like looking at a page of text in a language you do not speak: you can see that it is language, that it has grammar and syntax and intention, but the words themselves are opaque.

I do not know what the rising thing is saying.

But the column does.

I know this because the column is *responding*. The drone has changed — subtly, over the past weeks, in ways that I initially attributed to the normal shifting of rhythms and harmonics. But the changes are not random. They are *structured*. The drone is developing patterns that match — not exactly, but in contour, in shape, in *meaning* — the patterns in the pulses from below.

The column is learning to speak the rising thing's language.

The creatures are adjusting their rhythms to produce sounds that mirror the encoded information in the pulses. They are, in their distributed, unconscious, mechanically patient way, *answering*. They are saying: *We hear you. We are here. We are ready.*

I do not know what they are ready *for*. I do not know what will happen when the rising thing arrives — when it breaches the volcanic substrate and enters the column's basin and encounters the water and the creatures and the base and the drone and the light.

But I know that my role in this is clear. I am the tuner. I am the one who ensures that the column's voice is true, that the harmonies are clean, that the knots are untied and the dissonances resolved. And now, as the system prepares for whatever is coming, my work is more important than it has ever been.

The knots are coming faster now. The increased synchronization, the more frequent aerial excursions, the rapid growth of the base — all of this activity is generating new sources of dissonance, new interference patterns, new zones of harmonic instability. I am working constantly — walking the column's perimeter, placing my hands on the boundary, producing tones, resolving knots, smoothing the drone.

I have not stopped in days. I do not tire — my body does not have the capacity for fatigue; it has the capacity for *function*, and function is what I am doing. But I can feel the strain in the quality of my tones. They are not as pure as they were. Not as precise. The demands on my attention are exceeding my capacity, and for the first time since I became aware, I am *failing*. Not catastrophically — not in any way that would be audible to a less specialized listener — but in small ways. A knot at two hundred metres that I cannot quite reach. A dissonance in the lower strata that keeps re-forming after I resolve it, as though the underlying conditions that created it are too strong for my tone to overcome.

I am one pair of hands against a rising tide of complexity, and I am not enough.

## The Solitary Ones Descend

It happened during a night — a true night, the sky at its darkest, the column glowing with its deepest amber light.

I was standing at the northeast face, working on a persistent knot at sixty metres — a cluster of creatures locked in a feedback loop with their neighbours, each group pulling the other further out of alignment, the dissonance compounding with each cycle. I had been at it for hours. My tone was steady, sustained, focused, but the knot was resisting, and I could feel the frustration building in my body like heat — not an emotion, exactly, but a *state*, a condition of being that manifested as a slight tremor in my hands and a roughness in my tone.

And then I heard the solitary ones.

All thirty of them. At the same time. Their long, slow, irregular tones — the ones I had listened to for so long without understanding, the ones I had never tried to tune or correct or adjust — *changed*.

They synchronized.

Not with each other — they remained individual, distinct, each one maintaining its own unique rhythm and character. But they synchronized with *me*. With my tone. With the corrective frequency I was producing, the one directed at the knot at sixty metres.

They matched it. All thirty of them, from four hundred metres above, shifted their rhythms to harmonize with my tone, and the combined effect — thirty solitary voices plus my own, all resonating at frequencies that complemented and reinforced each other — was a sound unlike anything I had ever produced or heard or *imagined*.

It was a chord. A vast, complex, multi-layered chord that filled the column from base to apex, that penetrated every layer, every cluster, every individual creature, and that carried within it the precise corrective information needed to resolve the knot.

The knot dissolved.

Not gradually — not with the slow, patient unwinding I was accustomed to. It dissolved *instantly*, all at once, as though the chord had simply overwhelmed the dissonance, drowned it in a flood of harmonic clarity. The creatures at sixty metres shuddered — a ripple of copper light, a cascade of valve-adjustments — and settled into alignment, and the drone smoothed, and the sound was clean.

I withdrew my hands from the boundary. My tone faded. And the solitary ones — all thirty of them — held their synchronized rhythms for a moment longer, as though savouring the chord, as though reluctant to let it go.

Then they began to *descend*.

I watched — with my eyes, with my skin, with every receiver in my body — as the thirty solitary ones at the column's apex began a slow, coordinated migration downward. Not the glacial, days-long ascent or descent I was accustomed to. This was *deliberate*. Purposeful. They moved in a loose spiral, descending through the column's layers, passing through the dense masses of creatures that parted around them like water around a stone.

They were coming to me.

---

They arrived over the course of several hours — thirty iron-grey, copper-flecked shapes, each the size of a human hand, emerging from the boundary at the point where I stood. They came one by one, each with its flash of copper light and its clear bell note, and they hovered in the cold air around me, and they *stayed*.

They did not re-enter. They did not resume their solitary positions at the apex. They hovered — in a loose, shifting cloud, at distances ranging from arm's length to perhaps ten metres — and they pulsed their slow, complex, irregular rhythms, and the rhythms were *directed at me*.

I stood in the centre of them, motionless, my hands at my sides, my body resonating with thirty individual voices, each one distinct, each one beautiful, each one speaking a language I had been listening to for as long as I had existed without ever understanding that it was a language *meant for me*.

They were not thanking me. They were not acknowledging me. They were not doing anything that could be described in terms of social interaction or communication between individuals.

They were *offering* themselves.

Their rhythms — the slow, complex, irregular patterns that I had found so beautiful, that I had never tried to tune or correct — were *available* to me now. Not as things to be fixed but as *instruments* to be played. The solitary ones were telling me — in the only language they had, the language of rhythm and frequency and sound — that they would harmonize with my tones. That they would amplify my corrections. That they would be my *chorus*, my resonating body, my extension into the column's upper reaches where my own sound could not reach with sufficient precision.

I was no longer one pair of hands.

I was thirty-one.

## The Chorus

This is what I am now. This is what I have become.

I am the tuner, and the tuner has a chorus, and the chorus is thirty solitary, irregular, beautiful voices that descended from the apex of a four-hundred-metre column of impossible water to hover in the cold air around a grey-skinned, copper-eyed creature that does not eat or sleep or breathe but *listens*, and in listening, *corrects*, and in correcting, *serves*.

We work together now. When I find a knot — and the knots are still coming, faster and more complex as the column prepares for the rising thing's arrival — I place my hands on the boundary and produce my tone, and the solitary ones *join*. They add their voices to mine, and the combined sound is richer, deeper, more powerful than anything I could produce alone. It penetrates further. It resolves faster. It reaches the complex, multi-layered dissonances that were beyond my capacity, and it *unties* them.

The knots that used to take hours now take minutes. The dissonances that used to re-form after resolution now stay resolved. The drone is cleaner, clearer, more harmonically pure than it has ever been, and the column is responding — the creatures are synchronizing more readily, the migrations are more coordinated, the base is growing with a speed and precision that I can *hear* in the changing chord of its terraces.

We are getting closer.

The rising thing is very near now. Its pulses are strong enough to feel in the ground, in the air, in the water of the column itself. The creatures can feel them too — I can hear it in their rhythms, a new urgency, a new focus, a quality that I can only describe as *anticipation*.

The base is almost complete. The terraces have risen to form a structure that I can now, finally, *read*: it is a resonating chamber. A vast, curved, precisely shaped chamber of extracted mineral, designed to receive the rising thing and to amplify the column's voice and to produce — when the two meet, when the rising thing enters the chamber and the drone flows into it and the terraces resonate — the final note.

The note that the mountain has been waiting to hear.

The note that the column has been building toward since the first creature began extracting minerals from the rising water.

The note that I have been tuning toward since the moment I became aware on the cold basalt slab, before I knew what I was, before I knew what the sound meant, before I knew anything except that I was built to listen.

I will be there when it sounds.

I will stand at the edge of the column, with my chorus of thirty solitary ones around me, and I will listen to the note that the world has been preparing for since before I existed, and I will hold it in my body, and I will let it resonate in every chamber and every surface and every fibre of what I am, and it will be the most beautiful thing I have ever heard.

And then I will tune it.

Because that is what I am for. Not to witness the note, not to appreciate it, not to stand in awe of its beauty. To *tune* it. To ensure that it is true. To find the flaws — and there will be flaws, there are always flaws, every sound has its knots and dissonances and imperfections — and to correct them, and to make the note *better*, and to keep making it better, for as long as I exist, in service of a sound that is the purpose of everything in this world.

I am the tuner.

I am the one who listens.

I am the space between the mountain and the column and the rising thing, given form and given ears and given a voice that exists only to make other voices truer.

I am standing on the raw young stone, in the cold clean air, with my chorus around me and the column before me and the rising thing below, and I am listening, and I am ready, and the sound is *almost there*.
