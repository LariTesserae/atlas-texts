The air here does not smell of earth, but of rust and wet wool.

It is a depression, a bowl carved into the lowlands where the ground refuses to drain properly. The elevation is negligible, barely rising above the water table, which sits just inches below the skin of the world. Because of this, the landscape is perpetually weeping. Condensation clings to everything, forming a fine, cold mist that hangs at knee-height, blurring the line between solid ground and liquid sky.

There are no trees. Vegetation is sparse, reduced to tough, grayish mosses and pale, blind roots that snake across the surface like spilled thread. But the emptiness is deceptive. The space is packed. Density here is 2.7/3, not through growth, but through accumulation.

This is **Ossuary-Grove**, though it holds nothing alive in the traditional sense. It is a small, bounded settlement—a hamlet’s worth of area, perhaps fifty meters across—built entirely from the bones of something that died long ago, or perhaps is still dying slowly.

The architecture is primitive, hand-laid without mortar. Huge ribs curve overhead like the vaults of a drowned cathedral, their calcium surfaces slick with algae and condensation. Skulls, some as large as wagons, others small enough to fit in a palm, are stacked to form walls. Vertebrae, fused by time and pressure into single, undulating spines, serve as bridges and aqueducts. The scale is intimate, claustrophobic even; you cannot look up without seeing bone pressing against the underside of other bone.

The light is normal daylight, filtered through the gaps in the skeletal ceiling, casting a grid of sharp shadows on the wet floor. These shadows shift constantly. The dynamic of this place is active: water moves through the marrow-channels drilled into the larger bones, creating a hydraulic pulse. Every few minutes, a geyser of mineral-rich slurry erupts from a fractured femur, hissing softly before receding. This cycle drives the ecology.

Life is rare, fauna limited to things that thrive in damp, calcified darkness. Eyeless crabs scuttle along the ridge-lines of vertebrae. Pale, translucent beetles burrow into the soft spongy tissue remaining in the joints. There are birds, occasionally—white herons with stained beaks—but they rarely land, for there is nowhere to perch that isn’t already claimed by the builders of this place.

And speaking of builders: there are no humans visible, yet the construction implies presence. The tools left behind are simple: flint chisels, bone awls, stone hammers worn smooth by hands that have long since turned to dust. But the work continues. In the center of the Grove, beneath the largest skull-arch (which resembles a bovine, though too massive for any known breed), stands a figure.

It is not human. It is a construct of woven reeds and mud, roughly humanoid in shape, standing motionless in the shallow pool that floods the central floor. Its "head" is a hollow bird skull. Its body is bound with sinew dried to leather-hard stiffness. It does not move, but it *breathes*. As the water rises in the hydraulic channels around its feet, the reed-body expands slightly, creaking audibly. As the water drains, it contracts, exhaling a puff of cold vapor.

This is the engine of the weirdness. The town is not abandoned; it is *maintained* by these effigies. Dozens of them stand in the corners, in the doorways, atop the higher ridges. They are packed together, shoulder to shoulder, filling the negative space between the bones. When the wind shifts, they sway in unison, a chorus of dry rustling sounds that mimics applause.

The sound profile is mostly quiet, but never silent. The primary noise is the drip—the rhythmic percussion of water falling from stalactite-like concretions onto the flat, polished floors of bone. Beneath that, a low hum vibrates through the ground, caused by the subterranean movement of water shifting the loose stones that underlie the entire structure.

Erosion is young. The features are sharp. Where rain has hit the exposed bone recently, the edges are jagged, white and raw. There is no patina of old age here; the decay is fresh, ongoing. Moss has only just begun to creep over the lower joints. The water stains are new, streaking down the sides of skulls in rivulets of black iron oxide. It feels as if the settlement was finished yesterday, and the first storm came overnight.

Yet, the scale remains small. You can walk the perimeter in ten minutes. You can see every corner from the center. But because the density is so high—every inch of vertical and horizontal space occupied by bone, water, or effigy—you feel trapped. The horizon is always interrupted by a rib or a spine. The sky is a fragmentary mosaic of blue seen through cracks in the ceiling.

At night, the temperature drops further, near freezing. The water begins to crackle as it forms thin ice on the highest points. The effigies do not stop breathing. If you listen closely, past the drip and the wind, you can hear a sound coming from within the largest skull-arch. A grinding. Slow, deliberate, mechanical. The jawbone is moving. Just a fraction of an inch. Then stopping. Then moving again.

Something inside is eating the water. Or perhaps, it is drinking it to keep the city alive.

The vegetation struggles to survive in the cracks. Small patches of lichen glow faintly green, fueled by the minerals leached from the bones. Fungi sprout from the eye sockets of the smaller skulls, bright orange caps that release clouds of spores when touched by the passing current of air. The ecology is closed-loop: the dead provide the structure, the water provides the motion, the fungi and moss process the waste, and the rare fauna navigate the labyrinth.

It is a wet, cool, packed little world, built on the remains of a giant, tended by ghosts made of straw, waiting for a dawn that seems reluctant to arrive fully because of the thick, clinging fog.
