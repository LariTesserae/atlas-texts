The air here is thin, not because of altitude alone, but because it has been scrubbed clean. It tastes of copper and ozone, sharp against the back of the throat, carrying no scent of rot, bloom, or damp earth. There is no moisture to hold odors, so things smell only of themselves: hot metal, dry stone, static electricity.

This is the High Plateau of Oresund, a vast expanse of rock that sits so high above the cloud line that the sky is a permanent, bruised violet during the day and a piercing, star-dense black at night. The elevation is extreme—2.8 on the scale—meaning gravity feels slightly less burdensome here than in the lowlands, giving every movement a strange, dreamlike elongation. A person can leap higher; dust hangs longer before settling. But the wind, though rare, hits with the force of a physical blow.

There is no water. Not a drop. The concept of liquid rain is a myth from the lower strata, where the pressure is too great for anything solid to remain unchanging. Here, chemistry is non-aqueous. The "rivers" are flows of molten glass, cooled into jagged, translucent ridges that snake across the landscape like frozen lightning. They glow faintly amber from residual heat deep within the crust, providing the only warmth in an otherwise stark environment. The temperature is warm (1.8), not due to sunlight—which is harsh and white—but due to geothermal venting. Pockets of steam-free gas escape from fissures in the rock, heating the immediate surroundings to a balmy 25°C, while just ten meters away, the shade drops to freezing. Life clings to these thermal pockets.

Vegetation is absent in any traditional sense. No leaves, no stems, no roots seeking soil. Instead, the ground is carpeted in *silica-moss*, a slow-growing, crystalline structure that spreads over the bedrock like frost. It is brittle, chiming softly when stepped on. Interspersed among the silica-moss are *piezo-fungi*—not true fungi, but mineral networks that grow through electrical conduction. They form delicate, branching lattices of conductive wire-like filaments, pale gray and silver. They do not photosynthesize; they harvest ambient electromagnetic noise. When a storm passes miles below, visible as a churning sea of clouds far beneath the plateau’s edge, the piezo-fungi light up in synchronized pulses, turning the ground into a shimmering map of data transmission.

Fauna is abundant (1.9), but it is silent and strange. These are creatures of bone and crystal, evolved to survive in a dry, cold-thin atmosphere. The dominant species are the *Glass-Striders*: tall, multi-legged arachnids made of semi-translucent chitin. Their legs are long and spindly, designed to distribute weight over the fragile silica-moss without breaking it. They move slowly (dynamic 1.0), deliberate and patient. They do not hunt by sight, but by vibration. A footstep on the moss sends a ripple through the crystalline network; the Strider freezes, tilts its head, and waits. They feed on the mineral-rich secretions of the piezo-fungi, licking the charged crystals with tongues of hardened keratin. Their bodies refract the normal light (1.7) into rainbow spectra, making them appear as shifting ghosts against the gray backdrop.

Another common sight is the *Wind-Kite*: flat, triangular organisms that drift on the upper thermals generated by the geothermal vents. They have no limbs, only wings of membrane stretched over cartilage frames. They absorb solar radiation and convert it into stored kinetic energy, releasing bursts of speed only when threatened. In their idle state, they hang motionless in the air, hundreds of feet up, looking like discarded fragments of stained glass floating in the void.

The sound level is mostly quiet (1.2). This is a deceptive silence. There is no rustle of leaves, no chirp of birds, no roar of surf. But there is a constant, low-frequency hum—the resonance of the piezo-fungi network processing the planet’s magnetic field. It is felt more than heard, a subtle vibration in the teeth and bones. Occasionally, a Glass-Strider will click its mandibles, a sound like two stones striking together, sharp and sudden in the stillness. Or a shard of cooled glass river might crack under thermal stress, sending a ping echoing across the canyon walls.

Erosion here is established (2.3), but it is not done by water. It is done by time and temperature. The rocks are weathered into smooth, rounded shapes by the abrasive sandstorms that sweep up from the lower world, then quickly subside. The land looks settled, ancient, worn into its own equilibrium. Cliffs stand sheer and perfect, carved by eons of differential heating and cooling. Valleys are U-shaped, wide and open, suggesting glaciers once existed here in a different epoch, or perhaps formed by the collapse of massive lava tubes now empty.

Scale is vast (2.6). From the highest peak, you can see curvature of the horizon. The clouds lie below you like a floor, white and fluffy, obscuring the world beneath. Above, the stars are visible even at noon. The distances are measured in days of travel, not hours. The mind struggles to comprehend the emptiness between points.

And yet, there is construction. Urban (1.8), but not urban as we know it. Scattered across the plateau are the *Foundries of the Dry*. These are not cities of housing, but industrial complexes built directly into the rock faces. Great smokestacks vent colored gases—green, yellow, orange—that dissipate instantly in the thin air, creating ephemeral auroras. Factories stretch along ridgelines, their roofs made of reflective metal plates that angle precisely to capture sunlight and direct it into interior furnaces. Pipes run everywhere, thick and insulated, carrying molten silicon and refined metals between stations. The density is sparse (1.4); buildings are miles apart, connected by overhead rail lines that glide silently on magnetic cushions. You see one structure, then another on a distant peak, tiny against the immensity.

Technology is industrial (2.3), heavy and mechanical. Gears grind behind sealed bulkheads. Pistons thud with rhythmic patience. Robots move through the foundry yards, replacing failed components with spare parts from warehouses stocked with raw ore. There is no internet cloud, no wireless signal beyond short-range induction fields used by the piezo-fungi. Data is carried physically on tablets of etched crystal, delivered by runner-bots that zip along the rails. The machines are large, complex, and require constant maintenance due to the corrosive nature of the mineral dust and the thermal stresses.

Weirdness (2.2) pervades everything. The rules of this world are inverted. Heat comes from the ground, not the sun. Life eats electricity, not carbon. Sound is rare, but vibration is universal. Time moves slowly here, marked not by seasons, but by the cooling cycles of the glass rivers. A single river may take fifty years to cool completely from red-hot to opaque white. During that time, the ecosystem around it shifts subtly, adapting to the changing thermal gradient.

At dusk, when the sun dips below the cloud-sea, the Foundries light up. Amber glow spills from furnace windows. The piezo-fungi begin to pulse brighter, responding to the increase in atmospheric static. The Glass-Striders emerge from their crevices, moving toward the warmest spots near the exhaust vents. The Wind-Kites descend slightly, seeking stable layers of air. And the silence deepens, broken only by the hum of the network, the tick of cooling metal, and the soft, crystalline crunch of a thousand tiny feet walking on the singing moss.
