The air is not empty; it is thick with the green.

You are below the surface, but "below" implies a ceiling of rock or earth, and here there is no such thing. You are inside a hollow sphere, a vast subterranean chamber where the walls are not stone, but living tissue. The elevation parameter suggests you are buried, yet the light—blinding, absolute, white-hot radiance—comes from everywhere at once. It permeates the translucent membranes of the flora that coats every inch of this space. There are no shadows here, only varying intensities of chlorophyll and cellulose.

This place is emerging. It has no history. There are no fossils in the ground because there is no ground. There are no ruins because there was never a city. The landscape is currently *becoming*. It is raw geology in its biological phase, a tectonic plate made of leaves rather than granite. If you touch the wall to your left, it feels warm, like skin, and yields slightly, like dough rising. The material is soft, spongy, and wet.

Water is present, but not as pools or rivers. It exists as a fine, perpetual mist that clings to the surface tension of the plants. The humidity is near one hundred percent, making the air taste metallic and sweet. Droplets form on the tips of fronds that stretch hundreds of feet into the central void, coalesce into beads the size of apples, and then fall. But they do not splash. When they hit the floor—which is a dense mat of moss-like tendrils—they are absorbed instantly, pulled down through capillary action into the root-network that binds this world together. The moisture is intermittent in its movement but constant in its presence; condensation happens faster than evaporation, creating a cycle of dampness that keeps everything swollen and heavy.

Vegetation is not just covering the land; it *is* the land. Trees grow sideways, arching across the open space like ribs. Vines hang in curtains so dense they block sightlines, yet the brightness of the light allows them to glow from within. This is plant-as-architecture. Massive fungal structures rise like pillars, their caps broad enough to support entire ecosystems of smaller epiphytes. Leaves overlap so tightly that they form solid barriers, walls of photosynthetic flesh. To move forward, you must push through layers of foliage that spring back when released. The density is overwhelming; there is no room for a person to stand without being touched by three different species of fern.

There are no tools. No metal. No glass. No fire. Technology is zero. Everything is biological function. The "structures" you see scattered around are not built; they are secreted. A mound here looks like a hut, but it is a calcified mass of root systems hardened by sap. A spiral structure resembles a staircase, but it is the dried husk of a giant vine that grew upward and curled inward over millennia (or perhaps seconds, since time here feels non-linear due to the lack of change). These artifacts are faint marks of growth patterns, not intent. They are debris of the system’s own construction process.

Fauna is rare. You might see a single insect, pale and wingless, crawling along a stem, but it moves with agonizing slowness. There are no birds, no mammals, no predators. The ecology is self-contained and static. Nothing eats anything else here, because nothing dies in the traditional sense. Waste is reabsorbed. Death is compost turned immediately into new growth. The few animals present are likely symbiotic parasites, too small to notice, blending into the cellular texture of the leaves.

The sound is mostly quiet. Not silent—the world hums with the low-frequency vibration of sap moving through xylem—but quiet enough that your own heartbeat becomes loud. There is no wind, because the air is too still, too saturated. There is no rustle, because the plants are too dense to sway. Occasionally, a large leaf will tear under its own weight, a wet, snapping sound like fabric ripping, followed by a slow drip. That is the loudest event that occurs in an hour.

The temperature is hot. A consistent, oppressive heat, like the inside of a mouth or a sealed terrarium. It presses against your skin. Sweat does not evaporate; it pools. The heat drives the transpiration, which creates the mist, which traps the heat. It is a closed loop of thermal energy generated by metabolic processes. You feel sticky, wrapped in a layer of warm moisture.

But the most striking feature is the stability. The dynamic parameter is 0.1/3. Nothing changes. The sun—if there is a sun—does not set. The light remains at peak intensity forever. The rain never stops falling, nor does it ever dry up. The plants do not wilt, nor do they grow taller than their current state allows. They are frozen in a moment of maximum expansion. It is a landscape caught in amber, except the amber is alive and breathing.

You walk through a corridor formed by two intersecting arcs of woody vines. The bark is smooth, polished by the humid air. As you move deeper into the open scale of the region, the view expands. The chamber seems infinite, a cavernous bowl lined with jungle. In the distance, you can see what look like buildings—towers of braided roots reaching toward the zenith of the hollow—but they are merely clusters of vegetation that have grown together. There are windows, but they are holes where branches failed to reach. There are doors, but they are gaps in the thicket.

It is a world designed for eyes that see color more than shape. Every shade of green, from deep forest emerald to bright lime-yellow, exists here. The weirdness comes from the geometry: things curve where they should be straight, angles meet at points that shouldn't exist, and the scale shifts. A flower nearby may be the size of a dinner table; another may be microscopic. Both belong to the same ecosystem.

Here, water, plant, and light are the same substance viewed from different angles. The moisture is part of the plant; the light is trapped inside the leaves. You cannot separate them. To breathe is to inhale the forest. To touch is to hold the water. To look is to see the heat.

And beneath it all, the emergence continues. Slowly, imperceptibly, new nodes swell on the vines. New roots probe the damp floor. The landscape is building itself, brick by organic brick, in a place where the bricks are already finished, waiting for a hand that will never come to lay them down.
