The sky is not a sky. It is a ceiling of hammered copper, beaten thin by centuries of wind and prayer, oxidized into a verdigris so vivid it hurts the eyes. Below it, there is no horizon line in the traditional sense; instead, the world rises to meet you in layers of packed, wet stone.

This is the Plateau of the Damp Forge, a high-altitude expanse where the air is thin but heavy with moisture, hanging low like a bruised fruit ready to burst. The elevation pushes you toward the sun—light here is brutal, unfiltered, bright enough to bleach bone—but the temperature remains stubbornly cool, hovering just above freezing, kept in check by the relentless evaporation from the water that dominates every surface.

There are no trees. No moss. No lichen clinging to the rocks. Vegetation has been entirely absent since the Great Scouring, replaced by a mineral ecology that mimics growth through sheer accumulation. The ground is paved, not with natural rock, but with *made* things. Every inch of visible space is occupied by structures, tools, or debris, packed so densely that walking requires stepping over feet of stacked, wet artifacts. This is the density of 2.8: a saturation of presence. There is no empty air between objects; there is only the humid gap where breath condenses on cold metal.

The terrain is young in its current form. These are not ancient ruins crumbling under gravity; they are fresh cuts. Sharp edges define the landscape. Recently chiseled basalt blocks, still rough-hewn, lie alongside polished bronze gears that have not yet tarnished completely. The erosion score of 0.7 suggests that while the materials are old, the configuration is new. The land has been reshaped within living memory. A landslide last season revealed a sub-layer of ceramic tiles, each one hand-glazed, forming a mosaic floor that stretches for miles beneath the current rubble.

Water is the binding agent. It does not flow in rivers; it weeps. From the porous faces of the constructed cliffs, from the joints in the tiled floors, from the hollows of discarded clay vessels, water seeps out constantly. It is not rain, though rain falls occasionally—it is the land itself sweating. Pools form in the depressions of packed stones, shimmering with an oily iridescence. Rocks are slick, black, and glossy, reflecting the harsh sunlight in distorted mirrors. If you touch a wall, your hand comes away wet. If you lean against a pillar, your clothes dampen within seconds. The atmosphere is thick with mist that clings to the skin, cool and smelling of iron and wet chalk.

Technology is primitive but pervasive. There are no machines with moving parts driven by steam or electricity. Instead, the world is built of simple fabrication: levers, pulleys, wedges, and counterweights. Massive stone wheels, grooved for rope, sit idle at intersections, their surfaces worn smooth by hands that have long since turned to dust. Cranes made of bundled timber (imported, perhaps, from some distant forest that no longer exists) loom over the plateaus, silent sentinels. The tech level is 0.8: manual craft elevated to a civilizational scale. Every structure was built by hand, lifted by human strength aided by simple mechanics, and assembled without mortar, relying on friction and weight.

Sound is minimal. The quietness is oppressive, broken only by the slow, rhythmic *drip-drip-drip* of condensation falling from the copper sky onto the stone below. Occasionally, a shift in the temperature causes a metal beam to groan, a single note expanding in the thin air before dying out. There is no bird song, no insect hum, no rustle of leaves. The fauna parameter is 0.1: essentially nonexistent. Life, if it exists, is microscopic or fungal-like, thriving in the cracks between the wet stones, invisible to the naked eye. You feel alone in a way that transcends solitude; you are alone in a place that was never meant to hold life, only labor.

Yet, the place feels inhabited. Not by people, but by intent. The packing of the materials is deliberate. Tools are laid out in rows along the edges of pathways, as if waiting for workers who will return tomorrow. Clay pots are stacked in pyramids near water sources. The weirdness (1.4) emerges from this juxtaposition: the absolute stillness and absence of biology against the overwhelming evidence of recent, intensive human activity. It looks like the moment immediately after everyone left, but stretched over decades. The water keeps rising, filling the bowls and basins placed strategically across the plateau, overflowing them slowly, pooling in the low points of the constructed maze.

As you walk deeper into the packed environment, the path narrows. Walls of wet stone press in, decorated with geometric patterns carved deep into the rock face, filled now with standing water that turns the carvings into dark, fluid shadows. The light reflects off these wet surfaces, bouncing around the narrow corridors, creating illusions of movement where there is none. Shadows seem to drift like ghosts. The heat of the sun beats down on the open areas, making the air shimmer, while the shaded alleys remain frigid, breath visible in the sudden drop in temperature.

There are bridges spanning chasms that lead nowhere, connecting two walls of identical construction. They are slick with spray. To cross them is to risk slipping into the depths below, which are filled not with darkness, but with more water—black, still, and deep, reflecting the underside of the bridge like a mirror.

The dynamic is slow. Changes happen over years. A gear might turn once a day due to the weight of accumulated dew shifting a counterbalance. A crack might widen a millimeter per season. The entire landscape is engaged in a process of slow drowning, a gradual immersion in its own humidity. The water is winning. It fills the gaps, lifts the loose stones, softens the sharp edges of the new formations. But it is a patient victory. It takes decades for a puddle to become a lake. It takes generations for a wall to slide into the sea of wet stone that forms the base of this world.

You stand on a ridge of packed slate, looking out over the endless expanse of constructed, wet, mineral terrain. The copper sky glows overhead. The silence rings in your ears. And everywhere, the drip... drip... drip... of the world breathing in water, exhaling nothing.
