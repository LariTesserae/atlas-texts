The sky here is not black, nor blue, but a bruised violet, heavy with the weight of low-hanging clouds that do not break into storms, but rather exhale. It is a perpetual, gentle weeping. The air tastes of ozone and wet stone, thick enough to feel on the skin, warm like breath held too long.

This is **Ostra**, a city grown from the bones of a planet that was recently birthed. The ground beneath your feet is not old sedimentary rock, but fresh basalt, sharp-edged and unpolished by time. The erosion is minimal; the valleys are still V-shaped cuts in the earth’s crust, their walls sheer and jagged, revealing layers of cooling magma that shimmer with a faint, internal heat. Because the land is young, it has no soil to speak of—only regolith, crushed mineral dust, and vast, flat plateaus of obsidian-like glass that have been deliberately shaped by hands—or machines—that think in nanometers.

Yet, despite this harsh, geologically adolescent foundation, life has insisted upon itself. Vegetation here is lush, but not in the way of forests. It is fungal. Massive, pale-crested mycelial networks pulse through the cracks in the basalt, glowing with a soft, bioluminescent amber light that competes with the dim twilight. These are not plants that seek sun; they seek warmth and moisture, drinking the condensation that forms on the cool night rocks. They grow in dense clusters only where the water pools, leaving the rest of the landscape sparse, open, and eerily empty. A mile might pass between two such groves, connected only by underground threads of white hyphae visible as faint scars on the surface.

The architecture of Ostra does not sit *on* the land so much as it emerges *from* it. There are no roads. No bridges. The city is a lattice of suspended structures, floating just above the ground on fields of localized anti-gravity generated by quantum-tuned pylons sunk deep into the bedrock. These buildings are organic in shape, resembling hollowed-out seed pods or inverted bells, crafted from a material that looks like solidified smoke—dark, translucent, and rippling slightly when the wind passes. The technology is beyond modern comprehension; these structures are not built but *grown* using programmable matter, self-repairing at the molecular level. If you touch the wall of a building, your finger sinks slightly into a membrane that heals instantly, warm and yielding.

It is quiet. The sound of this place is not silence, but a low-frequency hum, a vibration that you feel in your teeth more than you hear with your ears. This is the song of the dampers, the atmospheric regulators that keep the temperature steady at 1.7/3—warm, tropical, yet lacking the sweating humidity of Earth’s jungles. Occasionally, a drop of rain falls, striking the smooth surfaces of the pylons with a crystalline *ping*, amplified by the sparse environment until it sounds like a bell tolling in a distant cathedral.

Fauna is rare, adapted to this strange equilibrium. There are no birds. Instead, large, slow-moving creatures drift through the air, their bodies composed of semi-permeable membranes filled with lighter-than-air gases produced by symbiotic bacteria within their cores. They look like jellyfish stranded in the sky, trailing tendrils that brush against the tops of the fungal towers, harvesting spores. They move without urgency, for there is no predation, no competition for space. The emptiness is their ally.

The weirdness of Ostra lies in its relationship with time. The land is young, the city is ancient in design but new in existence, and the inhabitants—if there are any—are indistinguishable from the infrastructure. Some say the city is alive. Some say it is dreaming. As you walk along one of the invisible pathways, guided by subtle magnetic pulls in your shoes, you notice the fog rolling in from the valley floor. It doesn’t obscure vision; it clarifies it. Within the mist, you see glimpses of other structures, taller and darker, rising from the center of the plateau. They are not lit by electricity, but by the slow decay of radioactive isotopes embedded in their cores, casting long, wavering shadows that seem to detach themselves from the objects and crawl across the ground.

There is a sense of immense scale here, not because everything is huge, but because everything is distant. The horizon is blurred by the perpetual dampness, creating an illusion of infinite extension. You can see the edges of the city, the termination of the gravity-fields, where the structures simply stop, leaving raw, untouched basalt stretching out into the unknown. This boundary is marked by a line of dripping waterfalls, not of water, but of liquid crystal—a clear, viscous fluid that flows endlessly from the edge of the engineered zone into the natural void below, evaporating into the humid air before it hits the ground.

To live here is to exist in a state of suspended animation. The warmth wraps around you, the light is dim enough to make your eyes adjust constantly, searching for sources that aren’t there. The vegetation breathes. The buildings pulse. The rain falls upward sometimes, pulled by the thermal currents of the pylons. It is a place of beautiful, terrifying stillness, active only in its own microscopic processes—the healing of stone, the growth of fungus, the drift of gas-jellyfish. It is a landscape that feels less like a home and more like a experiment, left running in a sealed jar, waiting for someone to crack the lid and let the world back in.
