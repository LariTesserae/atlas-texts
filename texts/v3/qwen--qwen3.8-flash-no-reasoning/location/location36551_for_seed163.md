The air here is thin, sharp, and tastes of copper and wet stone. It is a high place, an alpine plateau suspended in the upper troposphere where the sun hits with unfiltered, blinding intensity. At this elevation, the sky is not blue but a deep, bruised violet at the zenith, fading to a pale, bleached white near the horizon. The light is absolute; it does not just illuminate the landscape, it presses against it, flattening shadows into black voids that seem to drink the brightness around them.

Despite the intense sunlight, the temperature remains stubbornly cool, hovering just above freezing. This is because of the water. It is everywhere, though rarely seen as open pools. The ground is perpetually damp, a sponge-like substrate where moisture condenses from the thick, humid atmosphere that clings to the peaks like low-hanging fog. Rocks weep clear tears of meltwater. The soil itself is saturated, squelching underfoot with every step, releasing a faint, sweet scent of decay and growth intertwined.

This is a world being built, not one that has been worn down. Erosion is minimal; the terrain is raw, angular, and young. Massive slabs of basalt and granite rise abruptly from the mist, their edges sharp as knife-blades, untouched by the rounding hand of wind or time. They look placed rather than formed, geometric interruptions in the soft, green chaos.

And the green is overwhelming. Vegetation here is not merely covering the land; it *is* the land. There is no dirt visible for meters. The ground is a continuous, undulating carpet of mosses, lichens, and ferns so dense they form architectural structures. Huge, bulbous mounds of sphagnum rise like hills within the valley, their surfaces velvet-soft and dripping. From these mounds sprout tall, translucent stalks of bamboo-like flora, their leaves wide enough to catch the heavy dew. The plant life is aggressive, filling every crevice, wrapping around the sharp stones until the rock seems less like geology and more like scaffolding for a living cathedral.

Yet, for all this biomass, the sense of presence is sparse. The density parameter feels contradictory at first—how can a total vegetation be sparse? But looking closer, you see the pattern. The plants grow in isolated, massive clusters, separated by vast, empty stretches of shimmering, water-logged mudflats. Between these green islands, there is nothing but silence and space. The emptiness is palpable, a held breath between the roaring cycles of nature.

That roaring is the defining characteristic of this place: Sound. It is deafening. But it is not the sound of wind, nor of animals, nor of machinery. It is the sound of *growth*. In this hyper-active ecosystem, the cellular expansion of the moss, the hydraulic pressure forcing sap up through the translucent stalks, and the constant evaporation-condensation cycle of the atmosphere create a low-frequency hum that vibrates in your teeth. It is a wall of noise, a physical pressure that makes conversation impossible. You must shout to be heard, and even then, the voice is swallowed by the ambient drone of the earth building itself.

Scattered across this loud, bright, wet expanse are the remnants of industry. Not factories, not cities, but small, solitary machines left behind or currently operating in isolation. A single, rusted pump house stands on a hillock of ferns, its diesel engine idling, sending rhythmic thumps into the ground that compete with the organic hum. Nearby, a conveyor belt, long stopped, is overgrown with vines that have threaded themselves through the rollers, pulling the metal down into the earth. These artifacts are few—sparse dots of geometry in a sea of organic curve. They feel abandoned, or perhaps ignored. The tech level is industrial, evidenced by the crude combustion engines and heavy iron gears found half-buried in the mud, but there is no network, no grid, no city. Just scattered nodes of human effort struggling against the relentless pace of biological colonization.

Fauna is rare. You might spot a bird, high in the violet sky, a speck against the glare, but it never lands. The insects are silent, hidden beneath the layers of wet leaf-litter. The lack of animal sound makes the mechanical hum and the biological roar stand out more starkly. It is a lonely place, despite being full of life.

The dynamic is slow. Nothing changes minute-to-minute. The clouds move at a glacial pace. The moss grows imperceptibly, hour by hour. The water seeps slowly from the rocks. It is a landscape of patience, where centuries pass in the blink of an eye, yet the sensory experience is immediate and overwhelming due to the brightness and the noise.

As you walk further, the ground becomes softer, more yielding. The trees—if they can be called trees—are not wood but thickened stems of fibrous plant matter, bending slightly in the non-existent breeze, vibrating with the internal sound of fluid transport. The light catches the droplets hanging from every leaf, creating a million tiny prisms that scatter rainbows across the gray basalt. It is beautiful, terrifying, and profoundly alien in its normalcy. There is no magic here, only biology pushed to its logical extreme in a high-altitude incubator, where the rules of erosion are paused, and the rules of growth are accelerated.

You find yourself stepping carefully, not because the ground is treacherous, but because you feel guilty crushing the architecture. Every footprint fills with water within seconds, erased by the tide of the surface. The place heals itself instantly. The scars of the old machines are already disappearing under new shoots of pale green vine. The industry is dying, not because it fails, but because it is irrelevant to the primary force of this world: the wet, bright, loud, slow, and inevitable advance of the green.
