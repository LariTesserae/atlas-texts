The sky here is not empty, but it is not full in the way of clouds or birds. It is a ceiling of hammered copper and oxidized iron, suspended so low that the heat rising from the ground presses against it like a hand against a fevered forehead. There is no wind, only the thermal shunt, the slow, heavy exhalation of the earth meeting the unyielding metal above.

This is **Ossuary Prime**, though the inhabitants do not use names that imply death. They call it *The Foundry’s Breath*.

It exists at an elevation where the air should be thin and cold, yet the temperature is oppressive, a wet, clinging heat that feels less like sunlight and more like being inside a lung. The geometry of the place defies natural geology; there are no mountains carved by water or time. Instead, the landscape is composed of immense, vertical plates of fused glass and black basalt, stacked with a precision that suggests they were laid down rather than grown. These plates rise thousands of feet into the amber haze, creating canyons that are perfectly rectangular, their edges sharp enough to cut skin if one were foolish enough to touch them. But nothing touches them for long. Nothing grows on them.

Vegetation is absent. The concept of "green" is unknown here. In its place is a mineral biology, a crystalline lattice that pulses with faint, internal light. Along the walls of the glass canyons, vast sheets of silicate foam spread out like frost on a windowpane, but this frost burns. It is a pre-life formation, emerging from the steam vents, hardening instantly upon contact with the hot, dry upper air before collapsing back into slurry when the humidity returns. It is a cycle of creation and destruction that happens every few hours, leaving behind a dust of glittering shards that coats everything in a fine, abrasive layer.

Water is present, but it is not free-flowing. It is trapped in pockets, held in suspension by static fields generated by the dense infrastructure below. Occasional condensation forms on the cooler undersides of the overhead plates, dripping in slow, viscous beads that fall hundreds of feet without splattering, hitting the lower platforms with a dull *thud* that echoes through the quiet. These droplets are warm, tasting of sulfur and ozone. When they hit the heated surfaces below, they hiss, turning into steam that rises to join the fog bank hovering at eye level.

The scale is open, stretching far beyond the immediate perception, but the density is suffocating. The world is packed tight with machinery. This is an urban environment, but it is not built for habitation in the human sense. It is a city of engines. Massive pistons, some the size of skyscrapers, move with agonizing slowness—*dynamic: slow*—compressing the superheated air of the valleys. Gears made of obsidian and brass interlock in configurations that seem impossible, grinding against each other with a patience that spans centuries. There is no urgency here. The machines work because they must, driven by the planet’s own tectonic heat, which has been tapped, siphoned, and channeled into these vast, silent works.

The technology is industrial, but archaic in its aesthetic. Think of steam, think of clockwork, think of the Victorian age scaled up to cosmic proportions. Pipes thick as rivers carry glowing, molten coolant across the void between the glass plates. Walkways of grated steel bridge the gaps, connecting clusters of domed structures that look like inverted mushrooms, their caps shielding delicate instruments from the falling condensation. Inside these domes, the air is stiller, thicker. Here, the weirdness manifests most strongly.

There are no plants, but there are fungal-like structures—not biological, but energetic. Crystalline mycelia weave through the ventilation shafts, feeding not on decay, but on voltage. They glow with a pale, sickly violet light, pulsing in rhythm with the distant thrum of the main engine. If you listen closely, beneath the near-silence, you can hear them singing—a high, thin whine that resonates in the teeth. They are the nervous system of this place, carrying data from the sensors embedded in the glass cliffs to the central processing hubs deep within the crust.

Fauna is rare. To see anything alive is a shock. Occasionally, a shape detaches itself from the shadows of a gear assembly. It might be a scavenger-drone, constructed from bone-white ceramic and powered by a small nuclear cell, moving with jerky, insectoid grace. Or it might be something worse—a native entity, if such a word applies. A colony of semi-solid vapor that has learned to mimic the form of a quadruped, walking across the hot grates, leaving no footprints, evaporating slightly as it moves, then reconstituting further on. It does not eat. It absorbs. It watches with eyes that are just denser patches of fog. These entities are few, shy, and strange, existing on the periphery of the machine-world, tolerated by the engineers who maintain the great engines.

The light is normal in intensity but filtered through the particulate matter in the air. Sunlight, if it ever breaks through the metallic ceiling, arrives diffuse and gray, casting no sharp shadows. Instead, the illumination comes from below—from the furnaces, from the violet crystals, from the red-hot coils of the conductors. The atmosphere is saturated with this artificial glow, giving the whole region a hue of bruised plum and rust.

Erosion is emerging. This is a young world in the context of its construction. The glass plates are still settling. Micro-fractures appear daily, spiderwebs of white lines spreading across the black surfaces. When a fracture completes, a shard falls, crashing into the depths below, sending up a plume of dust that hangs for days. The buildings themselves are growing. New pipes are welded onto old ones overnight. Structures expand outward, consuming the available space. It is a place under constant, slow renovation, adding layers to its own foundation, burying previous iterations beneath new steel and stone.

Sound is mostly quiet, but it is not silence. It is a pressure. A low-frequency hum, so deep it is felt rather than heard, vibrates in the chest cavity. Interspersed with this are specific sounds: the *clank* of a valve opening, the *hiss* of escaping steam, the *drip-drip-drip* of condensation from a high ledge, the *whine* of the crystal-fungus. These sounds do not echo widely because the air is too dense, too thick with moisture and particulates. They die quickly, absorbed by the layers of machinery and mineral.

To walk here is to feel small. The elevation makes your head swim, not from lack of oxygen—the air is rich, heavy—but from the sheer drop into the dark pits below, where the lights of the foundry fade into infinity. The temperature is relentless; sweat does not evaporate, it pools, making clothes cling. The materials around you are slippery with oil and dew. You cannot rely on nature to save you; there is no moss to cushion a fall, no root to grab. Everything is engineered, and therefore, everything can fail.

Yet, there is a beauty in the order. The geometric perfection of the glass cliffs, the rhythmic pulse of the violet crystals, the majestic slowness of the gears. It is a cathedral of industry, built by hands we do not see, maintained by forces we do not understand. It is a place where life has been replaced by function, where growth is measured in millimeters of added alloy, and where the only wild thing is the weather—the intermittent rain of hot condensate that washes the world clean, then leaves it coated again in dust and shine.
