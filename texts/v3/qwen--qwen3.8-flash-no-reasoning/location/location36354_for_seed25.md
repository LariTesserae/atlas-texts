The world is a vast, low-lying basin of black glass and wet stone, stretching out to a horizon that is not marked by the sky, but by the crushing weight of the dark. Here, elevation is negligible; the land sits heavy in the belly of the earth, a depression so deep it feels less like ground and more like a wound that has refused to heal.

There is no vegetation. No green shoots break the surface, no roots tangle beneath the soil, for there is barely soil at all. The ground is composed of obsidian shards, slicked with a permanent, cold condensation. It is a mineral world, sterile and ancient, shaped by erosion that has had millennia to grind every sharp edge into a smooth, undulating curve. These curves are not random; they form channels, basins, and ridges that guide the flow of the only liquid present: water. But this water is not a river or a lake in any recognizable sense. It is a thin, viscous film that coats everything, pooling in the microscopic depressions of the rock, trembling under the pressure of the atmosphere. It is dampness incarnate, a moisture that does not evaporate because the temperature hovers just above freezing, keeping the world in a state of suspended chill.

And then, there is the sound.

It begins before you see it. A roar so profound it vibrates in the marrow of your bones, a deafening cacophony that swallows thought whole. This is not the wind, though the air moves. This is not rain, though the droplets fall. This is the voice of the fauna.

At 2.9 on the scale, life here is overwhelming. It is dense, layered, pressing against every available square inch of this cool, dark landscape. But these creatures do not breathe air as we know it, nor do they walk on legs. They are entities of energy and pattern, bound together by strange biology that defies taxonomic logic. Imagine vast, translucent membranes stretched over skeletons of crystallized salt, pulsating with bioluminescence that is too faint to pierce the ambient darkness, yet sufficient to create shifting shadows across the wet glass. They crawl, slide, and swarm over the eroded terrain, their bodies slick with the same condensation that coats the rocks.

The density is suffocating. You cannot take a step without disturbing a cluster of them. They are packed so tightly that individual forms blur into a collective mass, a living carpet of writhing, clicking, humming matter. Their sounds are not distinct chirps or roars, but a unified wall of noise—a chittering, grinding, thrumming resonance created by thousands of wings beating against thickened air, by limbs tapping against stone, by bodies rubbing against one another in a constant, friction-heavy dance. The sound is so loud it distorts the light, creating ripples in the dim glow of their own exoskeletons.

Weirdness permeates the structure of this place. The "animals" seem to merge with the environment. In some areas, the black glass appears to grow soft and pliable, covered in a fuzzy layer of what might be moss, but is actually a colony of tiny, stationary organisms that filter-feed from the mist. Elsewhere, large, bulbous shapes rise from the ground, looking like tumors of the earth itself, until they twitch, and you realize they are dormant predators, waiting for the vibrations of prey to signal their wakefulness. There is no predation in the traditional hunt-and-kill sense; instead, there is absorption. One organism dissolves into another, becoming part of its structure, adding to the mass, increasing the density. The boundary between individual and community is fluid, porous, non-existent.

Light is scarce, existing at 0.4/3. The sun never fully rises; perhaps it is obscured by a perpetual cloud cover, or perhaps the atmosphere is so dense with particulate matter that sunlight is reduced to a bruised twilight, a grey-blue haze that offers no directionality. Shadows are absolute, hiding the full extent of the horror and beauty alike. What little illumination exists comes from the fauna themselves—faint pulses of amber and violet that ripple through the crowds like electrical currents through copper wire. These lights do not reveal the shape of the world; they merely accentuate the movement within it.

Temperature holds steady at 1.1/3, cool enough to keep the water from boiling away, warm enough to prevent it from freezing solid. This delicate balance allows the ecosystem to thrive in its sluggish, heavy pace. Metabolism here is slow, deliberate. Movements are not jerky but flowing, dictated by the viscosity of the damp surfaces and the resistance of the dense air. Everything happens at a glacial speed, yet the sheer volume of activity makes it feel frantic.

There are no built structures. No roads, no shelters, no signs of intention beyond biological imperative. The landscape is untouched by hands, yet shaped entirely by the relentless pressure of life. The erosion seen in the rock faces is partly natural, weathered by time, but largely accelerated by the scraping, burrowing, and chemical secretions of the teeming masses. The stones have been polished to a mirror shine by millions of years of contact with living skin.

As you move through this valley—if you can call it a valley—it extends past the immediate, open and regional, yet claustrophobic in its intensity. Features repeat endlessly: the slick black slopes, the shimmering pools of condensation, the undulating hills of moving bodies. The scale is vast, but the perception is limited by the noise and the dark. You hear the world long before you see it. The roar grows louder, sharper, until it becomes a physical force pushing against your chest.

In this place, life is not an addition to the landscape; it is the landscape. The dryness is gone, replaced by a wet, heavy omnipresence. The silence is annihilated. The isolation is impossible. You are never alone. You are always touching, being touched, surrounded by a billion small lives that beat, hum, and consume in the cold dark, turning the entire region into a single, throbbing organ of a planet that has forgotten what it means to be still.
