The world here does not begin with the horizon, because there is no horizon. It begins with the breath of a single stone, trapped in the hollow beneath a root-knot.

This is **The Weeping Nodule**, a pocket-scale settlement embedded within the calcified throat of a giant, ancient fungal tree. The scale is so small it is invisible to any eye larger than a moth’s; to an outside observer, this is merely a damp patch on the underside of a mossy log in a cold, dark forest. But inside the cavity, the geometry expands into a sprawling, vertical city carved from living wood and mineral deposit.

### The Substrate: Plant as Architecture
Vegetation here is not decoration; it is the foundation, the wall, the roof, and the floor. The entire environment is composed of dense, wet fibrous matter—roots that have fused into load-bearing columns, and mycelial networks that act as insulation. The "buildings" are hollowed-out galls, swollen growths on the inner bark that residents have scooped out, smoothed with abrasive lichen, and sealed with resin. These structures do not sit *on* the ground; they grow *from* it, spiraling outward like nautilus shells made of blackened timber.

Because the vegetation rating is near-total (2.8/3), there is no bare earth. If you dig a hole for a post, the surrounding roots immediately bleed sap to fill the gap. The walls breathe. They expand slightly when the humidity rises and contract when it falls, making the interiors of the homes feel like they are gently inhaling and exhaling.

### The Atmosphere: Cold and Wet
The temperature is low (0.4/3). A permanent chill hangs in the air, not freezing, but numbing—a damp cold that settles in the joints. This is a place where condensation never stops. Water is everywhere (1.9/3). It beads on the curved surfaces of the wooden domes, runs down the grooves of the spiral staircases carved into the trunk-core, and pools in shallow depressions between the roots. The rocks and hard materials weep. Even the tools used by the inhabitants are slick with moisture, requiring constant wiping.

The light is absolute darkness (0.1/3). There is no sun here. The only illumination comes from bioluminescent fungi cultivated in glass-like pockets of amber resin along the pathways, and the faint, pulsing glow of the fauna. Shadows are not cast; they are absorbed. The darkness is thick, velvety, and intimate.

### The Fauna: Overwhelming Life
Life here is suffocatingly abundant (3.0/3). The air is thick with the hum of insects, the scuttle of arachnids, and the flutter of micro-wings. Every surface is alive. Walking through a corridor might mean stepping over a carpet of beetles or pushing aside a curtain of hanging spider-silk that has become a home for thousands of mites. The ecosystem is hyper-dense; predators hunt in the narrow spaces, and prey hides in the cracks. The sheer volume of life creates a background static—a constant, low-level buzz of chitin clicking against wood, mandibles gnawing at fiber, and wings beating against the damp air. Yet, the sound rating is mostly quiet (1.0/3) because the dampness absorbs high frequencies. The roar of life here is muted, felt more in the chest than heard in the ear.

### The Inhabitants: Primitive Urbanism
The people of the Weeping Nodule live in a sparse arrangement (1.2/3) despite the crowded ecology. Their homes are scattered among the roots, leaving vast stretches of empty, dripping space between dwellings. This emptiness is intentional; it allows for airflow and prevents the spread of mold. They are primitive (1.1/3), using hand-crafted tools made from sharpened thorns, polished pebbles, and hardened sap. They have no metal, no fire, and no wheels. Instead, they use leverage, pulleys made of twisted vine, and levers carved from bone.

Their society is urban (1.9/3) in structure but not in size. It is a tight-knit network of exchange. One family specializes in scraping moss for edible powder; another cultivates the glowing fungi; a third harvests the secretions of the large, slow-moving grubs that tunnel through the wood. They trade these goods along the root-paths. Infrastructure is organic: bridges are woven strands of dried tendons; roads are flattened layers of compressed mud and leaf-mulch, kept moist to prevent cracking.

### The Dynamics: Active and Strange
The world is in motion (2.1/3). The erosion is young (0.7/3), meaning the features are sharp and fresh. New galls form rapidly; old ones collapse. The residents must constantly repair their homes as the substrate grows around them. A crack in the ceiling today means a new room tomorrow. The weather cycles are internal to the tree: periods of heavy sap-flow (which floods the lower levels) followed by drying phases where the wood shrinks and doors stick.

There is a strangeness (1.6/3) to the rules of this place. Because everything is alive, nothing is truly dead. If a tool is lost in the deep crevices, it may be found years later, encased in a pearl-like nodule of mineralized saliva, having been "eaten" and regurgitated by a colony of termites. The boundary between object and organism is blurred. To build a wall, one does not stack stones; one prunes a branch until it thickens into a barrier. To make a cup, one carves a hollow into a living seed pod, waiting for it to harden.

### Sensory Experience
Imagine walking through this place. Your feet sink slightly into the spongy, wet floor. The air smells of ozone, rotting sugar, and cold rain. You hear the drip... drip... drip... of water falling from a stalactite of resin onto a broadleaf fern below. Suddenly, a shadow moves—not a person, but a cluster of moths disturbed by your presence. They scatter, revealing a path lit by soft blue-green lights. Ahead, a house shaped like a curled fern frond stands alone in the dark, its door open, inviting you into the humid, breathing warmth of the interior, while outside, the overwhelming tide of life continues to surge and recede against the walls of the world.
