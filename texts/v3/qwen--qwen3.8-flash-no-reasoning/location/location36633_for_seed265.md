The darkness here is not an absence of light, but a substance. It has weight and viscosity, pressing against the skin like cold water, though it does not wet you in the way rain does. It settles into the hollows of the collarbone and pools in the eye sockets if one were to look up, though looking up yields only more black.

We are at low elevation, in a depression that seems to have been scooped out of the earth’s crust by something vast and indifferent. The ground is young. It has not yet learned to be soil. It is a chaotic accumulation of fresh fracture lines—shards of obsidian-like glass, jagged ridges of slate, and piles of raw, unweathered rock that still bleed mineral dust when touched. Because the erosion is early-stage, there are no rounded stones, no soft sandy beaches. Everything is sharp, angular, and newly separated from the bedrock. The landscape looks as though it was broken yesterday.

But it is not silent, nor is it empty.

The air is thick with moisture. At a scale of 2.0/3, this water is omnipresent. It condenses instantly upon any surface that offers a foothold for nucleation. The rocks are weeping; they sweat beads of clear fluid that run down their jagged faces and collect in the deep, narrow fissures that crackle with potential energy. This is not a marsh in the traditional sense, for there is little vegetation to hold the mud together. Vegetation is sparse (1.4/3), limited to pale, fungal-like structures that cling to the underside of overhangs, feeding not on sunlight—which does not exist here—but on the thermal venting of the rock itself. These organisms are translucent, ghostly fronds that tremble in the heavy, humid air.

Despite the sparseness of plant life, the density of *presence* is high (2.0/3). The volume of space is crowded. It is crowded by the fauna (2.3/3/), which moves through the dark in swarms, schools, and clouds. They are not birds or fish, but things adapted to a world without photons: eyeless, chitinous creatures that navigate by sensing pressure changes in the dense atmosphere. They brush against each other constantly, a living layer of texture moving over the static, sharp terrain. Their bodies are slick with the ambient moisture, reflecting no light because there is none to reflect, but creating a faint, oily sheen that can be felt rather than seen.

In the distance—or what passes for distance in a region-scale void (2.5/3)—there are marks of industry (2.2/3). But this is not the loud, clanging factory of a human mind. It is slow. The dynamics here are glacial (0.6/3). Nothing happens quickly. The "engines" are geological. Massive, scattered structures of refined metal stand isolated in the plain. They are small (1.2/3 built), perhaps the size of a house, but they are placed with mathematical precision across the horizon. They do not hum; they resonate. They vibrate at frequencies so low they are felt in the teeth before they are heard in the ears.

These structures are extracting something from the young earth. They sink deep anchors into the fresh rock, drawing up heat and volatile gases. In return, they emit a slow, rhythmic pulse of warm vapor that rises into the dark canopy, mixing with the condensing mist. This interaction between the industrial exhalations and the atmospheric saturation creates a strange ecology. The vapor is not just waste; it is nutrient. Where the plumes rise, the sparse fungal growths thicken slightly, turning darker, richer.

The weirdness (2.7/3) manifests in the geometry of the shadows. In a place of absolute darkness, shadows should not exist. Yet, here, shapes cast "anti-shadows"—regions where the density of the air is thinner, allowing for a slight perceptual shift. If you walk toward one of the metallic spires, you do not see it get closer; you feel the pressure drop. The object appears to recede even as you approach it. Space is folding slowly around the points of extraction. The distance between two structures might change depending on how long you stare at them, though staring reveals nothing but your own retinal afterimages blooming in the black.

The soundscape is mostly quiet (1.4/3), but this quiet is deceptive. It is the silence of held breath. When sound does occur, it is singular and distinct. The drip of condensed water hitting a shard of fresh quartz rings like a bell. The chittering of the eyeless fauna is a dry rustle, like sandpaper on wood. The machines make a subsonic groan, a tectonic creaking that suggests the ground itself is being twisted by invisible hands.

Temperature is warm (2.2/3), a humid, enveloping warmth that feels like being inside a lung. There is no wind, only convection currents rising from the heated metal bases of the scattered structures. These currents carry the scent of ozone, sulfur, and crushed stone.

Life persists in the cracks. Small, blind mammals with elongated fingers scurry along the ledges of the young rock, hunting the insects that feed on the fungal mats. Larger predators move beneath the surface of the visible, their presence indicated only by the sudden displacement of the air, a ripple in the humidity that makes the hairs on your arms stand up. They are patient. They wait for hours, days, for the prey to slip on the wet, sharp edges.

This is a world that is trying to become solid, but is being held in a state of perpetual formation by the intrusion of industry. The rock is new, the water is endless, the dark is total. The structures stand like needles stuck into the flesh of the earth, pumping warmth into a place that has never known the sun. And everywhere, in every cubic meter of this dense, wet, warm void, something is touching you, brushing against you, waiting.
