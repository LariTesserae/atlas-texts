The sky is not a ceiling here; it is a floor.

At this elevation, the air has thinned to a crisp, brittle clarity that tastes of iron and ozone. The sun hangs low, its light unfiltered by much atmosphere, casting shadows that are absolute voids rather than mere darkness. It is high above the cloud layer—a dense, roiling ocean of white and gray that stretches to the horizon in every direction, obscuring whatever world lies below. Here, on the peaks, there is only rock, ice, and the structures.

They are young mountains. Geologically speaking, they have barely finished rising. The stone is raw, jagged, and fractured. There are no gentle slopes or rounded hillsides; instead, the terrain is a violent geometry of shards, spires, and sheer vertical cliffs where tectonic plates recently shoved themselves upward with brutal force. The erosion is minimal because the elements have had little time to wear these edges down. Rain falls here, but rarely as water. It arrives as sleet, or as fine, dry snow that hisses when it touches the exposed rock faces. In the crevices, pockets of meltwater gather—brackish, cold pools that never quite freeze solid, shimmering with a oily iridescence from dissolved minerals leached from the fresh stone. These pools are the only moisture, sparse and intermittent, catching the harsh sunlight and throwing it back in blinding glints.

Yet, despite the barrenness of the vegetation, the place is densely populated. Not with trees, which struggle to survive in the thin, freezing air, growing only in twisted, lichen-covered knots on sheltered ledges. The density comes from the fauna and the built environment.

This is a city of stone and bone, primitive yet immense. It sprawls across the ridgelines and fills the valleys between the new peaks. There is no metal, no electricity, no combustion. The technology is hand-crafted, ancient in its method but vast in its scale. Structures rise like stalagmites, carved directly from the mountain’s own flesh. They are towers of stacked slate and granite, buttressed by timber brought up from lower elevations—dark, heavy wood that smells of resin and old age.

The architecture is layered, climbing over itself. Bridges of woven rope and stretched hide connect spire to spire, swaying gently in the wind. The sound here is mostly quiet, a muffled hush imposed by the altitude and the cold, broken only by the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of stone tools working at dawn, the distant bleating of herds, and the wind singing through the flutes carved into the tower walls.

And the animals. They are abundant, strange in their abundance given the harshness. Herds of long-haired, shaggy beasts move along the passes, their hooves clicking against the frozen rock. Birds with wingspans wider than human arms circle overhead, riding thermal columns rising from the dark, sun-warmed rocks. Smaller creatures, pale-eyed and quick, scurry through the cracks in the foundations, scavenging. The ecosystem is tight, interlocked. Every surface is touched by life. Mosses colonize the north-facing walls; insects swarm around the condensation pools; predators stalk the narrow paths.

The people—if they can be called that—live within the stone. Their dwellings are hollowed out from the inside, windows punched carefully through the rock face. At night, when the temperature drops so severely that breath turns to frost before it leaves the lips, the city does not glow with fire. Instead, it pulses with the bioluminescence of cultivated fungi grown in the damp, shaded interiors of the caves. A soft, blue-green light spills from thousands of tiny openings, making the mountain range look like a constellation fallen to earth.

There is a slowness to everything here. The glaciers grind forward millimeters per year. The stone shifts under the weight of the seasons. The rain falls in cycles measured in months, not days. Even the movement of the crowds in the market squares, held in wide, flat basins carved from the summit plateaus, is deliberate. People wrap themselves in thick furs, their faces hidden behind masks of polished obsidian or bone. They trade goods carried up on the backs of the beasts: dried meats, herbs, carved talismans, stones that hum with faint vibration.

The weirdness is subtle, almost imperceptible. It lies in the physics of the height. Sound travels strangely in the thin air; voices carry further but lose warmth. Shadows seem deeper than they should be. And the scale is overwhelming. Looking out from the highest tower, one sees not a town, but a civilization spread across an entire mountain range, thousands of settlements linked by rope bridges and footpaths, a web of human industry stitched onto the raw, unfinished skin of the planet. Below them, the clouds form a solid barrier, hiding the rest of the world. Above, the stars are visible even at noon, pinpricks of cold fire in a deepening violet sky.

It is a place of contradictions: dry yet damp, sparse yet dense, quiet yet full of life, primitive yet sprawlingly urban. It is a world balanced on the edge of emergence, newly risen from the crust, waiting for time to smooth its edges, while its inhabitants cling to its sharp peaks, building their lives in the cracks of the earth.
