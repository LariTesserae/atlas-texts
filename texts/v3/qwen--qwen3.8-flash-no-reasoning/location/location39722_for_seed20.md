The air does not breathe here; it screams.

It is a high place, though "high" is a relative term when the ground beneath you has recently clawed its way up from the crushing depths of an ocean that no longer exists in any recognizable form. This is a plateau of jagged, wet stone, perhaps only a few hundred meters across—a cathedral-sized room carved by the violence of tectonic birth and the immediate, furious assault of the sky. It is small enough to walk around in an hour if the walking didn’t hurt so much, but vast enough that your eyes slide off the edges into a churning gray nothingness.

Everything is submerged, or rather, everything is *drowning*. The water level at 2.6 is not a static pool; it is a wall. A vertical rain that falls upward as often as it falls down, defying gravity with the casual arrogance of broken physics. Mist hangs in thick, suffocating clumps, heavy and warm (1.9), sticking to skin like oil. The temperature is stiflingly tropical, a humid oven heated by friction, by the constant collision of molecules that refuse to settle.

There is almost no vegetation (0.7). What little green there is does not grow; it festers. Patches of pale, translucent moss cling to the sharp, young rock (1.1), pulsing rhythmically, as if breathing. They are sparse, scattered like mistakes on a canvas, leaving most of the landscape bare and exposed. The rock itself is fresh—shattered obsidian-black glass and raw granite, edges still razor-sharp, cut open by the earth’s own convulsions less than a day ago. There is no soil yet, no softness, no decay to cushion the fall. Only hard surfaces and liquid violence.

The sound (3.0) is the primary feature of this world. It is not merely loud; it is deafening to the point of physical pressure. It is the roar of wind tearing through a canyon, amplified by the resonance of the stone, mixed with the hiss of steam where hot rocks meet cold water, and underlaid by a low, vibrating hum that shakes the teeth in your jaw. To stand here is to be vibrated apart. Thoughts cannot form clearly because the noise occupies every frequency, filling the mind with white-static chaos. You do not hear the environment; you are inside it, being shaken.

And then there is the weirdness (2.6). Reality here is thin, like ice over a deep lake. The boundaries between object and substance are dissolving. Stones sometimes turn into liquid for a second before re-solidifying, sharper than before. Shadows detach themselves from their casters and drift toward the center of the plateau, forming dark, swirling eddies that defy the light source, which is dim (0.6) and diffuse, coming from everywhere and nowhere. The sky above is not blue or black, but a bruised purple, swirling with clouds that move too fast, flashing with silent lightning that actually sounds like screaming metal.

There are no tools (0.3), no machines, no wires. But there are structures (1.1)—scattered, faint marks that look like they were made by something other than human hands, or perhaps by human hands acting in delirium. Small cairns of wet, smooth stones sit isolated in the mist, three or four feet high, arranged in perfect circles. Inside the circles, the air shimmers. Sometimes, looking into one, you see a reflection of yourself from ten seconds ago. Other times, you see a version of yourself that never existed, wearing clothes you have never owned. These structures are partial remains of a logic that used to apply here. Now, they are just anchors, barely holding the space together against the turbulence (2.7).

The ecology is rare (0.7), but what is here is wrong. Birds, if they can be called birds, hang suspended in the air, wings beating frantically but making no progress forward, trapped in the updrafts of the inverted rain. Their feathers are slick and heavy with water, dripping continuously. Insects crawl on the surface of the standing pools, but their legs pass through the water without breaking the tension, skimming across the liquid like it is solid glass. Fish leap out of the puddles, gasping in the warm, dense air, flopping on the wet stone, their scales reflecting the dim, chaotic light before sliding back into the shallow, boiling mud.

The dynamic (2.7) is relentless. Nothing stays still. A boulder shifts two inches to the left every minute. The mist swirls in vortices that create temporary vacuums, sucking debris upward. The temperature spikes and drops in waves, causing the air to crackle with static electricity that jumps between your fingers. The entire area feels like a hyperactive organism, twitching, spasming, trying to decide what shape it wants to be. It is a storm-state frozen in time, a moment of catastrophic change stretched out into eternity.

You feel small here (1.3). Not because the land is huge—it is bounded, finite, claustrophobic—but because the forces acting upon it are disproportionate to its size. A small room is being subjected to the energy of a planetary collision. The density of presence is low (1.2); there are large gaps of empty, screaming air between the features. This emptiness is not peaceful; it is tense, loaded with potential violence. The space between the stones feels like it is waiting to collapse.

To walk here is to fight the current. The ground is slippery, coated in a film of bioluminescent slime that glows faintly red in the dim light. Every step sends up a spray of warm, salty mist. If you stay still too long, the water begins to rise around you, not from below, but from the air itself, condensing on your skin until you are drowning in dry air. If you move, the turbulence catches you, pushing you toward the edges of the plateau, where the reality breaks down completely and the stone turns into vapor.

This is a place where the world forgot how to hold its shape. It is wet, it is hot, it is loud, and it is new. It emerged yesterday, perhaps, or ten thousand years ago—time is meaningless here, measured only in the cycles of the screaming wind and the shifting stones. It is a scar on the earth that refuses to heal, kept open by the constant flow of impossible rain and the grinding of young rock against the weight of a broken sky.
