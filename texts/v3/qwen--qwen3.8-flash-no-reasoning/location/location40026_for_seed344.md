The heat here is not a blanket; it is a pressurized lid. It sits low and heavy on the earth, thick enough to taste—a metallic tang of ozone and heated stone that clings to the back of the throat. The air shimmers, but not with the lazy distortion of a desert mirage. Instead, it ripples in sharp, angular lines, as if the atmosphere itself is being cut by invisible blades.

You are standing in a pocket. A geological blister that has recently burst from the skin of the world.

This place does not exist on any map because it was not here yesterday. The terrain is young, brutally fresh. The ground is composed of jagged shards of obsidian-like glass and dark, cooling basalt, still retaining the internal glow of its birth. There is no soil, only fractured rock that hasn’t had time to soften under the touch of wind or rain. Because erosion is at a minimum (0.6/3), the edges of every crack, every fissure, and every protruding shard are knife-sharp. Nothing has been worn smooth. The landscape looks like a shattered mirror held together by gravity and steam.

Water is everywhere, yet nowhere to drink. With a water level of 2.2/3, this is a hyper-arid wetness. The heat (2.7/3) flashes liquid water into vapor the moment it touches the hot rocks, but the pressure within this hidden pocket (scale 0.5/3) traps the steam close to the ground. It creates a perpetual, knee-high fog that swirls violently, condensing instantly on the cooler vertical faces of the rock walls, dripping down in rhythmic, sputtering bursts. These drops do not pool; they boil away before they can form a puddle, leaving behind faint rings of mineral salt that look like ancient, dried blood.

Vegetation is sparse (1.1/3), but what exists is strange. There are no green leaves here. Instead, scattered across the sharp ridges are clusters of crystalline fungi—pale, translucent structures that grow directly out of the heated stone. They resemble frost flowers made of quartz, vibrating slightly with the thermal energy of their substrate. They emit no scent, for there is no life to metabolize them beyond their own rigid, mineral structure. Fauna is absent (0.3/3). No birds, no insects, no rats. The silence of biological life makes the non-biological noise deafening.

And it is loud. (Sound 2.5/3).

The sound is not wind, for there is little air movement horizontally due to the enclosed nature of the pocket. The sound is *thermal*. The rapid expansion and contraction of the rock as it cools from its recent emergence creates a constant, high-pitched *tink-tink-tink*, like thousands of tiny hammers striking glass. This is layered over a deep, subsonic hum—the groan of the earth settling into its new shape. Then, the hiss. The relentless, aggressive hiss of water meeting rock at boiling point. It sounds like a factory floor where the machines are made of steam and stone. It is a wall of white noise that forces you to shout if you wish to speak, though there is no one to speak to.

Built elements are scattered (1.2/3), faint marks on this raw canvas. Half-submerged in the glassy rock face are fragments of rusted iron piping, twisted and severed, as if pulled apart by tectonic hands rather than broken by time. The metal is scorched black on the outside, glowing dull red on the inside where the steam escapes. These pipes do not connect to anything coherent. They are orphaned limbs of a larger industrial system (tech 1.6/3) that seems to have collapsed into this micro-environment. A single, intact valve wheel lies on the ground near your foot, its paint peeled away by the acidic steam, revealing the brass beneath, tarnished to a sickly green. It is half-sunk in a patch of crystalline moss, looking less like an artifact and more like a fossil from a future age.

Light is normal (1.8/3), filtering down from a small opening high above, perhaps thirty feet up, where the "roof" of this pocket breaks off into the sky. But the light does not behave normally. It hits the dense, swirling steam and scatters, creating halos around the jagged rock spires. The illumination is diffuse, shadowless in the fog, but blindingly stark in the clear patches between the vapor clouds. It casts long, sharp shadows that jump and flicker as the steam shifts, making the static landscape appear to dance.

Weirdness permeates the physics here (0.8/3). The temperature gradient is inverted in places. Near certain vents, pockets of superheated air rise rapidly, while just inches away, the rock surface is cool enough to touch without burning, covered in a fine layer of ice-like condensation that defies the ambient heat. This paradox causes the air to warp visibly, bending light in strange arcs, so that objects viewed through these columns appear stretched or compressed, like reflections in a funhouse mirror made of weather.

The dynamic is slow (0.6/3). Despite the loud noise and the active steam, nothing changes quickly. The crystals grow millimeter by year. The steam cycles in predictable, minute-long waves. The rocks cool degrees by hour. It feels like watching a glacier melt, but sped up to the speed of breathing. You could stand here for days, and the layout would remain identical, yet the sensory experience—the pitch of the ticking rock, the density of the fog—would shift subtly, imperceptibly, accumulating until one day the entire pocket might exhale a massive plume of steam and seal itself shut again, returning to the underground dark from which it came.

It is a machine that has forgotten its purpose, running on pure geothermal entropy, contained in a fist-sized space of broken earth, screaming quietly in a voice of glass and gas.
