The air here does not smell of rain, though it is always wet. It smells of crushed stems, oxidized iron, and the sweet, cloying rot of things that grow too fast and die too young.

We are in the **Hollow of Vess**, a settlement carved not into stone, but *into* the body of a forest that has forgotten how to be a forest. At an elevation where the clouds do not drift by but hang low enough to be breathed, the temperature hovers in a perpetual, damp chill—cool enough to numb the fingers, warm enough to keep the mold thriving. This is not a place of open sky; the canopy above is so dense, so layered with interlocking boughs and hanging mosses, that the "sky" is merely a ceiling of green-black leaves filtering the light into a diffuse, bruised twilight. Yet, within this enclosure, the light behaves strangely. It feels normal, yet heavy, as if the photons themselves are saturated with moisture.

The scale is intimate, claustrophobic. The entire area is no larger than a town square, perhaps fifty meters across, bounded by a ring of ancient, weeping roots that rise from the earth like the buttresses of a cathedral built by ants. But within this small boundary, the density is suffocating. There is no empty space. Every cubic inch is occupied by life or the memory of life.

### The Architecture of Green

Here, vegetation is not scenery; it is substrate. The ground is a spongy mat of peat and decaying fungal networks that squelch underfoot. The buildings—if you can call them that—are grown. They are structures of woven willow, hardened resin, and living bamboo, tied together with sinew and sap. The inhabitants, who move through these spaces with a jerky, frantic energy, have learned to prune rather than build. A house is a thicket of ferns kept in check by daily shearing. A wall is a row of brambles, their thorns facing outward, their interiors lined with soft moss to keep out the chill.

Because the water level is constantly near the surface, the foundations of these plant-homes are submerged in shallow, murky pools. Condensation drips ceaselessly from every leaf, every branch, every overhang. The sound of this dripping is deafening—a constant, rhythmic *plink-plonk-plop* that layers over the wind, creating a white noise that vibrates in the teeth.

But it is the turbulence that defines the place. The ecosystem is in a state of hyper-active creation and destruction. Vines surge forward overnight, choking out weaker shoots. Fungi bloom in bursts of orange and violet spores that vanish as quickly as they appear. The air shimmers with motion. Leaves curl and uncurl. Roots push up through the floorboards of homes, forcing inhabitants to hack at them with bone-handled axes and obsidian flakes. This is a primitive technology, yes—no metal, only stone, wood, and bone—but it is wielded with desperate precision. The tools are simple, worn smooth by hands that never stop working. You cannot rest here. If you sleep for more than an hour, your home will likely be consumed by a climbing ivy that grows inches per minute.

### The Overwhelming Fauna

If the plants are loud, the animals are louder. The fauna population is overwhelming, a teeming mass of small, quick creatures that fill every niche. There are no large predators; there is no room for them. Instead, the air hums with insects. Bees the size of thumbs dive into the nectar-rich blooms. Beetles with iridescent shells scuttle along the root-walls, carrying crumbs of decayed fruit.

Birds are everywhere, but they are not songbirds. They are chatterers, shriekers, and flappers. Small, brightly colored finches swarm in chaotic clouds, fighting over seeds with vicious little beaks. In the shadows of the lower branches, frogs cling to wet bark, their throats pulsing with croaks that add to the cacophony. Rodents with long, prehensile tails dart between the legs of the inhabitants, stealing scraps, dodging boots. The sheer number of moving bodies creates a visual static, a blurriness at the edges of vision. To stand still in the Hollow of Vess is to feel as though you are dissolving into the crowd of life around you.

### The Soundscape

The sound is not just loud; it is physical. The dripping water provides the bass note. The chittering, buzzing, croaking, and shrieking of the fauna provide the mid-range chaos. And the humans—or whatever passes for them—provide the high-frequency stress of survival. They shout to communicate over the din. They grunt as they pull vines away from their doors. Their laughter, when it comes, sounds strained, brief, quickly swallowed by the next wave of noise.

There is no silence in the Hollow. Even when the wind dies down, the plants seem to groan as they stretch toward the limited light. The erosion is established, settled in its own violent way; the landscape looks ancient because it has been reshaped by these forces for centuries, yet it looks new because nothing lasts longer than a season. It is a paradox: a place that feels both timeless and fleeting, stable in its instability.

### Life in the Damp

The people live close to the ground. They wear clothes made of treated bark and woven grass, dyed with berry juices to mask the scent of sweat, which attracts the biting flies. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, due to the lack of direct sunlight, marked with scratches from thorns and bites from mosquitoes. They move with a practiced fluidity, ducking under low-hanging fronds, stepping over puddles without looking, knowing exactly where the footing is solid and where it is mush.

They farm fungi in the dark corners of their dwellings. They harvest dew from spiderwebs stretched across doorways. They catch fish in the shallow, flooded basements using nets woven from tough reeds. Every resource is recycled, reused, repurposed. Nothing is wasted because everything is needed to maintain the delicate balance between being eaten by the jungle and eating it back.

Despite the weirdness parameter being low—this is not a magical realm, nor a sci-fi simulation—it feels alien because of the intensity of its biological reality. Here, nature is not a backdrop; it is the antagonist, the provider, the architecture, and the neighbor. It touches you constantly. A vine brushes your shoulder. A frog leaps onto your foot. Water drips into your collar. You are never alone, and you are never clean.

As you walk deeper into the center of the Hollow, the density increases. The paths narrow. The walls of living wood press closer. The noise rises to a crescendo. You see a child sitting on a stump, whittling a piece of antler with a sharp stone, while a dozen sparrows fight over crumbs at his feet. He doesn't look up. He doesn't need to. The chaos is part of him.

This is a small world, bounded by roots and mist, boiling with life, drowning in sound, and growing wildly out of control. It is cool, wet, and terrifyingly vibrant. It is a place where you must fight for every inch of space, even if the space is already full.
