The ocean does not rest. It screams.

Here, at the roof of a world that is barely formed, there is no land. There is only the Deep Sky Sea, an inverted atmosphere where the pressure of gravity holds water in suspension above a crust that has not yet decided what it wants to be. The elevation is absolute; you are looking down into the void, or perhaps up into the ceiling of existence. The boundary between "air" and "water" is not a surface tension, but a turbulent membrane of physics, churning with enough energy to tear atoms apart and knit them back together before they have time to cool.

It is cold here—a deep, penetrating chill that bites through any thermal shielding, because the water itself is supercooled liquid, metastable and trembling on the edge of freezing. Yet, the motion generates heat. Friction of fluid against fluid, wave crashing into wave in a chaotic dance that never resolves. This is the temperature paradox: the air is ice, the currents are fire, and the middle is a slurry of steam and sleet that defies phase transition.

The light is normal—starlight, harsh and white, cutting through the mist—but it behaves strangely in this medium. Because the density of matter is so low (0.2/3), there is almost nothing to scatter the light except the water itself. But the water is everywhere and nowhere. It exists in vast, hollow pockets, suspended droplets the size of cities, drifting in slow, majestic orbits around each other. Between these mega-droplets lies near-vacuum, a silent emptiness that contradicts the noise.

And the noise is deafening. Sound requires a medium, and while the spaces between are empty, the *interiors* of the suspended oceanic bodies are dense with turbulence. When two of these planetary-scale water-blobs collide—or nearly miss each other—the shockwave travels through the liquid faster than thought. It is a constant, rolling thunder, a bass note so low it vibrates the bones of anyone standing on the thin, embryonic shelves of rock that occasionally protrude from the chaos. It is the sound of creation grinding its gears.

Vegetation is sparse, yes, but "sparse" is a misleading word for what grows here. Nothing rooted. Nothing green in the traditional sense. Instead, crystalline filaments drift in the upper layers of the suspended oceans. They are semi-sentient structures of silicate and hydrogen, grown by the tech-level engineering of the environment itself. They look like frost flowers, fragile and white, blooming in the violent currents. They do not photosynthesize sunlight; they metabolize kinetic energy. Every crash, every eddy, every sonic boom feeds them. They spread slowly, threading through the chaos, trying to impose order on the turbulence. A single strand might span kilometers, anchoring itself to a passing debris-field of raw geology.

There are no animals. No fauna. The scale is too vast, the physics too unstable. To live here would require a body made of math, not meat. But the ecosystem is alive in a different way. It is a system of flows. Currents act as highways. Vortices act as predators, swallowing smaller droplets whole. The "weather" of this place is not rain falling down; it is water rising up, then exploding outward in radial bursts of mist that freeze instantly into diamond-dust, then melt again under the friction of new collisions.

The erosion is emerging. Below the suspended sea, if one could pierce the veil of vapor and vacuum, one would see the planet’s surface. It is young. Raw basalt, black and slick, still cooling from magma. The water above acts as a hammer. Periodically, a massive chunk of the suspended ocean collapses downward, pulled by gravity anomalies, slamming into the crust with the force of a nuclear detonation. These impacts create temporary lakes of boiling mud, which evaporate into the upward-flowing currents, feeding the cycle. The landscape is being carved not by rivers, but by falling skies.

Built? None. No artifacts, no ruins. But the "tech" parameter (2.7/3) suggests something beyond modern engineering is at play. This is not natural formation. The suspended ocean is held aloft by invisible fields, perhaps remnants of a stellar-scale machine that failed, or a deliberate terraforming attempt that went wrong. The water stays up because physics has been bent. The turbulence is not random; it is patterned. If you watch long enough, you can see the currents forming geometric shapes—hyper-cubes of flow, rotating fractals of spray. It is a computer program running on wetware, executing a code we cannot read.

The weirdness is subtle, woven into the texture of reality. Sometimes, the light bends the wrong way. A star appears in the east when it should be setting in the west. Shadows cast by the floating droplets point toward the source of light. In the moments of maximum silence between the crashes, you hear whispers—not wind, but the static discharge of ions recombining. The empty spaces between the water-pockets feel watched. Not by eyes, but by the potential energy waiting to become kinetic.

Imagine standing on a shard of obsidian, jagged and sharp, jutting out of the boiling mud below. Above you, a sphere of seawater, ten miles wide, rotates lazily. Inside it, whales-sized crystals grow and shatter. As the sphere passes overhead, its gravitational pull tugs at your hair, at your skin. You feel the weight of the ocean pressing down, not through contact, but through influence. Then, a crack splits the sky. Two spheres collide. The resulting explosion sends a shockwave through the air—a wall of sound so loud it stops your heart for a second—and a tsunami of mist rushes over your platform. For a moment, you are submerged in warm, salty fog, blinded, deafened, feeling the vibration of a dying star’s echo.

Then it clears. The emptiness returns. The cold bites. The next collision is already forming, distant and inevitable, in the high-altitude storm that is this world’s only weather. It is a place of beautiful, terrible isolation. Vast, loud, wet, and cold. A cathedral of chaos, built without hands, lit by stars, and filled with the breath of a planet learning how to breathe water instead of air.
