The wind does not blow here; it *screams*.

At this elevation—above the cloud layer, where the air is thin enough to taste like copper and cold enough to freeze breath in the lungs before it leaves them—the world is defined by its violence. The terrain is an ancient plateau, a vast, open shelf of stone that stretches for hundreds of miles, flattened by eons of erosion so complete that there are no sharp peaks left, only the gentle, rolling undulations of rock worn down to the bedrock of the planet’s memory. It is primordial smoothness, a landscape that has forgotten what it means to be jagged.

But it is not still. Nothing is still here.

The atmosphere is turbulent, a chaotic engine of thermal differentials. Warm air rises from the hidden valleys below, crashing into the freezing upper currents with such force that the sky itself seems to bruise. Clouds form and dissolve in seconds, streaking across the horizon like white scars. This is the source of the deafening sound: a constant, roaring static that vibrates in the teeth and settles in the marrow. To speak here, one must shout directly into another’s ear, or simply accept that words will be swallowed by the gale.

Yet, despite the screaming wind, there is water. Not as rivers, nor as lakes, but as condensation. The temperature differential between the warm updrafts and the cold stone creates pockets of intense moisture on the leeward side of every rock formation. In these hollows, tiny depressions in the eroded plateau, water gathers. It is intermittent, fleeting. A mist clings to the ground, heavy and low, turning the sparse vegetation into dark, dripping silhouettes.

And the vegetation *is* lush. It defies logic. In a place so high, so windy, so seemingly hostile, green things thrive in the micro-climates. They grow not upward, which would invite destruction, but inward, huddled against the earth. Mosses, thick and velvet-dark, coat the north-facing slopes. Fungi, pale and ghostly, erupt from the damp crevices after every brief shower of condensation-rain. Grasses, tough as wire, weave through the stones in dense, tangled mats. The life here is compressed, concentrated, exploding in bursts of green where the wind is broken by a mere inch of rock.

But there are few places where the wind breaks. The density of features is low; the emptiness is profound. You can walk for hours without seeing anything but stone, moss, and the endless, churning sky. The scale is open, terrifyingly so. Horizon lines vanish into haze. Distance is distorted by the heat shimmer rising from the sun-warmed rocks during the rare moments the clouds part.

In this vastness, there are structures. They are scattered, faint marks upon the land. Small, dome-like shelters made of stacked slate and woven grass, barely taller than a person. They look like cairns, like graves, like animal dens. They are primitive constructions, built by hand, with simple tools. There are no roads connecting them. No walls enclosing territories. Just single buildings, standing alone against the roar, each one a fortress of silence inside its curved roof.

Who lives here? Rare fauna suggests they are not many. Birds, if you can call them that, glide on the updrafts with wings too wide for their bodies, silent until they dive. Small, six-legged mammals with fur like coarse wool scurry in the shadows of the domes, eyes large and reflective, adapted to the sudden shifts between blinding light and deep shadow. They are solitary creatures, avoiding contact, driven apart by the sheer hostility of the environment.

The weirdness lies in the physics of the place. The turbulence is so extreme that time feels subjective. Standing in the eye of a gust, watching a leaf detach from a shrub and spiral away into the void, one feels the pull of the chaos. The wind carries sound strangely—it amplifies some frequencies, distorting others. A footstep on stone might echo like a thunderclap; a scream might vanish entirely.

Condensation pools appear and disappear within minutes. One moment, a puddle reflects the bruised sky; the next, the sun breaks through, and the water steams violently, leaving only a stain of mineral salts on the rock. The ecology is built on this instability. Plants do not wait for seasons; they wait for humidity. They bloom when the mist thickens, die back when the sun scorches the moss dry.

The built structures are not permanent. They are maintained daily. Stones shift under the pressure of the wind; roofs must be re-thatched after every storm surge. The inhabitants—if there are humans among them, though it is hard to tell from a distance—move constantly. They do not settle. They follow the pockets of moisture, the sheltered angles, the micro-habitats that survive the turbulence. A village is not a cluster of houses, but a trail of abandoned domes leading to new ones, scattered across the plateau like dropped coins.

Light is normal, yet harsh. At this altitude, the sun is unfiltered, white and biting. Shadows are absolute black, carved into the stone by the angle of the light. But because of the constant cloud movement, the lighting flickers—bright, dim, bright again—creating a strobe effect over the entire landscape. The world flashes in and out of existence.

There is no peace here. The erosion has settled the land into its final form, but the atmosphere remains in a state of perpetual war. The ancient plateau is a stage for the wind’s fury. The lush vegetation is a defiant whisper in a hurricane. The scattered structures are small assertions of order in a cosmos of chaos. And the emptiness between them—the vast, humming, roaring space—is the true character of the place. It is a void filled not with nothing, but with noise.
