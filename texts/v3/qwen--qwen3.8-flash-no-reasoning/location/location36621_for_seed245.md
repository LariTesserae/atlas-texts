The world is not a planet. It is a kiln that forgot to cool down, or perhaps it never started cooling at all.

Here, the concept of "ground" is a recent invention, barely three days old, and still settling into its own identity. The elevation is low, so low that the horizon is not a line but a suggestion, a hazy blur where the superheated air dissolves into the dark, bruised sky. There is no sun in the traditional sense; there is only a pervasive, dim luminescence radiating from the heat itself, a dull, arterial red glow that pulses with the rhythm of a dying star trapped underground. This light does not illuminate so much as it reveals texture: slick, viscous, and trembling.

Everything here is made. There is no geology, no tectonic plate, no sedimentary layer that formed over eons. The substrate is constructed by an intelligence that has long since vanished, leaving behind the machinery of its own creation without instructions for maintenance. The landscape is a vast, continental sprawl of hand-wrought obsidian glass, fused silica, and cooled magma flows that were shaped by primitive tools—chisels of hardened diamond, levers of woven basalt fiber, hammers of dense iron meteorite. But these are not natural deposits. They are placed. They are laid brick by brick, slab by slab, in a geometry that defies Euclidean logic because the builders did not understand space, only mass and heat.

Because the erosion parameter is *emerging*, the surface is raw. It has not been weathered by wind or rain, for there is no wind, and the water is too hot to fall as rain. Instead, the surfaces are sharp, jagged, and newly fractured. You can see the tool marks on the black glass—deep gouges where manual drills bit into the molten state before it froze. These marks are fresh. The world looks like it was just assembled, like a puzzle dropped on a table and left unfinished.

But it is turbulent. The density of matter is sparse—there is much emptiness between the structures—but the energy within them is chaotic. The "water," at 1.7/3, is not liquid H2O in any recognizable form. It is a heavy, metallic slurry, boiling slowly under the pressure of the atmosphere. It clings to the weeping rocks, condensing into droplets that do not run but slide sluggishly, trailing steam that smells of sulfur and ozone. Pools of this mercury-like fluid sit in depressions carved by hands, bubbling violently. When they pop, they release clouds of vapor that hang low, obscuring the view in shifting, opaque veils.

There is no plant life. Vegetation is absent. In its place is a mineral ecology—a slow, crystalline growth pattern that mimics biology but lacks metabolism. Fungal networks of silicate crystals spread across the floors, branching in fractal patterns that change shape as the temperature fluctuates. They are not alive, but they are reactive. If you step near one, the crystal spires might shiver, releasing a faint chime as their lattice shifts. This is the only sound besides the hiss of the boiling slurry and the distant, groaning stress of the ground settling. Sound is mostly quiet, dampened by the sheer scale of the emptiness and the softness of the fog.

The weirdness level (2.8/3) manifests in the physics of the construction. The structures do not follow gravity as we know it. Because the world is still being "built" in a metaphysical sense, the rules are tentative. A tower of black glass might lean at a forty-five-degree angle and remain standing, held up by an unknown tension in the fabric of the local spacetime. Another section of floor might ripple like water when walked upon, yet support your weight as if it were solid stone. The environment is inconsistent. One step forward, the heat intensifies until your skin blisters; another step back, and the air turns cold enough to frost the slurry pools instantly. This is not weather. This is glitching.

The technology is primitive, yet the output is colossal. We see simple machines everywhere: massive ramps of layered shale leading nowhere, pulleys made of braided carbon filaments hanging slack in the void, scaffolds of twisted metal rods jutting out of the ground like broken ribs. These were built by beings who used fire and force, not computation. They stacked mountains by hand. They poured oceans of glass from crucibles the size of cities. But they ran out of time, or fuel, or sanity. The construction stopped mid-sentence. The great arches are incomplete, ending in ragged edges against the dark sky. The roads lead off cliffs into nothingness.

Yet, the turbulence continues. Without the builders, the kiln runs wild. Geysers of steam erupt spontaneously from cracks in the constructed crust, launching jets of superheated gas hundreds of feet into the air, where they freeze into snowflakes of ash that drift down slowly, covering the sharp edges of the obsidian in a soft, gray fur. The ground shakes—not from earthquakes, but from the internal pressure of the world trying to find equilibrium. Rocks roll downhill, crashing into others, shattering into new, smaller shards that immediately begin to grow their own crystal lattices, starting the cycle again.

It is a landscape of interrupted sentences. The silence is heavy, pregnant with the expectation of noise that never comes. The darkness is thick, broken only by the pulsing red veins of heat beneath the translucent glass floor. To walk here is to walk on the skin of a fever dream. You feel the wetness on your boots, the sticky heat clinging to your clothes, the sharp bite of unweathered glass underfoot. And above you, the sky churns with storm clouds that are actually floating debris—shards of mirror, bits of furnace lining, fragments of the unfinished world orbiting in a lazy, gravitational dance.

There are no animals. No insects. No bacteria visible to the naked eye. But there is a presence. The world feels watched, not by eyes, but by the architecture itself. The corners of the buildings seem to shift when you look away. The paths rearrange themselves slightly when you return after a break. The primitive tools left lying around—the hammers, the chisels, the trowels—are warm to the touch, as if someone just put them down. But there is no one. There is only the work, continuing in a vacuum, driven by the residual kinetic energy of the creation, building nothing, destroying everything, repeating the same errors in different configurations forever.

The atmosphere tastes of copper and burnt sugar. The humidity is high, making every breath a struggle against the heat. The visibility is limited by the steam and the dust, creating a claustrophobic vastness. You are surrounded by miles of open space, yet you cannot see more than ten feet ahead. The scale is planetary, but the experience is intimate, terrifyingly close.

This is the Post-Construction Zone. The Hot Lowlands. The Glass Marshes of the Unfinished God. Here, reality is a draft, and the editors have gone home, leaving the text bleeding into the margins.
