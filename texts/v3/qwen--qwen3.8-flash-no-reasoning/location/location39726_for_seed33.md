The air here does not breathe; it vibrates.

We stand at the edge of the Shelf, a precipice that drops away into a sky so thin and high it has forgotten the concept of blue. The elevation is absolute—a vertical axis that feels less like height and more like a distance from sanity. Below us lies the City of Glass Throats, but it is not built of stone or steel, nor by hands that have ever held tools. It is grown, crystallized, or perhaps simply *remembered* into existence by the pressure of reality failing under its own weight.

There is no water. The moisture of this world was exiled eons ago, replaced by a viscosity of light and static. The ground beneath your boots—if you were to walk on it, which you should not—is composed of fused silica and pulverized quartz, heated and cooled repeatedly until it became a black, mirror-like obsidian sheet. It stretches out in vast, flat plains between the structures, reflecting the dim, bruised purple of the upper atmosphere. Because there is no erosion by wind or rain in the traditional sense, the forms are settled, sharp-edged, and eternal. They do not wear down; they accumulate. The city grows upward, layering new glass upon old, creating spires that twist like frozen smoke.

Vegetation is absent, save for the fungal geometries that sprout from the cracks in the obsidian floor. These are not plants. They are translucent, violet lattices, branching fractals of pure mineral interest. They pulse with a slow, rhythmic bioluminescence, responding to the magnetic fluctuations of the planet’s core rather than sunlight. They are the only living things here, if "living" is a term that applies to entities that eat lightning and excrete silence.

The scale is open, deceptive. What looks like a town block is actually a valley-sized depression filled with these geometric growths. The density is near-zero. There is almost nothing per volume of space. The buildings are hollow shells, vast cathedrals of empty air enclosed by razor-thin walls of vitrified sand. You can see through them, across kilometers of void, to the next structure, then the next, a chain of empty vessels stretching toward the horizon where the curvature of the earth seems to fold inward.

And yet, it is urban. Organized construction exists, but without builders. The layout follows a logic of resonance. The streets are channels where sound waves would travel if there were any sound left to carry. Instead, there is a profound, crushing quiet. A silence so deep it has mass. When you move, the displacement of air creates no rush, only a subtle shifting of pressure against your eardrums, like the feeling before a thunderstorm in a desert that has never known rain.

Tech is none. There are no wires, no screens, no engines. The "technology" of this place is thermodynamic and acoustic. The city is tuned. Each spire, each arch, each floating shard of glass suspended in mid-air (defying gravity through some local distortion of physics) is calibrated to a specific frequency. The weirdness is 2.9/3: reality is fraying. At the edges of vision, objects double and triple. Shadows detach themselves from their casters and pool in corners where no light hits. The geometry becomes non-Euclidean; a hallway may be shorter on the return trip than it was on the way in, or it may lead to a room that exists only when observed from the correct angle of regret.

Fauna is rare, but present. In the hollow centers of the largest towers, things drift. Not birds, not insects, but clouds of metallic dust that coalesce into temporary, predatory shapes—spirals, cubes, jagged stars—that hunt the violet fungi. They make no sound as they consume, only a faint shimmer in the air, a ripple in the light. They are the predators of a world without prey in the biological sense; they eat potentiality.

The temperature is cool, hovering just above freezing, preserving the stillness. But dynamic processes are active. The glass groans. Thermal expansion and contraction in the absence of atmospheric damping causes the city to sing in low-frequency infrasound, a vibration you feel in your teeth and bones rather than hear. The crystal lattices of the fungal forests grow visibly, extending microscopic tendrils toward the nearest source of electromagnetic charge. The floating shards of architecture slowly rotate, aligning themselves with the planet’s wobbling magnetic poles.

Look closer at one of the central spires. It is a tube of amber-colored glass, fifty meters wide, rising two hundred meters into the thinning air. Inside, there is no furniture, no people, no artifacts. There is only a single, perfect sphere of vacuum suspended in the center, held aloft by the standing wave of the building’s resonant frequency. Around this sphere, the air is distorted, rippling like heat haze over asphalt, though it is cold. If you stare too long, the ripple begins to mimic your heartbeat. The environment is reacting to your observation. This is the rule of the world: attention alters architecture. To ignore the city is to let it remain solid; to engage with it is to watch it dissolve into possibilities.

The horizon is not a line, but a gradient of fading detail. Mountains in the distance are inverted, growing downward into the sky, their peaks touching the cloudless zenith. Salt fields stretch endlessly, white blinding patches of pure sodium chloride that crunch softly underfoot if you dare to step off the main path. The salt preserves everything it touches. Fossils of creatures that might have been alive before the water vanished are embedded in the crust, perfectly intact, eyes wide open in eternal surprise.

It is a place of terrible beauty, balanced on the knife-edge of collapse. The silence is not empty; it is full of unspoken words. The emptiness is not vacant; it is waiting. The built environment is not inhabited; it is occupied by the laws of physics that have decided to take a holiday.

As night falls—or what passes for night, a deepening of the purple gloom—the city lights up. Not with electricity, but with phosphorescence from within the glass itself. The trapped memories of a wetter, greener age bleed through the silica, showing fleeting images of oceans, of trees, of rain, projected onto the interior walls of the towers. They flicker and die as quickly as they appear, illusions born from the stress of the material.

You are alone in a metropolis of ghosts made of glass, in a desert of frozen time, under a sky that is trying very hard to forget how to hold the stars. The air tastes of copper and ozone. Your breath fogs in the cool darkness, a small, white cloud that rises and vanishes instantly, swallowed by the vast, hungry emptiness.
