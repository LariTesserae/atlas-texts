The world is a single, continuous sheet of water. It has no floor that anyone has ever touched, and it has no sky in the traditional sense; instead, the horizon curves upward into a dense, opaque ceiling of cloud and vapor that reflects the gray light of a distant, cold sun back down onto the surface. The atmosphere itself is thick with suspended moisture, so heavy that breathing feels like inhaling through wet wool. Rain does not fall here; rather, the entire planet is submerged in a perpetual, vertical wall of mist and drizzle that moves at a constant, glacial speed.

Temperature hovers just above freezing. The water is not warm. It is a deep, steel-blue chill that penetrates bone within minutes. Because there is no landmass to hold heat, the thermal inertia of the ocean creates a stable, unchanging equilibrium. There are no seasons. There is no day-night cycle of warmth. There is only the eternal, numbing cold, held in perfect suspension by the vast density of the liquid mass.

Vegetation is absent. Not dead, but never born. There is no root system, no chlorophyll, no moss on stone. The concept of "plant" is foreign to this geology. Instead, the substrate—if one could call it such—is composed of shifting mineral plates, thin sheets of volcanic glass that float atop the deeper currents like ice floes made of obsidian. These shards are razor-sharp, remnants of a violent, early-formation epoch where the planet’s crust cracked open under pressure, spilling magma that froze instantly upon contact with the sub-zero oceans. These glass islands are young in geological terms; their edges are still jagged, still fresh from the shattering, eroded very little by the constant motion of the water around them. They do not weather down to sand; they remain sharp, brittle teeth biting into the silence.

But the silence is an illusion. The sound level here is deafening. It is not the crash of waves against rocks, for there are no large waves; the sea is calm, flat, a mirror of gray lead. The noise comes from *within* the structure of the world. It is the groan of tectonic plates grinding against each other miles below, transmitted through the infinite medium of water as a low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the chest cavity. It is the hiss of steam escaping from fissures in the glass islands. It is the roar of the atmospheric water cycling, a billion gallons per second falling and rising in a closed loop. To stand on these floating shards is to be enveloped in a white-noise storm so intense that individual thoughts are drowned out by the sheer acoustic pressure of existence.

Fauna is abundant, though not in the form of fish or whales. Life here is colonial, microscopic, and vast in aggregate. Floating in the upper layers of the ocean are trillions of translucent, jelly-like organisms—bioluminescent spheres no larger than dust motes, yet collectively forming clouds of soft, pulsing light beneath the surface. They are the only source of color in a monochrome world. Above, in the thick air, fly swarms of insectoid creatures with chitinous shells hardened by the cold. They do not hunt; they filter-feed on the airborne moisture and the spores of mineral-dust drifting up from the shattered crust. Their wings beat in synchrony, adding another layer to the deafening drone—a buzzing, electric hum that harmonizes with the groaning earth.

Scattered across the endless expanse of glass islands are structures. They are small, isolated, and industrial. A single smokestack rises from a shard of black glass, belching thick, dark plumes that mix with the white mist, creating streaks of gray pollution that hang in the stagnant air. Nearby, a rusted metal frame holds a generator, its gears turning slowly, driven by the tidal pull of the moon visible faintly through the haze. These are not cities. They are outposts. Single buildings, half-submerged, anchored to the floating crust with chains that have grown brittle and red with oxidation. They look abandoned, yet the lights flicker. The engines run. There are no people visible, but there are signs of maintenance: grease on a railing, a fresh cut mark on the glass near a door.

Technology is present but functional, stripped of aesthetics. It is the tech of survival and extraction. Machines designed to harvest energy from the thermal differentials between the deep ocean vents and the freezing surface. Pipes snake across the glass islands, carrying supercooled fluid to power turbines that hum in resonance with the ambient noise. The scale of production is local, confined to these tiny islands, because to expand would mean building upon water that offers no foundation. Thus, the industry is scattered, lonely, a constellation of blinking lights and roaring engines adrift in an ocean of glass and rain.

Weirdness manifests in the physics of the place. Gravity seems slightly inconsistent near the larger glass formations, perhaps due to the magnetic properties of the metallic ores embedded in the crust. Objects left on the edge of an island sometimes slide *up* before sliding down. The light bends strangely through the high-density moisture in the air, causing double shadows and halos around the industrial lights. Time feels viscous. The dynamic parameter of 0.4 suggests a lack of change, and indeed, if you watch one of the floating glass islands for hours, it appears to move with imperceptible slowness, locked in a dance with the currents that never accelerates or decelerates. It is a world frozen in motion, a snapshot of violence taken long ago and played back on a loop that cannot end.

The emptiness (density 0.1) is deceptive. Space is vast, yes, but it is filled with substance. Air is full of water droplets. Water is full of life. Sound is full of vibration. But *presence*—solid, grounded, permanent presence—is rare. Most of the volume is void, occupied only by liquid and gas. The few solid things—the glass, the metal, the bones of the creatures—are exceptions, anomalies in a sea of nothingness.

To walk here is to feel small against the infinite horizontal stretch of water and the crushing weight of the vertical column of air. The cold bites through clothing instantly. The noise makes communication impossible without shouting, and even then, the wind steals the words. You are alone on a fragment of a broken shell, floating on an ocean that has no bottom, under a sky that has no stars, surrounded by a ecosystem of glittering dust and chitin that ignores your existence. The landscape is a machine that has forgotten its operator, running endlessly, grinding its gears against the bedrock of reality, producing nothing but heat, noise, and the slow, inevitable accumulation of frost on its metal joints.
