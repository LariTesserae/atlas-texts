The world is a bowl of warm, black water, suspended in the void at an altitude so high that the air above it has thinned to a whisper, and below it, there is nothing but the dark.

This is not an ocean in the sense of tides or currents driven by wind. There is no wind here. The water is still, viscous, and deep—a liquid sky inverted. It is the highest point of existence, a sealed vessel where the pressure of the atmosphere outside presses against the membrane of reality, holding the warmth in like breath held in lungs.

Everything here was made.

There is no soil, no rock, no sand. The bottom of this submerged basin is a floor of constructed obsidian-like glass, polished by eons of static patience. Upon this floor, rising in concentric rings and spiraling towers, are the structures. They are built from hand-shaped ceramics, fired in kilns that burned for centuries until they cooled into permanent, unyielding forms. No metal exists here; the technology is primitive, limited to clay, bone, sinew, and the careful manipulation of heat and gravity. Yet, every inch of visible surface is engineered. Even the water itself is contained within walls of fitted stone, joined without mortar, relying on friction and weight alone to hold back the infinite drop beyond.

Vegetation is absent. Nothing grows because nothing can take root in the wet, sterile architecture. Instead, the ecosystem is mineral and animal, fused together by necessity.

Life abounds, though it moves with agonizing slowness. In the dimness—lit only by faint bioluminescent fungi that cling to the undersides of overhanging ceramic ledges, glowing a sickly pale blue—creatures drift. They are crustacean-like, armored in shells of layered silt and shellac, harvested from the very walls around them. Their legs move in slow, deliberate arcs, disturbing the water in ripples that take minutes to dissipate. Schools of translucent fish, eyeless and sensitive to pressure changes, weave between the pillars of the city-below-the-sea. They do not hunt; they filter-feed on the microscopic particulates that have settled from the ancient erosion of the structure itself.

Erosion here is primordial. The buildings have stood for so long that their edges have softened. Sharp corners have been worn into gentle curves by the silent, patient touch of water. Cracks in the masonry have been filled with calcified deposits, creating veins of white marble running through black stone. The entire environment feels like a ruin that never fell, simply settling deeper into its own foundations, compressed by time rather than destroyed by force.

Sound is nearly nonexistent. Because the water is dense and still, and because the builders used materials that dampen vibration, the silence is heavy. When creatures move, there is no splash, only a subtle displacement of mass. Occasionally, a distant *click* resonates—the sound of two shells brushing together—but it travels slowly, arriving seconds after it occurs, detached from its source. The quiet is not empty; it is full of presence.

Scale is intimate despite the vastness of the depth. The viewer’s perspective is confined to a single district: a plaza-sized area enclosed by towering, rounded buttresses that vanish into the gloom above. This small domain is densely packed. Every vertical surface is covered. Shelves carved into the stone hold thousands of dormant eggs, clustered like pearls. Corridors are narrow, forcing movement to be sequential, one body behind another. The density creates a sense of claustrophobia mixed with reverence; you cannot look anywhere without seeing something living, something made, something aged.

Light is scarce. The primary illumination comes from vents far above, where filtered starlight trickles down like oil, illuminating the upper tiers in a faint, silver haze. Below, in the warmer depths, the light is internal—glowing mosses cultivated in niches, providing just enough visibility to navigate by touch and memory. Shadows are soft, blurred by the diffusion of the water, making the world feel dreamlike, as if viewed through tear-stained glass.

Weirdness lies in the logic of construction. Why build in such a place? The answer is lost to time. Perhaps this was once a mountain peak, before the flood, before the waters rose to swallow the summit. Now, the inhabitants live inside the bones of the old world, repurposing the ruins of a dry civilization into a wet sanctuary. Tools hang from hooks in the walls—not swords or plows, but delicate instruments for harvesting algae, for polishing stone, for weaving nets from spider-silk-equivalents spun by cave-dwelling arachnids that thrive in the dark.

Temperature remains constant at 1.8/3—warm, tepid, comfortable. It is the temperature of blood, of sleep. This thermal stability allows the slow metabolism of the fauna to persist without urgency. There is no freezing, no boiling, no storm. Only the gradual cooling of the universe, resisted by the thick walls of this submerged cathedral.

As you watch, a large, flat-backed creature glides past, its skin patterned with geometric tiles that mimic the floor beneath. It pauses near a pillar, extending a long, segmented limb to tap the stone. A rhythm emerges—slow, percussive, felt more than heard. Others respond from nearby alcoves. They are communicating, maintaining the structure, checking for cracks in the foundation. This is not a wild place; it is a maintained one. A garden of stone and flesh, tended by hands that have forgotten the sun, working in the eternal twilight of a world that chose to drown itself in order to survive.
