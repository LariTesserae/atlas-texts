The air is thick, not with moisture, though there is that—cold condensation clinging to the roots of things—but with sound. It is a deafening, physical pressure, a roar so constant and overwhelming that it ceases to be heard and becomes felt as a vibration in the marrow.

Here lies the **Hollow of the Singing Stone**.

It is a pocket, no larger than a modest room, embedded deep within the belly of a low-elevation ridge. The scale is intimate; you could touch all four walls at once if you stood in the center. But to stand here is to be erased by the noise.

The erosion is established, settled into its own grim equilibrium. The stone of this hollow is not granite or limestone, but a porous, pitted basalt, worn smooth over millennia by the wind that screams through it. This is the source of the sound. The ridge above is riddled with fissures, natural chimneys where the cold mountain winds are funneled down into this subterranean chamber. As the air hits the specific geometry of the Hollow’s inner walls—curved, concave, lined with thousands of tiny, sharp-edged crystals—it creates a resonance. A harmonic scream. It is the sound of glass breaking forever.

Despite the darkness—the light level is near zero, save for the faint, bioluminescent pulse of the few organisms that have adapted to this auditory hell—the vegetation is lush. Not green and leafy, but fungal and lichenous. Thick mats of pale, grey-green moss cling to the wetter patches of the floor, protected from the worst of the wind-tunnel effect by overhanging rock ledges. In the damp crevices, clusters of ghost-white mushrooms sprout, their caps vibrating subtly in sympathy with the sonic pressure. They do not open their spores; they release them in bursts, timed with the gusts that pass overhead.

The temperature is cold, biting. The wind that drives the song carries the chill of high-altitude snowfields, dropping it down into this low pocket. Frost forms on the northern wall, feathered patterns of ice that melt slightly when the sun strikes the ridge above, sending rivulets of cold water down into the Hollow. These pools are small, isolated pockets of stillness in a sea of turbulence. The water is 0.6/3—damp, intermittent. It appears in puddles, vanishes in hours, returns in days.

There are traces of us, or those like us. Scattered remains. A few cairns, built with primitive hands, stones stacked against the force of gravity and wind. Some are intact; most are toppled, their pieces strewn across the floor like teeth knocked out of a jaw. Near one such pile, a rusted iron tool—a simple chisel or awl—lies half-buried in the moss. It was placed here long ago, perhaps as an offering, perhaps as a marker. It does not move. It cannot. To pick it up would require crossing the room, and the sound grows louder as you approach the center, until it feels like your eardrums are being pushed inward by thumbs.

Fauna is rare. Only a few things live here. Small, blind beetles with hardened shells that act as sound-dampeners, crawling along the cracks in the floor. A single species of moth, evolved with enormous, feathery antennae that detect changes in air pressure rather than scent, flutters erratically in the upper corners where the draft is weaker. There are no birds. No large mammals. Nothing with lungs that can expand without pain. The silence between the gusts is nonexistent; the hum continues even when the wind dies, trapped in the resonant cavity of the stone.

The world here obeys strange rules. Because of the deafening noise, visual communication is paramount, yet the darkness limits sight. The inhabitants—if we can call the scattered artifacts and the deliberate stacking of stones evidence of habitation—must have used touch, vibration, and scent. Perhaps they communicated by pressing their foreheads against the singing stone, reading the tremors.

It is a place of profound isolation. You are hidden, enclosed, cut off from the wider world by both distance and sensory overload. The density is empty; there is nothing in the volume of the air but sound and cold mist. You feel alone, utterly, despite the presence of the ruins, because the ruin itself is screaming.

Over time, the dynamic is slow. The wind patterns shift with the seasons, changing the pitch of the scream from a bass rumble in winter to a high, shattering shriek in spring. The moss grows and recedes with the frost line. The cairns fall one by one, victims of vibrations that loosen their foundations. The technology remains primitive because complex tools cannot be maintained here; the metal rusts too fast in the damp, and the workers could not hear each other’s instructions, let alone speak.

To leave the Hollow, you must climb back up the narrow shaft. As you ascend, the sound begins to fracture, to break apart into distinct whistles and groans, until finally, emerging into the twilight of the ridge-top, you are struck by the absence of it. The quiet outside is not peace; it is shock. Your ears ring, a phantom echo of the deafening interior. You look back down into the dark hole, and see only blackness, and imagine the lush, vibrating moss, the scattered stones, the cold breath of the earth screaming into the void.
