The place is not a room, nor is it a field. It is a **Kettle**, though no one remains to say who boils the water or what lives inside the steam.

It is small, bounded by walls that rise only forty feet high, yet they feel infinite because there is nothing beyond them but gray mist and the sound of dripping. The entire structure—floor, walls, ceiling—is built. There is no soil here, no bedrock exposed to the sky. Every surface is constructed from interlocking tiles of a black, vitreous material, like obsidian cooled too quickly, slick with a permanent sheen. This is the **Total Built** environment: a machine made of stone and glass, sealed against the world outside.

The atmosphere is heavy, cool, and wet (water 1.8, temp 1.1). Condensation does not form in droplets; it forms in sheets. Water runs down the walls in slow, viscous ribbons, pooling on the floor in shallow, trembling mirrors. The air tastes of iron and old rain. It is dim (light 0.8), lit only by bioluminescent mosses that cling to the grout lines between the tiles—a sparse vegetation (veg 0.6) that glows with a faint, bruised purple hue, pulsing slowly as if breathing.

But the emptiness is deceptive. The space is vast within its smallness (density 0.2). There are no furniture pieces, no tools left behind, no signs of human habitation other than the construction itself. The volume of air is mostly empty, hollowed out by the constant churn of something else.

**The Fauna** (abundance 2.4) does not walk; it flows.

They are called **Glass-Eels**, though they are not eels, and they are not made of flesh. They are colonies of single-celled organisms that have aggregated into long, translucent tubes, each about the length of a forearm. They move through the water films on the walls and floors, swimming in the condensation itself. Because the environment is fully constructed, these creatures do not eat plants; they eat the *surface tension*. They graze on the water’s skin, leaving trails of dry, matte stone behind them where they pass, which then re-wet themselves minutes later.

There are thousands of them. In the dark corners, where the light fails, their bodies catch the faint purple glow of the moss, making the walls appear to ripple with living veins. When you step onto the floor, the vibration travels through the tile. Within seconds, dozens of Glass-Eels converge toward your footstep, curious, hungry for the disturbance in the water film. They do not bite. They *listen*. Their bodies vibrate at frequencies that translate movement into sound.

This brings us to the **Noise** (sound 2.2). The place is loud.

Not with wind, for there is no wind in the Kettle. Not with machinery, for there are no moving parts visible. The sound comes from the erosion (established 2.1). Over centuries, the running water has carved tiny channels into the black tiles. These channels act as flutes. As the Glass-Eels swim through the narrow water-veins, their bodies push air trapped in the microscopic fissures. The result is a constant, choral whistling, a layered drone of pitch that shifts depending on how many eels are moving where. If you stand still, the sound builds—a rising tide of high-pitched shrieks as the eels crowd around you. If you run, the sound drops into a guttural hum as they scatter into deeper cracks.

The **Weirdness** (1.7) lies in the purposelessness of the construction. The tiles are fitted with such precision that no mortar is visible. Yet, every hundredth tile is slightly convex, bulging outward like a blister. From these blisters, a thick, warm sap-like fluid oozes intermittently. The Glass-Eels avoid these spots. Where the sap meets the water, it creates small islands of foam that smell of burnt sugar and ozone. No one knows what the sap is. Some say it is blood. Others say it is the sweat of the Kettle itself.

The **Dynamic** (active 2.0) is cyclical. Every few hours, the ambient temperature drops sharply (a micro-climate cycle within the sealed space). The condensation freezes instantly into a thin layer of ice across the entire floor and walls. The Glass-Eels go dormant, encased in clear crystal shells. The whistling stops. The silence is absolute, save for the cracking of ice as the pressure changes. Then, slowly, the heat returns—not from an external sun, but from somewhere beneath the tiles, radiating up. The ice melts in reverse, flowing back into the pores of the stone. The eels thaw, twitch, and begin to swim again. The chorus resumes.

You are standing in the center of the Kettle. You are the only thing that is not made. Your boots sink slightly into the water film, disturbing the surface. Immediately, the nearby tiles darken as Glass-Eels rush toward you, drawn by the displacement. The whistling rises in pitch, a swarm of invisible violins playing a frantic, beautiful fugue. Above you, the ceiling is low enough to touch, but when you reach up, your hand passes through a curtain of falling water that feels strangely oily, coating your skin in a substance that smells of deep earth and metal.

This is not a natural landscape. It is a wound in reality that has been cauterized with stone and filled with life. It is small, quiet in its geometry, but screaming in its biology. It is empty of meaning, yet full of motion. To stay here is to be watched by a thousand transparent eyes that have no faces, heard by a million throats that have no voices.
