The air here does not smell of earth or rot, for there is no soil to hold such things. It smells of heated copper, ozone, and the sharp, metallic tang of condensation hitting hot stone.

We are in a basin, low to the ground, where the horizon line is not defined by trees or mountains, but by the jagged, glass-like teeth of obsidian ridges that rise and fall in gentle, rolling undulations. The elevation is minimal; we are near sea level, perhaps slightly below it, in a depression where water collects and refuses to drain away.

The world is wet, but it is not a living wetness. There are no roots to drink, no leaves to transpire. Instead, the moisture comes from the heat itself. The temperature is oppressive, hovering at a sweltering constant that makes the air shimmer with visible distortion. Because the ground is non-porous—composed entirely of fused silicates, basalt, and industrial slag—the water cannot soak in. It sits on the surface, a thin, trembling film that covers every inch of terrain, reflecting the dim, reddish-grey sky above. When you step, your boot does not sink into mud; it splashes into a millimeter-deep sheet of lukewarm liquid that instantly begins to evaporate, rising as a dense, clinging mist that obscures visibility beyond ten meters.

There is no vegetation. No grass, no moss, no lichen. The concept of "green" has been erased from this place. What grows here is mineral and fungal, but not biological in any sense we recognize. The "plants" are crystalline structures that precipitate out of the steam, delicate ferns of salt and silica that snap if touched, dissolving back into the vapor when the humidity shifts. They cover the ground in a brittle, glittering lacework, constantly forming and collapsing under the weight of their own hydration cycles.

And yet, the world is not empty.

It is crowded. Overwhelmingly, suffocatingly alive with fauna. But these are not animals that hunt or graze. They are the builders, the maintainers, the components of the system.

Swarming over the wet, dark rocks are millions upon millions of mechanical insects. They are small, no larger than a thumb, crafted from blackened iron and ceramic, powered by tiny, high-efficiency thermal engines that drink the ambient heat. Their wings beat with a blur that creates a low, humming drone—a sound that is technically quiet, but pervasive, filling the head like tinnitus. They crawl over the salt-ferns, disassembling them, collecting the silica shards, carrying them toward the structures in the distance.

The scale of the place is regional, extending past the immediate splash of the footstep to a town-sized sprawl that hugs the curve of the basin. This is an urban landscape, but it was never built for humans. It is a settlement-scale infrastructure of industry, layered construction, and organized production. The buildings are squat, wide, and heavy, made of the same volcanic glass as the terrain, fused together rather than stacked. They look less like architecture and more like geological formations that have been carved by intent. Smokeless chimneys vent superheated steam into the atmosphere, feeding the cycle of evaporation and precipitation. Pipelines of brass and rubber snake across the ground, pumping the collected condensation into reservoirs that are then boiled again to drive the turbines.

Everything is in motion. The dynamics are active and relentless. A weather cycle exists here, though it is man-made: a pulse of heat from the central reactor core causes a rapid surge in evaporation, raising the water level on the surface, followed by a cooling phase where the mist condenses and falls back as a fine rain. The crystals grow during the rain and shatter during the heat. The mechanical insects adjust their patterns, moving from collection to transport, from maintenance to repair.

Despite the overwhelming number of creatures, the space between presences feels vast. The density is low because the entities are so small relative to the open expanse of the wet basin. You can see the curvature of the hills, the endless spread of the shimmering water, and the distant, dark silhouette of the processing towers against the dim light. The weirdness is zero; this is not a nightmare or a hallucination. It is a perfectly logical conclusion of a specific set of parameters: remove biology, add heat, add water that cannot be absorbed, add industry that requires cooling, and fill the void with the only life that can thrive in such conditions—machines that eat heat and breathe steam.

Walk carefully here. The ground is slick. If you slip, you will not land in soft earth. You will land on hard, hot glass, surrounded by a swarm of clicking, ticking mechanisms that will ignore you completely, unless you block their path. Then, they will climb over you, their legs cold and precise, checking for debris, before continuing their endless work in the humid, silent roar of the wet machine-world.
