The place is not a room, nor a cathedral, though it has the silence of one and the scale of a nave. It is a **Throat**, a vertical shaft of water roughly two hundred meters in diameter, plunging from the surface of a high-altitude plateau down into a darkness that has no bottom, only layers.

We are at an elevation where the air is thin and cold, around 3,000 meters above sea level. The temperature hovers just above freezing, a persistent chill that seeps into the bones, making the stone slick with condensation even before we touch the water. This is the "cool" parameter, but here it feels less like weather and more like a state of preservation. Everything here is kept in stasis by the cold, or perhaps slowed by it.

The water is 2.9/3 — submerged. But this is not a lake. It is a column. A pillar of liquid suspended between the sky and the deep earth. The entire volume of this shaft is filled with water, churning slowly, viscous with depth and pressure. It is a wall of rain frozen in mid-air, a waterfall that forgot to fall, held aloft by some geological impossibility or technological defiance. The surface, far below our feet (for we stand on a floating lattice of mineral debris), ripples gently. The light, 2.5/3 normal, filters down from the open sky above, piercing the upper ten meters of the column before being swallowed by the greenish-black murk below.

There is no vegetation. 0.2/3. No moss clings to the wet rock. No algae blooms on the glassy surfaces. The walls of the Throat are composed of **Obsidian** and **Basalt**, sharp-edged, young formations. They look freshly poured, still holding the memory of their liquid birth. The erosion is low, 1.1/3. These rocks have not been smoothed by time. They are jagged, crystalline, fracturing under the weight of the water pressing against them. The landscape looks like a volcanic vent that erupted not fire, but water, and then hardened instantly into black glass.

Yet, there is technology. Tech 1.8/3. Industrial.
It is not built structures in the human sense—no buildings, no roads. Built 0.0/3. Instead, the industry is embedded in the geology itself. Massive, rusted iron pylons, corroded beyond recognition, jut from the obsidian walls like ribs. They are part of the rock now, fused with it by heat and pressure. From these pylons hang thick cables, wrapped in insulating shells that have cracked and peeled away, revealing pulsating copper veins within. These cables do not carry electricity for lights or machines; they carry *sound*. Or perhaps they carry vibration. The machinery is ancient, a remnant of a project to stabilize this impossible column of water, to keep it from collapsing into the void below or erupting upward into the sky. The engines are silent now, dead, but their presence dictates the rules of this world.

The density is high, 2.3/3. The water is thick with particulate matter—mineral dust, shattered obsidian flakes, microscopic shards of volcanic glass suspended in the liquid. It looks like heavy syrup, or oil mixed with sand. The visibility drops sharply after ten meters. The space between the pylons, between the cracks in the rock, is filled with this dense suspension. Nothing moves through it easily. It is a medium that resists motion.

And yet, life abounds. Fauna 2.5/3.
But it is not fish. It is not mammal. It is **Mineral-Or-Simpler**. Life here is lithic.
Small, geometric shapes drift in the suspended particulate. Crystalline spiders made of quartz and mica, their legs articulated with hinge-like joints of pure iron sulfide. They do not swim; they *climb* through the water, gripping the suspended dust particles as if they were solid ground. Their bodies are faceted, catching the weak sunlight filtering from above, flashing briefly like diamonds before sinking back into the gloom.

Larger forms loom deeper down. Shapes that resemble jellyfish, but their bells are constructed from translucent layers of selenite gypsum. They pulse slowly, Dynamic 0.9/3. Their movement is glacial, a breath taken over the course of an hour. When they contract, they emit a sound—a deep, resonant hum that vibrates through the water, through the iron pylons, through the obsidian walls.

This leads to the Sound. 2.5/3 loud.
You might think a quiet, slow, submerged place would be silent. It is not. The water in the Throat is a conductor for the industrial remnants. The old pipes groan under thermal stress. The cables vibrate at frequencies that shatter small stones. The crystalline fauna chime when they collide, a constant, tinkling cacophony like wind chimes in a storm, amplified by the density of the medium. And the deep ones, the gypsum-jellyfish, sing. A bass note so low it is felt in the teeth rather than heard. It is a loud, oppressive drone, a mechanical choir singing the song of cooling magma and pressurized water. It never stops. It shifts slightly over centuries, but the average person’s lifetime sees no change in its pitch.

The Weirdness is 2.4/3.
Why does the water stay up? Why do the rocks hold their shape without eroding? Why do crystals grow in the dark?
The answer lies in the interaction between the dead tech and the living mineral. The pylons are leaking a substance—not fuel, but a catalyst. A heavy metal sludge, mercury-like, coats the interior of the Throat. This coating prevents the water from flowing downward via gravity. It creates a surface tension so immense it defies physics. The water is held in place by a shell of toxic, shimmering film. The crystals live on this film. They feed on the static charge building up in the insulated cables. The ecology is electrical-mineral. They eat lightning stored in copper. They breathe magnetic fields.

Scale is small, 0.8/3.
We see only a section of the Throat. Perhaps the middle layer. Above us, the sky is a circle of pale blue, framed by the jagged rim of the crater. Below us, the light fades to black. To the sides, the obsidian walls curve inward, meeting the opposite side about a hundred meters away. It is intimate, claustrophobic. You can reach out and touch a hanging cable. You can watch a crystal-spider climb past your eye, its facets reflecting your own terrified face. The boundaries are clear: Water above, water below, rock on all sides. But the depth is infinite, and the width is constrained.

Atmosphere is one of suspended dread.
Everything is waiting. The water waits to fall. The crystals wait to break. The cables wait to snap. The cold preserves this moment. There is no decay because there is no biological rot, only physical fracture, and the fractures happen so slowly they seem eternal. The smell is metallic, sharp, like blood and ozone. The taste of the air is dusty, gritty with volcanic ash.

As you descend further (if you dare, using the handholds carved into the obsidian by the crawling quartz-spiders), the light changes. The sunbeams become shafts of gold, cutting through the dense, murky water. Particles swirl in eddies created by the slow pulses of the gypsum-beasts. The noise becomes louder, a physical pressure against the eardrums. The iron pylons are thicker here, covered in barnacles of pure pyrite that glitter yellow in the dim light.

There is a rhythm here. Not fast, not chaotic. Slow. Heavy. Industrial heartbeat in a mineral body. The place is a tomb for a machine that failed to kill the world, and instead became the world. The water is the blood, the rock is the bone, the crystals are the nerves, and the hum is the thought that never finishes forming.

To exist here is to be still. To move is to risk shattering the delicate balance of surface tension and magnetic charge. The locals—if there are any, if humans ever came here and stayed—are likely blind, navigating by touch and vibration, speaking in sign language or humming in tune with the great beast-songs. They wear suits of woven copper mesh to shield themselves from the static discharge. They eat the pyrite, grinding it into powder, sustaining themselves on minerals, becoming part of the lithic cycle.

But looking from outside, from the rim of the crater, the Throat appears as a perfect circle of dark water, set in the white snow of the highlands. Steam rises from its surface, curling into the cold air. Occasionally, a flash of light pierces the depths—a crystal spider climbing too high, breaking the surface, falling back in with a splash that echoes like a gunshot across the empty plateau. Then silence returns. The hum continues. The water holds. The rocks wait.
