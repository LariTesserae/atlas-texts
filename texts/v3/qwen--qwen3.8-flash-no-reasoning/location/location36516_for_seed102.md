You are beneath the skin of the world. Not deep enough to feel the crushing weight of geology, but far enough down that the sun has forgotten your coordinates. This is the **Gullet of Roots**, a subterranean cathedral carved not by water, but by the slow, grinding expansion of living wood over millennia. The elevation parameter places you below the surface line; the scale tells you this is a hidden pocket, a room tucked between the ribs of a massive, dead—or perhaps sleeping—entity. The walls are not stone. They are fused layers of lignin and calcified bark, polished to a dull sheen by centuries of friction.

The temperature is warm. A breathless, stagnant heat, like the inside of a closed fist or the belly of an animal after digestion. It radiates from the floor, which is composed of compressed, fossilized seed husks and fine, dry dust. There is no water. You will find no pools, no dripping stalactites, no condensation on the cold rock faces. Instead, the moisture cycle here is internal, trapped within the cells of the vegetation itself. The air is dry enough to crack lips, yet the green things growing here do not wilt. They have evolved to drink light, or perhaps memory, pulling sustenance from the very atmosphere through pores that look like tiny, weeping eyes.

Vegetation dominates, despite the aridity. But it is not the leafy, chaotic lushness of a jungle above ground. Here, lushness takes the form of **crystalline fungi** and **bone-orchids**. Massive, shelf-like structures jut from the curved walls, their surfaces covered in velvet mosses that glow with a faint, bioluminescent pulse. Things do not rot here; they petrify in real-time, becoming part of the landscape while remaining alive. A bird lies on the floor, its feathers intact, its eye open, staring at the ceiling. As you move deeper into this hidden pocket, the warmth increases, but it becomes oppressive, heavy. The sound grows louder, not in volume, but in complexity.
