To enter **The Rooted Kiln** is to step inside a lung that has forgotten how to exhale fully.

The first thing you notice is not the sight, but the weight of the air. It presses against your eardrums, heavy with the scent of ozone, crushed flint, and the cloying, medicinal sweetness of heated resin. The light is amber, warm as candlelight but uniform, casting no sharp shadows. It feels borrowed from a sun that died long ago, filtered through miles of rock and coaxed into existence by fungi that pulse gently, like slow heartbeats.

Here, there are no strangers in the traditional sense, because privacy is an architectural impossibility when the walls themselves are listening. But if you seek the inhabitants—the *who* of this place—you must look for those who have learned to speak the language of growth rather than the language of stone.

### The Symbionts
The people here do not merely live in the city; they are woven into its biology. They call themselves **Grafts**.

A Graft is not defined by their job title or their social standing, but by the extent of their integration. You see them moving through the corridors of hollowed trunks, their movements fluid, almost insectile. Most wear the standard issue: tight-fitting suits made of hardened fungal leather, designed to repel the sticky humidity of sap leaks. But beneath the fabric, the skin tells the true story.

Look closely at the forearm of a maintenance worker hauling a crate of tubers. There, where the muscle meets the bone, you will see a patch of pale green lichen, grafted seamlessly onto the epidermis. This is a **Light-Eater**. During the High Tide, when the bioluminescent fungi blaze bright, these workers do not need to pause for meals. They stand still, arms raised slightly, drinking in the ambient energy, their bodies humming with a low-level charge that makes their hair stand on end. Their eyes, dilated to catch the faint glow, reflect the amber ceiling with an oily, chitinous sheen. They rarely blink. To blink is to lose connection.

Higher up, in the administrative spires carved from the heartwood of ancient trees, sit the **Vasculants**. These are the elite, those who have undergone deeper surgeries. Their circulatory systems have been partially replaced or supplemented by synthetic veins derived from plant matter. They do not bleed red blood; if cut, they weep golden-green sap. This grants them immunity to the toxic molds that thrive in dampness, and it allows them to interface directly with the city’s control nodes. A Vasculant does not press a button to open a door; they place a hand against the membrane, and the wood recognizes the hormonal signature of their own blood, relaxing its fibers to let them pass. They move with a predatory grace, conserving moisture, speaking in whispers so as not to disturb the delicate balance of the air.

### The Pruners
If the Grafts are the blood of the city, the **Pruners** are its antibodies. They are feared, respected, and necessary.

You can hear them before you see them: the shriek of chainsaws, but modified—powered by compressed pine vapor, sounding less like mechanical grinding and more like tearing flesh. The Pruners wear heavy exoskeletons grown from the carapaces of giant, eyeless beetles. These shells are fused to their shoulders and spines via nerve-links, allowing them to feel the vibration of the roots through their own bones. When a building begins to groan under the stress of unchecked growth, a Pruner presses their earplate to the bark and knows exactly which limb must be severed.

They are often scarred, their faces marked by splinters of living wood that healed incorrectly, embedding tiny thorns under the skin. In the lower tiers, where the tectonic plates shift and "root-shock" is common, Pruners work in pairs, one cutting, the other stabilizing the trunk with braces of iron lattice. They smell of burnt sugar and antiseptic alcohol. They are quiet, efficient, and utterly unsentimental. To prune is not violence; it is mercy. An overgrown branch will crush a home; a choked vein will kill a district. They carry shears that hum with a frequency that shatters brittle, diseased tissue without damaging the healthy core.

### The Sap-Walkers
In the deepest levels, near Zero Elevation, where the roots pierce the bedrock, live the **Sap-Walkers**. These are the engineers of the hydraulic system, those who maintain the arteries that pump life through the city.

Their gear is bulky, rubber-lined suits sealed with wax to prevent contamination. They spend their days elbow-deep in translucent pipes, fishing out impurities with nets made of spider-silk. They know the taste of the city’s health by the color of the fluid. If the sap turns dark, thick with sludge, they know a blockage is forming in the upper districts. If it runs too thin, pale and watery, they know the root systems are stressed, perhaps due to a leak in the mycelial concrete foundations.

Sap-Walkers are solitary creatures. The work requires intense concentration; a momentary lapse can result in a rupture, spraying boiling resin that hardens instantly into a suffocating foam. Many of them have lost limbs to such accidents, replaced with prosthetics carved from dense heartwood, articulated with joint-hinges salvaged from beetle exoskeletons. They communicate using a sign language developed specifically for high-noise environments—a series of sharp gestures involving the wrists and fingers, mimicking the flow of liquid. To them, silence is not peace; silence is death. Only the Thrum proves that the pumps are working.

### The Compost-Keepers
Finally, there are the **Compost-Keepers**, though they prefer the term **Returners**. They tend to the niches in the walls where the dead go.

When a citizen dies, their body is not buried in earth, for earth is scarce and precious. Instead, it is placed in a small, moss-lined alcove. Over months, the Returners monitor the decomposition, adjusting the humidity and introducing specific bacterial cultures to accelerate the breakdown of calcium and carbon. They sing to the bodies—not songs of mourning, but frequencies that encourage cellular dissolution.

The Returners are soft-spoken, often wearing masks of dried flower petals that filter out the sweet, rotting scent of decay. They believe that individuality ends at death, but contribution continues. The bones become scaffolding; the fat becomes lubricant; the water content (minimal as it is) is reclaimed and purified. To meet a Returner is to feel a strange, unsettling calm. They touch your face with hands stained brown from handling soil and spores, looking at you not as a person, but as future architecture.

### The Daily Rhythm
Life in The Rooted Kiln is dictated by the **Tides**.

During **High Tide**, the pumps surge. The Thrum deepens, vibrating in your teeth. Work intensifies. Factories ferment biomass, producing plastics and adhesives. Pollen clouds rise from the smokestacks-pores, settling like snow on the upper levels. Citizens rush to complete tasks, their lichen-glow patches brightening as they absorb the increased light output. The air smells sharp, metallic, alive.

During **Low Tide**, the flow slows. The lights dim to a soft, pulsing ember. Maintenance begins. Pipes are checked for micro-fractures. Vines are trimmed back from windows. The city settles into a deep, vibrating rest. Voices drop to a whisper. People gather in plazas of fossilized leaf-litter, sharing distilled sap from clay cups, watching the moths flutter near the ceiling. This is when the dreams happen. Not sleep-dreams, but shared hallucinations induced by the spore-clouds that drift down during the lull. For an hour, everyone sees the same thing: a memory of sky, a memory of rain, things none of them have ever experienced, yet all recognize.

### The Weirdness Beneath
But there is a darker side to the ecology. Not everything is symbiotic.

In the cracks between buildings, in the shadows of the ventilation shafts, lurk the **Strangers**. These are the failed grafts, those whose bodies rejected the plant-matter. Their skin peels away in sheets, revealing raw, pink tissue underneath. They scurry like rodents, hunting the serpentine eels that infest the hydraulic lines, trying to suck the nutrient-rich blood from the machines themselves. They are blind, deafened by years of screaming in pain, guided only by the vibration of the Thrum. They hate the light. They hate the sound. They are the waste product of a society that demands unity with nature, and they survive in the margins, stealing scraps of tuber and avoiding the gaze of the Pruners.

And then, there is the **Kiln itself**.

No one talks about it openly. But every inhabitant knows that the city is not just growing; it is *cooking*. The heat generated by fermentation, the pressure of the sap, the dryness of the air—it is slowly baking the organic material into something harder, more permanent. The builders know that eventually, the wood will petrify, the vines will turn to stone, and the city will stop breathing. It will become inert. And in The Rooted Kiln, nothing is allowed to be inert. So they must keep pruning, keep feeding, keep singing to the roots, lest the house becomes a tomb before the people inside have had time to return to the earth.

To stand here is to realize that you are not the guest. You are the ingredient.
