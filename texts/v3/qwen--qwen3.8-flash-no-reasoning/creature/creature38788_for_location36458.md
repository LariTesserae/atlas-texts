The air in the Hollow of Vess does not belong to us. It belongs to the spore, the sap, and the slow, grinding teeth of things that grow with malicious intent.

We are the **Pruners**. That is our name, though we rarely speak it aloud, for speech draws the attention of the vines, and attention brings growth. We do not build; we carve space out of chaos. We do not live; we endure, inch by inch, against a tide of green that wants to digest us.

Look at my hands. They are not soft. The skin has thickened into leather, stained permanently brown by tannins and black by soot from the damp-wood fires we struggle to keep lit. My fingernails are cracked, rimmed with dirt that no amount of scrubbing can remove because the water here is always carrying new silt. I hold an obsidian flake tied to a handle of bone. It is sharp, but it dulls quickly when it bites into the resin-hardened willow. I must sharpen it every hour. To stop sharpening is to die.

I am sitting on a stool carved from a petrified root-stump, located in what was once a living room before the ceiling collapsed inward under the weight of hanging moss. Above me, the canopy is a bruised purple-black, filtering the light into that heavy, wet twilight described in the texts you’ve read. But reading about it is different from breathing it. The air tastes like copper and crushed mint. It coats the back of the throat with a film that never fully clears.

There is a rhythm to our existence, dictated by the *plink-plonk-plop* of the condensation. We sleep in shifts. If we all slept at once, the ivy would find us. It grows inches per minute. It seeks warmth. It seeks carbon dioxide. We exhale, and it listens.

My neighbor, Kael, lives three feet away, separated only by a wall of brambles we have trained to grow interlocking thorns outward. He is coughing again. The mold thrives in his lungs just as it thrives in the walls. He spits into a clay basin, a dark, fibrous clump that he feeds to the compost pile. Nothing is wasted. Even our sickness is fertilizer.

Kael’s home is smaller than mine. A single chamber of woven bamboo, submerged up to the knees in murky water. He wears sandals made of dried lily pads and tough reeds, strapped tight with sinew. His toes are pale, wrinkled from constant immersion. He moves through his flooded floorboards with a grace that looks like dancing, but it is actually avoidance. One wrong step and the footing gives way into the peat, trapping him. And if he is trapped, the roots come.

We do not fear large beasts. There are none. We fear the small ones. The rats with prehensile tails that steal our stored seeds. The beetles that bore into our tool handles. The frogs that sit on our shoulders while we work, their cold bellies pressing against our necks, watching us with golden, unblinking eyes. They are curious. They are patient. They wait for us to be still.

Yesterday, a vine breached the eastern wall of the communal storage hut. It was a pale green shoot, delicate as a finger, yet strong enough to crack stone. It moved silently, sliding between the rafters. By morning, it had wrapped itself around the central support beam, squeezing until the wood groaned. We spent six hours hacking at it. The smell of the cut vine was sweet, cloying, like rotting fruit mixed with iron. It bled sap onto our axes, sticky and warm. When we pulled the severed piece free, it twitched for a moment, then lay limp, already beginning to sprout new shoots from its cut ends. We burned it. Fire is rare here; fuel is scarce. But we make exceptions for the enemy.

In the center of the settlement, where the density is highest, sits Elara. She is young, perhaps ten years old, which makes her old by our standards. Most children do not survive past five. The biting flies carry fever; the falling branches carry broken bones; the drowning roots carry suffocation. Elara survives because she learned early how to listen to the forest.

She sits on a stump, whittling an antler shard into a needle. Around her, sparrows fight over crumbs. She does not shoo them away. Their noise masks the sound of her own breath. Her eyes are wide, dark, reflecting the diffuse light. She wears a cloak of treated bark, dyed deep violet with berry juice to blend with the shadows of the fungal blooms. She is clean, in the only way one can be clean here: dry inside, covered outside.

When I look at her, I see the future of the Hollow. Not progress, not expansion, but persistence. We are not conquering this place. We are negotiating with it. Every day, we offer it our sweat, our blood, our discarded tools, and in return, it allows us to occupy the spaces between its leaves.

The wind picks up, causing the upper canopy to sway. The dripping intensifies. The water hits the surface of the pools below with a deafening roar. For a second, the light shifts, revealing the true scale of the enclosure: a bowl of roots holding a world within a world.

I stand up. My joints pop. The dampness is in my bones now, a permanent resident. I pick up my axe. There is a new shoot near the doorframe, curling toward the warmth of my body heat. It reaches out, tentacle-like, testing the air.

I swing. The blade bites into the stem. Sap splatters across my face, tasting of bitter almonds and decay. I wipe it away with a rough hand. The shoot falls, writhing slightly on the ground. I do not feel triumph. I feel only the immediate need to check the next corner.

This is who we are. We are the friction in the machine of life. We are the scratches on the lens of nature. We are wet, we are tired, we are alive, and we are surrounded by a beauty that wants to consume us whole. We do not leave. We cannot leave. The clouds hang too low to fly over, and the roots bind us to the earth. So we prune. We hack. We breathe the heavy, rot-sweet air. And we wait for the next shoot to rise.
