The things that live in Vellum’s Sump are not defined by the flesh, but by the friction. They are creatures of adhesion, born from the slow, grinding marriage of human persistence and geological patience. To speak of "who" lives here is to speak of a population that has been calcified by its environment, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture they inhabit.

They call themselves the **Keepers**, though few remember why, or if they ever did. They do not walk; they traverse. Their movement is a sliding, liquid grace, learned from the oil-slicked mud that refuses to hold a boot heel firmly. Because the air is thick with iron taste and fungal spores, their lungs have adapted. You see them first as shapes emerging from the amber haze: figures wrapped in layers of blackened rubber and salvaged canvas, their forms bulky, armored against the constant weeping of the walls. The hood of their gear is not merely protection; it is a sensory deprivation chamber, lined with damp wool to filter the heavy, syrup-like humidity before it hits the face. Behind the mesh of respirators, faces are pale, almost translucent, veins mapping blue rivers under skin that has forgotten the sun.

But the true inhabitants of Vellum’s Sump are not just the Keepers. There is a deeper symbiosis at work, one that blurs the line between the worker and the workspace. Over generations, exposure to the bioluminescent jars, the mineral-rich runoff, and the pervasive microbial slime has altered them. It is subtle, invisible to an outsider who only sees the grime. But look closer at a Keeper climbing a pipe carved with grip-rings. See how their fingers are elongated, the joints swollen like knotted wood, the nails thickened into dark, chitinous plates? This is not disease; it is adaptation. The calcium and silica suspended in the water table, drawn up through the pores of the stone and ingested via condensation on their lips, has begun to reinforce their skeletal structure. They are slowly turning into living stalactites.

Their skin bears the texture of the wet stone. A faint, iridescent sheen—similar to the oily film on the surface of stagnant pools—coats their arms and necks, repelling the excess moisture so it beads and runs off rather than soaking in. When the light flickers green, revealing the hidden ecology of the Sump, you can see that this sheen is alive. Microscopic colonies of the same orange and green slime that coats the pipes have taken root in the dead skin cells of the Keepers. They are not infected; they are composting. The bacteria feed on the sweat and the shedding epidermis, producing a faint, sweet-rot scent that masks the metallic tang of the blood. In return, the slime provides insulation against the cold drafts that cut through the caverns and acts as a secondary lung, processing the oxygen from the humid air more efficiently than their own compromised respiratory systems could manage alone.

Communication among them is minimal, shaped by the crushing weight of the silence and the roar of the machinery. Shouting is dangerous; it vibrates the fragile, rusted struts and draws attention to the cracks. So, they speak in gestures and touches. A hand pressed flat against a vibrating pipe tells another where a blockage lies. Two foreheads touching briefly conveys a warning about a structural weakness. They share a language of pressure and temperature. If a section of wall feels unusually warm to the touch, it means a boiler leak is nearby; if it feels slick and unnaturally smooth, it means the erosion has advanced, and the ground may give way. They read the city like a body feeling pain.

There are no children in the traditional sense. Births are rare, and those who are born are immediately immersed in the culture of survival. Infants are swaddled in waterproof linings from birth, their first cries swallowed by the muffled hoods. They learn to breathe the heavy air before they learn to walk. By the age of five, a child in Vellum’s Sump can navigate the vertical labyrinths of scaffolding without fear, understanding intuitively that the world below is a void of dissolution. They play not with toys, but with fragments of history: shards of ceramic plating, cogs stripped from idle engines, stones worn smooth by centuries of dripping water. These objects are handled with reverence, cleaned and polished until they glow, treated as relics of the Old Industry that still hums beneath their feet.

The elders, known as the **Rust-Saints**, are the most extreme manifestation of this integration. They sit in the high niches near the Great Cistern, their bodies so fused with the surrounding metal and mold that it is difficult to tell where the man ends and the chair begins. Their skin has hardened into a grayish-brown shell, resembling oxidized copper. Moss grows in the folds of their robes, tiny ferns sprouting from the crevices of their shoulders. They rarely move. They spend their days listening to the grinding roar, adjusting valves with hands that have lost sensation but gained precision, guided by vibrations transmitted through the floor. They are the memory banks of the Sump, holding the knowledge of which pipes will burst next winter, which staircases have become too brittle to climb, and where the water level will rise when the rains come from the ceiling.

Life in Vellum’s Sump is a negotiation with entropy. The inhabitants do not fight the decay; they inhabit it. They have made peace with the fact that everything around them is falling apart, and they have fallen apart with it, reshaping themselves into something durable enough to withstand the slow crush of time. They are not sad, nor are they happy. They are *present*. Their existence is defined by the immediate, tactile reality of wet stone, hot steam, and the pulse of the pumps. They eat what the city yields: tubers grown in the nutrient-rich sludge of the lower levels, fungi harvested from the walls, and fishless broth made from boiling algae. Food tastes of earth and iron, reinforcing the connection between body and basin.

When night falls—or what passes for night, marked only by the dimming of the chemical lights—the Sump breathes. The Keepers retreat into their squat dwellings, sealing the doors against the drip. Inside, they sit in circles, passing around jars of glowing moss-light, telling stories not of heroes or battles, but of repairs. How a rivet was replaced. How a crack was sealed with resin. How a pump was coaxed back to life after a century of silence. These stories are liturgies of maintenance, prayers to the god of Persistence.

In the end, whoever lives in Vellum’s Sump is less a person than a process. They are the human component of a mechanical ecosystem, their biology bent by the geology, their minds shaped by the noise. They are the ghosts in the machine, not because they haunt it, but because they *are* the machine, wearing flesh as a temporary casing over bones of calcium and souls of rust. They wait for the water to rise, and they endure, patient and slick, as the earth reclaims its throat.
