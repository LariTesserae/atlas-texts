In this place, the inhabitants do not live *in* the Structure; they live *within its metabolism*.

They are the **Keepers of the Hum**, a people shaped by the relentless, wet machinery that surrounds them. To know them is to understand that their bodies have adapted to the damp chill and the shifting ground just as the moss has adapted to the concrete. They are lean, wire-tough individuals, their skin pale and slightly translucent from a lifetime spent under the diffuse, amber-filtered light, rarely seeing the direct sun except as a glare through fungal panels. Their lungs are large, efficient filters, accustomed to pulling oxygen from air thick with spores and oil mist. They wear respirators not merely as protection, but as identity markers—hoods made of treated canvas or synthetic weave, stained green at the edges where the engineered vines touch them, breathing valves that click softly in rhythm with the distant pistons.

Their lives are governed by the **Algorithmic Logic** that dictates the valley’s movement. Because the layout changes hourly, because bridges retract and walkways rotate, there is no concept of "home" in the static sense. Instead, they possess a heightened spatial awareness, a proprioceptive instinct for the tilt of a floor before it shifts. They move with a fluid, cautious grace, stepping lightly on surfaces that might vibrate or slide. A sudden jolt does not startle them; it is their morning alarm, their noon bell, their evening signal to adjust footing. They read the tremors in the railing like sailors read the wind.

They are **scarce in number but absolute in presence**. In the density of 3.0, you cannot help but feel them. Even when unseen, their impact is felt: the warmth left behind on a metal seat, the scent of ozone and damp wool lingering in a corridor they just passed. When they do appear, emerging from the mist or stepping off a rotating platform, they are silhouettes first, defined by the sharp angles of their tools and the soft curve of their hoods. They speak little. The quietness (0.7) of the valley is not just an environmental feature but a social contract. Shouting is inefficient; sound dies too quickly in the fog. Communication happens through gesture, through the tapping of a wrench against a pipe to signal position, through the shared glance that acknowledges a shift in the infrastructure. They are a people who listen to the machine more than they talk to each other.

They are **maintainers of the infection**. They tend to the cultivated biomass—the velvet mosses, the filtering fungi, the strangling vines—not as gardeners tending flowers, but as engineers maintaining critical systems. If the fungi fail to filter the vents, the air becomes toxic. If the vines loosen their grip on the support beams, the structure collapses. So, they climb the vertical faces, checking the health of the greenery, pruning excess growth, replacing clogged filters. Their hands are perpetually stained with chlorophyll and grease, indistinguishable in color. They respect the life that colonizes the human-made, understanding that in this high-altitude bowl, nature and industry have fused into a single, precarious organism. Without the moss, the concrete cracks; without the concrete, the moss has nothing to cling to.

They are **adapters to the turbulence**. The world is unstable (2.9), and so they are resilient. They carry small, multi-functional tools: wrenches that double as climbing spikes, flashlights powered by kinetic motion from walking, water collectors woven into their clothing. They know how to navigate the labyrinth of corridors and catwalks even when it rearranges itself around them. They anticipate the retraction of a bridge, the sliding aside of a wall panel. There is no panic in their movements, only a focused, urgent efficiency. They flow with the chaos rather than resisting it, letting the algorithmic logic deposit them where they need to be, then moving immediately toward the next task dictated by the hum.

Among them live the **small fauna**: the rats with fur matted by grease, darting between shadows, tolerated because they eat the larvae of pests that could harm the filtration fungi; the birds with muted plumage, nesting in the insulation of hot pipes, singing faintly to avoid attracting attention. These animals are not pets or threats; they are fellow occupants of the interstitial spaces, sharing the crevices and the condensation.

To stand on the catwalk three hundred feet above the floor, watching the steam curl around rusted girders, you would see one of these Keepers emerge from a side shaft. They pause, tilting their head as the building opposite them shifts five degrees. They adjust their weight seamlessly, then continue walking, disappearing into the green-black foliage that chokes the street below. They leave no trace except the slight disturbance in the mist, the faint smell of rain and copper, and the echo of a bootstep that merges instantly with the omnipresent drip.

They are the living components of the machine, breathing, moving, and enduring in the perpetual, damp chill of a world that has stopped pushing against the sky and settled into its own weathered gravity. They do not sleep, because the valley does not sleep. They rest, briefly, in the hollows of the machinery, listening to the hum, waiting for the next shift, the next jolt, the next breath of filtered air.
