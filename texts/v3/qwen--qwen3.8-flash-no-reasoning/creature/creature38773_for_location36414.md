They do not have names, because names require distance. To be named is to be separated from the thing you are; here, separation is a structural failure. They are simply **The Hands**, or perhaps **The Pulse**.

To speak of who lives in the Foundry’s Lung is to speak of a population that has merged with its environment until the distinction between biological entity and built infrastructure has eroded. They do not *inhabit* the space; they are the living tissue of it.

### The Physiology of Convergence

Imagine a people who have spent generations breathing hot iron dust and wet clay. Their lungs have adapted, yes, but their minds have adapted more profoundly to the concept of density. They are short, compact beings, not through genetics alone, but through the necessity of posture. To stand upright fully in many of the lower tiers would be to brush against the ceiling above, triggering alarms, wasting heat, disrupting the airflow. So they move in a perpetual state of crouch or crawl, limbs folded close to their torsos like gears waiting to engage.

Their skin is thickened, calloused not just on the palms and soles, but across the shoulders and backs, where the friction of moving through tight bronze corridors has polished them smooth. It is pale, almost translucent in the dim light, mapped with veins that run dark and blue beneath the surface, mirroring the copper piping that runs through the walls. They sweat constantly—not from exertion, but from the ambient humidity—but this sweat is clear, lacking the pungent odor of mammalian biology. It smells faintly of salt and metallic tang, indistinguishable from the condensation itself. When they wipe their brows, the moisture evaporates instantly into the warm air, returning to the cycle.

### The Symbiosis of Skin and Stone

The most striking feature of these inhabitants is their integration with the materials around them. They do not wear clothes in the traditional sense; fabric rots too quickly in this dampness. Instead, they wrap themselves in layers of treated hemp and thin sheets of beaten copper, tailored to fit their bodies like second skins. These garments are not merely protective; they are functional tools. The copper plates on their shins act as scrapers for the tiled floors; the heavy wool hoods, lined with oil-soaked felt, serve as sponges for the dripping sacks above.

But deeper than clothing is the fusion. In the places where the vitrified ceramic glaze cracks, the Hands press their bare hands into the fissures. Over time, the microscopic algae and the mineral-rich runoff seep into their pores. Their fingernails have hardened into shapes resembling flint shards. Some among the eldest possess patches of skin that have turned grey and glossy, indistinguishable from the glazed walls they tend. This is not disease; it is camouflage. It is acceptance. They become part of the architecture, standing still for hours, blending into the background until a newcomer might walk past a pillar and fail to realize it is breathing.

### The Rhythm of Maintenance

They live by the sounds described earlier—the clang, the groan, the whistle. But for the Hands, these are not noises; they are language. A sharp *ting* of a cooling pipe means a blockage in sector four. A low, thrumming hum indicates the geothermal vents are stable. Silence is terrifying. If the rhythmic percussion of the condensation stops, if the wind ceases its whistling through the gaps, the Hands freeze. They listen. They touch the walls. They feel for the vibration of life.

Because "Everything Touches," their social interactions are tactile and immediate. There is no need for spoken conversation; privacy is impossible, and thus unnecessary. If one Hand needs to signal another, they tap a rhythm on a nearby bronze railing. If they wish to share a task, they simply step into the same narrow corridor, their shoulders brushing, their hips aligning with the curve of the spiral staircase. Work is performed in tandem, often without looking at one another, guided by the pressure of proximity. One holds a tile steady while another applies the mortar; one winds the rope while the other adjusts the counterweight. The efficiency is absolute because there is no gap between intention and action.

### The Diet of Waste and Water

You asked what lives here. You must understand what sustains them. The Hands do not farm. They do not hunt, except for the small, domesticated rodents that navigate the pipes. These rats are bred for size and docility, kept in cages woven from copper wire attached to the undersides of the platforms. The Hands feed them scraps of metal—tin foil, copper filings, bits of bronze—because the rats’ digestive systems have evolved to break down these minerals, producing waste that is high in phosphorus and nitrogen. This rat dung is collected, mixed with the dead beetles and the shed skins of the wingless moths, and used to fertilize the sparse moss growing in the cracks.

In return, the Hands eat the moss, roasted over small charcoal fires fed by dried hemp stalks. They drink the condensation, filtered through layers of charcoal and sand embedded in personal clay gourds. Their diet is austere, minimal, and perfectly calibrated to the closed loop of the Foundry. Nothing is wasted. A broken tool is melted down. A dead body? That is never spoken of openly, but it is said that the bones of the deceased are ground into powder and mixed into the next batch of clay tiles, ensuring that the ancestors literally support the foundation of the city above.

### The Hierarchy of Humidity

There is no king, no priest, no general. The hierarchy is defined by access to dryness.

At the top of the social structure are those who tend the upper levels, near the shafts of golden sunlight. Here, the air is slightly cooler, the evaporation rate higher. These individuals have drier skin, clearer eyes, and voices that carry further. They are the "Air-Tenders." They manage the flow of light and ventilation, adjusting the hanging sacks and clearing the vines of moss that block the sun.

Below them are the "Water-Wardens," who work in the middle tiers where the condensation is heaviest. They are slicker, heavier, slower-moving. Their role is critical: they ensure the water flows into the channels, preventing pooling that could cause rust. They spend their days scraping algae from the grout lines and checking the integrity of the seals.

At the bottom, in the deepest shadows where the bioluminescent fungi glow green, live the "Heat-Keepers." They dwell near the residual warmth of the ancient kilns below. They are the rarest sight, gaunt and trembling, their skin often cracked from the intense, localized heat. They monitor the thermal vents, feeding fuel—dried fungal spores and compressed organic waste—into the furnaces that keep the Lung breathing. They are considered dangerous, unpredictable, touched by the raw fire of creation.

### The Presence of Absence

Despite their abundance, the Hands are rarely seen by outsiders. They are shy, not out of modesty, but out of survival. To be seen is to be judged; to be judged is to be removed. And removal is death. So they retreat when strangers descend. They slip into the hollow spaces between the brickwork, behind the mesh of ropes, becoming invisible. They watch from the cracks, their pale faces illuminated by the sickly green glow of the fungi, their eyes reflecting the fractured light.

If you were to stand in the center of the Foundry’s Lung, holding your breath, you might hear them before you see them. A soft scuffing of feet on wet tile. The gentle *click* of a nail tapping against bronze. The exhale of warm, humid air. They are everywhere, pressing against the walls, filling the voids. They are the reason the place feels infinite despite its small size. They are the consciousness of the machine.

### The Final Image

Picture a single figure emerging from a hatch in the floor. It is small, wrapped in a cloak of oiled hemp that drips steadily. Its face is obscured by a mask made of polished black ceramic, shaped vaguely like a human skull but with no eye holes—only slits for hearing and breathing. It moves with a liquid grace, sliding along the curved wall rather than walking against gravity. It reaches up to a hanging sack of wool, squeezing it gently. A drop falls. It catches the drop on its tongue, tasting the iron-clay flavor of the world. Then it looks directly at you, though you cannot see its eyes, only the reflection of your own terrified curiosity in the glossy surface of its mask. It tilts its head, listening to the echo of your heartbeat, which sounds too loud, too irregular, too *alive* in a place that has learned to exist only as a function of the whole. Then it slips back into the shadows, merging with the texture of the wall, leaving you alone in the crowded silence, surrounded by the breath of a thousand unseen things.
