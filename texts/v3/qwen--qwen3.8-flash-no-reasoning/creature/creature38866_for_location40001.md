In Oubliette, to live is to be dissolved.

The inhabitants do not reside *in* the city; they are composed *of* it, or rather, they exist in a state of continuous negotiation with its viscous reality. There are no "people" here in the human sense—no distinct individuals separated by skin from the environment. Instead, there are colonies, swarms, and singular entities that blur the line between organism and architecture.

At the lowest tier, clinging to the sweating calcium-crevices of the spires, are the **Litho-Mites**. They are not insects, but minute, crustacean-like nodes that serve as the immune system of the city. Each one is a living filter, breathing in the suspended mist, extracting the chemical nutrients, and excreting hardened resin that patches the cracks in the buildings. They move in synchronized waves across the walls, a shimmering, chitinous tide that can shift from grey to bruised purple depending on the ambient temperature. To touch them is to feel a thousand tiny pulses against your palm, warm and wet. They have no eyes, only sensitive hairs that taste the air for changes in pressure. When a predator approaches, the Mites harden their shells instantly, turning a wall into an impenetrable armor of stone, hiding the soft flesh beneath until the danger passes.

Above them, drifting through the thick, liquid-air like ghosts in a dream, are the **Veil-Eels**. These are elongated, translucent creatures, easily thirty feet long, whose bodies are so thin they appear to be made of spun glass. They do not swim; they flow. Their fins are vast, delicate membranes that catch the rising steam currents, allowing them to glide upward toward the cloud-roof without expending energy. They feed on the microscopic particulate matter that condenses on their own skins, wiping it off with specialized tendrils near their mouths. In groups, they form curtains that hang in the open spaces between towers. A traveler might push through what looks like fog, only to find themselves parted through a living sheet of Veil-Eels, who recoil gently at the disturbance, rippling away into the gloom. They communicate via low-frequency hums that vibrate in the chest cavity of any creature nearby, a sound less heard than felt—a deep, resonant thrumming that signals safety or danger.

Deeper still, in the labyrinthine channels carved by erosion, dwell the **Stone-Shapers**. These are the engineers of the organic sprawl. Large, slow-moving beings resembling a cross between a tortoise and a slug, their shells are fused with coral growths that they actively cultivate. They spend decades moving a few yards, dragging their heavy, armored bodies over the floors worn smooth by time. Where they go, new structures grow. Their waste is a potent bio-cement, filling gaps, bridging chasms, and reinforcing weak points in the city’s lattice. They are blind, navigating by tasting the vibrations in the ground. They know every crack, every leak, every tremor. If a section of the city begins to collapse, a Stone-Shaper will arrive, slowly, inevitably, to seal it with its body and secretions. They are solitary, territorial, and ancient. To encounter one is to hear the grinding of its shell against the stone floor, a sound like tectonic plates shifting miles below.

And then, there are the **Hunters**.

They are the reason silence reigns in Oubliette. Known locally as **Pressure-Wraiths**, they are amorphous masses of muscle and nerve tissue, roughly the size of a bear, but lacking any fixed shape. They can compress themselves into narrow crevices or expand to fill large chambers. They have no limbs, no heads, no faces. They are sensory organs wrapped in stealth skin. They hunt by sensing the electromagnetic fields generated by the nervous systems of other living things. They drift silently through the dark, their bodies undulating in rhythm with the ambient currents. When they strike, they do not bite; they envelop. A Pressure-Wraith surrounds its prey, cutting off all vibration, all sound, all movement, suffocating it in a tight, warm embrace before dissolving it internally. They are patient. They wait for hours, days, sometimes weeks, perfectly still, indistinguishable from the shadows. Only when a heartbeat quickens nearby does the shadow begin to move.

High above, where the mist thins slightly and the faintest hint of starlight penetrates, float the **Cloud-Skimmers**. These are the largest creatures in Oubliette, resembling giant manta rays with wingspans wide enough to bridge two spires. Their bodies are covered in bioluminescent fungi that pulse with a faint, internal light—not bright enough to illuminate the darkness, but enough to create a constellation of moving lights in the upper atmosphere. They feed on the thermal updrafts, riding the heat rising from the vents below. They are gentle giants, often ignored by the predators because their size and speed make them difficult targets. They communicate through complex patterns of light and shadow, flashing sequences that ripple across their wings like data streams. To look up is to see these living constellations drifting through the ceiling of clouds, a beautiful, terrifying reminder that even in this submerged hell, there is grace.

But perhaps the most unsettling residents are the **Echo-Bones**. Scattered throughout the lower levels, lodged in the crevices of the older towers, are the remains of creatures that died centuries ago. In Oubliette, death is not final. The hollow bones of these fallen giants serve as natural pipes, carrying sound through the dense medium. Small organisms nest inside them, amplifying the clicks and hums of the city. But sometimes, if you press your ear to a particularly large femur embedded in a wall, you hear something else: a voice. Not a ghost, but a resonance. The bone has absorbed so many vibrations over millennia that it retains a kind of acoustic memory. It replays fragments of conversations, songs, or warnings from the past. Listening to an Echo-Bone is to hear the history of the city, spoken in whispers by the dead.

All these creatures share one truth: they do not fight the environment; they become it. They adapt their bodies to mimic the textures of the walls, the colors of the moss, the movements of the current. Camouflage is not just visual—it is tactile, auditory, and chemical. To stand still in Oubliette is to disappear. To move is to risk being eaten.

The central void, the anchor point where life refuses to grow, is patrolled by a single entity known as **The Stillness**. It is unclear if this is a creature, a phenomenon, or a deity. Those who have ventured too close report feeling their thoughts slow down, their heartbeats sync with the tectonic grind below, and their bodies begin to calcify. It is a guardian of the core, ensuring that the structural integrity of the city remains intact. It does not hunt; it simply exists, a presence that demands reverence and distance.

In Oubliette, life is not a struggle against nature. Life is nature’s way of expressing itself in the dark. Every breath is a sip of the warm, wet air. Every step is a dance with the erosion. Every sound is a prayer to the pressure. And in the end, when a creature dies, it does not rot. It becomes part of the city. Its bones become walls. Its flesh becomes soil for the fungi. Its echo becomes memory. To live here is to accept dissolution, to embrace the thick, warm substance of the dark, and to become, eventually, one more layer in the endless, breathing architecture of the Submerged City.
