They are the **Sap-Singers**, though they have no name for themselves that translates to a sound a human throat could make. To us, they might be called the Weeping Folk, or the Root-Bound, but these names imply a victimhood they do not possess. They are architects of the wet dark, inhabitants of the breath between the fibers.

Physically, they are small—no larger than a grain of rice—but their presence in the Weeping Nodule is dense and deliberate. Their bodies are translucent, revealing the faint, pulsing blue-green rhythm of their own bioluminescent organs beneath skin that resembles polished amber. This internal light is not merely decorative; it is their primary mode of communication and navigation. In the absolute darkness, where shadows are absorbed rather than cast, the Sap-Singers speak in pulses. A rapid flicker means *danger* or *scavenger nearby*; a slow, rhythmic glow signifies *safety*, *home*, or *trade*. When two families meet on the damp root-paths, they stand still, their bodies syncing their lights into complex, hypnotic patterns—a silent conversation conducted entirely through photons trapped in gelatinous tissue.

Their limbs are long and spindly, adapted for gripping the slick, curved surfaces of the living wood. Fingers end not in nails, but in soft, adhesive pads that can secrete a temporary resin, allowing them to hang from ceilings or climb vertical shafts with effortless grace. They move silently, their weight distributed across multiple points of contact so as not to disturb the fragile equilibrium of the water droplets clinging to every surface. To watch them walk is to see a ripple move through a pool; fluid, inevitable, and quiet.

But who lives here is defined less by their bodies and more by their relationship to the **Substrate**. The Sap-Singers do not live *in* the fungal tree; they live *as part* of it. Their society is built on a symbiotic exchange that blurs the line between resident and resource.

Consider the **Gall-Keepers**. These are the masons of the Nodule. They do not carve stone; they negotiate with growth. A Gall-Keeper spends weeks tending to a swelling on the inner bark, feeding it specific nutrients harvested from decomposing beetles, pruning its edges with thorn-tools until the plant’s immune response creates a smooth, hollow chamber. The resulting home is warm, humid, and alive. If the Sap-Singer leaves, the gall begins to close up, reclaiming the space for the tree. If the Sap-Singer stays, the gall stabilizes, hardening into a sanctuary. Thus, their architecture is impermanent. A house is a temporary agreement between the occupant and the host. When a family dies, their home dissolves back into the wood, leaving only a faint scar—a memory in the grain.

Then there are the **Lumen-Tenders**, the cultivators of the blue-green glow. They farm the mycelial networks that thread through the roots, encouraging certain strains of fungi to produce higher yields of light. This is dangerous work. Too much cultivation weakens the structural integrity of the root-columns; too little leaves the pathways in deadly darkness, inviting predators. The Lumen-Tenders move through the city like ghosts, their hands brushing against the walls, sensing the health of the light. They wear crowns of dried spider-silk, which amplifies the faintest glimmer, allowing them to see the microscopic fractures in the wood before they become collapses.

The most mysterious among them are the **Echo-Walkers**. Because the environment is a constant hum of muted life—the clicking of chitin, the dripping of sap, the groan of shifting roots—the Echo-Walkers have evolved an auditory sensitivity that borders on synesthesia. They listen to the Nodule. They know when a heavy rain outside has increased the moisture content of the upper canopy, because the lower levels begin to "sing" at a different pitch. They predict sap-floods by feeling the vibration of pressure building in the deep taproots. They are the historians and the weathermen, reading the story of the tree in its creaks and sighs. An Echo-Walker will press their forehead against a load-bearing column, eyes closed, listening for the heartbeat of the giant fungus, determining if it is sick, strong, or dying.

Their tools are extensions of this intimacy. A **Thorn-Auger** is not just a drill; it is a piece of the forest given purpose. Made from the stingers of dead wasps, hardened in resin, they use it to bore into seeds without killing them, creating cups that last only as long as the seed remains dormant. Once the seed sprouts, the cup is returned to the soil, becoming food for the next generation. Nothing is wasted. Everything is borrowed.

Socially, the Sap-Singers exist in **Resonance Clusters**. These are not neighborhoods in the traditional sense, but acoustic zones. Families arrange their homes so that the natural resonance of the galls harmonizes with those of their neighbors. If a new family moves in and their dwelling clashes tonally with the existing structure—creating a dissonant hum that disturbs the peace—they are asked to leave, or to modify their home until it fits the chord. Community, here, is literally the ability to sing together without making noise.

Yet, there is fear. It lives in the spaces between the light. The **Chitinous Ones**—the large, blind grubs and the predatory beetles that tunnel through the outer layers—are ever-present threats. The Sap-Singers do not fight them; they hide from them. Their defense is camouflage and silence. When a predator enters a corridor, the entire cluster dims its lights, becoming invisible in the velvet dark, pressing themselves flat against the damp walls, holding their breath until the beast passes. To be seen is to be eaten. To be heard is to be hunted. So they cultivate a culture of extreme stillness. Even their conflicts are resolved in whispers of light, never in shouts of anger.

In the deepest hollows, beneath the root-knots, lie the **Nodular Vaults**. Here, the oldest Sap-Singers go to die. They curl into fetal positions within tiny, pre-formed galls, surrounded by a bed of mineralized saliva pearls. As they fade, their bodies release their final burst of bioluminescence, which is captured by the surrounding mycelium. The light does not vanish; it is stored. Over centuries, these vaults have become libraries of pure energy, glowing with the accumulated memories of thousands of generations. The young Sap-Singers visit these vaults to recharge their own internal batteries, absorbing the faint warmth of the dead to sustain the living. Death is not an end, but a conversion—biomass into light, individuality into infrastructure.

So, who lives in The Weeping Nodule?

Not people, exactly. Not insects, either. They are the **Consciousness of the Damp**. They are the thoughts the tree has about itself. They are the reason the hollow feels warm, the reason the darkness has texture, the reason the silence has a pulse. They are the weeping nodules that learned to glow, trapped in the breath of a single stone, building a civilization out of moisture, decay, and the patient, relentless expansion of living wood. They are fragile, yes. A single drop of too-hot water could drown them. But they are also enduring, woven so tightly into the fabric of the substrate that to remove them would be to unravel the world itself.
