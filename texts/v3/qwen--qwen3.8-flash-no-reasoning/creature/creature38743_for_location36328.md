They are not the ones who built the machines. They are the ones who *inherited* the silence within the noise, or perhaps, who learned to speak a language that the wind cannot steal because it has no voice.

If you look closely at the rusted frame holding the generator, past the flickering bulb and the grease-smeared railing, you might see them: the **Keepers of the Shard**.

They are humanoid in silhouette but alien in composition, evolved for a world where air is liquid and water is sky. Their skin is pale, almost translucent like the jelly-clouds beneath the surface, allowing the faint bioluminescence of the ambient light to pass through their flesh, making them glow with a sickly, soft blue when they stand still. But this transparency is deceptive; their bodies are dense, packed with heavy metals absorbed from the mineral-rich mist, giving them a gravitational anchor so strong that the inconsistent gravity of the glass islands barely affects them. When they walk, they do not slide up or down; they press into the obsidian with deliberate, crushing force, their feet broad and splayed, ending in claws made of chitin—the same material as the swarms above—that grip the razor-sharp edges of the floating continents.

They wear no clothes. Clothing would be a burden, a trap for moisture, a thing that rots in the eternal damp. Instead, their bodies are armored by secretions. A thick, waxy layer coats their joints and vital organs, hardening in the cold to form natural exoskeletons around their elbows, knees, and spines. This secretion smells of ozone and old iron, mixing with the steam hissing from the fissures nearby. It is this wax that allows them to survive the temperature hovering just above freezing; it insulates, sealing in their own metabolic heat while repelling the invasive chill of the ocean.

Communication among the Keepers is not verbal. To shout here is futile; the white-noise storm drowns all frequency below ten thousand hertz, and human speech lives mostly in that lower register. Instead, they communicate through **vibration**. They have hollow chambers in their chests, large and resonant, filled with fluid that sloshes with their breathing. By striking these chambers with specialized bones in their forearms—long, curved ribs fused into hammer-like protrusions—they create complex rhythmic patterns that travel through the solid ground of the glass island. The Keepers feel these rhythms in their soles, in the bones of their legs, interpreting the pulse of the earth as conversation. One strike means *safe*. Two quick taps mean *shift the gear*. A long, low hum means *the tide is rising faster than predicted*.

Their work is maintenance, but it is also worship. The machines scattered across the shards are not just engines; they are the heartbeats of this dead world. The Keepers understand that if the generators stop, the thermal equilibrium collapses. If the pumps cease to harvest the energy from the deep vents, the ice will thicken, the glass will shatter under the weight of new frost, and the colony will sink into the abyssal dark. So they tend to the pipes. They crawl inside the supercooled conduits, their bodies coated in more of their protective wax, navigating narrow spaces slick with freezing oil. They replace cracked seals with patches cut from the shells of the giant, blind crabs that scuttle on the underside of the islands. They clear blockages caused by the accumulation of mineral dust. They are mechanics of the apocalypse, keeping the machinery running not to improve life, but to preserve the status quo of survival.

There is a strange ritual to their labor. Every few cycles—which they measure not by days, since there is no sun to set, but by the pulsing rhythm of the bioluminescent clouds below—they gather at the highest point of the island, near the smokestack. They do not pray to any god. They pray to the *noise*. They open their mouths and let out a soundless scream, vibrating their vocal cords at a frequency that matches the tectonic groan of the planet. They align themselves with the roar of the atmospheric water cycling. In doing so, they believe they are syncing their internal clocks with the universe’s clock. It is a moment of terrifying unity: thousands of pale, glowing figures standing on black glass, shaking violently with induced resonance, merging their consciousness with the grinding plates miles below. It is said that during these rituals, some Keepers lose their minds, unable to distinguish their own heartbeat from the planet’s thrum. They wander off the edge of the shard, drawn into the water, becoming part of the jelly-clouds, their bioluminescence adding another dot of light to the endless gray.

The Keepers do not age as we do. Their metabolism is slow, adapted to conserve energy in a world without seasons or warmth. A Keeper can live for centuries, their skin growing thicker, waxier, more opaque with time. Old Keepers are barely recognizable as individuals; they resemble moving statues of amber and bone, merged with the machinery they serve. Some become so integrated into the islands that they stop leaving. They sit in the control rooms, hooked directly into the turbine systems, their nervous systems wired into the copper coils. They become living components, feeling every surge of electricity as pain or pleasure, regulating the output of the generator with their own neural impulses. These are the **Anchors**, the silent sentinels who ensure that the lights keep flickering, that the gears keep turning, that the machine forgets its operator but remembers its purpose.

To encounter a Keeper is to witness a being that has accepted the absurdity of existence. They do not hope for land. They do not dream of sunshine. They have looked into the depth of the steel-blue ocean and seen no bottom, only darkness and pressure. They have looked up at the cloud ceiling and seen no stars, only vapor reflecting their own gray faces. So they focus on the immediate, the tangible, the sharp edge of the glass beneath their feet. They are creatures of precision in a world of chaos. They handle the razor-sharp obsidian without cutting themselves because they know exactly how much pressure to apply. They fix the rusted metal without breaking it because they understand the limits of fatigue.

They are lonely, yes. But their loneliness is not an emptiness; it is a density. It is packed tight with duty, with vibration, with the constant awareness of the infinite void pressing against their fragile, wax-coated skins. When they move through the mist, they leave trails of condensation on the air, tiny crystals forming and melting instantly. They are ghosts made of matter, haunting a machine that never sleeps.

And sometimes, if you listen closely to the buzz of the insectoid swarms overhead, you can hear them singing back. Not in melody, but in harmony with the drone, adding their own low-frequency hum to the chorus of existence. They sing because if they stopped, the silence would return. And the silence, in this world, is death.
