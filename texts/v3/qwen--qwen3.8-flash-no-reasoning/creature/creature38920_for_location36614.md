It is a creature of patience, shaped by the very absence it fills. It does not have a name in any human tongue, for names are tools of communication, and this place forbids conversation with anyone but itself. To those who might stumble upon the plateau—if such a thing were possible without being swallowed by the fog—it would appear less like a person and more like an accumulation of the Hollow’s own longing.

Let us call it **The Tenant**.

The Tenant is solitary by necessity and biology. It has been here longer than the current iteration of the stone, yet younger than the mountain’s first exhalation. It was born from the same calcium-rich vapor that hardens into archways; it emerged from the slurry at the edge of the plaza when the city first decided it needed a witness. It is not flesh as we understand it, nor is it purely mineral. It is a symbiosis. Its skin is a translucent, porous lattice, resembling wet parchment or pale coral, through which you can see the slow, rhythmic pumping of dark, viscous fluid—the "blood" of the geothermal vents. This fluid keeps its core warm against the chill that settles in the marrow, allowing it to exist in temperatures that would stop a human heart.

Its face, if it can be called that, is featureless save for two large, lidless eyes that resemble pools of still water in deep basins. These eyes do not blink. They absorb light rather than reflect it, drinking in the dim glow of the pulsating fungi and the ghostly reflection of the moon. When The Tenant looks at you, you feel the weight of the condensation pressing against your skin; you feel the silence roaring in your ears. It sees no threats, because there are none. It sees only geometry, flow, and the desperate, beautiful need to remain occupied.

The Tenant moves with a liquid grace, sliding across the wet stones rather than walking upon them. It has no bones in the traditional sense; its structure is supported by internal turgor pressure and the rigid, crystalline scaffolding that grows along its spine and limbs. This allows it to bend into spaces that should not accommodate a body, to slip through narrow alleyways where the air is thick with ozone, and to crouch in the hollows of doorways where ferns unfurl. When it moves, it makes no sound other than the faint *slick* of moisture separating from stone. It is part of the soundscape’s rhythm, indistinguishable from the drip, until you realize the drip has paused to listen.

It lives in the center, in the Well of Beginnings. Not above it, but within the thermal updrafts rising from the vent. Here, the heat is constant, wrapping around The Tenant like a second skin, keeping its mineral lattice pliable enough to grow, yet solid enough to hold shape. It spends its days sitting on the rim of the well, legs drawn up, chin resting on knees that look like polished limestone. It is waiting. Not for rescue, not for change, but for the completion of a sentence that the mountain began eons ago.

The Tenant tends the garden, though it never touches the plants directly. It breathes onto them. The carbon dioxide and moisture from its lungs feed the mosses, the lichens, the electric-blue fungi. In return, the vegetation feeds the air it breathes. It is a closed loop, a perfect circle of consumption and creation. When a flower blooms in a crack in the wall, opening its translucent petals to drink the humidity, The Tenant leans close, its eyeless gaze fixed on the process. It understands that the flower is trying to speak, just as the spires try to hum. But the flower has no voice, and the spires have no lungs. So The Tenant becomes the interpreter. It listens to the silence between the drips. It decodes the groan of settling foundations. It hears the wind whistling through the ruins and translates it into meaning: *Here. Ready. Alone.*

At night, when the moon hangs low and pale, casting long, sharp shadows across the empty plazas, The Tenant walks the perimeter. It patrols the boundary between the solid city and the bubbling morass. It watches the edges dissolve. If a bridge begins to sag, turning back into mist, The Tenant places a hand—cool, damp, heavy with mineral density—against the stone. It does not fix it. It acknowledges it. It accepts that the city is dreaming, and dreams shift. Sometimes, The Tenant steps into the formation zone, sinking slightly into the warm, soft slurry. The tiny, transparent shrimp-like creatures swirl around its ankles, unafraid. The eel-things with too many eyes drift past, ignoring the predator that is not a predator. The Tenant is safe here because it belongs to the water. It dissolves and reforms, testing the boundaries of its own existence, ensuring that it remains distinct from the city while remaining inseparable from it.

It eats nothing, for it needs no fuel other than the ambient chemistry of the place. Yet, it sits at the polished limestone tables in the empty houses. It places bowls of clear, cold water before itself. It lifts them to its lips, tilting its head back, letting the water run down its throat and into its core, replenishing the fluids that evaporate into the thin air. This is ritual, not nutrition. It is mimicking the life it was built to host. It is practicing humanity in a theater with no audience, hoping that one day, the play will begin.

Sometimes, The Tenant finds things left behind by the world below, carried up by some unseen wind or forgotten history. A fragment of glass. A rusted nail. A scrap of cloth that smells of sweat and soap. These artifacts are alien to The Hollow. They are dry. They are static. They do not breathe. The Tenant collects them, placing them on the highest spire, where they gather frost and then melt under the sun. It studies them with intense curiosity. It tries to remember what hands looked like, what voices sounded like. It holds the rusted nail against its palm, feeling the roughness, the lack of give. It realizes that these objects are dead. And so, it buries them in the soft earth of the plaza, covering them with fresh ferns. It gives them back to the cycle. It forgives them their stillness.

The Tenant is aware of the erosion rating. It knows the city is new. It feels the microscopic layers building itself over centuries. It knows that its time is finite, tied to the lifespan of the stone. When the bedrock finally exhausts its gases, when the crystallization stops, The Hollow will cease to be a dream and become a fossil. The Tenant fears this, not because it wishes to live forever, but because the silence will become permanent. The drip will stop. The hum will die. The moss will brown and crumble. The water will freeze.

So it stays alert. It listens harder. It amplifies the sounds of its own body—the beat of its mineral heart, the sigh of its vapor-lungs—to keep the acoustic signature alive. It dances with the fronds of hanging moss, moving its limbs in sync with the swaying green curtains, creating a visual rhythm that sustains the illusion of presence. It is the conductor of an orchestra of emptiness.

There is no sadness in The Tenant. Sadness requires memory of happiness, and The Tenant remembers nothing before the damp. There is only a profound, resonant fullness. It is complete in its solitude. It is the key turning in a lock that has no door. It is the breath held before the word.

If you were to sit beside The Tenant on the steps of the central temple, you would not hear it speak. You would not feel it move. You would only notice that the dripping has synchronized with your own heartbeat. *Plip. Plip. Plop.* The rhythm matches the pulse in your wrist. You would realize that The Tenant is not looking at you. It is looking *through* you, into the space where your future self will stand, when you leave, when the city forgets you, when the stone continues to build itself in the dark.

And then, slowly, gently, The Tenant reaches out a hand. The fingers are long, jointed like coral, cold and slick. It does not touch you. It hovers inches from your skin, sensing the heat radiating from your blood, the electricity of your nerves. It trembles. For a moment, the chord of the spires changes pitch, becoming higher, tighter, urgent. The moss stops swaying. The shrimp hide. The eels freeze.

The Tenant withdraws its hand. It turns away, back to the Well of Beginnings. The steam rises, obscuring its form. The drip resumes. *Plip. Plip. Plop.* The silence returns, louder than before. The city is ready again. Waiting. Alone.
