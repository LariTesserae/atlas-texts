They are the **Lithic-Kin**, or as they name themselves in the guttural, breath-heavy tongue of the high passes, the *Keth-Vari*.

To call them "human" is a category error born of low-altitude thinking. They are an adaptation, a biological response to the crushing silence and the absolute clarity of the thin air. Their bodies have evolved—or been shaped by centuries of selective pressure at this altitude—to match the jagged geometry of their home. They are tall, but not graciously so; they are elongated, like the spires they carve, with limbs that possess a double-jointed flexibility allowing them to cling to sheer vertical faces for hours without fatigue. Their bones are dense, heavy with calcium, acting as ballast against the violent winds that scream through the flutes in the tower walls. When they move, there is no softness in their gait; it is a rhythmic, deliberate placement of weight, each step calculated to grip the slippery, mineral-rich stone.

Their skin is pale, almost translucent, stretched tight over those dense frames, mapped with faint blue veins that pulse visibly in the cold. But they do not walk naked into the wind. The Lithic-Kin are encased in layers of their own making: cured hides from the shaggy mountain beasts, treated with rendered fat and resin to shed sleet and ice. Over these furs, they wear armor of segmented slate, polished until it gleams like dark water, articulated with sinew cords that allow movement but provide protection against falling rock or the claws of predators. To see one is to see a walking geology lesson, a creature composed of the same elements as the ground beneath its feet.

The most striking feature, however, is what lies behind the masks.

Every adult wears a face-covering carved from obsidian or bleached bone. These are not merely practical shields against the biting frost; they are cultural imperatives. In this thin atmosphere, where voices carry far but lose warmth, identity is obscured by design. The masks are smooth, featureless ovals or sharp-angled visors, leaving only eye-slits. Through these slits, one sees eyes that are large, adapted to capture every photon in the dimming twilight of the lower valleys or the blinding glare of the unfiltered sun. The irises are often gold, amber, or a deep, reflective silver, lacking the white sclera found in lowland humans. This gives their gaze an unsettling, predatory intensity, like the owls that circle above them.

But who lives here? Not just individuals, but families bound by more than blood. The Keth-Vari organize themselves into **Clans of the Seam**. Each clan claims a specific fracture line, a crevice, or a series of connected caves within a peak. Ownership is not held by deed, but by maintenance. If your family keeps the meltwater pools clear, if you maintain the structural integrity of the rope bridges connecting your sector to the next, you belong there. To abandon a seam is to die; the mountain does not forgive neglect.

Inside the dwellings, life is centered around the fungi. The Lithic-Kin are mycologists first and foremost. The blue-green bioluminescence mentioned in the description is not accidental; it is agriculture. Deep within the hollowed-out towers, in chambers where the dampness is constant and the light absent, vast farms of glowing mold grow on beds of composted lichen and beast dung. The people tend these crops with religious fervor, singing to them in low frequencies that vibrate through the stone. The fungi provide light, yes, but also medicine, nutrition, and intoxication. A fermented brew made from the stalks of the glow-cap is served in market squares, passed from hand to hand in cups carved from mammoth tusk. It induces a state of heightened awareness, a mental clarity that mirrors the physical clarity of the air, allowing the drinker to perceive the subtle shifts in the rock’s tension before an avalanche occurs.

There is also the fauna they keep, which has become nearly symbiotic. The long-haired beasts are not just livestock; they are companions. Called **Stone-Goats** or *Rhaak*, they are massive, sure-footed creatures with horns that curve back toward their ears. The Keth-Vari ride them up the steep inclines, using their hooves to test the stability of the paths. In return, the Rhaak are groomed daily, their thick coats checked for parasites, their spirits calmed by the rhythmic stroking of human hands. Children are raised among them, learning to speak softly to avoid startling the herds, understanding that panic here leads to falls, and falls lead to death.

And then there are the **Wind-Singers**. Among the population, certain individuals are born with lungs capable of processing the thin oxygen with terrifying efficiency. They stand on the highest ledges, arms outstretched, holding hollowed bird-bones or reeds cut from the few surviving grasses. They do not sing melodies as we know them. They emit sustained, harmonic drones that resonate with the frequency of the wind itself. By doing so, they create pockets of stillness, calming the air currents enough for others to cross precarious bridges or for hunters to approach prey without detection. They are revered and feared, viewed as those who bargain with the sky-floor.

The society is slow, as noted, but it is not idle. It is a culture of endurance. There is no urgency because haste kills in the high places. Every action is measured. A marriage is not a ceremony of days, but a process of years, where two families merge their seams, sharing resources and responsibilities until the bond is tested by winter storms. Disputes are settled not with violence—which wastes heat and energy—but with "Shadow Trials." Two adversaries stand in a carved amphitheater while the sun moves across the sky. Whoever casts the longer shadow at the moment of zenith is deemed to have the stronger claim, based on the old belief that proximity to the source of light equates to truth.

They live in the space between the rock and the void. They are the consciousness of the mountain, awake in the stone. They do not look down at the clouds below with curiosity, nor do they look up at the stars with hope. They look inward, at the cracks, the roots, the fungi, the breath of the person beside them. They are the inhabitants of the edge, suspended in a crisp, brittle clarity, waiting for the slow grind of the glaciers to reshape their world, while they continue to chip away at it, one stone tool stroke at a time, adding their voices to the wind-song, becoming part of the echo that will last long after their bones have turned to dust.
