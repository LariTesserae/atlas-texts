They are not people, not in the way you might imagine a person to be. They have no faces that smile or frown; they have no skin that burns under a sun, for there is no sun to burn them. They are **The Weavers of the Wet**, though they call themselves something else, a sound like water sliding over smooth stone, which translates roughly as *Those Who Hold The Line*.

To look at them is to struggle with depth perception. Their bodies are semi-translucent, composed of a dense, gelatinous cartilage that shares the same refractive index as the air around them. When still, they seem to dissolve into the mist, becoming merely a slight distortion in the light, a ripple in the fabric of the room. Only when they move do they become solid—long, slender limbs ending in hands with too many fingers, each finger tipped with a chitinous nail carved from their own shed bone, used for pruning the aggressive orchids and stitching the kelp-fibers of their homes.

They live inside the architecture they grow. There is no distinction between inhabitant and habitat. A family does not live in a house; a family *is* the house’s nervous system. The bioluminescent walls pulse in sync with their collective heartbeat, a slow, thrumming rhythm that keeps the structural integrity of the spiraling towers stable. If one of them dies, the wall where they slept dims slightly, turning a bruised purple before fading to black, leaving a scar of cold resin that must be carefully excised by the mid-level guilds.

Their society is organized by verticality, but not in a hierarchy of power. It is a hierarchy of sensation.

**The Upper Canopy Keepers** are the tallest among them, stretched thin by the lower pressure above. They wear masks made of polished obsidian to protect their sensitive eyes from the faint, distant glow of whatever lies beyond the ceiling-water. They spend their days hanging suspended from the rock shelves, harvesting the dripping moss and watching the blind fish glide through the liquid sky. They speak rarely, communicating through gestures that trace the patterns of salt-crystals left on the streets below. To them, the world is a place of silence and vast, empty spaces. They feel closest to the "beyond," and this proximity makes them melancholic, prone to long periods of stillness where they seem to forget they are alive.

**The Mid-Level Residents** are the bulk of the population, the heart of the city. Here, the air is warm and thick with the scent of fermenting fruit and ozone. They tend to the residential stacks, maintaining the delicate balance of heat and humidity required for the buildings to continue growing. Their bodies are stockier, adapted to the weight of the vapor. They use clay pipes to channel geothermal vents, directing warmth into the roots of the spiral towers. In these quarters, life is communal and crowded. Walls are shared membranes; privacy is an illusion. A conversation in one room vibrates through the floorboards of another. They know each other’s moods by the temperature of the air in their immediate vicinity. When anger rises, the local humidity spikes, condensing on their skin. When joy spreads, the air thins, carrying the scent of mint from the blooming orchids.

**The Lower Ward Dwellers** are the most changed. Down here, where the light is almost nonexistent and the air is so dense it feels like breathing soup, the Weavers have evolved—or perhaps devolved—into creatures of pure touch and taste. Their eyes have atrophied into dark, glossy spots, useless in the gloom. Instead, their fingertips have grown long, splayed, and highly sensitive, capable of reading the geometric salt-patterns on the floors with microscopic precision. They descend into the murky depths to harvest nutrients from the fissures, collecting the mineral slurry that feeds the city’s foundations. They are the keepers of the "deep memory." It is said that if you press your ear to the ground in the Lower Wards, you can hear the grinding of the newborn basalt rising from the abyss. These dwellers do not fear the darkness; they embrace it. They believe that true sight comes only when the eyes are closed.

But what truly defines who lives here is not their physical form, but their relationship with time and truth.

They do not walk in straight lines because they cannot trust geometry. They navigate by smell and by the vibration of the pumps. They carry small, sealed gourds filled with stagnant water from the pools below. When faced with a decision—a dispute over resources, a choice of path—they pour a drop of this water onto a flat stone and watch how it spreads. The way the droplet fractures, the tiny tributaries it forms, is interpreted as the will of the basin. This is their law. It is capricious, shifting, and utterly non-Euclidean. A ruling given in the morning may contradict itself by evening, depending on the current of the mist.

They are haunted, too. Not by ghosts, but by reflections. Because the water remembers, the Weavers often see others in their mirrors—not their ancestors, but strangers. A woman in the Upper Canopy might look into her obsidian mask and see the face of a man from the Lower Wards, centuries dead, weeping silent tears of brine. She does not scream. She simply nods, acknowledging the debt of existence she owes to this unknown soul. They collect these faces. They carve them into the walls of their homes, creating mosaics of stolen identities. To walk through the city is to walk through a gallery of the displaced, a labyrinth of borrowed visages.

There are children, though few survive the transition from birth to maturity. Born in the heated pools, they emerge as soft, amorphous lumps of tissue, lacking distinct features. For the first year, they are fed by the ambient moisture of the air, absorbing nutrients directly through their skin. It is during this phase that the parents decide whether to prune the child’s growth—shaping its limbs toward the elongated grace of the Upper Keepers, or the sturdy density of the Mid-Levels, or the flattened sensitivity of the Lower Wards. This pruning is done with bone-blades, guided by the salt-patterns read on the nursery floor. It is a painful process, but necessary. Without shape, one dissolves back into the mist. Without identity, one becomes just another drip in the endless rain.

And then there are the outsiders—the rare visitors who fall from the sky above, breaking the membrane of the water-ceiling. They arrive in bubbles of trapped air, gasping, their rigid bones clattering against the organic walls. The Weavers watch them with curiosity, not hostility. These "Dry Ones" are alien, brittle things, unable to understand the fluid logic of the city. They try to build structures of right angles, but the walls refuse them, pulsing away from the sharp corners. Eventually, the Dry Ones either suffocate in the heavy air, or they learn to soften. Some choose to stay. Over years, their joints loosen, their skin takes on a translucent sheen, and they begin to dream in currents rather than words. They become part of the weave, their memories absorbed into the pools, their bodies integrated into the spiraling architecture until they, too, are indistinguishable from the mist.

So, who lives here?

The living and the dead, fused together.
The architect and the material, inseparable.
A civilization that breathes fog, reads salt, and sees with its skin.

They are waiting for the ceiling to break, or for the floor to rise. Or perhaps they are waiting for nothing at all, content to exist in the eternal, viscous twilight, building their beautiful, impossible maze one millimeter at a time, unaware that they are already dissolved, that they are already the ocean dreaming it has a shape.
