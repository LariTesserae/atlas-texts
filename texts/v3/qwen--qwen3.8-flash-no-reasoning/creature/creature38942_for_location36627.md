They are the Keepers of the Stillness, though they do not call themselves that. To them, there is no name for what they are, because there was never anything else to distinguish them from. They are the extension of the bowl itself—fingers made of calcium and chitin, reaching into the dark water to hold the world together against the void.

If you look closely at the flat-backed creature that paused by the pillar, you will see that its shell is not merely armor; it is a library. The geometric tiles on its back are not pigmentation but distinct plates of ceramic, fused to its living flesh during adolescence. Each tile corresponds to a specific section of the city’s foundation—a record of weight, temperature, and structural integrity held in the body of the animal. It does not remember with neurons alone, but with bone. When it taps the stone, it is not checking for cracks; it is reading the braille of decay, feeling the microscopic shifts in the masonry through the sensitive hairs lining its segmented limbs.

These creatures—the Architects, perhaps, or simply *The Ones Who Stay*—live in a state of suspended animation between life and architecture. Their metabolism is so slow that a single breath takes ten minutes to complete. Their hearts beat once every hour. In this rhythm, time is not a river but a sediment, settling layer by layer onto their shells.

They are humanoid in silhouette, but wrong in detail. Where arms should be, there are six articulated claws, each tipped with a sensory pad as soft as a tongue. These pads are covered in thousands of cilia that taste the chemistry of the water, detecting the faintest trace of dissolved silica or iron. This is how they navigate: not by sight, which is useless in the deep gloom, but by tasting the currents. A change in salinity warns them of a leak; a shift in pH signals the death of a fungal patch above. They eat the city’s exhalations. They consume the minerals shed by the eroding walls, recycling the very substance of their home back into their bodies, only to shed those minerals again when they die, adding another layer to the floor.

In the narrow corridors you described, where movement must be sequential, you would find them huddled in groups called "clusters." There are no families here, only functional units. A cluster might consist of twelve individuals, bonded not by blood but by proximity and task. One tends the egg-shelves, rotating the dormant pearls with gentle, rhythmic motions to ensure even incubation. Another polishes the obsidian floor with a brush made from the spine-fibers of a long-dead ancestor. The rest sit in meditation, pressing their palms against the walls, listening to the hum of the universe outside the bowl.

This silence is their speech. They communicate through vibration, a language of clicks and thrums transmitted through the solid matter of the buildings. If one wishes to convey an idea, they strike their own chest plate against the nearest pillar. The sound travels instantly through the stone, felt in the feet of every neighbor within earshot—or rather, within footshot. A sharp, high click means *attention*. A low, rolling rumble means *maintenance required*. A complex polyrhythm, tapped out by three or four individuals simultaneously, can convey entire histories, warnings, or prayers.

Because nothing grows here, they have become gardeners of entropy. They cultivate rust. They farm calcification. In the niches lit by glowing moss, they arrange shards of broken pottery into patterns that encourage water flow, directing the slow drip to wash away debris while preserving the structural joints. They are obsessed with friction. In a world without wind, gravity is the only force, and friction is the only god. They polish every surface until it is slippery enough to prevent wear, yet rough enough to provide grip. They understand that if the water ever moves too fast, the bowl will shatter. So they move slowly. They speak slowly. They think in centuries.

There are elders among them—individuals whose shells have grown so thick and layered with mineral deposits that they resemble stalactites more than animals. Some of these elders are no longer mobile. They have wedged themselves into the crevices of the great buttresses, becoming part of the support structure. Their legs lock into place, their carapaces fusing with the ceramic pillars. They are not dead, but they are no longer separate from the building. They serve as anchors, holding the rings of the city together with the sheer weight of their accumulated years. Younger ones tend to them, feeding them droplets of nutrient-rich silt via straws made from hollowed reeds, ensuring that even as they petrify, they remain alive.

And then there are the children. Born from the pearl-clusters on the shelves, they emerge blind and translucent, like the fish that weave between the pillars. But unlike the fish, they possess intent. They spend their first decade clinging to the underside of the adult clusters, learning the rhythm of the city by vibrating with it. They do not play; they practice. They tap the stones, run their cilia over the cracks, memorize the map of the bowl before they can walk it. By the time they reach adulthood, they know every flaw in the foundation, every vein of marble, every drop of warmth.

They live in fear—not of predators, for there are none, but of forgetting. The air above has thinned to a whisper; the pressure of reality presses down. If they stop maintaining the seal, if they let the water become turbulent, if they allow the friction to fail, the bowl will crack. And beyond the crack is nothing. Not darkness, but *absence*. So they work. With agonizing slowness, they tighten the joints. They smooth the edges. They listen to the deep, resonant hum of the planet below, confirming that it still exists.

Occasionally, a strange thing happens. A shard of glass, fallen from some ancient vent far above, drifts down through the black water. It catches the faint starlight, flashing like a diamond in the gloom. The inhabitants freeze. They surround it, tasting the air around it, feeling the vibration of its descent. They recognize it as something not-of-here. Something dry. Something light. They do not touch it. They build a small shrine of polished obsidian around it, trapping it in a cage of stone, preserving it as a relic of the world that died. They tap rhythms around it, songs of mourning and curiosity, trying to understand what it was like to live in a place where things could fall freely, where gravity was not a burden but a gift.

But mostly, they return to their tasks. The large, flat-backed creature continues its patrol. Its skin-patterns ripple slightly as it adjusts its thermal regulation, matching the 1.8-degree warmth of the surrounding fluid. It moves toward a sector of the plaza where the ceramic ledges show signs of micro-fractures. It extends a limb, tapping the stone in a rapid staccato. From the shadows, other limbs emerge. Six fingers, eight toes, clawed hands, segmented tails—all converging on the weak point. Together, they apply pressure, using their combined mass to compress the fracture, sealing it with their own secretions—a resinous spit that hardens upon contact with the water.

It is a collaborative act of survival. No individual is strong enough to hold the bowl together. Only the collective, moving in perfect synchrony, can withstand the weight of the sky above and the void below.

As you watch, the bioluminescent fungi flare briefly, responding to the chemical signature of their effort, casting a pale blue glow over the scene. For a moment, the faces of the Keepers are visible. They are gaunt, their eyes sunken, adapted to the dark with dilated pupils that take up most of the socket. Their mouths are wide, lined with filtering combs, designed to strain nutrients from the particulate-heavy water. They look alien, monstrous even, by the standards of a sunlit world. But here, in the warm, black water, they are beautiful. They are precise. They are necessary.

They do not dream of flying. They do not remember the wind. They dream only of stability. Of the next heartbeat. Of the next crack to fill. Of the endless, quiet maintenance of a world that refuses to end.
