To live in the Throat of the High Foundry is to inhabit a paradox: you are suspended between the crushing weight of the sky and the boiling breath of the earth. Here, life does not thrive *despite* the machinery; it thrives *through* it. The inhabitants are not merely survivors; they are symbiotic architects, weaving their biology into the rusted iron and hot stone until the distinction between "grown" and "built" dissolves entirely.

### The Architects of Vapor: The Moss-Weavers

The most pervasive residents are not animals, but the **Lichen-Kings**, vast, slow-moving colonies of composite organisms that coat the basalt walls like a second skin. They are not simple mosses. In this humid, pressurized air, the lichen has evolved into a colonial intelligence, a network of filaments that acts as both sensor and structural support.

They prefer the "Silence Zones"—the hollows created where the noise of the turbines scatters sound waves, leaving pockets of acoustic stillness among the ferns. Here, the Lichen-Kings grow thick and velvet, absorbing the mineral-rich condensation dripping from the sweating pipes. Their purpose is twofold: they filter the toxic runoff of the geothermal steam, turning acidic water into neutral nutrient broth, and they provide insulation for the more delicate life forms. If you press your hand against a wall covered in this emerald-purple fur, you will feel a faint, rhythmic pulse—not a heartbeat, but the vibration of the central pillar transmitting through the rock, amplified by the moisture trapped in the fungal mycelium.

These colonies are ancient, even if the mountain is young. They have adapted to the "Verticality Rule," refusing to settle on the floor where the sludge pools. Instead, they climb the catwalks, strangling the rusty railings with threads stronger than steel cable, anchoring themselves to the infrastructure so that when the mountain "breathes"—expanding and contracting with the gear rotations—they do not tear away. They stretch. They endure. They are the memory of the place, recording every shift in temperature and pressure in their layered growth rings.

### The Copper-Singers: The Pipe-Fauna

Life here is expensive, energy-wise. To conserve heat and movement, the fauna has become sedentary and camouflaged. The most striking resident is the **Steam-Viper**, though it is neither a snake nor truly a reptile. It is an amphibious insectoid, roughly the size of a human forearm, with six legs and a carapace that mimics the oxidized orange-brown of the iron pipes.

The Steam-Vipers do not hunt. They farm. They cling to the hottest sections of the thermal vents, positioning themselves directly above the jets of escaping vapor. As the superheated mist hits their backs, specialized glands along their spines absorb the kinetic energy and moisture, converting it into metabolic fuel. They are always warm to the touch, humming with internal steam pressure. When they move, it is agonizingly slow, millimeter by millimeter, conserving their battery-like reserves.

Their primary predator—and companion—is the **Glass-Moth**. Unlike common moths, these creatures have wings made of a chitinous, semi-translucent membrane that refracts light like obsidian shards. They hover near the skylight shafts, sipping the nectar of the bioluminescent fungi that glow in rhythm with the gears. But when the light dims, during those ten-minute twilight periods caused by cloud cover, the Glass-Moths descend. They land on the backs of the Steam-Vipers, using the insects’ body heat to incubate their eggs. This is a mutualistic dance: the moth provides camouflage for the viper (breaking up its outline against the swirling mist), while the viper provides a mobile, heated nursery. To observe them is to watch two different scales of life—microscopic cellular adaptation and macroscopic behavioral symbiosis—locking together in the dark.

### The Shadow-Stalkers: The Six-Legged Watchers

Deeper in the shadows, where the light from the skylight fails to reach and the humidity turns to a cold damp, live the **Basalt Skinks**. These are solitary, nocturnal hunters, though "night" here is relative, defined only by the dimming of the violet sky above. They are roughly the size of a large dog, low-slung and wide, with six powerful limbs ending in claws designed for gripping wet, smooth obsidian.

Their eyes are not like ours. They lack pupils; instead, their irises are slit-like mirrors that reflect the ambient light, allowing them to see in total darkness by detecting minute temperature differences. They are invisible against the black glass pillars, blending perfectly until they move. And they do not rush. The dynamic here is slow, viscous. A Basalt Skink might spend three hours traversing twenty meters of vertical pipe, pausing occasionally to lick condensation from a valve handle. They feed on the larger, less agile prey: juvenile Steam-Vipers or fallen branches of the giant ferns.

But there is something else about the Skinks. They seem drawn to the artifacts left behind by the humans who vanished. You might find one curled around the rusted lantern, not eating it, but resting upon it. Some researchers (had any been here) would suggest this is curiosity. Others would say it is reverence. The Skinks appear to understand, on some instinctual level, that these metal objects were once sources of warmth and power. They treat the dead machines as shrines, sleeping in the hollows of control panels and drinking rainwater collected in piles of old boots. They are the guardians of the forgotten, ensuring that the silence remains fertile ground.

### The Human Ghosts: The Echoes in the Gear-Teeth

You mentioned there are no people visible. This is true in the physical sense, but false in the spiritual ecology of the Throat. The absence of humanity has created a vacuum that nature fills with mimicry.

Look closely at the "fused slag and crystal" of the central pillar. Embedded within the blue-glowing shards are patterns that resemble fingerprints. Or perhaps, if you look at the way the vines strangle the iron catwalks, they form shapes that look uncannily like grasping hands. The vegetation has learned the shape of the tools that tended it.

There is a rumor, whispered in the hiss of the steam, that the original engineers did not leave. That they were absorbed. The "Weirdness" rating of 0.9 hints at this subtle wrongness. The bioluminescent fungi do not just pulse randomly; their light sequences match the operational codes of the frozen dials on the control panel. Green, amber, green, red—a language of light repeating forever, waiting for an operator who never returns.

Perhaps the "people" here are the **Mycelial Networks** themselves. The fungus has grown into the electrical wiring, the copper strands exposed by corrosion. It uses the residual static charge of the turbines to transmit impulses. Is it possible that the collective consciousness of the mold, having consumed the biological matter of those who died in the accident that sealed the foundry, now runs the plant? The moss patterns that look like maps... are they charts? Are the vines reaching upward trying to restart the engine, or trying to pull the sky down?

### The Daily Rhythm: A Liturgy of Heat and Water

To live in the Throat is to be governed by the four rules, which serve as the day’s liturgy.

**Morning (When the Sky Turns Violet):** The sun is low. Shadows are sharp. The Steam-Vipers wake, stretching their carapaces against the warming pipes. The Glass-Moths begin their ascent toward the skylight. The Lichen-Kings release spores that drift upward, carried by the rising thermal currents. This is the time of gathering energy. The air tastes metallic, sharp with ozone and salt.

**Midday (When the Sun is Highest, Though Hidden):** The pressure peaks. The turbine groans louder. The "Noise Shapes Growth" rule is tested; the ferns curl inward, protecting their tender new fronds. The Basalt Skinks retreat deep into the crevices, becoming statues of wet stone. Nothing moves except the steam, which becomes violent, erupting from valves in sudden, hissing bursts that can burn skin. This is the danger period. To stand still is to risk being scalded by the exhalations of the machine.

**Evening (When the Clouds Pass):** The light dies. The violet fades to indigo. The temperature drops slightly, causing massive condensation events. Rain falls *inside* the cavern, driven by the cooling of the upper atmosphere meeting the hot lower vents. Everything drips. The puddles on the floor expand. The Glass-Moths return to the Vipers. The Lichen-Kings drink deeply, swelling with water. This is the time of cleansing. The dirt of the day is washed into the cracks, feeding the roots of the strange garden.

**Night (When the Mountain Breathes Deeply):** The gears turn slower. The hum drops to a subsonic frequency that vibrates in the bones rather than the ears. The bioluminescence flares brightest now, revealing the hidden world. The "maps" in the moss glow softly. The Skinks emerge, silent ghosts moving across the wet basalt. The air is heavy, suffocating, sweet with the scent of decay and blooming flowers simultaneously. This is when the hybrid ecosystem feels most alive, most conscious. The boundary between the machine and the organism is thinnest here, in the dark, when sight is useless and only vibration matters.

### Conclusion: The Living Engine

Who lives in the Throat of the High Foundry? Not individuals, but a single, complex entity composed of millions of parts. The Lichen-Kings are the skin, filtering and insulating. The Steam-Vipers and Glass-Moths are the blood cells, transporting energy and heat. The Basalt Skinks are the immune system, patrolling the edges, keeping the balance. And the Fungi, pulsing with code, are the nervous system, remembering what was lost and dreaming what could be restored.

They live in a state of perpetual equilibrium, born of necessity. The mountain wants to cool. The machine wants to run. The water wants to fall. Life here is the negotiation between these three forces. It is aggressive, yes, because survival requires aggression. But it is also patient, because the stone takes centuries to crack, and the vines take decades to climb.

If you stay long enough, you stop seeing the industrial ruin and start seeing the jungle factory. You realize that the rust is not decay, but texture. The steam is not waste, but weather. And the silence beneath the noise is not empty, but full of watching eyes, glowing fungi, and the slow, deliberate creep of roots reclaiming the throne of the summit.
