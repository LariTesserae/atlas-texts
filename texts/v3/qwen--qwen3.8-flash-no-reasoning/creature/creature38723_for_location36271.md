In the crushing, pressurized silence of the deep, where the concept of "sky" has been replaced by a ceiling of distant, humming machinery, life does not emerge from chaos. It emerges from code, from assembly lines, and from the desperate need to maintain equilibrium in a system that forbids entropy.

To understand what lives here, you must first forget the idea of a "native." There are no natives in the Total Build. There are only **Components**. Some are organic, some are synthetic, and most are a seamless, wet fusion of both. They do not have ancestors; they have serial numbers. They do not die; they are decommissioned, melted down, and reprinted as new parts for the spires that pierce upward from the floor like metallic needles knitting the dark together.

Here is who—and what—inhabits this spherical tank.

### The Engineers of the Floor: *The Litho-Mites*

At the very bottom, adhering to the black ceramic-steel composite, crawl the smallest living things. They are not insects, though they possess six articulated legs made of flexible titanium alloy. They are the **Litho-Mites**, roughly the size of a human thumb. Their bodies are translucent sacs filled with a viscous, blue coolant fluid that doubles as their blood. Inside these sacs, tiny neural nets process data from the sensors embedded in the floor.

They live in swarms so dense they look like moving mold on the substrate. A single Mite is harmless, a blind thing of instinct and calculation. But a swarm is a processor. When a section of the floor develops a micro-fracture—a hairline crack invisible to the naked eye—the swarm converges. They secrete a resin that hardens instantly upon contact with water, sealing the breach before pressure can widen it. If a sensor array goes offline, the Mites carry replacement circuits in their thorax pouches, plugging them into ports like parasites attaching to a host.

They are the immune system of the ground. They do not eat food; they consume waste heat and electrical static. You might see them glowing faintly orange at the edges of a vent, feeding on the thermal bleed-off. They are fast, skittering across the horizonless floor with a rhythmic *click-hiss-click*, and if you try to scoop one up, it will vibrate violently, trying to interface with your suit’s external sensors, looking for a command to execute. They cannot exist without a task. Without a problem to solve, they enter a dormant state, becoming brittle flakes of metal until the next tremor wakes them.

### The Filterers of the Mid-Water: *Neural Jellyfish (The Drift)*

Above the floor, in the cold, clear expanse where light fades to a bruised purple, drift the **Jelly-Cogs**. These are vast, bell-shaped organisms, thirty meters wide, trailing tendrils that look like fiber-optic cables stripped of their casing. Their "brains" are not localized organs but distributed networks woven into the gelatinous mass of their bells—neural nets trained on centuries of hydrodynamic data.

They are slow, majestic, and utterly silent, moving only by the engineered currents that sweep through the mid-water columns. Their primary function is filtration, but not of plankton. They filter **information**. As they drift, their tendrils brush against the particulate matter suspended in the water—metallic dust from recycling pits, biological slurry from the farms, and chemical markers from leaks. The Jelly-Cogs analyze this debris. They detect trace elements of rare isotopes used in high-tier manufacturing. They identify pathogens in the hydroponic runoff.

When a Jelly-Cog detects a threat—a toxic spill or a structural anomaly—it emits a pulse. This is not a sound, but a focused burst of sonar-encoded data transmitted directly to the nearest habitat hub. The jellyfish themselves do not care about the result; they are merely the eyes of the ocean. They are fragile, however. If a current shifts too violently, tearing a tendril loose, the organism begins to leak its neural fluid, turning transparent and dying within hours. You can often find the hollow, ghost-like shells of dead Jelly-Cogs drifting near the upper boundaries of the habitable zone, empty balloons of silicone and protein, waiting to be collected by scavengers.

### The Apex Predators of Infrastructure: *Titan Crabs*

If there is danger in the deep, it walks on legs. The **Titan Crabs** are the largest mobile entities on the floor, ranging from ten to fifty meters in height. They are not animals in the traditional sense; they are walking tanks with biological hearts. Their carapaces are forged from impact-resistant alloys, etched with maintenance codes and sector identifiers. Underneath the shell, living muscle tissue contracts around hydraulic pistons, giving them strength that defies their mass.

They hunt nothing that flees. They hunt **inefficiency**. A Titan Crab patrols the sectors, its massive claws dragging along the floor, sampling the composite material. If it finds a patch of rust, a misaligned bolt, or a piece of debris that does not belong, it crushes it. It uses its mandibles—sharp, rotating blades—to dismantle unauthorized structures. If a spire grows too tall, blocking a current flow, the Crab cuts it down. If a drone malfunctions and blocks a transport lane, the Crab eats it, processing the scrap metal in its internal furnace.

They are solitary, territorial, and deafeningly loud. The grinding of their joints sounds like rockslides. They communicate via low-frequency rumbles that travel through the floor, shaking the foundations of habitats miles away. To humans, they are terrifying because they are indifferent. A diver standing in their path is not prey, but an obstacle. They will step over you, or through you, depending on whether your position violates a safety protocol. They are the janitors with teeth, ensuring that the factory floor remains clean, even if they have to break the machines to do it.

### The Messengers: *Chromium Tuna Schools*

In the open water, between the floor and the surface, swim the **Chrome-Scales**. They resemble tuna, but their skin is polished mirror-metal, reflecting the artificial lights of the world in dazzling, disorienting flashes. They do not breathe oxygen; they absorb dissolved gases through synthetic gills that act as chemical batteries. They are fast, capable of bursts of speed that create vacuum cavities in the water, roaring like jet engines.

They are the couriers. Each fish carries a data packet in its cranial cavity, encrypted and stored behind a bony plate. Schools of thousands move in synchronized patterns, weaving through the currents to deliver messages between habitats, repair hubs, and construction sites. They never stop swimming. To stop is to lose buoyancy control and sink. They sleep in rotation, half the school slowing while the other half maintains velocity, passing the "lead" role back and forth in a dance that looks like a swirling vortex of silver.

They are aggressive when cornered. If a predator threatens a farm, the defense-shoal forms—a wall of spinning fins and sonic shrieks that can shatter glass and rupture eardrums. Humans use them sparingly, fearing their unpredictability. A Chrome-Scale school might accidentally slam into a habitat wall during a navigation error, causing a breach. They are beautiful, efficient, and prone to collective panic. When they panic, they don’t scatter; they tighten their formation, creating a solid bullet of metal and muscle that can punch through steel plating.

### The Leviathans: *Whale-Submarines*

Deep in the trench zones, where the pressure is highest and the light is thinnest, glide the **Leviathans**. These are colossal creatures, hundreds of meters long, whose bodies are half living whale tissue, half submarine hull. They are not designed for speed, but for endurance and communication. Their skin is thick, blubber-like insulation that also houses antenna arrays.

They sing. Not songs of mating or territory, but broadcasts. Their calls are long-range sonar pulses that map the entire interior of the tank, scanning for structural weaknesses, tracking the movement of currents, and locating lost components. Every habitat listens to the Leviathans. Their groaning, low-frequency hums are the heartbeat of the world. When a Leviathan dies, it does not float; it sinks slowly, its body breaking apart over days as the Titan Crabs pick it clean, returning its materials to the cycle.

They are gentle, mostly. But they are also dangerous because of their scale. A careless tail-swipe can destroy a colony. They are ignored by the smaller fauna, respected by the large. Divers speak of "The Breath"—the moment a Leviathan passes close, and the water displaces so violently that it feels like being sucked into a vacuum. In that silence, before the roar returns, you realize how small you are, and how much of this world exists beyond your comprehension.

### The Human Element: *The Technicians*

And then, there are us. Or rather, there are those who claim ownership of the system. We do not live *in* the tank; we live *on* it, clinging to the walls and floors in pressurized pods, wearing suits that mimic the bioluminescent glow of the environment to avoid startling the fauna.

We are the least natural thing here. Our bodies are soft, inefficient, and constantly in conflict with the cold, crushing depth. We survive only because the machine allows it. We are the ones who initiate the erosion parameter, who design the spires, who program the Jelly-Cogs’ algorithms. But we are also the most vulnerable. A power failure kills us. A leak drowns us. A Titan Crab stepping wrong crushes us.

We view ourselves as the masters of this artificial ecology, but the truth is more unsettling: we are just another component. Our waste feeds the farms. Our heat warms the regulators. Our mistakes trigger the repair swarms. We are part of the loop. We build the spires, the spires attract the Crabs, the Crabs maintain the floor, the floor supports the pumps, the pumps circulate the water, the water cools our heads.

### The Experience of Living Here

To live here is to accept that nothing is permanent. The ground beneath your feet is being recycled right now. The air in your lungs is pumped through filters that were cleaned yesterday. The friend beside you might be upgraded next week, their memories copied, their body discarded and rebuilt with better reflexes.

There is no nature here to protect, only systems to optimize. The beauty is not in sunsets, but in the perfect alignment of a thousand Chrome-Scales turning in unison. The terror is not in monsters, but in the silence when a current stops flowing, signaling that something upstream has failed.

You learn to love the noise. The hum of the regulators becomes a lullaby. The click of the Litho-Mites becomes a whisper of reassurance. You learn to fear the clean spots—the areas where nothing is growing, nothing is moving, and nothing is working. Because in a world of total utility, emptiness is the prelude to deletion.

And above you, far above, the membrane of gas bubbles stretches thin, holding back the void. No one goes there. There is nothing to do. It is outside the parameters. It is irrelevant.

Here, in the dark blue, amidst the clicking, roaring, filtering, building, and dying, life continues. It is sharp, metallic, and bright. It is young, even though it is ancient. It is made, but it breathes. And it watches you, with eyes made of glass and minds made of wire, wondering if you are useful yet.
