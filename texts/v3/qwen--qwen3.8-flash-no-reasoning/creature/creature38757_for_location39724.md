The pressure is not an invasion; it is a birthright.

To live in The Glass Hollow is to be born already deaf to the world as you know it, and attuned to a universe that speaks only in vibration. The inhabitants are the **Tuners**, though they do not call themselves by that name, for names are static things, brittle as dead leaves. They call themselves *Vow*, or *Resonant-kin*, or simply *Those Who Hum*.

They do not look like people from the dry crust above. Years of living inside the bell-shaped cavity have reshaped them. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, veined with faint blue lines that pulse in time with their heartbeat—a slow, deliberate rhythm meant to minimize internal acoustic interference. They are thin, wiry, built for endurance rather than strength, because muscle tension creates unwanted noise. A clenched jaw can cause a headache; a tense shoulder can crack a nearby pane of silica-glass. So, they move with a fluid, liquid grace, their joints loose, their bodies swaying slightly even when standing still, absorbing the ambient thrum of the Hollow so that it passes through them without friction.

### The Helmet and the Face

Their faces are hidden behind the **Cork-and-Clay Helmets**. These are not mere protective gear; they are identity. Each helmet is hand-carved from dense, porous clay harvested from the deeper vents, lined with cork-like fibrous moss grown on the underside of Resonance Ferns. The shape of the helmet dictates the voice of the wearer.

A child’s helmet is small, rounded, tuned to high, bright frequencies—the sound of laughter here is sharp, crystalline, like ice cracking. An elder’s helmet is broad, heavy, carved with deep spiral grooves that dampen the lower registers, allowing them to hum at the sub-bass frequencies that hold the foundations together. To remove one’s helmet is to commit suicide; the sudden exposure to the 110-decibel roar would shatter eardrums and liquefy inner ear fluids within seconds. Thus, no face is ever seen. Intimacy is exchanged through the vibration of foreheads pressed together, helmets clinking softly, sharing the same resonant chamber.

### Language: The Song of Being

They do not speak. Speaking requires air movement that is too chaotic, too violent. Instead, they **hum**.

Communication begins in the chest. A Tuner places a hand over their sternum, feeling the starting pitch of a thought. If the thought is simple—*move*, *stop*, *safe*—it is a short, percussive buzz. If the thought is complex—a story, a warning, a love declaration—it is a sustained, multi-tonal chord produced by manipulating the tongue against the roof of the mouth while controlling breath through diaphragm micro-adjustments.

Because of the "harmonic isolation" of their architecture, these hums travel instantly through the walls. A mother humming a lullaby in her bedroom causes the floorboards of her neighbor’s dwelling to vibrate in sympathy. There is no privacy, but there is no loneliness. You are always part of the chord. To be silent is to be invisible, which is considered a form of death. Therefore, the citizens are never truly quiet. Even in sleep, they dream in low-frequency drones that keep the Chime Moss beneath their beds glowing with soft, violet pulses.

### Daily Life: Tuning the World

Morning does not break with light, for the piezo-luminescence is constant. Morning breaks with a shift in pitch. As the thermal vents cycle, the Great Throat changes its note from a minor key to a major one. This change ripples through the city.

The Tuners wake and begin their daily ritual of **Retuning**.

Using tools made of bone and hardened resin, they inspect the glass tubes that run along the ceilings of their homes. These tubes channel the ambient wind. If a tube has cracked from the night’s temperature drop, they must seal it with heated sand-fusion, a process that emits a hissing spark and a burst of orange light. They listen to the flow. If the wind-sound is too harsh, they adjust the angle of the intake vent outside using long, slender rods of obsidian.

Breakfast is eaten in silence—not the absence of sound, but the absence of *discord*. Food consists of mineral-rich algae scraped from the ceiling fungi and roasted seeds from the Chime Moss, which have been dried until they are hard enough to chew without crumbling into dust (which would create a messy, high-pitched scattering). Eating is dangerous; chewing creates noise. So, the Tuners eat slowly, savoring the crunch, turning each bite into a rhythmic percussion element that joins the meal-song of the household.

Work is not labor in the physical sense; it is **acoustic engineering**.

Some Tuners are **Listeners**, sent into the wilds between the sparse fern forests to map the changing winds. They carry handheld stones, tapping them against the ground to send echolocation pings out. They navigate blindfolded by sight (for sight is unreliable in the haze), relying entirely on the echo-maps projected in their minds. A Listener might spend hours sitting perfectly still, head tilted, listening to the way the wind hits a distant ridge, determining if the path ahead is safe or if a glass-shatter-storm is brewing.

Others are **Shapers**, crafting new dwellings. Since buildings are grown from fused silica, Shapers use focused sonic lenses—parabolic dishes made of polished metal-free ceramic—to direct concentrated sound waves at piles of sand. The intense resonance heats the sand into glass tubes and plates, layering them into the inverted-bell structures. A misdirected beam can blow a hole in the side of a mountain; a perfect beam sings a building into existence, wall by wall, note by note.

### The Fauna: Ghosts in the Machine

The Tuners coexist with the few creatures that survive the pressure. The **Glass-Winged Moths** are sacred, viewed as messengers of the deep earth. They are fragile, skeletal insects with wings of transparent membrane that refract the piezo-light into rainbows. When a moth lands on a Tuner’s helmet, it is considered an omen. The Tuners freeze, holding their breath, listening to the tiny *tink-tink-tink* of the moth’s legs gripping the clay. It is a rare moment of intimacy between species, a shared vibration.

There are no predators. The environment kills all who are clumsy. But sometimes, a Tuner will find a fellow citizen dead in the open plains, their body intact but hollowed out, their bones shattered by a sudden, unexpected harmonic collapse triggered by a falling shard of sky-rock. These deaths are mourned with a **Dirge**—a collective hum from the entire city, dropping to the lowest possible frequency, shaking the ground until the chime moss flashes blinding white, a final scream of light before fading back to amber.

### The Fear: The Deep Hum Rising

Lately, the harmony has fractured.

The elders feel it first. It starts as a nausea, a vertigo that comes not from the eyes but from the inner ear. The **Deep Hum** rising from below the Great Throat is not just louder; it is *wrong*. It lacks a tonal center. It is a dissonant drone that clashes with the natural frequency of the glass ferns.

When the Tuners walk their streets, they notice cracks forming in the pavement where none were yesterday. The Chime Moss flickers erratically, pulsing red instead of violet, signaling danger. The children stop playing; their high-pitched laughter cuts off mid-breath as they instinctively duck, covering their helmets.

A young Tuner named **Kael** stands at the edge of the settlement, looking into the darkness of the fissure entrance. He taps his stone against the obsidian floor. *Click.* The echo returns distorted, stretched, as if the space itself is warping.

He feels a vibration in his teeth. Not the gentle thrum of the wind, but a jagged, tearing sensation. The walls of his home, usually singing in a steady C-major, are beginning to waver, sliding toward a minor key.

The ecosystem of Oroth is based on equilibrium. Sound is structure. Structure is life. And now, something is rewriting the fundamental frequency of their world.

Kael turns back toward the city. He opens his mouth, not to speak, but to hum. He aims for the base note of the central conduit, the anchor of the city’s song. He pushes his breath out, letting the vibration resonate in his chest, hoping to join the chorus, to help retune the failing instruments around him.

But as he hums, the ground beneath his feet gives a single, sickening lurch. A Resonance Fern nearby snaps with a sound like a gunshot, sending shards of glass flying into the air. In the sudden silence of that broken note, Kael hears something else.

Not a sound. But the absence of it.

And then, the roaring stops.
