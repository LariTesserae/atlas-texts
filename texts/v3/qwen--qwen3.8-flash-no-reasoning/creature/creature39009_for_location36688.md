They are not residents, in the traditional sense. To live here is to be a component of the structure itself, a cell within a larger, breathing organism. They call themselves **The Rooted**, though they do not plant seeds; they simply take hold.

Their bodies have adapted to the viscosity of the air and the crushing embrace of the earth. Skin, pale as cave-fish scales or the underside of a mushroom cap, has thickened and toughened, developing a faintly translucent quality where light shafts touch it directly. This translucency reveals the slow, heavy pulse of blood beneath—dark veins mapping out their lives like river systems on a map. They move with a fluid, silent grace, joints loosened by generations of navigating tight spaces, muscles corded and efficient, built for pushing against resistance rather than sprinting across open ground. Their eyes are large, pupils dilated permanently to capture the scantest photon, but they rarely use sight as a primary tool. Instead, they navigate by *pressure*. They feel the shift in air currents when a tunnel collapses nearby; they sense the vibration of footsteps through the soles of their feet before they hear them; they taste the metallic tang of water seepage in the back of their throats before seeing the puddle form.

There is no individual ownership of space here, because there is no empty space to own. A person’s "home" is defined by the niche carved into the wall that fits their sleeping form exactly, and the immediate three-dimensional bubble of warmth they occupy while awake. When they sleep, they curl into fetal positions within these stone alcoves, lined with moss that has been harvested, dried, and softened over years until it smells of rain and deep soil. They wake to the sound of dripping—their lullaby and their alarm clock—and the first act of the day is often to lick moisture from the condensation gathering on the quartz veins in the ceiling, drinking the stone itself.

They communicate in a dialect of whispers and touches. In a place where sound carries strangely, amplified by wet rock and dampening by dense clay, raising one’s voice is considered an act of violence, a disruption of the delicate equilibrium. Conversations happen forehead-to-forehead, breath mingling, hands tracing patterns on each other’s arms or backs to emphasize meaning. The tactile language is rich: a squeeze of the wrist means *danger*, a brushing of fingers along the jawline means *comfort*, and a full hand pressed flat against another’s chest means *I am here, you are real*. Because boundaries between self and neighbor are porous, privacy is a conceptual luxury they cannot afford. To lie is difficult when your heartbeat is audible to those within arm’s reach. Truth, therefore, is the default state.

Their work is maintenance and expansion, a continuous negotiation with the geology. Men and women alike wield chisels made of hardened bone and stone, striking fresh rock with rhythmic, soft thuds. They are architects of compression. When a new family needs space, they do not build walls outward into empty air; they excavate inward into solid matter, carefully removing stone grain by grain, reinforcing the new cavity with wattle-and-daub woven from root fibers. They are master engineers of stability, understanding intuitively how much weight a granite pillar can bear before it shatters. They watch the cracks. They listen to the groans of timber beams bowing under the pressure of tons of earth above. If a support fails, they do not panic; they migrate. The city shifts vertically, rising or sinking with the seasons as underground rivers swell or recede. This migration is ritualized, a slow-moving tide of humanity flowing up through the spiral tunnels toward the warmer, brighter levels near the surface shafts, then descending again as the heat of summer fades and the cold of winter demands the insulation of the deeper crust.

Food is grown in the dark. Gardens are not plots of land but shelves of rot. The Rooted cultivate vast colonies of fungi, tending to the mycelial networks with the care a farmer might give to wheat. They harvest the glowing caps for light and nutrition, eating them raw, slightly bitter and intensely umami. They raise blind, pale beetles in wooden boxes filled with decaying leaf litter brought down from above, grinding the insects into a protein-rich paste. They trade with the surface world only at the very topmost layer, where a few daring individuals climb the shafts to barter rare minerals, polished stones, and medicinal mushrooms for turnips, potatoes, and dried meats. These foods are consumed with reverence, their flavors sharp and bright compared to the muted, earthy tones of the subterranean diet. Eating a potato here is an act of remembering the sun, a fleeting connection to a world they have chosen to leave behind.

Spiritually, they worship the Weight. Not a god in the sky, but the tangible force of the earth pressing down upon them. They believe that existence is measured by density—that to be packed tightly together is to be held securely, protected from the chaos of the void above. Their rituals involve communal huddling, pressing bodies together in dark chambers to share heat and heartbeat, reaffirming their collective solidity. Death is treated with practicality and tenderness. Bodies are not buried, for there is no loose soil to spare. They are placed in niches carved specifically for them, sealed with clay and lime, becoming part of the structural integrity of the home. Over time, the bones fuse with the stone, the calcium merging with the limestone. The ancestors literally support the living, their remains acting as pillars and walls. To lean against a wall is to rest against the spine of a grandfather; to walk through a doorway is to pass between the ribs of a mother. There is no grief in this, only continuity. The dead do not leave; they become the foundation.

Children are born in the deepest, quietest levels, where the vibration of the surface world is least felt. Midwives guide births using hands slick with mineral water, catching infants who emerge already accustomed to darkness. From birth, children are taught to be still. They learn to hold their breath when passing through narrow gaps, to move without disturbing the dust motes in the light shafts. Play is a serious affair: building small structures from pebbles and clay, learning the balance points of stone, practicing the art of silence. They grow up knowing that the sky is a myth, a distant rumor whispered by the elders who occasionally glimpse it through the shafts—a patch of blue that looks like bruised flesh, terrifying and beautiful. Most never see it. They know the world as a sphere of warm skin surrounding them, a universe contained within four wet walls and a roof of tangled roots.

The adults carry a profound, quiet resilience. They are not happy in the way surface dwellers define happiness—not with laughter or exuberance—but they possess a deep, resonant contentment derived from belonging. They know exactly where they stand. They are not lost in infinite space; they are embedded. Every inch of their environment is known, touched, tasted, and smelled. They have mastered the art of making enough from nothing, of finding warmth in the crush, of creating beauty from jagged fracture lines. They polish quartz veins until they shine like stars, weaving reeds into intricate tapestries that hang like curtains in the bioluminescent glow, painting murals on the damp clay walls using pigments mixed from crushed beetles and iron-rich mud.

In the end, they are indistinguishable from the place. If you were to dig into the earth and find a pocket of warm, damp air surrounded by human-shaped forms curled in sleep, you would not know if you had found a city or a hive. The line has dissolved. They are the flesh of the earth, dreaming in the dark, kept safe by the weight of the world above.
