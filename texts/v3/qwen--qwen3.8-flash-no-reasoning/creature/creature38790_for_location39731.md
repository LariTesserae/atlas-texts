The people of the Under-Vat do not look like people who live under a sky. They are shaped by the absence of it, and by the presence of the heat that does not burn so much as it *inhabits*.

They are soft-edged things. The skin of their faces is perpetually dewy, a sheen of lukewarm condensation that never quite evaporates because there is no wind to take it away, only the slow, humid exchange of breath with the room. Their pores are wide, dilated in a permanent state of surrender to the temperature. They sweat not in bursts, but in a steady, silent weeping from every gland, a constant lubrication that keeps them from sticking to the iron grating or the basalt walls. When they move, there is a faint, wet sound—the friction of damp skin against rough stone, the squelch of boots on oily floors.

Their bodies are compressed. In a place where ceilings hang three meters overhead and dwellings are stacked like honeycomb cells, height is a disadvantage. The residents are stocky, dense-boned, and broad-shouldered. They have learned to duck instinctively, a collective choreography of lowering heads when passing beneath the primary conduins. Their spines curve slightly forward, not from age, but from the posture of survival: leaning into the weight of the air, bracing against the pressurized intimacy of the tunnels. To stand upright here feels like defying the architecture; to stoop is to belong.

They wear layers of synthetic fabrics, treated with hydrophobic waxes that repel the sulfur-tasting mist. But these clothes are rarely clean. The fabric grows stiff with dried salt deposits and fungal spores. Over this, they wear protective gear that has become part of their silhouette: thick rubber aprons stained black with oil, heavy gloves made from woven asbestos fibers (now fraying at the cuffs), and goggles with cracked lenses that sit atop their foreheads when not in use. These goggles are essential tools, not just for safety, but for sight. Without them, the sodium-vapor glare bleeds into the purple shadows, and the eyes struggle to focus. With them, the world becomes a framed series of sharp contrasts—bright orange pools against absolute black.

But it is their faces that tell the true story of the Under-Vat. There is a specific expression common to all of them: a look of **patient vigilance**. It is the face of someone listening to a clock tick. Because the rule of the rock is *listen*, and the silence is deceptive. Their ears are large, often adorned with small copper studs that act as rudimentary hearing aids, amplifying the subsonic groans of the basalt. They tilt their heads frequently, not toward each other, but toward the walls, toward the pipes, toward the floor. They are attuned to the mechanical heartbeat of the settlement. A slight change in the pitch of the turbine’s thump makes them pause mid-step. A new creak in the ceiling causes them to freeze, holding their breath until the pressure equalizes or vents. This hyper-awareness gives their eyes a darting, restless quality, scanning the darkness between the lamps for the glint of a leak or the shadow of a collapse.

The children are different. They are smaller, yes, but also lighter, less burdened by the physical memory of the heat. They run faster through the narrow bridges, their bare feet slapping against the slick stone, leaving fleeting prints that vanish into the steam. They are drawn to the danger zones with a curiosity that adults have long since suppressed. You see them clustered near the fresh fissure you described earlier, their faces illuminated by the swirling cloud of superheated vapor. They do not fear the heat; they fear boredom. In a world without sky, without seasons, without the open horizon, the eruption of raw energy is the most interesting thing that can happen. They reach out to touch the mist, laughing as their fingers recoil, red and steaming. They treat the planet’s bleeding wound as a playground. The adults watch them with a mixture of terror and resignation, knowing that the rock will eventually demand payment for this spectacle.

The workers—the ones who maintain the veins—are harder, more angular. Their hands are scarred by thermal burns and chemical etchings from the acidic condensation. They speak little, communicating in gestures and shouts over the roar of the fans. They are covered in a fine layer of dust—not dirt, but mineral powder scraped from the tunnel walls during drilling operations. This dust settles in their creases, in their hair, making them look like statues carved from the same bedrock they inhabit. They move with efficient, jerky motions, conserving energy because the heat saps it constantly. Every step must be deliberate; slipping means falling into a pool of stagnant, scalding water, or worse, being caught in a sudden venting of steam.

There is a profound **physical closeness** among them. Personal space is a luxury that cannot be afforded. In the dining halls, which are simply larger geode cavities fitted with communal tables bolted to the floor, knees touch under the table. Elbows brush against elbows. The air is so thick with body heat and moisture that breathing requires effort. People lean on each other when tired, resting their heads on shoulders in the dim light. This intimacy is not necessarily romantic; it is survivalist. Warmth is generated by proximity. Isolation in the Under-Vat is not just lonely; it is cold, relatively speaking, and dangerous. To be alone in the dark corners between the lamps is to invite the mold, the stagnation, the unseen hazards of the unmonitored tunnels. So they cluster. They huddle. They form tight knots of humanity around the sources of light and warmth.

Their voices are low. The acoustics of the stone corridors amplify sound strangely, causing echoes that bounce off the basalt and confuse the ear. Shouting is difficult; the noise of the pumps drowns it out, and the humidity absorbs the sharp consonants. So they speak softly, almost whispering, their voices raspy from the dry, filtered air of the ventilation shafts. They hum tunes, too, to mask the constant drone of the machinery. You hear snippets of melody drifting from the residential niches—old songs about rain and sun, sung by people who have never seen either. The music is melancholic, minor-key, reflecting the closed loop of their existence.

And then there are the **Scrapers**, the specialized harvesters of the fungal mats and thermophilic mosses. They move like ghosts through the hottest sections of the settlement, their suits sealed tight against the steam. They carry buckets filled with the pale, glowing slime and bundles of velvet-green moss. Their work is delicate and dirty. They scrape the life off the pipes, the walls, the very bones of the city. They are respected but pitied, for they come home reeking of rot and sulfur, their skin blistered from handling the volatile adhesives. They are the immune system of the Under-Vat, cleaning the wounds of the infrastructure, ensuring the leaks are sealed, the engines insulated, the city alive.

In the end, the people of the Under-Vat are not distinct from their environment. They are extensions of the plumbing, participants in the pressure cycle. They breathe the recycled air, drink the distilled condensation, eat the fungus harvested from the walls. They are wet, warm, close, and listening. They are a hive mind of flesh and bone, bound together by the shared understanding that the ground beneath them is sleeping, but not dead, and that one day, it might wake up. Until then, they keep dry, they respect the veins, and they gather around the cracks to watch the world bleed light.
