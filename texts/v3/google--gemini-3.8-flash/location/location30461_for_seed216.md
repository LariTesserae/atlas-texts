There is no stone beneath the foot. There is no bedrock, no silt resting upon magma, no tectonic continental plate. 

The floor of the world is a wicker of living heartwood, braided by hand and allowed to heal into itself across ten thousand generations. It is an architecture of pure joinery, executed with flint scrapers, steam-bent saplings, and cords of cured bast fiber, scaled until it spans a horizon so wide it bends the light at its edges. From the surface of this floor, if one looks down between the thick, swollen joints of the grain, there is only a warm, green-gray drop of four or five leagues, ending in another tier of wicker, and below that another, receding into a damp, luminous haze.

The air is high and thin, yet deeply warm—a tropical stratum suspended where mountains ought to be, kept hot by the slow, fermenting respiration of the timber itself. Moisture is everywhere, though it rarely gathers into ponds. It weeps from the bark. The air is so laden with warm vapor that breath feels like a drink; every surface—the enormous suspension burls, the guy-lines twisted from tough river-willow thicker than galleons—glistens under a coat of slick, clear condensation. Water trickles down the vertical trusses in broad, silent ribbons, occasionally detaching from a curve to fall into the abyss, breaking apart into a mist that never seems to reach the lower frameworks.

Yet despite the colossal volume of the landscape, it is mostly void. 

It is an emptiness shaped by structure. A traveler can stand upon the crest of an arch—a single, peeled log-beam twenty yards wide, scarred by the gouge-marks of bone adzes from an era before memory—and gaze across twenty miles of nothing at all. The atmosphere between the arches is clean, humid, and still. Far off, against the low-lit ceiling of interwoven runners, another structural rib arches through the fog, looking no thicker than a single hair plucked from a horse’s tail, though it too is a freeway of spliced ash and hazel. There are days of walking through empty space where the only company is the rhythm of one's own weight compressing the fibrous ground, a dull, deadened thud that produces no echo. Sound cannot carry far here; the soft, damp mosses that scab every joint swallow it whole. The prevailing sound is merely the slow, rhythmic *tic-tic-tic* of thermal drops falling from one shelf of foliage to the next, accompanied by the deep, structural groan of the world settling under its own growth.

The craft is undeniably primitive, which makes its scale terrifying. There are no iron bolts, no mortar, no pulleys of complex design. Where two timbers meet—each the width of an ancient oak—they have been cleft by stone wedges, slotted together like simple tongue-and-groove fencing, and lashed with leagues of two-ply hemp cordage. The knots are vast, bulbous things, the size of haystacks, now grown over by bark until they resemble giant, arthritic knuckles. The people who tied them are either gone or dispersed into the deep recesses of the weave; what remains is the work itself, ancient enough that the grain of the joined trees has grafted across the seams, turning millions of hand-fitted components into a single, continuous, circulating organism.

Here, the physics of the world have begun to soften around the edges.

Gravity is not entirely vertical. It leans. Near the thickest knots, where the density of the living mass gathers in tangled clusters the size of hills, a body feels an inward pull, so that walking upon the side of an arch feels no different than walking upon its spine. The light, too, fails to follow the straight rules of an open sky. There is no visible sun, only an ambient, pearlescent glow that filters down from somewhere high above the upper thatch—a light that turns around corners, following the grain of the wood, illuminating the undersides of the cantilevers with a dull, wet amber. 

In certain hollows, where three great braces meet at an angle that should not close, the emptiness inside the triangle appears larger than the space containing it. You can cast a pebble through the gap and watch it travel away for an hour, shrinking to a speck, until it re-emerges from behind your left shoulder, slick with the dew of some intervening, folded air.

Life thrives in these hollows, quiet and profuse. Without soil, the fauna has adapted to the damp wood-skin. There are herds of soft-shelled, many-jointed things—flat as shingles and pale as leeches—that graze upon the fungal mats that line the under-ribs of the bridges. They move in absolute silence, hundreds of them together, sliding over the wet bast with a sound like wet paper being dragged across oilcloth. High in the mist, between the arches where there is nothing to cling to, drift wide, membranous creatures with no bones, like freshwater rays that have forgotten the weight of water, propelled by slow, undulating skirts of skin. They feed on the moisture droplets suspended in the thermal columns, their bodies so translucent that when they pass between an observer and a distant strut, the wood appears only slightly warped through their flesh.

There are no sharp seasons, only an endless, drowsy afternoon of sap and damp. At regular intervals, the trusses swell; a pulse of warm, bitter sap surges through the interior fibers, causing the bridges to tighten, lifting slightly into the air with a long, low creak that travels along the wood for minutes like a muted trumpet. When the sap recedes, the world sags again, settling inches downward into the quiet air.

Everything has been handled. Look closely at the bark of the sheerest drop, where the eye expects the raw indifference of nature, and there are old, regular notches carved to receive a thumb, or the ancient, healed-over spiral of a vine trained to bridge a fault-line by someone whose hand was small and patient. The world is entirely an artifact, yet it has lived so long in the warm, wet twilight that it has forgotten its makers, closing its wood over their intentions, dreaming itself out into the high, empty air one inch of lashing at a time.
