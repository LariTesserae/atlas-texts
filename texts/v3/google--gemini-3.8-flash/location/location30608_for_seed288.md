The descent into the basin is a drop of nearly four hundred meters, steep enough that the desert above disappears behind sheer, sun-bleached ramparts of calcified shale. Down here, beneath the lip of the outer world, the heat of the surface fails to penetrate. The air settles into a permanent, cellarlike cool—chilled not by ice or damp, but by deep stone shade and the vast thermal mass of the floor.

It is completely dry. No stream beds cut the valley; there are no dried mud-cracks or alkaline sinks that might hint at seasonal rains. The ground is a uniform carpet of pulverized feldspar and powdered chalk, soft and bone-pale, rising in small drifts against the footings of enormous, eroded masonry. 

Yet the basin is dense with life. Between the low-lying, wind-scoured foundations grows an astonishingly heavy, waterless thicket: a sprawling, dry-fleshed scrub that has adapted to absolute aridity. There are pale, cork-barked succulents as thick as cisterns, their skins grey-green and waxy to hold what little vapor the night brings down from the high plateaus. Spiny brush with leaves like slivers of dry horn blankets the broad gaps between ruins, interlaced with pale lichen that grows in mats several feet deep, spongy underfoot but dry as dry paper. The foliage does not wilt; it crackles, stiff and fibrous, forming a silent, grey-tinted jungle that persists across centuries with barely a leaf turning or falling.

Rising through this petrified garden are the remains of an ancient city, settled so deeply into its final form that it is hard to tell where the dressed masonry ends and the bedrock begins. The scale of the settlement is regional, stretching across the entire width of the sunken valley floor. Long, straight causeways of dry-stacked granite blocks run for miles, though their surfaces have been planed smooth by millennia of windborne dust. The lintels and colonnades are massive, low, and without ornament; whatever tools carved them left no marks of iron or bronze, only the rounded, patient contours of stone ground against stone. Entire quarters of low, flat-roofed habitations stand open to the cool air, their doors and light-wells filled to the sill with silt and the shed shells of insects.

Long before the eye adjusts to the dimness of the rift floor—where direct sunlight never strikes, filtering down only as an indirect, grey-blue dusk—the sound arrives as a physical weight. 

The acoustics of the basin are catastrophic. The sheer perimeter cliffs and thousands of stone facades act as an unbroken echo chamber, amplifying a sound that never stops: the breeding call and wing-beat of an immense, teeming fauna. 

Millions of birds—pale swifts, ash-grey martins, and desert-adapted nightjars—roost in the honeycombed facades of the megaliths. They fill the upper half of the rift in great, wheeling clouds that blot out the pale slit of the sky. Their shrieks, multiplied by the millions and thrown against the smooth basalt floor, merge into a roaring, oceanic static that drowns all thought. Below them, within the dry scrub and deep within the foundation vaults, lives an equally massive population of subterranean orthopterans: pale, blind stone-crickets whose stridulations vibrate through the rock itself. To stand in the center of an ancient plaza is to feel the vibrations in the marrow of one's shins. The noise is not an event; it is a permanent atmospheric condition, as deafening and indifferent as an unending waterfall, though there is not a cup of water in the entire basin.

Movement in this world belongs entirely to the small and winged. The larger landscape possesses an immobility so vast it borders on the eternal. The stone does not fall; the arches that were weak collapsed ten thousand years ago, and what remains is the indestructible skeleton of the place, fused by time and gravity into the underlying shelf. The grey thickets grow by fractions of an inch a decade, nourished by the dust of bird droppings and the mineral-rich flour of the eroding walls. 

A bird drops from a ruined pediment, its wings beating silently against the deafening backdrop of the colony, snaps a beetle from the waxy spine of a succulent, and climbs back into the cool twilight. In the dry air, feathers do not rot; dead husks of creatures dry into parchment where they fall, preserved indefinitely beside the stones that sheltered them.
