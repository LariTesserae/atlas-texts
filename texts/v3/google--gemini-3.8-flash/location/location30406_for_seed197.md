The scale of the place is roughly that of a sunken cathedral square or a four-block municipal quarter, enclosed on all four flanks by sheer, vertical cliffs of pale green and russet timber. But this is not timber felled and raised; it is timber that has extruded itself along the lines of a dense, multi-tiered city.

There are five and six stories of balconies, colonnades, arched casements, and cantilevered walkways, all grown seamlessly from a single continuous root-system of iron-hard lignum. It mimics the density of a high-density merchant town—there are distinct facades, recessed loggias, overhanging eaves, and what appear to be public conduit-channels—yet no tool has ever touched it. The wood displays no grain of the saw, only the fibrous, braided swirl of living vascular bundles that have deliberately hardened into joists, lintels, and spiraling access ramps. 

Despite this immense architectural complexity around the perimeter, the central volume is extraordinarily hollow. It is an empty bowl of warm, amber-lit air. The interior floor of the precinct is a sunken court of level, hard-packed cambium, wide and bare, spanning perhaps two hundred paces across, completely vacant. From this floor, looking upward, the layered facades rise seventy cubits to meet the open sky, which pours down ordinary, warm afternoon daylight. The atmosphere is damp, smelling intensely of bruised mint, wet bark, and vinegar, with droplets of thick condensation weeping rhythmically from the under-ribs of the upper galleries and falling the long distance to the floor with tiny, erratic snaps.

Yet the space cannot be called quiet. It is, in fact, almost unendurably loud.

The deafening roar—a grinding, percussive vibration that sits in the sternum like an engine at full throttle—comes from two sources. First, the walls are running at hydraulic pressures that violate basic fluid mechanics. Within the thick, pillar-like trunks that form the structural corners of the city-facades, sap is being pumped upward at hundreds of feet per second. You can see it through the translucent amber nodules of the bark: high-velocity slurry tearing through internal channels, producing a scream identical to a pressurized steam main or an idling jet turbine. 

Second, the air—though visually empty from a distance—is saturated with fauna whose scale and density break down upon approach. At the center of the empty floor, where nothing seems to stand, the sound shears through the ears because thousands of transparent, chitinous organisms, thin as parchment and shaped like flattened vertebrae, are vibrating in fixed positions in mid-air. They do not hover by beating wings; they are pinned to precise coordinates in the empty space, vibrating so rapidly that their bodies disappear into a heat-shimmer, producing a continuous, mechanical shriek. When one passes a hand through the vacant space, the air resists like cold water, and the ear feels an intense barometric pop. The creatures are anchored to invisible thresholds where the room’s geometry folds.

The fauna along the layered timber-facades is larger and relentlessly active. Long, segmented creatures with the texture of boiled leather scuttle along the high balustrades in continuous, regimented lines, moving between what look like domestic entryways. They carry pellets of gray, chewed fiber from the lower tiers to the upper roofs, navigating the complex layout of the walls with the urgent, purposive rhythm of commuters in an overcrowded port. Inside the dark, hollowed "rooms" behind the facades, thousands of tiny, blind birds with oily plumage roost in inverted tiers, chattering in sharp, metallic clicks that reverberate against the wood until the echo itself hardens into a physical vibration that ripples the puddles below.

The water here is not abundant enough to form streams, but it is persistent. It collects in the hollows of the architectural details—in the grown gutters, the natural cups of the cornices, and the shallow cisterns formed where three arches meet. In these high pockets, the water is dark and brackish, home to tiny, twitching larvae that feed on the drifting cellular dust shed by the ceiling-canopy. Periodically, an architectural shutter—a single, massive leaf of stiffened, dead bark—will drop under the weight of accumulated dew, slamming shut over an empty window-frame with the concussive boom of a dropped shipping container, sending a shockwave across the quiet, cavernous central floor.

The breakdown of reality here is quiet and structural rather than violent. The architecture repeats certain civic mistakes: a flight of grown stairs will spiral up for three turns, perfectly proportioned for bipedal ascent, only to twist through a ninety-degree lateral shift and continue horizontally across a sheer face, its steps still worn smooth in the center as if millions of heels had trodden them sideways against gravity. If one looks closely at the lintels above the third-story apertures, the grain of the wood does not follow rings; it forms tight, legible alphabets of eight or nine repeating glyphs that dissolve into ordinary cellular fibers when touched. 

The heat is heavy, humid, and low-lying, trapped between the dense biological palisades. A breeze occasionally enters from the top, where the wild, ragged crowns of the giant trees feather out into the clear sky, but by the time the air sinks into the square, it has been stripped of its freshness by the sheer breath of the place. It smells of fermentation, clean tallow, and old sugar. 

In the center of the empty cambium floor, where the silence ought to gather, the sound only intensifies. Standing there, surrounded by four tiers of unpeopled, beautifully engineered vegetative apartments, the noise of the sap-drive and the invisible, humming fauna creates a physical weight. The shadows cast by the afternoon sun do not move smoothly; they jerk forward every several minutes by several inches at a time, accompanied by a dull, wooden groaning deep within the bedrock, as if the entire district were an enormous, living mechanism clicking through its internal escapements, adjusting its angles to the sun with the precision of a clock grown from seed.
