The district is roughly six hundred paces across, sunken thirty feet below the general grade behind battered retaining walls of ashlar granite and riveted boiler-plate. From above, it looks like an unroofed machine hall or a dry dock that was converted, generations ago, into a terminal exchange for some liquid that is no longer named.

The air inside the basin stays at cellar draft: a steady, flat coolness that does not fluctuate with the noon or the midnight. It smells of wet graphite, tallow, and cold lake water. Because the air above the basin is marginally warmer, a perpetual skim of condensation bleeds down the vertical surfaces. Droplets gather along the lower lips of I-beams, drop every three seconds into lead gutters, and run into dead-end cisterns where the water stays clear, cold, and slightly alkaline. It does not rain here in sheets; moisture is simply the baseline skin on every bolt head and granite edge.

There is nothing green anywhere in the basin. Not a tuft of goose-grass between the flags, not a lichen scale on the mortar, not an algal smear in the sluices. The stone is scrubbed to the gray grit of split feldspar; the iron is either oiled or sealed with black pitch that has crazed into tiny, dry hex-patterns. Even the rot here is mineral: iron oxide blooms in dry, powdery blisters that flake off before they can grow fur.

Yet the space is deafeningly, claustrophobically inhabited.

Between the three great walking-beam pumps that occupy the center of the flagstones—engines whose twenty-foot iron arms rise and fall with the hydraulic apathy of a church bell tolling for nobody—the stones belong to the *thrum-gulls* and the *spindle-whelks*. They are not vermin in the sense of an infestation that arrived from outside; they are the proper inhabitants of the hardware. 

The whelks cover the damp pedestals of the engines like wet gravel. They have segmented shells the color of wet lead, out of which extend three jointed, rubbery arms tipped with horn scraper-plates. They do not graze on weeds; they scrape the greasy film left by the steam seals, processing the tallow and the fine metal dust worn off the brass bushings into dry, pelletized gray cordage that they cement behind them as they move. They move without stopping, millimeter by millimeter, so dense that one cannot see the iron footings of the gantry legs for the churning, gray-blue crust of them.

Above them, on every stay-wire, valve handwheel, and overhead crane-rail, roost the gulls. They have no feathers; their skins are tight, pebbled parchment the color of zinc, and their wings are double-jointed, four-vaned kites of ribbed gristle that snap when they fold them. There are thousands of them. They do not fly in flocks; they merely drop from one pipe to another to catch the midges—blind, hairless things that hatch continuously from the warm condenser sumps and rise in gray columns toward the flat white sky.

Because there are thousands of these creatures and three great engines running under thirty atmospheres of steam, the sound inside the basin is immense and level. It does not rise to crescendos and it does not die away. It is an industrial roar made entirely of small impacts: the hiss of high-pressure gland packings, the rhythmic *thung-chunk* of the walking beams striking their leather arrestors, the dry, hard clatter of millions of horn-tipped arms working against iron, and the dry, coughing rattle that the gulls make deep in their throats when they swallow. The volume is high enough that speech is impossible two paces away; one feels the vibrations not in the ear canal first, but in the soft meat between the ribs and the soles of the boots through the paving.

The town itself—if town it can be called—is laid out around this mechanical chore with an austere, sparse geometry. The structures are narrow, four-story housings of black brick with steep iron dormers, spaced wide apart along the perimeter walls like watchtowers or switch-houses. Most of them have no ground-floor doors, only iron ladders that drop into the damp air ten feet above the stones to keep them clear of the whelk-tide. Overhead, heavy copper bus-bars run on porcelain insulators between the houses, their drop-lines feeding power down to low-voltage arc lamps that stay lit day and night.

Under the gray daylight, the arcs are almost invisible—just clicking violet needles in glass bell-jars—but their smell is sharp: ozone and burned hair. The daylight itself does not shift. It comes through an overcast that seems structural rather than meteorological, an unbroken ceiling of tallow-colored cloud that neither thins into sun nor thickens into storm. It illuminates the basin evenly, casting short, hard-edged shadows only directly beneath the boilers and the bellies of the great beams.

Nothing here is being dismantled, and nothing is being enlarged. The machinery has the smoothed, settled surfaces of things that have moved through the same arc four million times. The bronze bearings are worn down to their shims, but the shims have been hammered to fit the wear so closely that there is no knock, only a wet *slump* at each reversal of the piston. The whelks do not overpopulate and starve; as they reach the size of a fist, their shells harden until their arms can no longer clear the rim, at which point they become motionless gray stones, incorporated into the masonry of the plinths, while a hundred smaller ones hatch from under their skirts to scour the freshly cleared inch of iron.

It is a world in a dead lock: a low, cold room of steam and grease where animal tissue has found a way to live entirely upon the friction of iron on bronze, and where the iron has found its only preservation in being perpetually scrubbed by living horn. If you stood upon the iron walkway that circles the rim and watched for twenty years, you would see the same piston descend three times a minute, the same column of blind flies drift into the same row of zinc-skinned jaws, and the same thin trickle of distilled water drop from the cold corner of the rail into the stone gutter below, without gaining or losing a drop.
