The ground, if it can be called that, is not mineral. It is grain.

Miles above whatever floor this world once possessed, the landscape exists as an open, monumental lattice of ancient heartwood—timbers the girth of mountain ranges, curving through the sky like the stripped ribs of an extinct continent. There is no dirt, no granite, no shale. Every cliff face is an exposed growth ring; every plateau is the flattened, wind-scoured crest of a trunk that ceased growing ten thousand centuries ago. 

Time here has not fractured the world into scree; it has polished it. The wood is pale, bleached to the color of dry bone on its sunward flanks, and worn so smooth by primordial winds that it resembles river-rubbed marble. Broad furrows, carved by epochs of air and boring larvae long since gone, run like valleys down the backs of these colossal spans. Between them lies almost nothing: sheer, vertiginous gulfs of empty air, two leagues wide, where the eye plunges unobstructed into the pale lavender haze of the lower altitudes. It is a hollow world, vast in its vacancy, propped up only by these sweeping, petrified arches of living timber.

Yet it is warm. The sun strikes the pale wood directly through the thin, clean sky, baking the old tannins until the air smells of parched cedar, dry resin, and sun-struck dust. Moisture does not come from a water table, but from the migration of low, traveling clouds that shear against the undersides of the great burls. Every few mornings, a damp fleece drags through the lower scaffolding, leaving the shadow-sides of the boughs glistening. Water gathers in deep, ancient knot-holes—natural cisterns three fathoms wide, rimmed with oily liverworts and slick with cold algae. From these hollows, small, clear runnels spill out, trace the curve of a bark-ridge for a hundred paces, and dissolve into mist over the void.

Because the spans are so isolated and the air between them is so vast, one might expect silence. Instead, the landscape is violently loud. 

The sound is not geological; it is entirely biological, and it is deafening. The empty air is an ocean of wings. Millions of creatures—creatures that never touch rock, because rock does not exist—wheel, drift, and plunge through the gulfs in numbers that turn the daylight into a flickering, strobing twilight. There are colonies of spine-winged martins nesting in the fibrous fissures of the grain, their shrieks multiplying against the concave curve of the timbers until it sounds like iron plates shearing in two. There are heavy, blubbery beetles the size of ox-skulls that drone across the chasms on four sets of glass-clear wings, their collective hum a low, physical vibration that rattles the sternum. Everywhere, down every wind-flute and through every dried resin-seam, there is a constant, frantic rustling, chirping, scraping, and screaming: hunting swarms descending on the moist undersides, aerial serpents snapping gliders out of the thermal shears, and billions of translucent mantids sawing their forelegs against the bark in a rhythmic, clattering percussion that drowns out human speech.

Against this terrifying volume of life, the marks of hand-craft are minute, sparse, and stubborn.

Tucked into the leeward shoulder of an immense burl, where the wood has cracked into a sheltered ravine, clings a cluster of dwellings. They do not use stone or metal; they are fashioned from the shed chitin of the great beetles, stitched together with boiled sinew and pegged into the heartwood with fire-hardened dowels. Hand-twisted ropes of bast fiber loop precariously between knot-hole terraces, swaying over the five-mile drop. 

On a smoothed ramp overlooking the drop, two people work with a long, two-man push-knife tipped with a fire-blackened tooth. They are peeling curls of ancient, dried cambium to boil into glue. Their skin is dark, leathery, and greased with beetle-fat to keep the dry wind from cracking it. Beside them rests a long bamboo-like pole tipped with a barbed bone hook—a skyline gaff, meant to be thrust out into the screaming clouds of passing fowl when the flock sweeps close enough to brush the edge.

There is no sense of beginnings here; the world has settled into its final, worn configuration. The spans will not fall; the wood has turned to something harder than basalt through sheer age and desiccated pressure. The water will continue to condense in the hollow knots at dawn and evaporate by noon. And the creatures will continue to scream across the empty gulfs, filling the daylight with an unyielding, wild engine of sound, while the tiny settlements cling like lichen to the grain, listening to the world roar beneath the sun.
