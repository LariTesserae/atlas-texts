The floor of the basin is made of glass that has not yet learned how to shatter. 

It is a young crust—vast sheets of black, ropy basalt and obsidian tongues that pushed up from a shallow mantle and cooled before wind or scouring gravel could knock the needle-points from their crests. The edges of the rock are as keen as fresh-struck flint, razor-sharp ridges and unbroken volcanic blisters extending across a depression that stretches for miles. Yet there is no desolation here. The raw, jagged geometry of a newborn earth has been instantaneously claimed, buried beneath an uninterrupted, suffocating blanket of living matter that has somehow skipped the slow patience of soil-making.

The heat is total. It does not rise and fall with the passage of hours; it remains pinned at the threshold where protein threatens to loosen its bonds. The air is thick, hot as an exhaled breath held too long in the throat, saturated with a moisture that cannot decide whether to fall as rain or simply hang as a suspension of warm grease. Water collects on every surface not from storms, but because the air can no longer contain it: it weeps continuously from the underside of leaves, slides down the glassy black basalt slopes in silent, scalding laminas, and pools in the sharp-edged crevasses. There are no smooth riverbeds here; the water simply coats the sharp, embryonic stone, lubricating a world that never cools down.

Above, the light is smothered. A low, seamless lid of yellow-green vapor and overlapping leaves traps the basin in an eternal, stagnant twilight. It is never bright enough to cast a crisp shadow, and never dark enough to hide the churn of bodies. 

Life does not merely occupy the basin; it is packed into it like meat in a casing. Because the stone is non-porous and violently fresh, the vegetation has abandoned root systems that drill deep into earth. Instead, it anchors itself through suction, acidic glue, and dense, tangled mats of wet fibers. Giant, liver-colored vascular ferns spread leaves as wide as skiffs, their surfaces ribbed and rubbery to shed the continuous drip of condensing steam. Pale, spongy liverworts and mosses three feet thick swallow the sharp edges of the lava fields, turning jagged basalt traps into soft, yielding mounds of green velvet that sink when pressed, weeping lukewarm water from their pores. Stems do not bother to grow bark. In this heat, without frost or drought, plants expose their raw, emerald-veined flesh directly to the wet air, swelling until they touch, branch to branch, leaf to leaf, leaving no empty space between the stone and the canopy.

And moving through this carpet, through every fold of it, is an overwhelming, suffocating tide of animal life.

The fauna does not scatter; there is nowhere to run where there is not already another living thing. The moss is alive with things that scuttle, crawl, and slide. Millions of segmented creatures—broad-backed, many-legged, their shells the dull yellow of damp horn—clamber over one another in a dense, continuous drift across the volcanic glass. Their plated bellies scrape the unweathered stone, a sound like a thousand sickles gently sweeping across slate. Blind, bloated amphibians with skin like wet lard nestle into every crack of the lava, wedged into crevices three and four deep, their throats pulsing in a synchronous, heavy panting that matches the rhythm of the basin’s heat. 

Above them, within the tangled under-canopy, soft-bodied mantids and heavy, flightless beetles crawl along the ribs of the ferns. They do not hide; camouflage has no purpose where every square inch is populated. Life eats life with an unhurried, mechanical indifference, and for every creature dissolved or crushed, three more hatch from the warm, gelatinous egg-clusters that coat the underbelly of every stone and stalk. The scale of the basin allows this mass to stretch unbroken to the distant, steaming rims of the rift, an expanse of low, undulating green hummocks that seem to gently vibrate from the sheer volume of muscle and hemolymph shifting beneath them.

The sound is not a collection of individual cries, but an immovable slab of noise. It is loud—a steady, crushing drone that occupies the ears until it ceases to be heard as sound and becomes a physical pressure in the skull. It is the friction of trillions of claws on fresh basalt; the wet suction of slug-like mollusks inching over moss; the endless, wet clicking of mandibles stripping rubbery foliage; and the dull, subterranean gurgle of condensation draining down into blind, glass-walled faults. There are no songs, no mating calls that rise and fall. There are only the sounds of respiration, digestion, and friction, sounding at the exact same decibel every minute of every day.

Nothing here is waiting to happen. The rift is not eroding; the stone is too hard and the moisture too gentle to carve canyons, and the plants protect the rock from the very water they feed on. The climate does not cycle. There is no dry period, no winter, no dawn that clears the mist, no dusk that brings relief from the fever. The basin sits in a permanent, hyper-saturated equilibrium, an engine running at top speed without moving an inch, holding its breath in the dim green heat forever.
