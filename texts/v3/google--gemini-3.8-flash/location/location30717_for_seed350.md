The water here does not yield; it crowds. It is dense, warm as blood from a vent, heavily laden with colloidal iron, silicates, and dissolved tannins that turn the flood the color of tea brewed in an engine block. To exist within it is to be gripped by a hydraulic fist. There is no surface in view—only hundreds of fathoms of hydrostatic weight overhead, pressing down upon a sunken valley whose floor cracked open so recently that its basalt margins remain razor-sheared, black glass edges unground by silt, slicing through the churn like guillotine blades.

And the sound is an absolute, structural violence. Sound travels five times faster through this brine than through air, arriving not as an auditory sensation but as a concussive kneading of the sternum. It is the sound of submerged industrial scale: four-story reciprocating pistons hammering inside flooded pump-houses, their housings cracked and venting high-pressure steam that collapses back into liquid with the percussive crack of artillery; massive centrifugal turbines screaming against the drag of cold grease; the cavitation of ship-sized propellers buried in the silt, shredding the water into boiling, milk-white ribbons of negative pressure. 

Yet despite the iron and the salt, the rift is violently green.

It is choked with a vegetation so dense, fast-sprouting, and heavy-walled that it behaves less like an ecosystem and more like an uncontrolled cellular eruption. Enormous, ribbed kelp-analogues—tough as cured belting leather, slick with vegetable tallow—sprout directly out of the fractured basalt and the riveted hulls of drowning infrastructure. They do not drift; they are whipped. The valley is caught in a closed-loop current, an underwater thermal hurricane driven by submerged geothermal manifolds and bank after bank of cooling towers that continue to discharge boiling, chemical wash into the abyss. 

Fronds eight feet wide and fifty feet long are flung through the dark, slapping against the rust-eaten lattices of electrical substations with the report of wet canvas snapping in a gale. They grow at an impossible rate, feeding directly on the dissolved hydrocarbons and electromagnetic leakage of the drowned plants. Where a leaf is torn in two by the turbulence, the raw edge does not rot—it seals instantly with a bead of black latex, which immediately crystallizes, extruding thousands of stiff, translucent silica needles that knit into a new blade within minutes. 

Human mark is everywhere, but it has no master. Scattered across the jagged shelves of the rift are the remnants of a manufacturing province that was submerged before it could be shut down. Monolithic concrete transformer yards, cracked into fresh, angular blocks by the sudden drop of the valley floor, sit tilted on the talus. High-voltage cables thick as human torsos writhe across the rock like blind eels, severed ends arcing in silent, violet bursts that boil the surrounding brine into brief clouds of gas. 

This is where the coherence of things fails. 

The heat, the pressure, and the hyper-saturated current have begun to erode the distinction between the metallurgical and the botanical. Reality inside the rift has developed a slow, oily grain. In the eddies behind drowned factory gables, cavitation bubbles do not rise; they freeze. Spheres of empty vacuum, stable as quartz marbles, drift in suspension, bobbing against the kelp blades until the pressure finally remembers them and they implode with sharp, rhythmic clicks that pit the steel of nearby cat-walks. Copper conduit, exposed to the boiling current, has begun to sprout pale, tubular taproots that drill downward into the concrete, drawing up lime to sheath the metal in porous, bone-like bark. 

Around the intake flues of the lowest pump-stations, the water behaves incorrectly: it flows outward from the vacuum, falling upward in jagged, prismatic sheets that refuse to mix with the darker flood until they strike a thermal boundary and shatter into microscopic, buoyant ice crystals that burn with cold green bioluminescence before melting back into the scald. 

There are no fish here. There are no crabs scuttling over the fractured slag, no worms threading the newly settled silt, no drift of gelatinous plankton. The rift is biologically deaf and blind; it possesses immense vegetative biomass and staggering kinetic power, but zero fauna. The kelp does not feed anything. It grows, thickens, entangles itself in the spinning shafts of open alternators, gets pulped into green meal by the thousands of horsepower churning in the dark, and fertilizes its own stump with its torn flesh.

The light is dying and local. The sun never reached this floor even before the breach, so the illumination is entirely technological and weird: the sour orange glow of high-pressure sodium bulbs still burning in water-tight housings half-crushed by the sea; the steady, phosphor-green simmer of irradiated runoff leaking from cracked heat exchangers; and the violent, strobing white arcs of heavy current grounding out into the kelp beds. When these arcs fire, they illuminate miles of the rift in an instantaneous freeze-frame: a nightmare of sharp, newly split basalt canyons entirely upholstered in violently thrashing, fibrous viridescence, through which the black, rusted ribcages of iron works, overhead cranes, and pipe-bridges thrust like the skeletons of an iron civilization caught in the act of being digested.

In the next fraction of a second, the light dies back to an iron twilight. The current catches a floating engine block, swings it like a pendulum against a basalt wall with a dull, ringing boom that vibrates through the rock for ten miles, and the deafening, blind work of the drowned factories goes on, churning water into froth, feeding the green, building nothing.
