It is an accretion that does not rest on stone. If there is a bedrock beneath it, it lies tens of leagues below in the dark, crushed into silence; up here, at an elevation where the air should be thin and freezing, the atmosphere is held captive within a continuous, continent-sized raft of cold, wet fiber. It is vast enough that the horizon does not curve away so much as dissolve into its own rising respiration—a blue-gray mist that smells of crushed celery, wet parchment, and iron.

The entire terrain is newly born, yet completely full. There are no clearings, no open valleys, no barren patches of dirt. The substrate is not soil, but an unyielding, pressurized weave of hyper-vegetative muscle that was never seeded: it was extruded. The fibers have the geometric precision of algorithmic lattices, yet they weep sap, expand by hydraulic pulse, and possess the pale, bruise-colored translucence of unpigmented stems. Because the world is embryonic—only just brought online, unweathered, having suffered no seasons of rot or freeze—nothing is rounded. Every crest is an unblunted seam, every channel a freshly parted rift with fiber-ends still trailing raw, dripping filaments like frayed cables. 

Overhead, the light is choked before it can touch the surface. It is a dim, bruised daylight, filtered through miles of overhead canopy-vaults: colossal, rib-like fronds that lock together in a vaulted ceiling so dense it traps its own climate beneath it. The light that survives is green-gray and sour, casting almost no shadows because there is no single direction from which it arrives. It simply bleeds down from the saturated upper reaches.

What dominates the senses, obliterating any possibility of internal monologue, is the sound. It is a deafening, monolithic roar that does not come from wind or falling water, though both are present. It is the hydraulic shriek of a planetary system pumping billions of metric tons of water upward through microscopic pores under immense, non-biological pressure. It is a tectonic thrum—a vibration so deep and loud it rattles the fluid in your eyes, layered over with the abrasive hiss of endless, high-pressure condensation spraying off trillions of slick, upright blades. Standing anywhere on this mass, you feel the noise in the soles of your feet as a perpetual, trembling revving, as if an unimaginably massive turbine were wrapped in living skin just beneath you.

The moisture is relentless. The air is at full saturation, but it is cold—a brisk, cellar-like chill that keeps the water from vaporizing into light fog. Instead, the water hangs as a heavy, pelting suspension of droplets that constantly bead and run down the vertical architecture. Rivers do not cut through the ground; the ground *is* the aqueduct. Deep flumes, miles wide, are formed simply by the parallel growth of fibrous ridges that channel freezing torrents of clear, viscous run-off toward the outer margins. The water here does not erode the banks, because the banks have not existed long enough to yield. The current scours against tough, slick cellulose that resists friction with the resilience of fresh industrial polymer.

There is no animal life. No birds break the dense vaulting overhead; no insects scavenge the slick leaf-mold, for there is no mold yet, no decay, no compost. The ecology is closed, sterile, and singular. It is a pure autotrophy running at an industrial pitch, sustained by an invisible, post-material logic. You can see the signatures of this origin if you look closely at the growth nodes: where two massive shoots braid together to bridge a chasm, the wood does not knurl or knot. It interfaces with microscopic, cellular teeth that lock together with the cold, absolute snap of manufactured gears, before sealing themselves in a skin of translucent amber resin. 

The dynamics of the place operate in sudden, violent biological shifts. Periodically, a whole sector of the terrain—stretching beyond sight—will shudder. The roaring sound climbs an octave, becoming a hair-raising whine as the internal vascular pressure spikes. Then, with a series of wet, percussive cracks like artillery fire, millions of new shoots will burst from the slick flats simultaneously, stabbing upward through the mist to seek the dim light. They do not grow slowly; they inflate, driven by pressurized sap and programmed cellular expansion, solidifying within minutes into cold, rigid pillars that crowd the already suffocating space even further. 

To look across it is to see a landscape made entirely of tendons and sluices, green-black and wet as a freshly cut organ, stretching out under a low, dripping ceiling that belongs neither to nature nor to a forge, functioning with absolute, deafening indifference to anything that might try to walk upon it.
