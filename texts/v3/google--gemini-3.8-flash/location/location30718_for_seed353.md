There is no dampness to rot what dies here, so nothing ever truly clears away.

The floor is not earth, nor has it been for an epoch so vast that the rust has ceased being red and turned the dull, vitreous violet of fully oxidized alloy. Miles above any sea level, across an undulating upland basin that spans as far as an industrial district without borders, the ground is an interlocking expanse of perforated steel decking, sheared boiler plate, and dead rail beds. Yet you cannot see more than an arm’s length into it, because the flora has colonized every seam, every rivet head, every flue.

It is hot enough to warp tin. The air does not move in drafts; it shivers with pure convective radiance, baked dry by an under-floor furnace network that must have lost its regulators ten thousand cycles ago. But there is no light. The sky above is entirely blocked by a dense, interlocking canopy of black fibrous timber and sagging iron trusses. At best, there is only the faintest, soot-blinded red seeping through the floor-grates—not flame, just iron plates glowing with the stubborn, suffocating cherry of an enclosed forge.

Here, the vegetation does not drink. It feeds on heat and ambient grease. The plants are made of dry carbon, silicate whiskers, and a dense, aromatic lignin that snaps like parched bone when stressed. They grow in a state of hyperactive, blind panic. Ropes of braided, hairless vine—thick as locomotive boilers—writhe out of the machine housings, extruding yards of dry, corded mass each hour. They do not have leaves; they produce wide, wafer-thin plates of brittle mica-leaf that rasp against one another in the dark, gathering the thermal upwelling.

Because of this speed, the landscape is in perpetual, silent collision.

A massive gantry crane, its gears seized with petrified sap, will stand for an hour as the anchor for a towering hedge of needle-spines—leafless, gray-black brush that packs the space between girders until the density is absolute, leaving no air to swing a hammer. Then, somewhere deep in the root-knot, a dry tuber the size of a water tank swells, cracks from its own rapid desiccation, and fractures the gantry’s cast-iron footings with a dull, velvet-wrapped *thump*. The iron twists. The structure leans three degrees. A shower of dead, soot-caked timber collapses into the tangle below, only for fresh, hair-like shoots to spear upward through the wreckage instantly, sewing the ruin back together before the dust has even settled.

It should be deafening, but it is nearly noiseless. The plant matter is so packed, so cellular and arid, that it acts as acoustic baffling. The tearing of iron plate sounds like paper ripping under a thick wool blanket. The collapse of an entire elevated walkway, choked to death by dry pyrophyte burls, registers only as a faint, distant cough and the whisper of dry chaff raining down through the dark. The primary sound is a continuous, subterranean ticking—like a millions-strong chorus of cooling engine blocks, punctuated by the faint *snick-snick-snick* of silken seed-cases bursting without moisture in the heat.

Nothing breathes air here in the way animals do. There are no grubs in the dry wood, no bats in the iron rafters, no moths beating against the dull, deep-floor coals. The biome has closed the loop between machine and marrow entirely on the side of the vegetal. The plants tap the ancient hydraulic arteries, sucking up tallow, mineral oils, and pressurized hydrocarbons that still push through the deep pipework. In return, their roots—fibers as tough as wire rope—crawl into the pistons and valves, serving as flexible packing, holding the dying pressure systems together through sheer biological tension. The factory cannot stop because the root-mats are its gaskets; the plants cannot cool because the factory’s heart remains trapped in a runaway thermal feedback.

Everything has been smoothed by age, even in its violence. The steel has no sharp edges left; thousands of generations of fast-growing, silica-heavy lichen have rubbed the structural beams down until they resemble graying whalebones. The stacks of long-abandoned dynamos are rounded hummocks, buried under feet of shed chaff and dry scale, looking like the barrows of ancient kings if kings were made of copper windings and carbon brushes.

When the turbulence peaks—an event that occurs without weather, triggered only by the heat building beyond some internal threshold—a structural section of the upland will shift. A square mile of decking, bloated with root-mass and warped by 300-degree heat, will buckle upward into an arch. You feel it in your knees through the floor: a long, shuddering sigh of tortured metal. In the blackness, unseen, thousands of dry limbs shatter at once, an enormous shearing event that smells intensely of toasted chicory, scorched wire, and dry cedar. 

For a few minutes, there is an open space—a rare, hollow pocket in the suffocating weave where the sky might almost be imagined, though it is only more iron and more black vine overhead. But almost immediately, the shoots begin again. Out from the freshly snapped stumps, white-hot with the friction of their breaking, long questing tendrils whip through the gloom, uncoiling in erratic spirals, seeking iron to grip, heat to suck, and another vine to crush against, racing to fill the empty dark before it can cool.
