There is no bedrock here, no tectonic foundation of granite or basalt. The floor of the world is an infinite floor of brick.

It stretches thousands of leagues in every direction, dead-flat and low, a vast plain of hand-pressed terracotta, sun-dried adobe, and raw gray lime-mortared flags. Nothing here was born of the earth; everything has been pressed, turned, stacked, or slaked. Yet there is no machinery, no metal rebar, no glass. The logic is that of the kiln, the pit, the wooden mold, and the thumb—only executed on the scale of an ocean.

It is suffocatingly hot. The heat does not fall from a sun—the sky is a dim, uniform ceiling of sagging, ochre steam—but radiates up from the masonry itself. Beneath the uppermost skin of brick, the deeper strata are still firing, glowing with a slow, stifling bake that never cools. The air smells of wet pottery, wood ash, and the sharp, alkaline burn of fresh quicklime.

Water is everywhere, but it has nowhere to go. It weeps continuously from the seams between the blocks: hot, mineral-heavy water, thick with dissolved silt and chalk. It forms shallow, scalding sheets that skin over the low plazas, creeping in sluggish currents across the brickwork. In some places, it collects in miles-wide depressions where the floor has sagged under its own weight, creating warm, milky shallows barely ankle-deep that hiss gently against the porous tiles. Steam curls off these flats in lazy, waist-high braids, dimming the light to a perpetual, copper twilight.

There are no plants. There are no weeds sprouting from the mortar, no moss, no lichen. Life has not had time to arrive, or perhaps the substrate rejects it. The only things that grow are the mineral crusts: delicate, brittle rimes of salt and potash that flower along the edges of the drying puddles, crunching underfoot, only to be dissolved an hour later when the water shifts.

The scale is oceanic, but mostly it is empty. One can walk for days across a single, unbroken expanse of herringbone paving, where the only feature is the rhythm of the joints and the occasional, enormous impression of an unknown hand—ridges and whorls pressed into the clay while it was soft, each groove wide as a trench.

Then, miles apart across the misty flats, the forms rise. 

They are sparse, monumental, and turbulent. They do not look planned; they look like sudden, violent spasms of construction. A tower will heave itself upward from the pavement—a ragged cylinder of stacked mud bricks, thousands of courses high, lashed around its middle with braided hemp ropes as thick as tree trunks. These ropes stretch out into the empty air, pinned to the distant floor with wooden stakes the size of obelisks, groaning under immense, phantom loads. 

The turbulence here is not weather; it is the construction itself, which moves with a sickening, Solaris-like instability. The architecture behaves like living matter trying to remember primitive human craft. A wall of lime-mortared cobble will erupt from the wet floor over the span of twenty minutes, wet mortar bubbling and boiling as it cures in the fierce heat. It will rise forty feet into the dimness, raw and dripping, its lines wavering with the crude unevenness of a child’s coil-pot—and then, as if seized by an architectural doubt, the lower courses will shear. The entire mass slides into its own slurry with a deep, muffled *thwack*, splashing scalding slip across the flags, instantly melting back into a flat, wet shoal of clay.

A mile away, an impossibly vast wooden scaffold, built from rough-hewn, unsquared timbers tied with wet rawhide, begins to assemble itself in reverse. Cross-braces creak and bend against an invisible strain, joints weeping resin in the heat, only to snap with the sound of distant cannon-fire, dropping bundles of wet reed mats into the shallows below.

The sounds are strange, heavy, and spaced far apart. There is no wind. The air is too dense, too trapped by steam. Instead, the landscape is mostly quiet, dominated by the soft, ceaseless *plip-plop* of hot condensation dripping from unseen overhangs in the mist, and the low, wet suction of curing earth. Occasionally, the quiet is torn by the deafening, dry crack of a miles-long ceramic fault opening through the floor, followed by the dull, resonant boom of structural failure far out beyond the horizon, where some titanic colonnade has toppled into the mud.

Physics here is slightly unmoored from weight. You might find a single course of heavy river-stones floating eight inches above the pavement, perfectly aligned, held in place by nothing, while beneath them the wet mortar waits patiently to receive them. In other places, the puddles drain upward, droplets of warm, red clay water rising like hot sparks toward the blank ceiling of cloud.

There is a sense of absolute infancy to the landscape. It has no history; it has no erosion. There are no worn edges, no rounded corners carved by centuries of wind. Every brick is sharp-edged, newly turned out of the frame; every mortar line is still yielding to the finger; the timber smells raw and sappy. The world is being made, frantically and clumsily, by an unseen builder that possesses only the simplest tools—mud, straw, fire, rope, and water—but commands the volume of a continent, forever laying down the foundations of a city that it cannot stop tearing down.
