High on the spine of the ridge, where the thin exterior atmosphere is sharp enough to cut the throat, there is an opening—a vertical fracture in the mountain’s slate-and-schist crest that swallows the cold and does not give it back. 

Step over its lip, down through the pinch of the split, and the world reverses. The freezing gale ceases instantly, walled off by a curtain of thermal resistance. The interior is warm, humid, and airless in the way of a long-occupied bed. It smells heavily of ammonia, wet down, crushed seed-husks, and fatty secretions. The heat is not geothermal; it is kinetic and metabolic, generated by sheer, frantic biomass packed into a bell-shaped vault no larger than a parish chapel.

It is near-total dark. At noon, the cleft above admits only a dull steel thread of light that dies thirty feet down, diffused into the suspended vapour of ten thousand respirations. Everything else must be navigated by touch, echo, and the slight, burning itch of ammonia in the eyes.

The rock here has long ceased to be jagged. Over centuries—perhaps millennia—the limestone ribs of the chimney have been rounded, polished, and grooved by the passage of soft bodies and clinging claws until the stone resembles oiled saddle-leather or worn black soapstone. Moisture is constant but never generous; it does not flow in streams, but gathers on the ceiling from the animals’ breath, condensing into heavy, tepid drops that strike the shoulder or the scalp with rhythmic, predictable weight.

The sound is immense. It is not the roar of wind or the crash of rockfall, but a dense, chittering roar composed of infinite smallness: the clicking of claws against calcite, the dry rasp of flight-membranes brushing stone, deep internal murmurs, teeth grinding on seeds brought up from the low valleys, and a persistent, high-frequency hiss of echolocation that vibrates the bones behind the ear. It fills the chamber entirely, leaving no silence for a human thought to form without being crowded out by the chorus.

They cover every surface. They are high-altitude swifts or some lineage of high-metabolism bats—broad-chested, covered in dense, slate-colored pelt that sheds water like grease, equipped with four-jointed grasping limbs adapted to cling to inverted inclines. They hang three and four deep. Where the ceiling domes, they form living shingles, a shifting quilt of hot, vibrating flesh that breathes as a single organ. When one shifts, a ripple runs through twenty feet of them, accompanied by a dry, rustling clap like a gale shaking dry reeds.

Beneath them, the floor is an accumulated, shifting mulch of droppings, beetle carapaces, dropped feathers, and fine bone-meal, six or eight feet deep. It is soft to tread on, yielding and warm underfoot, fermenting slowly in the dark. It harbors its own world of blind, pale troglobites—cave crickets with antennae six inches long, flat brown dermestid beetles that work tirelessly at the edges of things, cleaning fallen fledglings down to white filigree within hours.

There are almost no plants. Here and there, where the moisture drips down a slick trough of rock, a greasy, liver-colored smear of cyanobacteria clings to the dark, sustained entirely by the animals’ waste and the dark heat. 

Human presence here is absent, save for what was abandoned long ago by whoever possessed the endurance to climb this high. Wedged deep into a dry horizontal seam, just beyond the reach of the deepest guano, sits a single, ancient artifact: a notched length of ibex horn, bound to a heavy river pebble with dried gut. It is polished by handling, its binding petrified by centuries of mineral-laden dust. Perhaps it was a weight for a snare, or a tool used by ancient hunters who crept up from the timberline to harvest the fat, oil-rich squabs before the winter set in. It has not moved in generations. The cave has accepted it; a family of long-legged arachnids has strung their tight, non-sticky tripwires between the horn and the rock.

Outside, the mountain is dying of cold, scoured to the bone by frost-shatter and high-altitude storms that rip snow into powder. Inside, protected by fifty feet of mountain stone, the heat never fails. The flock maintains it. Generation after generation hatches, feeds in the lower clouds miles below, returns by twilight through the razor-slit of the peak, and adds its tiny measure of fever to the dark, ancient hollow.
