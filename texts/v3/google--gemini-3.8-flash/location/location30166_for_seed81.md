The plateau sits four thousand meters above whatever plain lies below, but the cold of that height never reaches the floor. Heat rises here from underneath the flagstones—a steady, feverish pulse that turns the thin mountain air into a thick, sodden wash. 

It is an urban geography without a populace, built on a plan that seems to have budgeted for vast, echoing distances between anything of substance. Broad avenues—wide enough for eight rail-beds side by side—run straight into the sulfurous murk, paved with heavy blocks of basalt that have worn smooth and concave under centuries of dripping runoff. On either side, the architecture does not consist of human-scaled dwellings, but of immense, modular ironworks: cooling bastions, tiered transformer banks the size of cathedrals, and colossal, open-sided pipe galleries whose rooflines vanish upward into the gloom. Yet for all this built density, the place is overwhelmingly empty. The air between the iron columns is enormous and hollow; you could walk for twenty minutes across a single drainage terrace without passing a wall, surrounded only by the colossal, rhythmic spacing of riveted pylons rising into the dark like legs of wading beasts.

It is relentlessly wet, but it does not rain from clouds. The rain is entirely local, born of the breath of the stone. 

Boiling water courses through thousands of subterranean flues just inches beneath the basalt. The heat drives moisture outward through every fissure, weep-hole, and joint. It strikes the colder mountain draft overhead and condenses immediately, rolling back down along the rusted girders in thick, greasy braids. The iron is permanently weeping. Sheets of hot runoff glide silently over the canted floors, sliding into deep gutters that gurgle in the distance. When the wind stirs, it does not bring fresh mountain air; it merely pushes a hot, blind curtain of vapor across the avenues, soaking the skin within seconds and leaving behind a faint, mineral grit that tastes of tin and salt.

There is no green. Nothing leafy or rooted could survive the thermal wash and the acidity of the condensate. Instead, the lower courses of the masonry are coated in a pale, rubbery scum—neither lichen nor fungus, but a dense, sulfur-eating biofilm that forms yellow-white blisters where the water pools. In the dry margins under the overhanging gantries, where the steam cannot reach, the iron simply crusts over with hard, turquoise-colored precipitates of copper and sulfate, forming brittle blooms that crunch like eggshells underfoot.

The light never resolves into dawn or dusk. It remains stuck in an iron-colored twilight, filtered through the boiling ceiling of mist that blankets the shelf. The only internal illumination comes from the infrastructure itself: dull orange pilot flames that burn behind thick, soot-crusted glass ports set into the retaining walls, and the occasional, silent violet flicker of a high-tension insulator grounding out against a wet pylon far overhead.

For long stretches, the landscape is almost entirely quiet. The soundscape has been softened by moisture and distance. The thunder of the immense machines that must exist somewhere below is muffled by miles of stone, reduced to a subsonic vibration that settles behind the ribs rather than entering the ear. What remains is fragile: the hollow, chime-like *plink* of hot condensation falling eighty feet into an empty cistern; the long, breathless sigh of a steam release opening in another precinct three kilometers away; the persistent, slick rustle of water sliding over flat rock.

Very rarely, something living cuts through the gloom. They are not warm-blooded things. Long-limbed, pale arthropods, the size of dogs and flattened like river stones, cling upside-down to the wet iron beams, their carapaces coated in the same yellow film that covers the stone. They move with an unsettling, hydraulic slowness, grazing on the sulfur-mats, undisturbed by the boiling water that cascades over their joints. Occasionally, one will lose its grip, detaching with a wet suction sound to drop into a sluice, washed away into the dark throats of the floor without a struggle.

The city operates on an automatic, hydraulic pulse. Every hour or so, a shift in pressure ripples through the flagstones. The gutters fill suddenly with an inch of scalding foam; the iron girders groan as they expand, popping rivets with dull, hammer-like reports that travel through the skeleton of the valley; the mist thickens until visibility is reduced to the reach of an outstretched hand. Then the surge passes. The water recedes to a glaze. The silence closes back over the avenues, vast and vacant, warm as blood, under a sky that has forgotten the sun.
