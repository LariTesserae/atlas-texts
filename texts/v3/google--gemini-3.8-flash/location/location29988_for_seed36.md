It is a sunken rift, miles across, sunk three hundred cubits below the rim of a dry continent, but there is no shadow down here. The light is blinding, white and unblinking, descending not from a sun in the sky but from the ceiling of the vault: a vast, flat lens of selenite and fused quartz that catches the high desert glare above and magnifies it into an unvarying, perpendicular radiance. Down here, midday is continuous. It does not rotate or dim.

Because the rock beneath the green was split only yesterday in the earth’s reckoning, the geology has no softness. The basal floor is not sand or soil—there has been no wind or rain to grind one—but fresh-cleaved basalt and slate, sharp as broken glass, its fractures still razor-clean and black. Yet one rarely sees this stone, because the vegetation has overtaken it before the air could even oxidize its edges.

The plants do not grow out of dirt; they *are* the ground. A dense, mattress-like stratum of pale, succulent wood, three to ten meters thick, forms a living false floor over the chasm. It is composed of interlocking horizontal columns—flattened, pale-green trunks like bleached ribs, fused to one another by calcified bark that feels warm to the touch. Walking upon it, if one were to walk, would feel like treading on warm bone cushioned by velvet moss. The plants are hot; their internal metabolism operates at a furious, quiet simmer, generating an ambient fever-heat that stays locked at body temperature or higher, day and night without drift.

Moisture exists here entirely as an act of surface tension. There are no streams, no showers, and no puddles that splash. Instead, there is an eternal guttation: from the margins of the enormous, tongue-shaped leaves that crowd the air, clear water is squeezed out by hydraulic pressure from within the wood. It gathers in perfectly spherical beads along the leaf teeth. These drops do not fall. The air is so still, devoid of even the faintest draft, that the drops swell until they touch an adjacent stem, bridge across, and are reabsorbed through specialized felted pores, or evaporate into the humid blanket of the understory only to condense once more against the quartz ceiling and trace their way down silent, glass-smooth walls. It is damp, but static—a closed circulatory loop that loses nothing and gains nothing.

The density of this green mass is oceanic. There are no vistas here that do not end in foliage within twenty paces, yet the scale of the whole trough is vast, a regional basin stretching to distant pale horizons where the green meets the white wall of the rift. Broad, fleshy fronds, stiff as lacquered leather and veined with translucent latex, layer over one another in geometric shingles. Beneath them, woody brackets the size of tables jut from vertical stems, stacked like shelves up toward the blinding light. Every cubic yard of space is contested and occupied, yet there is no struggle visible; the leaves have already slotted together into an unbroken tessellation, each taking its measured fraction of the glare.

Scattered across this vegetal plain, half-swallowed by the root-mat, are dry-stone works. They are not cities, nor even villages, but low, circular enclosures and square platforms built from the same sharp-edged basalt that forms the rift's floor. They are primitive, mortarless, and entirely unweathered—their corners are as keen as the day they were stacked. In some places, a trunk has simply grown through the doorway of an enclosure, conforming to the stones without knocking them over, its wood flowing around the basalt like wax around a wick. There are no tools left behind, no potsherds, no rust; whatever hands raised these platforms used neither metal nor fire, leaving only the geometric arrangement of heavy dark blocks against the violent pale green.

Fauna is nearly absent, reduced to rare, specialized forms that obey the law of the vault's stillness. Now and then, on the underside of a shelf-leaf, one finds a flat, disc-shaped arthropod, broad as a platter and entirely white, clinging with dozens of hooked claws. It does not scuttle. If watched for hours, it does not move at all, feeding through a hair-thin proboscis inserted directly into the vascular tissue of the host plant. There are no birds, no flying insects to fret the light, no scavengers. Decay has no purchase here; a dead leaf does not rot into humus, but is slowly overgrown, enveloped, and mineralized by the living tissues surrounding it, preserved indefinitely within the green matrix like an insect in amber.

The acoustics are muffled and heavy. The endless overlapping leaves act as sound baffles, swallowing resonance within arm’s reach. What remains is not silence, but an interior pressure on the ear: the infinitesimal, synchronized ticking of xylem vessels under immense osmotic load, a low, tectonic hum produced by thousands of tons of living pulp respirating under heat, and the occasional dry, crystalline snap of a quartz inclusion shifting beneath the roots.

Nothing is pending here. No season approaches to strip the branches or bring the flood. The rift is locked in its high, brilliant noon, damp and boiling-quiet, growing steadily out of its own unblemished wounds, self-illuminated and complete.
