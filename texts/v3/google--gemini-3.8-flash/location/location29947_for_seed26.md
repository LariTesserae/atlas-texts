The ceiling is not rock, but a trussed horizon of corrugated galvanized deck, bolted so recently that the zinc still glints with a dull, oil-spotted spangle beneath the moisture. Miles below the daylight crust, this is the floor of a sunken distillation bay—a vast, hollowed quadrant where the ground is entirely made of poured aggregate, rolled steel plate, and pre-cast concrete culverts that stretch out into a fog-muffled distance the size of a low-country county. 

The cold here is raw, hovering just a degree or two above freezing. It carries no frost, only an aggressive, persistent soak. The air is pressurized, driven by deep intake turbines whose rhythmic, concussive whuffs arrive every four seconds, shuddering through the soles of your boots and rattling the water beaded on the edges of every I-beam. It smells of wet iron slag, sulfurous machine grease, and the faint, bitter tang of cold ozone. 

Water does not fall as rain, but it is everywhere in transit. It sheets across three-acre concrete flats, flowing thin and fast toward slotted perimeter drains that gulp with a steady, hollow slurping. High overhead, condensation gathers on cold-water supply conduits three meters thick; every few moments, a load of icy runoff slaps down onto the steel gangways below with the sound of dropped wet canvas. In the lower tiers, where the floor steps down in terraced lock-basins, the water collects into black, glassy sumps that lap against raw form-work. The edges of these basins are brutal and unsoftened—no silt has had time to collect, and the bevels of the concrete are still crisp from the removal of the wooden forms, the grain of the plywood immortalized in grey Portland cement.

Light is sparse, low-frequency, and pooled. Amber high-pressure sodium lamps hang at broad intervals from heavy messenger cables, their protective glass cages filmed with mineral scale. Between them lie hundreds of yards of liquid gloom, where the only illumination is the dull, shifting reflection of wet deck plates catching the glow from an adjacent bay, or the occasional blue-white pop of an automatic circuit-breaker tripping on a pump tower half a mile out. 

Because the architecture is so young, life has not established a deep bed, but it has rushed into the seams. In the expansion joints—packed with tar and braided neoprene—pale, stringy pellicles of cold-hardy liverworts and waterlogged mold have taken root. They are colorless, nearly white, fed by the constant wash of aerated condenser runoff and the low-lux bleed of the sodium fixtures. Here and there, clinging to the rust-primed legs of structural pylons, are thick, fibrous mats of an amphibious, fern-like weed with translucent, rubbery frills. They do not grow upward; they stream horizontally, combed out by the steady rush of the sluices, trailing ten feet into the current like drowned horsehair.

The fauna is sparse, but what exists is adapted to the hard surfaces and acoustic brutality. In the quiet backwaters behind the baffle walls, where the water idles before dropping into the next tier, blind amphipods the length of a thumb crawl over the rivet heads, their carapaces stained dark amber by industrial coolant. Above the water, clinging to the underside of the catwalks, are pale, heavy-bodied isopods that feed on the weed-mats; when the turbine pulse shakes the framing, they pull their legs in and remain motionless, resembling dropped zinc washers until the metal stops ringing.

The motion of the bay never settles. It operates on an automated schedule indifferent to day or night. Every twenty minutes, a klaxon sounds three low, vibrating blasts, and a bank of hydraulic gates half a mile upstream heaves upward. A wall of black, freezing effluent—surging from some unseen processing floor deeper still—roars through a central spillway. The noise drowns out the turbines entirely, climbing from a rumble into a deafening, white-water tear that sprays a fine, ice-cold mist hundreds of yards into the adjacent plazas. For ten minutes, the low concrete causeways are submerged, the pale weeds are battered flat, and the entire valley-floor shivers with hydraulic torque.

Then the gates drop with a dead, iron thud that echoes back and forth between the bulkheads for seconds after. The water recedes through thousands of precision-drilled floor grates, leaving the steel plates bare, steaming faintly in the chill, reflecting only the distant, solitary lanterns and the cavernous, manufactured sky.
