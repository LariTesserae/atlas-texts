The light arrives without the softening filter of moisture: a cold, magnesium-white glare that drops sheer shadows like cut iron across the plateau. At this altitude, the sky is not blue so much as a hard, receding violet, taut and empty from horizon to horizon. There is no haze, no vaporous drift, no dew. The air has the razor-thin, biting clarity of an open freezer, desiccated to the point where breathing it leaves a faint taste of dry copper and ash upon the tongue. 

Yet the continent is entirely alive.

It is made of wood, though wood of an alien, mineralized constitution—a calcified, iron-heavy lignum that has long since forgotten the memory of sap or rain. The floor underfoot is not soil or rock, but a deeply grooved, continental pavement of petrified root-plates, worn smooth by centuries of dry scouring winds. These root-mats stretch for thousands of leagues, cracked into hexagonal stress-plates like dried lake beds, but composed entirely of braided, fibrous deadwood impregnated with silica and graphite. Nothing rots here. The dry air arrests decay, leaving every shed splinter, every fallen spar from ten thousand years ago intact, polished by dust-storms into obsidian-black needles.

Though the living mass is vast and complete—there is no bedrock here other than what the plants have laid down—the landscape is profoundly empty. The living colossi do not crowd one another; they stand miles apart, solitary and titanic, punctuating an immensity of void. A single specimen might command a horizon twenty leagues wide, rising two thousand feet into the thin glare like a skeletal cathedral or an open-frame drilling derrick, its flanks stripped bare of foliage, composed entirely of stark, jointed trusses and fibrous scaffolding.

And they are running.

Without water, the flora has evolved an autogenous, high-pressure mechanics of friction, thermal differential, and hydrocarbon combustion. They are engines of fiber and pitch. High within the crown of an isolated tower-tree, massive hollow chambers—cylinders of dense, tightly wound ironwood banded with natural brassy resins—intake the volatile dry gases of the upper atmosphere. As the midday sun superheats the dark southern face of the trunk while the northern face remains plunged in freezing shadow, thermal expansion drives enormous, reciprocating timber pistons. 

The sound is unceasing and monstrous. It is an all-enveloping industrial roar that vibrates the floor-plates through the soles of one’s feet: the rhythmic, concussive *thwack-chug* of biological connecting rods forty paces thick; the high-pitched, tea-kettle shriek of dry static vents purging pressurized gas; the groaning of dry lignum under hundreds of tons of torque. Because there is no humidity to dampen the acoustics, the clatter travels unimpeded across vast leagues of space, overlapping until the world sounds like a thousand shipyards and locomotive works operating at full, frantic shift beneath the bare sky.

Between these monumental engine-trees lies only the naked root-plain, wind-sheared and blindingly illuminated. In the great wind-lanes, kinetic storms rage constantly—not storms of rain, but violent squalls of kinetic movement and dry chaff. Heavy counterweights of hardened burl swing on half-mile pendulums from the tree flanks, stabilizing the spires against the gale and, in doing so, churning the lower air into vicious, unpredictable eddies. Flocks of fauna ride these shear-winds: creatures with skin like drum-tight parchment and wings made of gossamer, stiffened horn. They have no eyes to be blinded by the glare; instead, they navigate by the deafening acoustic landscape, pitching and diving into the turbulent exhaust plumes of the trees to feed on floating tallow-spores and dry flakes of friction-wax.

Smaller animal life scuttles across the root-pavement with a dry, clicking urgency. Many resemble segmented calipers or jointed copper wire, drinking not water, but the thin, volatile mineral ethers that weep from the trees’ friction-joints. They nest inside the reciprocating guide-sleeves, risking being crushed by the sliding timber rams to lap at the smoking, resinous lubricants that keep the biological machinery from catching fire.

There are no chimneys, no rivets, no human hands in any of this; the forms are entirely dendritic, grown according to the blind, ancient architecture of pressure, strain, and leverage. Yet the logic is unmistakable: it is a landscape that has bypassed the chemical sluggishness of damp biology to become a scaled, self-assembling factory. The spires shudder under the stroke of their internal cylinders. Overhead, a miles-long gantry-branch twists violently under a sudden thermal surge, venting a plume of white, chalky exhaust into the freezing sunlight, its hollow core booming like a dry cannonade into the tremendous, brilliant void.
