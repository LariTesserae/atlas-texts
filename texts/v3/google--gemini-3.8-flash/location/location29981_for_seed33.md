The floor of the world is not missing, but it has fallen so far below the horizon of perception that the eye stops looking for it. What exists here is entirely vertical, held in a pale, indigo twilight that does not change its angle. 

It is a city, or the skeleton of one, constructed at a height where the air has grown too thin to carry moisture, too cold to rot, and too still to scream. Great plinths and colonnades of dry, unmortared shale rise thousands of meters out of the blue-gray trough below, terminating in broad terraces, stepped ziggurats, and narrow, knife-edge bridges that leap across miles of empty space. There are no machines here, no wires, no hearths, no sign that an engine was ever conceived. The architecture is pure masonry—hewn, stacked, interlocking, and colossal—yet its origin belongs to an era that preceded the wedge and the chisel. The blocks bear no tool marks; their faces are smoothed by the passage of dry eons, worn like river stones that have known only a current of dust and vacuum.

Despite the absolute lack of water—not a smear of frost clings to the highest parapets, nor a drop of brine in the deepest cisterns—the city is not dead. It is restless in a slow, tectonic fashion. 

Every few hours, without the accompaniment of a sound, a district will shift. A terrace half a league wide, stacked five hundred tiers deep with windowless tenements, will slide laterally along a fault line of frictionless dust, extending itself over the void like a drawn tongue. It does not fall. Here, gravity is not a constant downward pull but an established habit that occasionally forgets its jurisdiction. In certain courtyards, pebbles of dry slate drift upward at the speed of rising bubbles, hovering thirty yards above the flagging until a gentle draft carries them over the lip of an abyss, where they abruptly remember their mass and plummet.

The light is permanent dusk, filtered through a high, dry haze of pulverized mica. It casts long, razor-thin shadows that fail to align. The shadow of an archway might fall toward the north, while the shadow of the pillar holding it leans southeast, crossing each other without blending, leaving dry crisscrosses of pitch across the flags.

Plant life does not exist, though in the deeper alcoves of the tenements, sheltered from the shearing winds, there are pale, brittle crusts. They are not lichens, but an inorganic efflorescence—dry salts that have crystallized in the precise shape of fern-fronds and cup-moss, growing by a millimeter each century as mineral-heavy breezes draft up from the basement chasms. If stepped upon, they do not crush into mulch; they burst into a soundless, talc-fine powder that hangs suspended in the air for days, refusing to settle.

The emptiness of the place is vast. One can walk through twenty miles of contiguous galleries, passing through thousands of identical, dust-scoured chambers, and encounter nothing more substantial than the change in air pressure. The rooms are devoid of furniture. They are hollow cells of dry, cool basalt, their doorways tall and absurdly narrow, as though designed for things with no breadth.

Yet the ecology is active, running on non-aqueous rules. 

High up, where the megaliths narrow into needles that scrape the blackening upper vault of the sky, things move among the lintels. They are rare, solitary, and made of the same dry mineral logic as the city. Long-limbed, chalk-white wanderers—neither animal nor golem, with segmented carapaces that resemble dry flagstones—crawl along the undersides of the inverted stairs. They move with an erratic, insectile patience, pausing for weeks in the joints between blocks, their limbs wedged tight into the masonry. They do not drink; they do not eat in any metabolic sense. They appear to feed upon the spatial distortions, congregating along the seams where two streets overlap in the same coordinates. When one moves, its joints make no scrape, only the dull, muted *thwip* of air rushing into a suddenly vacated vacuum.

Beneath them, in the central plazas, weather cycles occur without a drop of rain. 

A "storm" here is a descent of pressure. The dim light dims further, turning the color of bruised slate. A profound, ringing quiet settles over the terraces, muffling even the scrape of one’s own boots. Then, from the chasms, a thermal of absolute dryness rises, carrying upward vast, swirling sheets of fine, gray shale-leaf. The flakes, paper-thin and sharp as flint, do not fall; they dance in complex, interlocking gyres between the towers, orbiting the pillars like rings around an ice giant. They create geometric lattices in the air, shifting in response to invisible magnetic corridors, hanging there for hours in terrifying, suspended equilibrium before the pressure snaps and they shatter back down into the depths.

In these moments of high pressure, the city’s geometry loosens further. A street that was straight becomes an endless loop; you can see the back of your own coat walking three hundred yards ahead of you, grayed by the mica-haze, perfectly mimicking your stride. If you stop, it stops. If you turn into a lintel, you emerge behind yourself, your boots stirring the very dust your previous self just disturbed.

It is a place entirely balanced on the edge of falling, yet preserved forever by its own desolation. The cold is dry and clean, biting into the skin with the crispness of a dead star’s periphery. Far out across the gulf, miles from the nearest ledge, a lone, cyclopean gate stands upright in the middle of the sky, anchored to nothing, framing a patch of dim air that is slightly darker than the rest. Through its arch, from time to time, the dry salt-dust blows horizontally, outlining the shapes of towers that have not yet decided to be built.
