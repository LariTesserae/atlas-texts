The hollow is perhaps fifty paces across, sunken just enough below the surrounding terrain that the horizon disappears, replaced by the softly rounded crests of low, buff-colored limestone bluffs. 

The air within the depression does not move. It holds a stable, unhurried warmth—not the stinging heat of open sun, but the retained thermal mass of thick stone that has absorbed light for centuries and breathed it back out into a sheltered space. It smells faintly of slaked lime, warm dust, and the cool iron of damp sand.

Light reaches the floor only as a diffuse, shadowless amber-grey. The overhang of the bluffs and the smallness of the enclosure keep the sky from ever fully opening overhead; one is always under the rim, in a perpetual late-afternoon gloom where edges are soft and colors are flattened into tones of fawn, chalk, and pale rust.

The ground is predominantly bare, composed of dense, compacted clay and flagstones worn down until their surfaces are slightly convex, like the backs of sleeping tortoises. Decades—or eras—of intermittent moisture have not carved deep gullies here; instead, the water has acted slowly, dissolving the sharp facets of the rock into broad, gentle scallops. In the shallowest depressions of the stone floor, thin skiffs of water sit motionless. They do not feed any stream, nor do they boil away in the warm air; they simply exist at a point of balance between seepage and evaporation, their surfaces taut, clear, and perfectly flat.

Vegetation has found little purchase, and seems to seek none beyond a few stable footholdings. There are no thickets or tangled canopies. Along the northern base of the limestone wall, where the rock sweats a slow, barely perceptible condensation, a film of liverwort forms a thin, olive-colored pelt, interrupted here and there by an isolated tuft of pale sedge. These few plants have the stiff, timeless quality of things that neither grow rapidly nor die back; they remain anchored in hairline fissures, sustained by the constant, low-grade humidity of the stone.

Near the center of the hollow, the hand of an old, minimal human presence shows itself, thoroughly reconciled with the geology. A low, curved revetment of unmortared stones arcs across the dirt, perhaps a foot high. Its components are field stones, rounded and pocked by the same weathering that shaped the canyon walls. Some have settled deeply into the red silt; others have a veneer of chalky calcium carbonate welded across their seams. It is impossible to tell if it was intended to retain soil, mark an enclosure, or merely clear the floor. It looks less like an abandoned project and more like a permanent feature of the terrain, as fixed and unyielding as the bedrock beneath it. A single broad slab, propped slightly by two smaller cobbles, forms a low ledge nearby, completely dry on top, damp underneath where the earth stays cool.

No birds cross the opening above. There is no hum of flies or rustle of lizards in the crevices. The space is profoundly, inertly quiet. 

The only sound occurs at wide, unpredictable intervals: the detachment of a single drop of water from the lip of an overhanging brow of stone, falling six feet to hit one of the shallow rock pans below. The sound is a sharp, musical *clink*, followed instantly by the complete restoration of silence. The concentric rings spread across the small pool, reach the stone margins, and vanish without disturbing the silt. 

Nothing here seems to be in transit. There are no dead leaves to decay, no wind to scatter dust, no rising or falling of a water table to reshape the banks. The hollow rests in an enduring equilibrium, warm, dim, and heavy with its own permanence.
