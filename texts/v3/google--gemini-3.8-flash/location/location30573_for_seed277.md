The elevation is felt first as a thinning of the air, though the cold that usually accompanies such height is absent here. It is warm—a steady, radiant heat that rises through the stone underfoot, carrying a faint, oily humidity that clings to the skin. 

This is an upland of continental scale: an unbroken, high-altitude plateau that extends for thousands of leagues beneath an absolute, sunless sky. The darkness is nearly total, broken only by the rarest dull-red glow of friction or the occasional brief, pale stutter of static discharge leaping between tectonic teeth miles away. Because there is no ceiling to catch the light, the darkness feels cavernous rather than claustrophobic, like standing on the naked back of a world suspended in a void.

The terrain has settled into its forms over geologic spans of time. The basalt has been smoothed, not by rushing rivers, but by long, slow cycles of steam and condensation. It sits in vast, rounded swells, interrupted by dry troughs and broad, shallow depressions of iron slag that have oxidized into a fine, damp gravel. Water is not plentiful, but it is persistent in its cycles. It does not flow in rivers; instead, it lives as a heavy, intermittent dew that beads along the underside of granite shelves and coats the massive, exposed drive-rods of deep-earth pumps. Every few hours, an invisible pressure shift across the plateau causes an adiabatic flash, turning the condensation into a warm, drifting drizzle that lasts for ten minutes, coats the slate-colored hills in a glossy sheen, and then evaporates back into the warm, grease-scented air.

There are no buildings here. There are no shelters, no towers, no roads, no evidence of habitation or deliberate architecture. And yet the land is profoundly, violently mechanized. 

The industrial presence does not inhabit the plateau; it *is* the plateau’s skeletal system. Deep within the rift-valleys and spanning the high saddlebacks are colossal, unhoused mechanisms—flywheels the size of small moons set flush into the rock, turning once every three days with a displacement that can be felt in the marrow rather than heard; naked transmission lines as thick as ancient trees, slung between raw stone pinnacles without insulators, humming at a pitch so low it registers only as a nausea in the inner ear. Along the dry washes, open-air piston galleries, forged directly into the native hematite, breathe in slow, hydraulic gasps. They leak an endless, thin mist of synthetic tallow and hot distilled water, feeding the dampness of the surrounding stones. These are not ruins; they are running. They have always been running, operating with an indifferent, self-sustaining logic that produces no finished goods, only torque, heat, and planetary motion.

Because of the vastness, these machines do not crowd the landscape. One can walk for half a day across bare, warm shale—past nothing more than the occasional iron-stained boulder and scattered patches of tough, wire-like black scrub—before encountering the rim of another trench where an exposed crank, three hundred yards long, sweeps through its grease-slicked arc. The emptiness between these mechanisms is immense, dominated by the silence of high, wide spaces.

In this dark, warm upland, life is surprisingly thick, having long adapted to the cadence of the iron. 

Sparse, leathery vegetation clings to the edges of the heat plumes: low mats of lichen that synthesize mineral grease, and pale, leafless tubers that drive their roots into the pulverized graphite around ungreased bushings. But the fauna is dense and quick. Blind, many-legged scavengers the size of deer navigate the basalt by sensing the vibration of the great shafts through their hooves. They graze on the oil-crusted mosses and lick the warm water that pools in the indentations of cold-forged fasteners. Above them, drifting on the thermal columns created by the cooling vents, are wide-winged, quiet creatures with translucent skin, trailing sensory filaments that brush against the stone to detect the swarms of grease-moths that breed in the warm slurry of the lowlands.

There is a subtle, predatory ecology at play in the dark. Smaller carnivores with dense, bristled coats nest in the dry chambers behind reciprocating crossheads, waiting for the return stroke to flush out blind lizards that feed on the carbon dust. When an animal is crushed by the rhythmic, unavoidable sweep of a counterweight, it is picked clean within the hour by scavengers that have no eyes, only an acute sense for the warm iron smell of spilled blood mixing with lubricating oil.

Despite the activity, the landscape remains mostly quiet. The machinery is so massive, its mass so enormous, that its sounds are sub-audible—a rhythmic kneading of the bedrock rather than a mechanical clatter. The occasional sharp hiss of a pressure-relief valve venting warm steam three ridges over sounds soft, like the breath of a sleeping whale. 

Overhead, nothing looks down. The black air is thick enough to swallow whatever stars might exist, holding down the damp warmth of the engines, cycling the moisture from iron to stone and back again, in an equilibrium that has not needed an operator for ten thousand years.
