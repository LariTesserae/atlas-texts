The water here is not an ocean resting in a natural basin, but a high-altitude sea held aloft on an artificial world.

Miles above whatever true earth might once have existed, a colossal platform of fitted stone extends into the dim, cold reaches of the upper atmosphere, completely drowned. The water is glacial, dense, and gray-green, chilled almost to the point of freezing, yet kept from solidifying by the sheer violence of its movement. 

Everything visible is masonry. There is no mud, no sand, no native continental crust. The seabed is an endless expanse of gigantic, hand-worked blocks—granite slabs the size of townhouses, cyclopean stairs, and vast, leveled plazas that stretch across miles before breaking into tiered parapets and sunken avenues. It is a completely constructed topography, yet entirely devoid of machinery, cables, or engines. It resembles the ruins of an impossibly scaled mountain redoubt, built without mortar by some culture possessing only muscle, levers, and stone, then immediately submerged and abandoned to the deep.

Time has ground this place down to its ultimate bone. The edges of the megaliths have long ceased to be sharp; millions of years of hydraulic friction have dissolved the right angles into rounded, bulbous swells, like wet cakes of tallow or bars of river-worn soap. The joint lines between the stones are now deep, scouring trenches, five feet wide and ten feet deep, carved out by the constant passage of grit and water. 

Over this drowned plateau, the water does not rest; it rages. Massive, invisible currents tear across the open flagstones with the force of an alpine gale. These are not rhythmic tides, but chaotic, tearing shear-currents that collide at oblique angles, creating standing hydraulic jumps and spinning whirlpools that corkscrew down from the dark surface to scour the paving stones. Plumes of pale, pulverized mineral flour rise in violent, twisting braids where two currents bash against a submerged bastion, only to be sheared away horizontally into the murk. The sheer instability of the fluid makes any sense of orientation fleeting; up and down remain distinct only because of the crushing pressure from above, while the horizontal axis is a tumbling war of lateral drags and sudden downdrafts.

Yet the world is largely quiet. Because the air does not touch it, the chaos loses its sharp edge, translating instead into a pervasive, low-frequency shudder. It is an immense, sub-audible vibration that passes through the chest rather than the ears—a deep, hydraulic hum interspersed with the hollow *clack-thud* of loose boulders being rolled along submerged avenues like dice, or the faint, glass-like hiss of cavitation bubbles tearing open and collapsing along the eroded lips of the high stone terraces.

There is not a single sprig of vegetation. No kelp sways in the surges; no moss or lichen films the granite; there is no green anywhere. The stone is bare, polished to a dull sheen by the grit-laden wash, or stained a dead, slate-blue by dissolved minerals. 

In place of plants, the architecture belongs entirely to animals. The fauna is abundant, specialized to an extreme degree to withstand the cold and the ripping turbulence. Clustered tightly in the eroded crevices between the great blocks are colonies of blind, pale creatures—jointed things like porcelain crabs, with wide, flat carapaces that they wedge into the stone seams using heavy, calcified claws. Thousands of them pave the floor of every sheltered trench, their white carapaces clattering faintly against one another as they strain the violently rushing water with feathery, translucent appendages. 

Above them, out in the open tear of the main currents, schools of elongated, blind swimmers pass in long, undulating ribbons. They have no scales; their skin is a milky, tough hide, and their bodies are dense with muscle, moving with a manic, shivering efficiency to hold themselves in the lee of the rounded stone towers. They feed on drifting matter—shreds of molt, mineral detritus, and each other—darting through the chaotic eddies like strips of torn parchment caught in a squall. Occasionally, something much larger drifts past: a bloated, barrel-shaped scavenger, twenty feet long and armored in thick, segmented plates of gray bone, tumbling deliberately through the hydraulic wash, letting the chaotic water roll it from terrace to terrace until its blunt snout catches on the carcass of a crushed swimmer pinned against the stone.

The light that reaches this plateau is feeble, filtered down through vast depths of cold, churning water. It is a dead, late-dusk pewter that casts no sharp shadows, only soft gradations of charcoal between the rounded crown of a megalith and the black throat of the trench beneath it. 

Standing on the edge of one of the high southern esplanades, the artificial plateau simply ends. The paving stones curve over into a massive, smoothed lip, and the floor drops away into a vertical wall of fitted blocks that descends into total, lightless depth. From this abyss, cold upwellings surge violently upward, boiling over the lip of the high shelf like an underwater waterfall running in reverse, shattering into plumes of icy spray that tumble into the plateau's crosswinds, washing the ancient masonry forever clean.
