The floor of the valley is an alluvial basin that has surrendered completely to lime, iron slag, and brackish condensate. It sits barely above the water table of an unseen, slow-draining estuary. There is no green here—not a weed through the cracked slabs, not an alder or a blade of marsh grass along the channels. The ground is too heavily leached with ancient brine, copper salts, and heavy-run oil to permit seedlings. What remains is a hard crust of gray-black mud, crushed coal, and rusted aggregate, continually dampened by a sky that hangs low and gray, trapping a wet, suffocating heat like a glass bell.

The heat is heavy, thick with the smell of wet oxidized steel and the ammonia of millions of living bodies. Despite the vastness of the basin—which stretches across kilometers of dead rail yards, defunct settling basins, and the skeletal frames of open-hearth mills—it feels vacant in its architecture and dense in its inhabitants. The structures are massive, spread wide apart with immense gulfs of flat, wet earth between them. Corrugated roofs sag between corroded trusses; high-line aqueducts and pipeline bundles span the empty spaces on concrete footings that have softened at the water-line into round, silt-colored knuckles.

Water does not flow so much as weep. It sweats out of the joints of cast-iron pipes that were abandoned with their valves half-turned decades ago; it pools in shallow, paper-thin sheets across the bitumen flats; it falls from the undersides of high steel gantries in a steady, warm metronome of heavy drops. 

Yet the quiet is vast, muffled by the sheer moisture of the atmosphere. The only sustained sound is a faint, dry carpet-hiss: the movement of tens of millions of shore crabs.

They occupy every square yard of the open flats between the machinery. They are pale, gray-shelled mud crabs and fiddlers, perfectly matched to the color of wet industrial slag. When the tide rises through the subterranean drain culverts, backing up hot, sulfurous water into the lower yards, the crabs shift outward in enormous, soundless waves—acres of armor clicking softly, leg against carapace, a murmur no louder than grain sliding down a wooden chute. They feed on the bacterial mats that film the warm, chemical-rich pools and the endless drift of organic matter brought down from upstream.

Above them, occupying the architectural heights, are the birds. Because there are no trees, the entire avian population of the regional wetlands has colonized the steel. They roost in numbers that bend the iron wire of the catenary towers. Cormorants, night herons, and pelicans stand shoulder to shoulder along every rusted catwalk, crane arm, and rail bridge, their silhouettes dark and motionless against the milk-pale overcast. They do not sing. They rarely call. When a flock of thousands shifts from the roof of an empty rolling mill to the skeleton of a gasometer, the sound is only the low, windless beat of feathered bone through dense air, followed by the dull, flat *thwack* of their feet settling onto wet zinc.

Decades of occupation have cemented the relationship between the steel and the bone. Guano lies three feet deep on the upper gantries, petrified by the heat into a white, plaster-like casing that protects the metal beneath from the rain while dissolving the rivets with acid. In the cavernous interiors of the processing sheds, where the light is reduced to an amber murk filtering through silt-coated skylights, the beams are packed with roosting swifts and small river bats. Their droppings drop steadily into the stagnant pools on the factory floors, feeding the blind, pale amphipods that drift in the sumps.

Everything has found an equilibrium that feels ancient, despite the machine-age origins of the bones. The rust has reached its dark-brown, velvet stage where it no longer flakes, having formed a stable crust over the cast plates. The ditches have found their natural grades, carrying hot, iron-tinted runoff past the rows of dead electrical substations toward the central marsh-creek. 

A bird drops from a high crane, folds its wings, and slips into a shallow cooling ditch without a splash. A moment later it emerges with an eel-like fish thrashing in its bill, shakes the sulfur-yellow water from its crest, and labors back up to its girder. Below it, the gray tide of crabs parts smoothly around the concrete pedestal, closes behind itself, and continues its endless, quiet scuttle across the warm silt.
