At this altitude, the sky has lost its blue and curdled into a hard, bruised indigo that never quite lifts into day. The air is so thin it tastes of nothing at all—an absence that catches in the lungs like powdered glass—yet it is laden with a clinging, oily wetness. Moisture here does not fall as rain. It precipitates directly out of the stillness, condensing against surfaces in beadlets of cold, viscous dew that take days to slide an inch downward.

The scale is oceanic in its emptiness, though there is no sea. Great, tiered massifs rise miles above the lower atmosphere, separated by gulfs of dead space so vast that a falling stone would be forgotten before it struck anything. But these massifs are not simply stone. The geology here is metallurgical, cyclic, and unmade: beds of native pig iron, shale layered with dense lead-veins, and towers of cold basalt that have crystallized with the precision of machine components. 

There are no ruins here; nothing was ever constructed. Rather, the mountain functions as an unauthored engine.

Every few miles across the high shelves, colossal granite rockers—balanced on natural knife-edge fulcrums of meteoric iron—tilt through arcs of thirty or forty feet. They move with the agonizing slowness of glaciers, completely without screech or clatter, lubricated by seepages of dark, petroleum-scented tallow that weep from fractures in the higher scree. The only sound they make is a displaced sigh of displaced air, like a diver exhaling underwater, followed by the dull, bone-deep *thrum* through the bedrock as a counterweight settles into its seat of shale. Below these rockers, deep clefts act as atmospheric valves, drawing down the thin, freezing fog and breathing it back out as warm, greasy plumes of vapor that immediately freeze into soft, fibrous frost along the rims.

Despite the near-total darkness and the biting chill—just a fraction of a degree above the point where water stiffens into brittle rime—the shelves are choked with growth. 

It is a low, heavy, velvet-textured lushness, black and liver-red. Without sun, the flora feeds on the low-grade galvanic currents running through the damp iron lodes. Thick mats of broad, tongue-shaped succulents blanket the basalt plates, their skins frosted with condensation that never dries. From the cracks between interlocking rock-pistons burst dense thickets of ribbon-weed, whose flat, rubbery fronds trail across the ground like untied cables. They shudder in rhythmic sympathy with the rockers, twitching whenever a subterranean cylinder releases its vacuum. High above, suspended across the yawning voids between the massifs, hang great hanging draperies of a dry, fibrous moss that collects the iron mist; it looks like coarse steel wool, but when touched, it is warm, soft as wet fur, and smells of peat and machine oil.

The fauna moves through this quiet machinery with an easy, blind familiarity. 

They are creatures of heavy mass and soft footfalls. Herd-animals the size of draft horses, built low to the rock on six splay-toed limbs padded with thick, leathery grease-glands, browse the ribbon-weed in total silence. They have no eyes; where their faces should be, broad, naked concavities of pale, sensor-pitted gristle tilt toward the rock, reading the vibrations of the mountain’s slow strokes. When the great balancing stones shift, the herds do not startle; they lean into the seismic pulse, using the momentary slack in the stone to rip away the deeper, tap-rooted lichens exposed by the opening fissures.

Above them, drifting across the immense voids that separate the plateaus, glide long-bodied things with wide, rib-supported mantles of black membrane. They are light-boned and hollow, buoyed by the warm venting of the rift-valves below. They navigate the pitch-black gulfs without cry or rustle, their passage marked only by the momentary occlusion of the faint, violet stars overhead. Occasionally, one will plane down onto a moss-hung terrace, folding its great sails with a wet, leathery slap, to pick through the crevices for cold-blooded ticks that swell on the grease-weeps until they are as large as apples.

It is an active world, but an unhurried one. The biological pace matches the slow, hydraulic respiration of the stone. Everything is damp, everything is cold, and everything is waiting out a cycle that has no season of light. 

At the edge of the shelf, where the iron shelf drops into a shear three-mile void, you can look out across miles of nothing. Far below, beneath the freezing inversion layer, a floor of white cloud stretches out to the horizon, catching what little starlight exists and glowing like salt. But up here, there is only the wet iron, the black moss drinking the galvanic thrum, and the massive, unbuilt linkages of rock rocking quietly through the dark, keeping some forgotten measure that the living things have learned to call home.
