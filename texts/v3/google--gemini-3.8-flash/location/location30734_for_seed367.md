High on the wind-sheared forehead of the plateau, where the red sandstone has been rubbed as smooth as a river pebble by three million years of blowing grit, there is a fissure no wider than a cart-track that plunges straight down and swells into an enclosed belly. 

Outside, the world is flat, blistered, and bleached under an immense sky. Inside this hanging hollow, held four thousand cubits above the salt plains, the air drops into a warm, rustling twilight.

It is an arid pocket, nearly bone-dry, yet it is thick with growth. Deprived of rain, the plants here do not drink from soil; they feed upon the warm, rising drafts that whistle up through the floor vents from deeper thermal faults, filtering mineral dust and the shed dander of a million living things. The flora is not soft or wet. It is a jungle of parchment, cork, and thick, jade-hard rind. Vast curtains of pale, beard-like lichen drape from the ceiling vaults, so dense they brush the crown of your head. Below them, fat-stemmed spindle-trees and pleated succulents jostle for every inch of ledge, their leaves coated in a waxy, fragrant varnish that smells of burnt honey and bitter resin. When the updrafts push through them, they don’t whisper like river willows; they click and clatter like decks of bone cards.

Deep within this verdant, moistureless crevice sits the settlement. It is not a camp or a ruin, but a full, crowded town compressed into the proportions of an amphitheater. 

The architecture is built from the ancient mountain itself: centuries of dry-stack masonry made of shale slabs, chinked not with mud—for there is no water to spare for mortar—but with powdered resin, tallow, and packed insect meal that has hardened into dark, insoluble seams. The houses are stacked ten, twelve tiers high against the curving belly of the cave wall, their flat roofs serving as streets for the tier above. Ladders of bound reed and fire-hardened cedar connect the terraces. In the dim, amber bounce-light reflected from the slot canyon overhead, the town looks like an immense, petrified wasp nest, riddled with small square doors, timber-reinforced balconies, and hanging bins woven from tough, fibrous roots.

The human presence here is ancient and manual. The tools lying on the thresholds are flakes of chipped chert, smoothed basalt grinding bowls, and bone awls bound to handles with sun-cured gut. There is no iron, no forge-smoke to choke the pocket. Life here is carved, scraped, and braided. Along the lower alleyways, people in tunics of woven plant fiber sit cross-legged, using round stones to crush dried beetle husks into pigment, or scraping hides stretched taut over timber frames with rhythmically scraping flint blades.

Yet despite the dry dust and the dim light, the pocket is violently, noisily alive. The fauna does not merely inhabit the place; it saturates it. 

The walls hum. In every crevice not sealed by masonry, colonies of fat, velvet-skinned cliff-mice burrow and groom, their round black eyes reflecting the weak copper light. Above them, nesting by the tens of thousands in the high vaults, are fork-tailed martins that drop from the gloom like stones before catching the updrafts, filling the dome with a continuous, deafening chatter of clicks, whistles, and the leather-snap of small wings. Down in the dense scrub between the house stilts, long-legged cursorial lizards, banded in ochre and slate, hunt the thick-bodied, flightless crickets that crawl over every horizontal surface. Nothing sits still for more than a breath. A lizard strikes; a cloud of small, chalk-winged moths explodes upward from a disturbed succulent; a child throws a woven net to catch a scuttling land-crab that has lived its whole life without seeing an open pool of water, drinking only the oily dew that beads on the fat waxy leaves before dawn.

The air carries a persistent, dry roar: the unison chorus of millions of chitinous legs rubbing against ribbed abdomens, the squabbling of birds, the dry scuff of bare feet on swept rock, and the subterranean groan of the mesa catching the gale miles above.

There is no stream here, no cistern, no well. Moisture is a precious, non-liquid currency. It is milked from the heavy, oil-bearing seeds of the spindle-trees, or tapped from the bladders of the great subterranean burrowing frogs that the hunters haul up from the deep cracks with long bone hooks. Dust settles on everything—a warm, talcum-soft powder that smells of flint and dried guano—yet the city does not choke on it. The constant, chimney-like draft sweeps the settlement clean, drawing the dust up and out through the narrow ceiling slot, leaving the rooms polished, dry, and bustling.

As the sun shifts overhead, the slot above acts like a dial. A single beam of sharp, yellow light slices down through the gloom, striking the town’s communal threshing floor for an hour. In that beam, the density of the life within the pocket becomes visible all at once: a vertical column of shimmering martin feathers, drifting plant floss, spiraling insects, and the glittering mica dust kicked up by a hundred people grinding seed, living out an entire, dense civilization within the smooth, hollow tooth of a dying mountain.
