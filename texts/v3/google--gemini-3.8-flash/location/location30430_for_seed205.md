There is no stone here, or if there is stone, it has been buried for geological ages beneath a crust of continuous, living lignite. The ground is a floor of compressed, petrified heartwood that extends from horizon to horizon, elevated thousands of paces above whatever mineral mantle once supported it. It forms a high, sun-baked plateau—vast enough to generate its own thermal fronts and wind shears—yet it is not flat. It is furrowed into continent-scale swells, ridges, and dry valleys, all formed from the slow, ancient growth of a single, planetary root system that has settled into an immutable, weathered balance.

The air is thin, glaring, and intensely hot. The heat does not have the sodden, suffocating weight of a swamp; rather, it is a dry, baking radiant warmth that draws intermittent beads of sap and condensation out of the deep grain of the floor. In the mornings, when the cool of the high altitude collides with the baked timber, a brief, greasy sweat coats the bark, pooling into shallow, scalloped depressions before the sun burns it back down to a tacky residue. These small, fleeting catchments of amber-tinted water are the only moisture for miles.

Despite the absolute totality of the plant matter—the fact that every cliff, bridge, and plateau is composed of wood, bark, and dried fibrous mass—the landscape feels profoundly sparse. It is not a jungle. There is no underbrush, no tangle of vines, no green clutter. Instead, there is immense, vaulted emptiness between monumental entities. A single pillar of fibrous, sun-bleached cork might rise three hundred fathoms into the sky, solitary as a watchtower, with nothing beside it for two leagues but a pavement of grey, grooved wood. These colossal growths stand like the pillars of an unfinished world, spaced far apart across a sweeping, arid savannah of dry grain. The scale is so enormous that distance is difficult to gauge; a dark speck that looks like a fallen leaf on the horizon reveals itself, after hours of walking, to be a dead seed-casing the size of a hillfort.

What fills this vast, open spacing is sound, followed immediately by motion. 

The sound is immense and unbroken, an abrasive roar that presses against the eardrums like physical weight. It is the friction of the fauna. Because the vegetable architecture is so open and dry, the plateau acts as an amplifier for an ecology that exists at an unbearable saturation. Billions of dry-shelled things—chitinous, multi-jointed, sun-hardened—inhabit the deep cracks of the heartwood. They do not scuttle in silence; they stridulate. Vast communities of long-legged locustids, each the size of a wolfhound, line the ridges in precise, widely separated rows, flexing their ribbed abdominal plates against the baked wood to throw territorial shrieks across the canyons. Beneath the floor, inside the hollow capillaries of dead taproots, colonies of blind, pale borers churn the wood dust, producing a rhythmic, grinding thunder that vibrates up through the soles of the feet. 

Above, the sky is not blue, but constantly striated by the passage of animal life: drift-swarms of pale, wingless floaters carried by the heat thermals; broad, flat gliders made of leathery skin that drop from the high columns and drift for miles over the wooden plains, their skin creaking like ship timber; and clouds of tiny, iridescent mites that rise off the warm sap-pools like smoke, so dense in places they cast traveling shadows on the floor. 

The dynamic of the place is steady, brutal, and biological. There is little decay here; the heat and the wind desiccate everything before rot can take hold. When one of the great wolf-sized locustids dies, it does not decompose. It is stripped within minutes by armies of swift, brittle-legged scavengers that reduce it to a dry husk, which then tumbles across the grain in the midday wind until it catches in a crevice and becomes mortar for the next layer of living matter.

Signs of human presence are tiny, fragile, and wholly provisional. They appear only where the wood has formed natural shelters—under the curled lips of peeling bark plates, or within the hollow burl-caves that form at the base of the pillars.

These marks are modest. A traveler might find a row of hand-notches hacked into a vertical wall of hard, reddish timber using chipped flint or teeth pulled from the jaws of a dead glider. Near the condensation hollows, there are sometimes low retaining walls made of woven wicker and animal tendon, built to keep the morning moisture from draining away into the deep grain before it can be scooped into bladders. There are fire-rings, too, though the wood of this place is too dense and dry-seasoned to catch easily; small, smoldering hearths burn dung and fibrous pith, casting a thin, aromatic smoke that hangs flat in the hot, high air.

In these scattered encampments, life is stripped to basic mechanics. The inhabitants wear garments made of beaten, oil-softened plant skin, scraped thin with serrated bone blades. They move slowly through the heat, always tracking the shadows cast by the distant, giant pillars, keeping their ears tuned through the deafening white noise of the insects to the specific, grinding tone that warns of a borer colony chewing its way toward the surface. 

When the sun reaches its zenith, the landscape turns the color of dried bone and red clay. The air wavers, bending the horizon into shimmering bands. The heat forces the larger beasts into the deep structural fissures of the wood, but the noise never drops; if anything, the midday glare drives the smaller, invisible things into a frenzy of scraping, rasping, and tapping, until the entire continental shelf of timber feels as though it is vibrating at the speed of a plucked string. You stand on wood that has never seen an ocean, looking out across an emptiness that is both completely empty and completely alive, where the ground itself is breathing, drying, and waiting for the sun to fall.
