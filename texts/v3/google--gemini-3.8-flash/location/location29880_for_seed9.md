The copper has not yet had time to turn green. In the high, humid heat of the gallery, its broad plates retain the dull, oily rose of fresh lathe-turned metal, sealed with lacquer that is only just beginning to milk and whiten along the wettest seams. 

This is the Seventh Humidifier Core, suspended four thousand meters above the valley floor on the ribbed flank of the upper pylon. Outside, the alpine air is thin and knife-cold, but within these bounded walls—a space roughly thirty paces wide and sixty paces long, roofed by an arched vault of wire-reinforced greenhouse glass—the atmosphere is held under positive pressure, thick as a greenhouse after an August storm, dense with the breath of steam-engines and the wet respiration of ten thousand broadleaf cuttings.

Every square inch of the basin is fabricated. There is no earth underfoot that was not sifted and packed into the zinc-plated troughs last month. The flooring consists of heavy cast-iron grating, checker-patterned to prevent slippage, beneath which a continuous sheet of warm water glides over sloped lead pans. The metal is unpitted, crisp-edged, fresh from the foundry molds; the bolts are bright cad-plated steel with the machine-shop grease still lingering in their socket heads, perfuming the steam with a faint, clean reek of kerosene and tallow.

Yet the vegetation has already exploded across the steel. It was brought up in cold-storage tubes and grafted directly into felt sleeves and coconut-coir blankets strapped to the structural trusses. Fed by the relentless mist of the cooling manifolds, the plants grow with the furious, cellular urgency of an open wound healing over. Monstera and fleshy-stemmed *Philodendron* have thrown out thick aerial roots that do not search for soil, but instead glue themselves directly to the rivets, tracing the warm copper water-mains like living insulation. Ferns, tiny and translucent, emerge from the weeping packing-glands of the reciprocating pumps. 

Water is everywhere, but it does not sit still; it is an active circulatory fluid. It sighs through overhead brass atomizers every four minutes—a hiss that begins at the southern bulkhead and travels overhead like a localized squall, dropping a warm, dense fog that cuts visibility to ten paces before dying out against the north flue. The drops hang in the air, heavy and slow-drifting, before collecting on the underside of the glass vault and running down the copper ribs in continuous, glassy braids. When they hit the hot iron casings of the pneumatic valve-chests below, they sizzle softly, returning instantly to vapor. 

The place is remarkably quiet, swallowed by the sheer mass of wet leaves and the constant, soft hiss of vaporizing condensate. The industrial mechanism does not scream; it breathes. Somewhere beneath the central staging platform, a low-pressure steam beam rocks upon its trunnions with a slow, heavy clank—*chuff-thump*, pause, *chuff-thump*—measuring out the displacement of warm spring-water drawn up through the core of the pylon. It sounds like a heart heard through a thick mattress.

Fauna is nearly absent, save for what came in the soil bags or was intentionally seeded to prevent mold. In the warm runoff channels between the walkway supports, small, translucent blind amphipods—scut-bugs no longer than an eyelash—flicker through the shallow water, feeding on the gray scum of bacterial paste that forms where the warm pipe-dope leaches into the flow. Occasionally, a single fat, black-bodied carpenter bee, drowsy from the altitude and the overwhelming sucrose of giant, forced calathea blooms, buzzes heavily between the girder-webs, its drone resonant against the hollow zinc siding.

Because everything is so new, the interaction between the machines and the jungle has a strange, raw clarity. There is no decay here yet—no leaf mold, no rotten wood, no rust. When a leaf turns brown, it does not rot into humus; it drops onto a hot bronze intake screen and is slowly parboiled by fifty-degree washwater, its veins pressed flat against the mesh like an herbarium specimen until an automatic scraper clears it into a collection drum. 

Light falls from above with a washed, milky brilliance. The high mountain sun hits the misted glass of the vault and scatters into an even, omnidirectional glare that casts no hard shadows. You can look straight up through the thick green web of leaves and see the rivets of the roof trusses glowing softly white, outlined against the intense cobalt of the stratosphere just millimeters beyond the double-paned glass. 

The room is an artificial lung, newly manufactured and immediately saturated, trembling slightly on its high mountain moorings as its twin internal cycles—the mechanical injection of vapor and the biological drinking of it—lock into their wet, unceasing rhythm.
