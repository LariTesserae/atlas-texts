It exists inside a fracture no wider than an open book, caught between two slabs of raw slate at the bottom of a submerged quarry, or perhaps in the hollow of a lead reliquary dropped into a sunless sinkhole three winters ago. The scale is so contracted that a dropped coin would wedged between its "canopy" and its floor. Yet within this seam, space has softened its geometry, pooling downward and inward, admitting an impossible, crowded world that smells faintly of crushed mint and wet iron.

The medium here is water, but water under an intimate, choking pressure that feels less like depth and more like heavy oil. It does not churn. It barely moves at all, save for an almost imperceptible capillary creep—a migration of molecules drawn from one side of the cleft to the other over the course of weeks. Because there is no ambient light, the darkness is complete, save for an occasional, curdled luminescence that clings to the edges of things without illuminating the fluid around them. A leaf glows along its serrations, but two millimeters away, the black is absolute.

Despite the near-freezing chill, the cleft is entirely stuffed with growth. It is not the soft, rotted kelp of sea floors, but a brittle, aggressively young thicket. The stems are fresh, faceted, and sharp as cleaved quartz. Miniature hornworts, their whorls stiffened with mineral salts, crowd against densely braided mats of black liverwort. They do not sway; the water is too dense, the space too tight. Instead, they lock together in a three-dimensional lattice, interlocking their needle-like bristles until there is virtually no free water left—only water held in tension between microscopic hairs and cold, green-black tissue. 

If you were to press a fingertip into it, there would be no yielding cushion. You would feel the prick of thousands of tiny, newly formed thorns, clean and unweathered, like the edges of broken glass before the sea has rolled them. The plants do not grow toward a sun; they grow toward an inverted center, curling tightly inward around whatever small objects have settled into the crack.

Among these stems are the built things. They are minute, but deliberately fashioned. Small, flat tiles of fired clay, each no larger than a child’s tooth, have been laid down in herringbone patterns directly over the slate, though half of them have been cracked open by the violent, slow thrust of white root-needles. In one corner of the crevice, wedged between two dense bundles of hair-moss, sits a tiny archway of unmortared river slate—barely three inches high, its keystone held in place only by the hydrostatic friction of the black water. Faint, chisel-cut grooves mark its face, crisp and unsoftened by age. Whoever or whatever stacked these stones did so recently, yet already the green filaments have begun to lace across the opening like iron bars, stitching the portal shut.

The weirdness here is quiet, structural, and cold. Space within the pocket does not behave like the space outside the stone. Though the exterior cleft is a mere slit in the rock, the interior feels recursively deep; you could reach your hand in to the wrist, then to the elbow, without ever meeting the back wall of the stone, feeling only the cold, wet press of stems growing denser, colder, and more tightly packed the further you go. Gravity operates at an angle, pulling not down toward the Earth’s center, but sideways, toward the south-facing slab of slate, so that the dense thicket leans horizontal, its fronds pointing into the stone face as if seeking to burrow into the solid mineral.

Fauna is nearly absent, save for things that seem more like moving splinters than animals. Occasionally, a creature the shape of an uncoiled watch-spring, pale as lard and possessing no eyes, threads its way through the gaps between the hornwort stems. It moves with a terrifying slowness, taking half a day to slide its segment across a single inch of leaf, feeding on the invisible bacterial grease that coats the rock. When it brushes against the stone, there is a minute, high-frequency tap—the sound of hard enamel striking slate—which travels through the liquid and dies instantly in the velvet mass of the moss.

It is a landscape held in stasis, yet brimming with the terrifying momentum of life that has nowhere to go. It is fresh, cold, and crowded to the point of suffocation. Everything has just begun—the masonry is new, the leaves are stiff with their first growth, the fractures in the rock have not yet accumulated silt—yet it already feels sealed away for an eternity, thriving in the dark, icy crush of a place that should not be able to contain it.
