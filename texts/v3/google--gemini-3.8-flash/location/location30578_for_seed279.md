The basin is crowded with standing blades of slate and brittle ironstone, an endless expanse of upright, razor-thin strata thrust up from beneath the low floor like the teeth of a jammed comb. Because the upheaval is young, nothing has been smoothed. The edges are raw fracture lines, sharp enough to part skin at a touch, packed so densely together that a person could not walk three paces without turning sideways to wedge through the gaps. Across miles of this sunken plain, these mineral laths rise five, ten, twenty feet into a dim, ash-colored gloom, standing shoulder to shoulder in the millions.

It is loud here with an acoustic violence that has little to do with wind. The air barely moves, yet the valley thrums with a continuous, deafening percussive drone. Every upright slab acts as a resonator. Water—cold, heavy with dissolved lime and iron—condenses continuously out of the low-hanging fog and sheets down the vertical faces in thick, greasy films. From the overhanging caps and higher spires, massive drops fall constantly, striking the hollow-set stones below with the sharp, metallic ping of struck iron. Multiplied across thousands of acres, this relentless dripping becomes a roaring, harmonic wall of noise: a wet, ringing clamor that vibrates in the teeth and ribs, periodically underpinned by the low, subterranean hydraulic groans of water shifting under high pressure through narrow fissures in the basin’s floor.

There is nothing green. No moss clings to the wet faces; no lichens have had the centuries needed to take root on the freshly cleaved stone. What biology exists is pale, slick, and rudimentary. The bases of the slats, where the water pools in black, skin-deep troughs between the uprights, are choked with a cold, rubbery biofilm. It is translucent, the color of boiled sinew, forming thick, gelatinous skirts that bind the bottoms of the stones together and deaden their lower footings. 

Very rarely, something alive moves through the maze: broad, flat-bodied things the size of shields, armored in jointed plates of calcified grit, creeping along the drowned alleys between the blades with glacial patience. They have no eyes in this dimness; they graze on the bacterial jelly, leaving behind scrubbed, glistening bands of dark shale that immediately begin to weep red iron-water again. Their carapaces clatter heavily against the stone uprights as they wedge themselves through, adding dull, hollow thumps to the ringing air.

Scattered throughout this jagged mineral thicket are marks of an older, crude presence. They are not settlements, but interventions against the stone. In places, ancient hands have jammed heavy, unworked boulders of foreign granite between the upright slate slabs, wedging them apart to force narrow, straight-sided alleys through the teeth. Elsewhere, slabs have been methodically snapped off at chest height, their broken stumps capped with flat stones to create low, flooded causeways that wander without obvious purpose before disappearing into the mist. Bone wedges, blackened and fossilized by the mineral-rich water, remain driven into vertical stress-cracks, frozen attempts to split or quiet the sounding-stones. There are no tools left behind, no signs of fire—only these brute, mechanical adjustments to the geometry of the rock, preserved perfectly in the cold, wet air.

The dynamic here is one of patient, mineral accumulation. The cold brine does not wash things away; it crusts them over. Where water drips continuously from one suspended ledge to another, it leaves behind pale, knobby stalactites of hard travertine that slowly bridge the gaps between the blades, fusing them into massive, ringing walls. A stone displaced today will stay wedged for a thousand years, sealed in place by iron rind and wet slime.

Above, the sky is never seen directly, only felt as a heavy, bruised-gray ceiling that sags into the tops of the tallest spires. The light is flat, shadowless, and uniform, filtering through the wet mist without source or direction, turning the labyrinth of sharp, crowded stone into a monochrome world of lead, wet shale, and cold, iron-stained milk.
