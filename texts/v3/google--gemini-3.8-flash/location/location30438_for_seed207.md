The ceiling is low enough that an upright human would have to bow their head, though there is no human here, and no light to see the stone by. 

It is a pocket no larger than a root cellar, hollowed out or left behind hundreds of meters beneath the permafrost line. The cold here is steady, fixed at a fraction of a degree above freezing—a biting, unmoving cold that preserves rather than kills. The air does not circulate with any wind, yet it is never stale; it breathes with an imperceptible, rhythmic pressure, expanding and contracting over days or weeks, like the slow bellows of the deep crust.

Every surface is cushioned. In the pitch darkness, the room is densely, almost suffocatingly carpeted in vegetation. These are not pale, stringy cave-weeds reaching for a sun that never arrives, but thick, leathery growths, cold-adapted and ancient in their habit. Deep mats of a moss so dense it feels like wet wool under the palm coat the floor and rise halfway up the curved walls. Interwoven with the moss are stiff, dark fronds—ferns or liverworts with waxy, grease-thick cuticles that resist the frost. They have settled into an equilibrium that has held for centuries; there is no competition here, only an unhurried, saturated overcrowding where frond overlaps frond, root-hair tangles into fungal net, and every millimeter of exposed basalt has been claimed, softened, and rounded off.

They do not feed on light. Embedded in the far wall, nearly choked by the cold green felt, lies the curved flank of a dark, seamless material. It is neither stone nor iron; it has the matte texture of compressed slate, but it possesses no grain, no joints, and no tooling marks. It belongs to an engineering that manipulated matter below the atomic threshold, built to endure tectonic drift without shearing. The object—whatever vast, buried system it belongs to—leaks no radiation, no hum, and almost no warmth, but it exerts an extremely faint, continuous field gradient: a subtle polarization of ionic charges that the root systems drink from directly. The plants do not photosynthesize; their dark tissues metabolize the field itself, transmuting the ambient electrostatic potential into slow cellular growth. 

Because of this, moisture behaves strangely along the casing of the artifact. It does not pool or run in rivulets. Instead, fine micro-droplets of water condense out of the stagnant subterranean humidity and hover as a glistening, microscopic film across the leaves. The dampness is quiet and patchy. In the hollows between the roots, small reservoirs of water sit crystal-clear, chill as meltwater, held in place by surface tension against the mossy cushions. Occasionally, once an hour or once an afternoon, a single drop grows too heavy, breaks away from the stone vault above, and falls into a reservoir with a sharp, glass-like *plink* that echoes briefly before the velvet density of the growth swallows the sound entirely.

There are no animals. No mites crawl in the loam; no blind beetles pick through the dead under-strata; no nematodes stir the thin silt. The silence is profound, broken only by the rare drop of condensation and the almost-inaudible respiration of the artifact—a vibration felt in the teeth rather than heard, a sub-acoustic resonance that stabilizes the pocket against the crushing weight of the earth above.

The dead matter below the living carpet does not rot. In the cold, without detritivores, fallen fronds simply press down under the weight of new growth, slowly turning to peat, compressed over millennia into a dense, fibrous stratum that cushions the stone floor. It smells of wet slate, ozone, and the sharp, clean bitterness of cold tannic acids. 

Nothing will enter this space tomorrow, or the decade after. The slow pulse of the buried machine will continue to coax the waxy fronds outward millimeter by millimeter, their roots wrapping ever tighter around its black, unyielding skin, holding the dark room in a heavy, frozen, perfectly balanced life.
