There is no bedrock here. Beneath the warm, standing film of water lies an uninterrupted pavement of rolled lead and corrugated iron, joined with thick beads of sulfurous solder that have never been allowed to cool past thirty-eight degrees Celsius. The plates stretch to every horizon, perfectly flat, bowed only by the curvature of the world itself. Because the grade never rises above a few meters across thousands of square kilometers, the water does not flow; it sits. It rests in a universal sheet two or three inches deep, clear as optical glass, kept tepid by the vast network of steam jackets and resistance coils thrumming beneath the flooring.

Above this wet floor rises an infrastructure of absolute, fresh manufacture. The steel is not pitted; its bluing is intact, marked with crisp stencilings of lot numbers and alignment hashes that have not begun to smudge or run. Towering banks of galvanized A-frames support miles of overhead gantry systems, steam-manifolds, and suspended conduits of spun basalt. Everything was built yesterday, or perhaps an hour ago, or perhaps centuries ago under terms of maintenance so rigid that time leaves no scuff. The joints are sharp enough to slice skin. There is no grit, no soil, no silt.

Yet it is wet everywhere, an engineered wetness. Fine, warm condensation falls without rainclouds from high, vaulted zinc rafters that vanish into their own white haze. The atmosphere is saturated, heavy with the clean, mineral breath of distilled steam and alkaline wash. High-pressure misters hiss in rhythmic cycles that overlap so completely they cancel one another out, producing a profound, deadened silence. The sound of this place is a padded, low-register sigh—the submerged rotation of giant brass impellers in deep sumps, the dull slosh of water displaced by passing mass, and the constant, microscopic clicking of claws against milled zinc.

Plant life does not exist here. Not even a green slime coats the rivets; no lichen crusts the stanchions, and no spores drift through the brightly lit fog. Photosynthesis has been excluded from the design. The light—relentless, amber-white, poured down from unbroken grids of sodium-vapor tubes and quartz-halogen arcs suspended five stories overhead—strikes only metal, water, and animal flesh.

And the animal flesh is everywhere. It is a biological density so extreme that the landscape appears to be carpeted in living linoleum.

Without a single sprig of weed to anchor them, animals have taken over every horizontal plane and submerged flange. They are pale, smooth, hairless things, bred or arrived to fit the industrial tolerances of the troughs: broad-backed blind skates with skin like scrubbed lard, their undersides constantly working the metal plates to ingest the nutrient grease that weeps from the machine-bearings; millions of soft-shelled crustaceans, semi-transparent and as large as serving platters, stacked three deep in the shallows, their segmented legs knitting together to form false floors over the drowned catwalks; and vast, quiescent colonies of tubular siphon-feeders that sprout directly from the exhaust vents of the hot-water pumps.

These creatures do not fight, nor do they flee. They are sustained entirely by the plantless, carbohydrate-rich broths metered out through the industrial piping, discharged through perforated brass diffusers that run along the seams of every pontoon. Great herds of hairless, pig-like amphibians, their eyes reduced to pale beads beneath translucent skin, wallow in the warm flumes between transformer banks. Their breathing is slow, synchronization without thought. When one shifts, fifty square meters of pinkish-grey hides roll and settle with the sound of wet leather sliding across grease.

Because the system is in perfect, locked equilibrium, nothing changes. The water depth does not fluctuate by a millimeter; the automated weirs, counterweighted with massive blocks of pig iron, tip and recover with hydraulic precision, spilling sheets of water that make no splash as they slide into lower-tier reservoirs. There are no seasons. The warm, flat light does not dim, flicker, or cast long shadows; it comes from everywhere at once, diffused through the hanging mist until the corners of the great gantry cranes are dissolved into a uniform, luminous cream.

If you walk across the submerged walkways—your boots finding purchase on the diamond-tread steel beneath three inches of bath-warm fluid—the crustacea do not scatter. They merely compress underfoot with a faint rubbery yield, then expand back to their resting geometry once the weight lifts. Overhead, suspended from monorails that run straight for a hundred leagues, empty iron cradles pass on greased rollers. They move at a walking pace, endlessly, carrying nothing from an unknown origin to an identical destination, their electric motors murmuring behind sealed cast-iron housings.

The scale is oppressive in its levelness. There are no hills, no valleys, only the slight stepped drop of drainage terraces every five miles, where the sheet of warm water drops four inches over a planed copper weir-lip with the heavy, frictionless pour of warm glycerin. From the edge of such a drop, looking out across the blinding, pale expanse, the vista is an endless repetition: steam-riser, gantry, trough; steam-riser, gantry, trough; submerged plate, pale carapaces, resting snouts.

The air smells faintly of scalded tin, tallow, and the mild, clean musk of billions of cold-blooded bellies warming against metal. It is quiet. Not the quiet of an empty cathedral, but the smothered quiet of an upholstery shop or an incubator. Millions of hearts are beating in the water, but their pulses are slow, matched to the steady, unvarying cycles of the cooling coils below, which keep the world at thirty-eight degrees, without end, without weather, without rot.
