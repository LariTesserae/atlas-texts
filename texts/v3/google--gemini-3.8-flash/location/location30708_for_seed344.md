It is a sunken trench, perhaps seventy paces long and no wider than a two-lane road, dropped cleanly into freshly fractured basalt. The break is recent enough that the rock faces are still razor-edged and unpolished, their crystalline facets catching the daylight like chipped obsidian and wet cast iron. Above, the sky is flat and pale, pouring ordinary midday glare straight down between the sheer, dark walls, illuminating a world that feels privately, violently hot.

The air here does not circulate. It hangs at the temperature of a scalded greenhouse, super-saturated with vapor that clings immediately to any surface cooler than itself. Water is everywhere, but it does not pool into tranquil ponds; it weeps out of horizontal fractures in the rock, slicks the sheer stone sheets, and condenses on overhead ironwork until heavy, warm drops fall in a relentless, scattered percussion. The floor of the trench is a floor of black shale chips and wet grit, cut through by a central sluice of clear, near-boiling runoff that slips silently over a bed of pale, mineralized silt.

The sound is immense and close. A bypass valve or cracked subterranean manifold somewhere beneath the basalt is venting pressurized steam into the water table, producing a hollow, earth-shaking roar that vibrates directly into the marrow of the shins. Overlaid on this tectonic white noise is the rhythmic, mechanical *clack-thump* of an isolated steam trap—some cast-iron float mechanism cycling over and over in the murk—and the high, needle-thin hiss of vapor escaping a flared flange twenty feet up the wall. It is loud enough to flatten thought, yet entirely steady, a permanent condition of the pocket rather than an emergency.

Human intent has touched this place, but with an austere, scattered economy. There are no catwalks or full structures, only the heavy hardware of extraction left to bake in its own exhaust. A single, massive centrifugal pump housing sits half-submerged in the wash at the southern end, bolted to a fresh concrete plinth that has already turned mottled gray and yellow from sulfur leaching. Its intake pipe, thick as an oil drum and wrapped in decaying lagging that drips like a wet dog, disappears into the rock face. At intervals along the trench, heavy steel stanchions are driven directly into the basalt, carrying bundles of lead-sheathed cable that sag under the weight of condensation. Some of these lines hum with current; others have been severed cleanly, their copper cores turned chalky turquoise where the steam has licked them.

Vegetation has crept in only where the water has cooled enough to allow cell walls to hold together. There are no trees, no shrubs with woody stems. Instead, the margins between the running hot water and the weeping stone are lined with thick, spongy mats of liverwort and an unnamed, rubbery moss that grows in overlapping shingles the color of bruised plums. In the shallows of the runnels, long filaments of chartreuse cyanobacteria stream in the current like combed hair, thriving in temperatures that would blanch a human finger. High up, near the lip where the air is marginally drier, a few stubborn tufts of hard-bladed grass have taken root in the shatter-dust of the initial blasting, their tips scorched brown by intermittent plumes of sulfurous mist.

No insects skim the water; the surface tension is broken by heat, and the vapor is too dense for wings. There are no birds nesting in the clefts. The emptiness is physical, a complete absence of animal breath, leaving the trench occupied solely by the machines, the stone, and the patient chemistry of heat and water.

Nothing moves quickly here. The water flows, but the system it belongs to is fixed in an immovable equilibrium. The boiler beneath the world continues to vent; the steam condenses on the cold ribs of the iron stanchions; the droplets fall back into the grit to be heated again. A thin, crusty skin of white silicate slowly, molecule by molecule, armors the rusted bolts of the pump, sealing them into the stone forever under a deafening, sunlit roar.
