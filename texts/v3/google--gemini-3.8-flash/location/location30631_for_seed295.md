There is no native earth here. For miles across the high mountain saddles, every foot of terrain was laid down long ago in coarse, hand-struck brick, slate flags, and river-mortar that has calcified into something harder than limestone. The foundations sit tens of thousands of feet above sea level, perched where the air ought to be thin and cold, but a steady thermal inversion holds the heights in a thick, stagnant warmth. Dense cloud blankets the sky continuously, blotting out the stars, moon, and sun alike, leaving the expanse in a deep, brown-black gloom where the eye rarely penetrates more than thirty paces.

It is an architecture so vast that it has become geography. Terraces fifty meters wide step down into artificial ravines; giant retaining walls three stories thick lean inward at slight, deliberate angles to cradle the slopes. Vast ramparts link one flattened ridge to the next. Because there is no raw mountain face left exposed, water behaves according to the logic of masonry: it seeps into horizontal lime joints, sweats down the faces of vertical brickwork, and gathers in long, shallow silt-traps where dead leaves have composted into rich, pitch-black loam. 

Out of this accumulated decay rises an unbroken, dripping jungle. Fig roots, thick as ship’s hawsers, run straight down the faces of the ashlar towers, forcing apart mortar that gave up its lime two centuries ago. Ferns, giant nettles, and broad-leafed nightshades carpet the paved courts. The greenery is lush, heavy-leaved, and dark, almost black in the meager light, nourished by the warm mountain vapor that condenses perpetually upon the brick. When rain falls, it comes as a fine, lukewarm drift that lasts twenty minutes, leaves every vertical surface slick, and drains away through thousands of small, square scuppers into sunken vaults below.

The air does not smell of iron or smoke; there are no machines here, no glass, no treated timber or wire. The work was finished with bone tools, wicker baskets, and millions of bare hands before it was abandoned to the wet dark, and what remains is purely mineral and biological. 

And it is not empty. The life here is dense to the point of suffocation. 

The sound is the first thing that asserts the sheer mass of living bodies: a roar so constant, layered, and loud that it vibrates in the cartilage of the chest. It is not mechanical, but the combined acoustic output of millions of cold-blooded and warm-blooded animals occupying every crack of the stonework. High-pitched, rhythmic trilling from tree crickets the size of thumbs overlaps with the hollow, wooden clatter of millions of clicking geckos that line the eaves of the high arcades. Below them, in the silted canals and flooded basement stairs, thousands of small, blunt-headed frogs produce an unbroken, iron-toned thrumming that sounds like heavy cables under tension.

High above, in the arches of the upper viaducts that bridge the artificial chasms, colonies of free-tailed bats stir in their millions. The musk of their guano is hot, ammoniac, and sweet, rising from deep shafts where it has settled over decades into dry, powdery dunes. Every few minutes, a section of the colony shifts, producing a sound like tearing canvas as tens of thousands of leathery wings slap the warm air in absolute darkness, circling beneath the vaulted ceilings before settling back into the crevices.

On the ground, life moves in continuous, low friction. The terraces are alive with small bodies: blind skinks slipping through the fern root-mats, banded kraits coiled on the heat-retaining flagstones, and enormous millipedes—jointed, polished, the color of wet lichens—marching over the weathered copings in endless processions. Flying foxes, heavy and slow-winged, drop from the overhanging fig branches to feast on the wild persimmons that have rooted in the balustrades, their wet, coughing squabbles audible over the insect drone. 

Because the architecture was built with patient, massive redundancy, it erodes with glacial slowness. An arch does not fall in a season; it sheds a single brick every forty years. The brick drops through the canopy, hits a flagstone, shatters, and is immediately overrun by moss and the small clawed feet of rodents that use the fallen rubble as a ramp to reach the lower hollows of the wall. 

In the gloom, you feel the mass of it all: the tonnage of fired clay holding back the bulk of the high ridges, wrapped completely in wet, breathing matter. It is a world made by human sweat that has slipped back into the animal kingdom without losing a single line of its artificial geometry—a colossal, warm rookery of brick and leaf, murmuring and thrumming in the dark.
