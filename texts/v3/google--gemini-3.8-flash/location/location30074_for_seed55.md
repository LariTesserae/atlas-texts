The water is black and heavy, held at an unvarying temperature just a degree or two above freezing. There is no surface in sight, nor any memory of one; if there is an atmosphere above, it is separated from this place by leagues of cold, unyielding pressure. 

Every surface here was shaped by human hands, or by hands that worked with the same blunt physics. There is no bedrock, no natural river silt, no volcanic ridge. The sea floor is an endless expanse of fitted stone—monolithic slabs of dark, coarse-grained basalt and dense slate, laid out in terraces, stepped ramps, and vast, sunken plazas that stretch uninterrupted for hundreds of leagues. The joints between the stones are tight, dry-stacked without mortar, but the corners have long ceased to be sharp. Uncountable centuries of slow, thick water have smoothed the margins, rubbing the edges of five-ton blocks into soft, rounded loaves. Where two stones meet, there is now a gentle, dished groove filled with a fine, compacted mineral sludge that has never seen a wave.

Despite the near-total dark, the stonework is smothered in growth. It is not coral or animal reef, but an immense, cold-water flora that requires no sun. A dense pelt of hair-like water-moss covers every flat expanse, rooted directly into the minute pitting of the stone. From the seams between the slabs rise towering, jointed reeds the thickness of a wrist, pale as lard and rubbery to the touch, their hollow stems rising straight toward the upper dark before arching under their own weight. Beneath them, carpet-weed and thick, leathery fronds of liverwort spread in overlapping layers, several feet deep, turning the terraces into soft, yielding pastures. If disturbed, these plants release no scent and very little debris; their tissues are tough, fibrous, and saturated with the same mineral-bitter brine that surrounds them. 

Nothing swims here. There are no fish to brush through the reeds, no mollusks clamped to the stone, no worms burrowing in the sediment. The water is totally devoid of animal motion. The vegetation lives on whatever slow drift of dissolved salts and iron leaches upward through the paving, sustained by a chemistry that does not depend on sunlight or decay. Because there are no grazers, the plants grow until their sheer bulk pulls them from their anchors, slumping down to form thick, peat-like mats that compress into the lower terraces, slowly petrifying beneath the water’s weight.

The architecture itself is vast and plain. There are no carvings, no inscriptions, no columns turned on a lathe. Everything was raised with levers, rollers, and wedges. The walls are cyclopean retaining dikes that step down across continental gradients, built to hold back nothing more than the water that already fills them. Broad causeways, forty yards wide, run arrow-straight for days of travel before terminating in blank, megalithic platforms that look like foundations for towers that were never built, or perhaps bases for structures that were dismantled before the water arrived. 

The light is negligible—a dull, ambient gray-black that carries no direction, barely enough to outline the shape of an outstretched hand against the paler stalks of the reeds. It does not fluctuate. There are no tides here, only a deep, planetary drift that sets the forest leaning southward for decades, and then, with glacial hesitation, rights it and leans it back toward the north.

Sound moves through the density with peculiar clarity, yet there is almost nothing to make it. There is the muffled, subterranean thud of a jointed stem snapping under its own bulk miles away, a sound that arrives not through the ear but through the chest. Occasionally, a deep, hydraulic creak echoes along the stone floor as a thousand square miles of pavement settle a fraction of an inch into the forgotten hollows beneath them. Then the stillness closes back over the terraces, cold and complete, and the pale weed continues its silent, blind expansion across the ancient stones.
