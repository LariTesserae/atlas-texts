At eleven thousand feet, the atmosphere should be frigid, but here the rock breathes a heavy, mineral fever. The caldera-ridge of the Scarp spans thirty miles of open, jagged skyline—a vast serration of basalt plugs and ancient basaltic steps, weathered over millenia into soft-shouldered saddles and sheer, fluted columns. The air is razor-thin, yet it sits like a hot wet cloth over the mouth. 

The heat is dual-sourced: the unobstructed glare of a pale, high-altitude sun burning through the sparse stratosphere, and the steady, sub-crustal furnace below. The ground is dry to the touch in the open, but the fractures are dripping. Steam boils out of hairline cracks in the dark rock, strikes the colder upper drafts, and immediately collapses into a hot, slick dew. It coats the vertical faces in a continuous, greasy sheen of condensed moisture that gathers in narrow runnels, nourishing black crusts of endolithic lichens and little else in the way of green. What vegetation exists is stunted, wiry, and rare—brittle tufts of sulfur-yellow sedge wedged into the dead spaces between stones, adapted to boiling roots and searing wind.

Yet the terrain is overwhelmingly full. Where plant life has failed to claim the rock, animal bodies have filled every available square foot. 

The basalt shelves are saturated with life, packed shoulder-to-shoulder with thousands of heavy, slate-backed lithophages and heat-seeking reptiles the size of river otters. They overlap like wet shingles on the vertical cliff faces, their scaled bellies pressed flat against the stone to absorb the geothermal warmth, their serrated mouths scraping the bacterial slime that blooms in the condensation trails. Higher up, on the razor-sharp cornices, colonies of crest-necked shear-vultures roost in jammed, motionless tiers. Millions of brittle, carapaced tick-wasps crawl over their plumage, cleaning them in total, frantic silence. The density is claustrophobic; there is barely room to place a boot without disturbing a cold eye, a shifting shell, or a tail twitching away from a scald vent.

Despite the sheer mass of living things, the ridge is extraordinarily quiet. The low air density robs sounds of their resonance. The skittering of claws over basalt sounds paper-thin, like dry leaves tumbling down stairs. Steam vents vent not with a roar, but with a smothered, wheezing sigh. When a predator—a lean, red-necked caracal-lizard—slips out from an undercut to snap a roosting chick from a terrace, the colony doesn't shriek; they simply shift a few inches to the side in a silent, fluid wave, closing the gap like grease filling a pan.

Interspersed across this biological congestion are the rusted, heavy-boned skeletons of industrial machinery. They are not cities or settlements, but scattered utilitarian installations: automated geothermal pump-stations, bolted directly into the granite ribs of the mountains with twelve-inch steel pins. Thick, insulated piping—encrusted with white salt deposits and sulfur rime—snakes along the precipices, disappearing into boreholes that plunge miles straight down. Overhead, thick steel haulage cables hang slack between pylons, carrying iron-mesh skips that haven't moved in a century, now used as high-rise nesting platforms by the largest raptors. 

Occasionally, an automated valve deep inside an iron shed will trip with a muted, metallic *clonk*, venting a jet of scalding gray vapor into an intake baffle. The nearby lizards don't startle; they merely lean into the new plume, their black tongues flicking to taste the iron-rich mist. 

Predators work the terraces along fixed schedules dictated by the sun and the vent pulses. When the high sun reaches its noon zenith, the rock faces heat past the tolerance of even the scaled dwellers, triggering a mass, sliding migration toward the shaded, dripping fissures. The mountain seems to liquefy as hundreds of thousands of bodies shift downward at once, a silent cascade of shell and scale seeking the moisture-slick pockets beneath the cliffs, where water pools in stone bowls just inches deep before evaporating back into the blinding sky. Above them, the machinery rusts in silence, humming at a frequency so low it is felt only as a faint, rhythmic tremor in the rock.
