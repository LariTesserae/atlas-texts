The basin is shaped like a fresh cleaver-strike in the slate: a sunken terrace no wider than a four-track rail depot, its perimeter bounded by sheer, flat-faced rock walls that have not yet had time to shed their quarrying tooth-marks. The rock is unsoftened. Where the drills drove down, vertical half-cylinders still score the stone in crisp, geometric flutes. There are no scree slopes here, no tumbled ramps of time; only ninety-degree corners where the pale grey floor meets the vertical dark of the cut.

Above, the sky is flat, high, and brightly lit, dropping a clean, shadowless daylight straight into the trench. The air stays stubbornly cool, held in the pocket of the rock like cider in a cellar. It smells of ozone, cold grease, and bruised celandine.

Water does not gather in pools, but it is everywhere in transit. It weeps from thin fissures along the lower three feet of the rock face, sheet-sheened and clear, slipping into open concrete culverts that border the tracks. In the corners behind the compressor houses, condensation runs in thick beads down corrugated galvanized walls, dripping with a steady tap against the gravel. When the air shifts, it carries the cold vapor of the pneumatic exhausts—a fine, dry mist that vanishes before it can puddle.

Yet the floor of the cut is unexpectedly, stubbornly lush. Between the widely spaced brick sub-stations and the iron turning-pads, the earth has been seized by an intense, low-standing green. Fat-leafed dock, horse-tail, and thick, rubbery ferns shoulder their way through the rail-ballast. Wherever a cable tray runs along the ground, wild bindweed has braided itself around the lead sheathing, its pale trumpets open to the high daylight. The vegetation does not look wild so much as opportunistic and robust, fattened by the lime in the concrete dust and the endless draft of damp air pushed out by the ventilation grates.

The layout is civic in its order, but sparse. A single brick pump house with round-arched windows stands fifty yards from a high steel gantry; between them lies nothing but raked shale, a pair of rusted narrow-gauge rails, and three solitary transformer masts whose ceramic bells gleam white against the slate. It is an industrial ward built with room to spare, every machine granted its own perimeter of silence that it fails to keep.

The noise inside the basin is immense, trapped and magnified by the sheer stone perimeter. Two two-stroke stationary diesels, housed in an open-sided shed, turn enormous iron flywheels with a slow, concussive *thung-clack* that rattles the marrow of the jaw. Belt-drives forty feet long slap through the air with a rhythmic, wet whipping sound. Above this mechanical bass rises a shrill, manic living treble: thousands of cliff swallows have nested under the flange of the steel gantries, their chatter boiling out into the cold air alongside the hum of three-phase current. Heavy green ground-beetles, broad as pocket watches, crawl openly across the concrete footings, clicking their mandibles against the gravel.

Life here has adapted instantly to the metal. Weasels run the hollow lengths of the pipe-trays, hunting the fat voles that nest inside discarded coils of hemp rope. On the warm housings of the electric dynamos, yellow-striped snails cluster in tight rings, feeding on the film of grease and pulverized spores thrown off by the drive belts.

Nothing here moves quickly. The industrial processes have the patient, indifferent pace of municipal works: an iron arm rises once every four minutes to tip a counterweight; a siphon gurgles in the drainage sluice on an hour-long cycle; the creeping ivy advances down the telegraph guy-wires fraction by fraction. The basin is an engine of low urgency, brightly illuminated, walled in by raw cut stone that still smells of dynamite, where the green world and the iron world have met without hostility, settling into a loud, cool, and permanent truce.
