The stone here has never learned how to be old. It rises into the thin, cool upper sky not as weathered crags, but as freshly cleaved facets of deep slate and cold ironstone, so newly thrust out of the zero below that its edges remain glassy, razor-sharp, and unplaned. There is no talus at the foot of these spires because gravity has not yet had time to shatter them into scree; there are no rounded shoulders, no soil paths. The geology is raw, embryonic, still drying from whatever violent conceptual bath produced it, smelling faintly of struck flint and ozone.

Yet clinging to these colossal, unfinished horns of earth is a city that feels as though it was designed by someone who mistook an ironworks for a cathedral, and then forgot where the floor was meant to be.

The city does not sit on the mountains; it clamps them. Enormous trusses of black-painted pig iron, weeping orange tears of flash-rust, bite into the unweathered rock with pneumatic teeth. Spanning the gulfs between spires—chasms so sheer and sheerly dropped that the bottom is lost in a purple-black well—are tiered viaducts carrying heavy hydraulic lines, steam conduits lagged in dripping horsehair, and the narrow gauge tracks of ore-tenders. The density is strange: the structures themselves are massive, fully realized instances of civic and industrial pride, with brick pumping stations five stories tall and multi-tier blast furnace complexes, but they are flung thousands of yards apart across the high void. Between them lies only the damp wind, the cold, and the terrifying nakedness of uncarved verticality.

The light never properly arrives. It is caught somewhere between dawn and a permanent eclipse, a dense, slate-colored twilight that seeps through low-hanging bank clouds. The dampness does not fall as rain; it condensates against the cold metal and the virgin rock, beading on rivets, slicking the handrails, gathering in oily troughs along the iron gutters until it spills over into freefall, breaking into fine, luminous spray before it has dropped fifty feet.

Despite the cold and the height, the place is unnervingly verdant. Wherever steam vents vent their warm, sulfurous breath against the newly born cliffs, vegetation erupts with an aggressive, opportunistic hunger. Thick cushions of liverwort and glistening velvet mosses mantle the brickwork and the iron flanges, their fronds swollen with moisture. Pale, broad-leafed alpine ferns push out from between the iron brackets, dripping steadily, their root-tendrils finding purchase not in dirt, but directly adhering to the crystalline micro-textures of the unweathered stone. In the broad shadow of a suspension tower, an entire suspended terrace has been swallowed by hanging curtains of waxy, blue-green ivy that drinks the soot from the chimneys and grows fat upon it.

It is here that the nature of the space begins to fail its own logic.

The industrial grid is rational—built with slide rules, rivets, and heavy boilers—yet the world it inhabits has begun to drift off its axis. In certain reaches between the spires, smoke from the lower furnaces does not rise; it falls slowly, like heavy gray dough, pooling in mid-air and forming temporary, buoyant islands upon which ferns take root without any stone beneath them. Workers on the high gantry lines secure themselves with two lanyards: one for the earth’s pull, and one for the lateral tides of mass that pulse outward from the newer peaks as they swell upward. Some mornings, a crane arm that points due north will cast a shadow that curves at an impossible right angle, bending around an invisible corner in the air. Time, too, has a mechanical drag here; a piston stroke can take three seconds to fall and twenty minutes to recover its stroke, the steam sighing through its valves with a long, drawn-out groan that rattles the teeth in your gums.

The sound is immense, though it moves slowly through the chilled air. It is not an echo, because the rock has no texture to scatter it; sound strikes the virgin basalt and bounces back flat, loud, and intact. There is the steady, rhythmic *tchunk-clank* of steam-hammers working iron thousands of yards away, sounding as clear as if they were behind a thin partition. There is the endless hiss of pressurized condensate escaping copper fittings, the dry hum of high-voltage transmission cables vibrating in the downdrafts, and beneath it all, the deep, groaning shear of the earth itself—the mountains physically rising by millimeters every hour, their crystalline bonds groaning against the iron stays that pin them to the sky.

Life swarms through these rifts with a total disregard for the vertigo. The damp ledges and the ironwork are alive with animals. Flocks of oil-black swifts with stiff, comb-like flight feathers roost by the thousands in the cooling jackets of the idle turbines, their shrill, mechanical chittering cutting through the machinery’s hum. Along the undersides of the steel bridges, dog-sized lizards with pale, suction-cupped pads crawl upside down, hunting the grease-beetles that feast on tallow-lubricated gears. Farther out, drifting along the calm, lateral winds that blow between the peaks, immense mountain rays—creatures made of leathery skin and dense, muscle-ribbed bladders of marsh-gas—glide through the dimness. They browse on the moss that covers the telegraph poles, undisturbed by the sparks that crackle across the porcelain insulators, their slow, undulating wings leaving pale wakes in the hanging mist.

Down in the lower tiers of the city, where the brick foundations disappear into the unformed rock, men in oilskin coats move with the deliberate, heavy gait of divers. They carry grease-guns and carbon-arc lamps that hiss and flicker in the damp draft, illuminating the wet, black sides of stone that has never seen an insect, never felt a drop of water until the city was bolted to it. They check the tension gauges on the tie-rods. They listen to the stone. 

The earth here is not finishing its work; it is only just considering what it might become, and the iron city is there to ensure it does not change its mind too quickly. Everything moves at the speed of cooling slag, deliberate and massive, while the swifts scream through the gaps and the water gathers, drops, and vanishes into the dark below.
