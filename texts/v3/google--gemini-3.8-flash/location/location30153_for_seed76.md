The air at this altitude does not hold moisture; it holds light. 

It is a high, plateau-broken world whose floor lies miles above any planetary sea level, perched in a thin, radiant troposphere where the sun is a sharp, white puncture in a sky of hard violet. The light is so fierce and unfiltered that shadows do not merely darken the ground—they cast precise, ink-black geometries across the terraces, cooling the mineral dust within them by twenty degrees in an instant.

There is no rain here, nor has there been for ages that could be measured by the strata. Yet the high plains are far from bare. Across expanses that roll outward toward horizons curved by sheer scale, life has abandoned the need for water, organizing itself instead around a chemistry of cool, volatile hydrocarbons and heavy atmospheric gases. 

The vegetation grows in expansive, deliberate islands—dense, sprawling cloisters separated by miles of wind-polished slate and dry anhydrite gravel. These are not stunted desert shrubs, but an opulent, heavy-limbed flora. Low canopies of *vitreous cedar* spread horizontally, their trunks composed of layered, fibrous silicates as dense as horn, cold to the touch. Their foliage consists of paper-thin, triangular scales of translucent amber and smoky quartz that rattle with a dry, papery clatter when the pressure-tides sweep the shelf. Beneath these canopies, where the glare of the white sun is tempered to a tea-colored dimness, grow thickets of sprawling, bladder-leaved succulents. These plants do not store sap; their interior chambers are filled with pressurized, liquid carbon dioxide and cold aromatic ethers, sustained by deep taproots that bore through hundreds of feet of fractured limestone into the planet’s frigid, unquenched mantle.

The topography is immense and finished. It does not look newly violently broken; its cliffs and terraced shelves have been filed down by two billion years of dry, laminar gales. Colossal flat-topped massifs rise from the plains like the hulls of stranded armadas, their flanks fluted into regular, organ-pipe columns by the endless rubbing of airborne dust. The dust itself is not soil—it is talc and powdered corundum, white as bone and so dry it behaves like fluid, pooling in zero-angle drifts across the passes. 

Despite the lack of water, there is weather—complex, rhythmic, and active. It is a weather of temperature differentials and gas densities. 

Twice in every local diurnal cycle, as the cold floor of the high shelves warms beneath the blinding white star, colossal waves of chilled nitrogen pour down from the jagged peaks that rim the northern edge of the continent. They do not bring fog or dew. They arrive as clean, silent atmospheric walls—visible only by the refraction of distant mountain ribs through the sudden change in air density. As these fronts strike the amber forests, the groves wake into motion. The plants respond to the plummeting barometric pressure by shifting their postures: canopies of quartz-scale leaves tilt in unison toward the sky, pivoting on fibrous ball-joints to present their broad surfaces to the descending chill, radiating the heat they gathered during the noon glare into the black void above. 

A sound accompanies this, though it is so faint that an unaccustomed ear might mistake it for tinnitus: a dry, vast sibilance, like millions of deck-cards being shuffled across a velvet table. It is the friction of the leaves, moving dry against dry, stem against stem. 

Fauna exists here, but it is solitary and sparse, scaled to the enormous intervals between the groves. High above the shelves, sailing on the thermal interface between the cold lower gas and the thin, superheated upper strata, drift creatures shaped like wide, flat keels. They have no wings that beat; their skeletons are honeycombed cartilage, light as dried reed-pith, lined with dark pigments that absorb the blinding solar radiation to warm the gas pockets within their hides. They drift for days without altering their headings, descending only when the shadow of an isolated grove offers a pocket of heavy, aromatic gas rising from the bladders of the floor-succulents. They feed by filtering the dry airborne spores that drift constantly off the canopies—invisible, microscopic grains of vitreous silica that float on the air currents like diamond dust.

On the ground, motion is measured. A solitary cursorial herbivore, jointed like a heavy mantis, with an exoskeleton of bleached bone-white plates, moves from one island of amber-trees to the next. It covers thirty miles of bare, white gravel in a single afternoon, its six padded, splayed feet making no more sound than the dropping of dry beans onto felt. It pauses at the wind-lip of a terrace, standing three times higher than a horse, absolutely motionless for an hour. It watches the light change. It has no lungs, breathing through long, porous slits along its flanks that flutter faintly as the cool wind purges them of dust.

As the sun lowers, it does not turn red; there is not enough moisture in the atmosphere to scatter the long wavelengths. Instead, the white point shrinks to an unbearable diamond, then drops instantly behind the horizon of a tableland two hundred miles away.

Night does not bring humidity or frost. It brings an absolute, mineral cold that contracts the stones. Then, across the vast plain, the rocks begin to speak—tiny, sharp, metallic clicks that travel through the bedrock as the cliffs lose their daytime expansion. In the amber groves, the leaves fold down tightly, locking edge-over-edge into continuous sheets of smooth stone, sealing each tree into a dome of solid shell to wait out the black wind. Between these islands of sleeping glass and fiber, the plains stretch out under an ocean of stars that do not twinkle, unbroken by any light made by hands, completely self-contained, settled, and indifferent.
