Nothing here can be seen by the eye; the darkness is total, uncompromised by star, moon, or fire. To exist in this highland is to experience space entirely through skin, lungs, and ear.

It is high—thousands of meters above the planetary datum, resting on an uplift of ancient, deeply rounded basalt that stretches for hundreds of leagues. Despite the altitude, it is not cold. The air is warm, thick, and swampy, trapped beneath an impenetrable, ceiling-like thermal inversion that locks in the exhaust of the world below. The atmosphere is perpetually at its dew point. Warm water sheets down every vertical surface; it sweats from the faces of gargantuan, water-softened cliffs, drips in heavy, rhythmic cadences from unseen overhangs, and pools in wide, shallow peat-pans that gurgle with slow methane belches.

Without sunlight, the lushness has found other currencies. The vegetation is dense to the point of suffocation, but it contains no chlorophyll. Beneath your hands, the foliage feels like oiled leather, chilled lard, or soaked velvet. Enormous, rib-thick tendrils—calcified roots that function like vascular plumbing—twist over the eroded terraces of stone, anchoring vast hanging gardens of chemosynthetic ferns and rubbery, sprawling lichens that feed directly on the heavy sulfur and iron salts carried in the mist. These plants do not reach upward; they reach outward, spreading flat across the ground and draping down chasms, maximizing their surface area to catch the condensation that falls in continuous, warm sheets.

And it is deafening. The soundscape does not rest.

First is the water: an omnipresent, multi-tonal roar. It is the hiss of billions of droplets striking broad leaves, the low bass rumble of subterranean flumes carving out smooth, potholed channels through the black rock, and the sudden, thunderous slumps of mud-saturated root-mats peeling off cliffs into the void.

Then there is the industry. It is scattered across this high shelf—not as a human city, but as an ancient, autonomous infrastructure whose origin is lost to the geology. Miles apart through the wet tangle, massive steel installations crouch in the blackness like tectonic organs. You stumble into their presence first by the heat, then by the vibration: a seismic 60-cycle hum that vibrates the teeth in your jaw. These are enormous, iron-clad pump-houses, blind dynamo-stations, and hydraulic siphons three stories tall, bolted directly into the basalt with rivets the size of dinner plates. Their iron is black, slick with wet rust and furred with iron-eating slime molds. Piston shafts as thick as tree trunks cycle in the dark with massive, wet pneumatic strokes—*ch-clank, sigh, thud*—driving unseen subterranean pressures through buried pipelines that groan underfoot. High above, thick insulated cables, sagging under the weight of wet moss, span between invisible pylons, buzzing with high-voltage current so intense it ionizes the warm fog, producing a sharp, metallic smell of ozone and wet copper.

The wildlife has integrated itself into this mechanized geology. The darkness belongs to things of vast vibrational sensitivity. There are no eyes here; even the vestigial sockets have smoothed over into sensory pits and acoustic domes.

Creatures the size of cattle, with hide like waterlogged wood, graze upon the thick bacterial slime that coats the cooling fins of the dynamos. They move on six or eight splayed, pad-like limbs, their claws worn dull against the basalt, communicating not with cries, but by stamping their sternums against the bedrock—hollow, resonant thumps that carry for miles through the stone to map the terrain and find mates. Overhead, through the dense layers of blind, hanging vegetation, things glide: broad, flat, leathery organisms that orient themselves along the electromagnetic fields of the power lines. They swoop through the dark on silent, muscular wings, dropping onto the fat, blind gastropods that crawl along the warm, vibrating iron conduits.

The water moves constantly through its cycle. The highland is so vast that it generates its own interior weather in the black: warm vapor rises from the engine casings and geothermal fissures, gathers into low-hanging, turbulent masses among the dripping tree-analogues, and dumps itself back down as torrential, tepid squalls. 

Everything is weathered, rounded, and long-settled. The machines do not feel like an intrusion; they have run for so many centuries that the rock has curved to fit their foundations, and the roots have grown around their intake grates, forming living strainers. When a valve vents—a sudden, screaming blast of superheated steam that cuts through the fog like a physical blow—the local fauna do not scatter in panic. Instead, they converge toward it, clustering around the sudden plume of warmth and moisture, jostling blindly against each other in the wet, roaring dark.

You move here entirely by touch and pressure. The air pushes against you, thick with the spray of invisible waterfalls and the oily grease of working engines. Every handhold is slick: either the cold, fibrous pulp of wet lichen, or the hot, trembling flank of an iron casing weeping condensation into the mud. You are suspended on the high roof of a world that does not know dawn, caught between the blind hunger of an ancient, warm ecology and the tireless, mechanical pulse of machines that produce nothing but noise, heat, and water.
