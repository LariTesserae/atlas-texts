The plateau sits three thousand meters above the plains, raw and newly hoisted against the sky. Geologically, it has barely begun: the terraced fault-blocks of dark, fine-grained basalt have razor-sheared margins and unweathered fracture faces that have not yet had time to round, crumble, or gather talus. They are fresh steps of planetary crust, clean-edged as quarried slate. 

Yet they are entirely buried in plant matter. 

A continuous, heavy fleece of vegetation covers every flat bench and drapes over the vertical joints. It is not a forest, for trees cannot keep their footing in the wind, but a compressed, monolithic blanket of mosses, vascular cushion-plants, liverworts, and tough, fibrous alpine mats that have formed a self-contained substrate over the rock. In places, this living peat is two meters thick—spongy, springy, saturated to the point of liquefaction. To step onto it is to sink shin-deep into cold, tea-colored water that wells instantly through the weave of dwarf sedges. The greenery is vivid, but pale: lime-greens, cold celadons, and rusty ochres that take on an almost metallic sheen beneath the sky.

The light up here is searing. At this altitude, stripped of dust and sea-level haze, the sun strikes with unfiltered, optical violence. Every pool of standing water, every drenched blade of alpine grass, and every weeping vertical face of rock acts as a flat mirror. The brilliance is nearly painful, despite the chill in the air—a thin, biting cold that smells of iron, clean cold water, and the faint, sweet decay of compressed moss.

What dominates the plateau, obliterating all thought, is the sound. It is a continuous, concussive, deafening roar. 

It carries no rhythm of surf or squall; it is the uniform, mechanical thunder of massive, scattered industrial infrastructure operating at peak hydrostatic load. Miles apart from one another, immense run-of-the-river intake heads and low-head hydro-pneumatic pump stations are anchored straight into the basalt shelves. Built from heavy, unpainted plate steel, coated in wet verdigris and red lead, their housing walls vibrate visibly against the rock. Water—drawn from the high snowfields above and squeezed through deep, raw-cut bedrock trenches—is funneled through gigantic bypass siphons and open-chute discharge gates. 

Where the water shoots free from the relief valves, it does so in solid, blindingly white jets the thickness of locomotive boilers, striking the stepped basalt with an impact that shakes the soles of your feet through two feet of peat. The roar is so absolute and constant that it ceases to be heard as a distinct noise and becomes the medium through which the landscape is felt: a low, gut-churning hum and a high, sizzling hiss of pulverized spray that drifts for thousands of yards downwind.

Between these monumental, lonely machines, there is vast, empty space. A single service gantry on rusted lattice pylons might trace its way across three miles of soggy green plateau, carrying an insulated steam line or high-voltage conduit, before vanishing into a concrete abutment. There are no roads, only the occasional catwalk of open steel grating laid directly atop the moss, its diamond-mesh pattern long overgrown by creeping, succulent stems. No operators are visible. A small corrugated-iron control shack, its roof pinned down with river stones, perches near an overflow weir, its windows opaque with condensed moisture, unattended for seasons.

The water moves everywhere. It is not confined to the concrete flumes; it weeps from every seam in the basalt, seeps out of the cushion-plants, and sheets over the flats in braided, centimeter-deep films that run silent until they drop off a shelf to join the bellowing industrial spillways below. 

Life here belongs almost entirely to the flora. Animal presence is virtually nonexistent—rarely, a solitary dark-winged stonefly crawls over a warm bolt-head on an engine casing, or a pale, grease-legged wading bird pauses at the edge of an intake screen before being driven off by the wind. There are no fish in the cold, hyper-aerated runnels, only long ribbons of green filamentous algae that whip frantically in the current.

Everything moves on an enormous, patient scale. The raw cliffs have not begun their erosion; the steel casings are thickening with undisturbed skins of rust; the great cushions of moss add a millimeter of damp fiber each short summer. Above it all, the bright mountain light pours through thin air, and the machines roar into the wet silence, completely indifferent to the living mat that is slowly, relentlessly climbing their sides.
