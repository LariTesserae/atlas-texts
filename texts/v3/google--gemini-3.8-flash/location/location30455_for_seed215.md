The plateau does not look like something that time made. Its edges are sheer, cleaved with an accuracy that leaves miles of planar basalt standing vertically against the cloud floor below, unmarred by the scree slopes or alluvial fans that centuries of frost-wedging would normally accumulate. It is raw, extruded earth—rock that cooled only recently in the geologic sense, perhaps drawn up from the mantle by the silent, localized gravitational sinks whose anchor-spires still rise at fifty-kilometer intervals like silver needles piercing the overcast.

Up here, four thousand meters above the oceanic basins, the air is cold enough to keep breath visible and skin tight, lingering just above freezing. Yet it is saturated with water. The clouds do not merely hang overhead; they drag their bellies across the stone. Moisture condenses in steady, heavy beads upon every surface: on the un-weathered obsidian ridges, on the flanks of the megalithic pylons, and in the coats of the animals that move through the gaps.

Water runs everywhere, but it has not yet learned to make rivers. It sheets across mirror-flat shelves of dark stone, pools in the shallow basins left by tectonic cooling, and weeps down the faces of stepped terrace-cities that stretch across the horizon. These settlements are immense in footprint, organized along strict geometric networks of subterranean logistics and elevated transit spines, yet they feel sparsely populated by bodies. They are built of smooth, semi-translucent composite materials that seem to sweat cold mist back into the atmosphere, functioning more like synthetic mountain roots than traditional streetscapes. One might walk for an hour along a broad, empty concourse overlooking a three-hundred-meter drop before passing a low-resonance transit conduit or an automated intake valve humming at a pitch felt only in the jaw.

Because the land is newly surfaced, soil has not yet gathered. The vegetation is consequently thin—a pioneer ecology of black lichens, fast-growing silver liverworts, and pale, fibrous mosses that glue themselves into the stress-fractures of the rock. They don't form a carpet so much as a faint greenish-grey tint along the drainage cracks.

Yet the plateau is alive with motion.

Where the plants are hesitant, fauna has rushed in with astonishing vigor. Animals thrive in the humid, cold vastness, taking advantage of the geothermal vents and atmospheric scrubbers built into the foundations of the urban ribbons. Massive, long-legged ungulates with thick, oiled pelts that shed the mist traverse the basalt flats in loose herds of hundreds, their broad, split hooves clicking softly against the unscarred rock. They graze on the dense blooms of nutrient-rich biofilm that coat the warm exhaust grates of the subterranean works.

Above them, birds—or things that fill their niche, broad-winged and heavy-bodied, adapted to the dense, moisture-laden air—ride the updrafts generated by the city heat-diffusers. They wheel in total silence, dropping suddenly into the clefts between high-rise conduits to hunt rock-mice that nest in the dry fiber insulation of the structural joints. There is a continuous, low-key churn of life: small amphibious things slipping through the shallow sheet-wash, cold-blooded and sluggish until they find a conduit carrying warm coolant, where they pile atop one another in tangled heaps.

The soundscape is remarkably muffled. The moisture acts as a baffle, swallowing sound within a few hundred yards. There is no roar of combustion engines, no city clamor; the technological substrate of this place operates on field-manipulation and solid-state energy transfers that make no more noise than a falling drop of water. The dominant sound is that drop: water dripping from the underbelly of a cantilevered civic deck into a still, clear cistern; the soft *whuff* of a grazer’s breath; the wind scouring the sharp, pristine corners of architecture designed to stand for ten thousand years without maintenance.

Light here is honest, if slightly dimmed by the low ceilings of stratus. When the clouds tear open, which happens rapidly as the thermal currents of the vast infrastructure churn the lower sky, the sun hits the wet obsidian and composite towers with a clean, blinding glare. For twenty minutes, the entire continent-shelf gleams like wet iron and poured glass, crosshatched by the long, thin shadows of pylons that climb into the upper stratosphere. Then the vapor reknits, the temperature drops three degrees in a breath, and the mist returns to weeping quietly off the eaves of an empty, newborn world.
