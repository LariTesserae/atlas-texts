The trench opened cleanly, like cut beef, leaving the bedrock with the sharp, unground edges of a break that has not yet seen rain enough to rub off a single burr. It lies sixty paces below the flat horizon of the surrounding scrub plains, a sunken rift nearly a league long and three bowshots wide, its floor paved with clean, unweathered limestone that fractures in long rhomboids under the weight of anything that moves.

Here, out of the wind that scours the upper rim, a town has been squeezed into the split. 

There are no streets in the customary sense—only the gaps left between things too heavy to move. The settlement occupies nearly every square cubit of the floor and climbs the terraced cleavage planes of the rock walls in dense, stepped stacks. The construction is entirely manual, built from what the break yielded: rough-quarried blocks stacked dry without mortar, braced by massive baulks of green larch brought down from the rims by simple timber gin-poles and hemp tackles. The roofs are flat slabs of gray slate or bundles of bundled reed-grass lashed down with split osier, so tightly fitted that one dwelling’s ceiling serves as the threshing floor or timber yard of the next. You can walk across half the town without touching the bedrock, moving across an undulating roofscape of drying hides, salt-piles, and stacked laths.

It is cool down in the cut, several degrees lower than the dry plain above. Even at midday, when the sun reaches straight between the canyon lips and floods the white stone with clean, unfiltered white light, the air retains a cellar chill. The cold rock sweats. Intermittent sheets of condensation gather along the vertical shear faces, beading along the hairline fissures of the fault until they let go, slipping down the smooth stone in thin, silent runnels. The inhabitants catch this water before it reaches the floor; every wall-face is hung with vats, carved-out cedar troughs, and split-log gutters that lead into narrow cisterns jammed between the foundations. Aside from these deliberate catchments and the rare, dark puddles gathered in tectonic depressions, the rift is not wet—only continuously, faintly damp, smelling of cold chalk and damp bark.

Plant life has barely found the place. A few gray-green liverworts have smeared themselves into the wet rock-seams near the water-gutters, and here and there along the lip, where dust blows over the rim, wild chicory and pale spiny brome hang over the edge like fringe, starved of soil. Inside the rift itself, there are no trees, no gardens. What wood exists is dead and working—hewn posts, peeled poles, ladder rungs polished dark by grease from palms and bare feet.

Despite the press of thousands of lives packed rib-to-rib, the rift is strangely hushed. The dense, irregular mass of wood, thatch, and hanging wool dampens vibration, eating sound before it can travel twenty strides. Voices carry only as low murmurs through the floorboards. The work of the place goes on in a low, continuous rhythm: the muffled *thwack* of wooden mauls driving wedges into split-stone, the rasped breath of hand-saws biting into unseasoned larch, the wet squeak of twisted rope under tension, and the periodic clink of stone chips dropping onto stone. There is no iron foundry here, no steam, no wheelwrights turning iron tires; metal is scarce, kept to the edges of adzes, pick-points, and small knife-blades that are sharpened down to slivers before being reforged.

Movement is constant, but slow and structural. The trench is alive with a quiet, bodily physics: lines of laborers leaning into breast-straps to skid a four-ton lintel across roller-logs; the steady, hand-over-hand raising of reed baskets filled with limestone meal; the settling of timber frames as the green wood cures in the cool air, groaning occasionally as joints bind tighter.

Life other than man is sparse. There are no dogs—the space is too precious and the drop too sheer. Now and then a flock of small, soot-colored swifts drops into the trench from the high sky, sweeping the narrow slot between the canyon walls for gnats before vanishing back up into the open sun. A few blind crickets live in the dark clearances beneath the foundation timbers, where the condensation pools on the stone floor, their bodies pale as lard. 

At dusk, the shadow comes all at once, rising from the floor like cold water. The white chalk faces of the rift turn the color of lead, and the city retreats into itself, its lanterns—hollowed stone cups burning rendered fat—blinking on behind wicker screens and hide hangings, until the trench looks like a deep crack in the world stuffed full of embers.
