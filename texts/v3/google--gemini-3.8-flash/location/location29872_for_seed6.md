The fluid is neither sea nor rain, but forty thousand tons of clear, demineralized process-water held in an elevated pressure-casement five thousand meters above the plains. It is kept at an unvarying thirty-four degrees Celsius. Because the chamber sits in the high thin air of a mountain ridge, the immense atmospheric drop outside is kept at bay by walls of three-inch rolled steel plate; inside, the hydrostatic pressure is steady, warm, and utterly enveloping.

Everything here is made. There is no bedrock, no silt drawn from an ancient river, no pebble that was not turned on an automated lathe. The floor, thirty meters down through the green-tinted transparency, consists of interlocking checker-plate catwalks, copper heat-exchanger banks, and the flanged bells of drainage conduits sealed with wax-impregnated gaskets. Because the installation is new—commissioned perhaps only months ago—the metal retains the clean, uncompromising edges of the milling shop. Zinc galvanic coatings gleam with a faint, crystalline spangle; stamped serial numbers along the web of every I-beam remain sharp enough to raise a blister if scraped against bare skin. There is no rust. There is only a micro-thin sheen of pale machine oil that has lifted off the threaded rods to hang in motionless, prismatic globules near the structural joints.

Through this bath of warm, engineered broth, the vegetation has erupted with mechanical ferocity. 

It does not grow as wild things grow, fighting for a patch of sky; it has been planted with agrarian precision into perforated brass sleeves that run parallel to every steam line. Immense, strap-shaped leaves—rubbery, veinless, and an intense, chlorophyll-saturated jade—rise vertically, tethered to the ironwork by thousands of curled tendrils. They climb the structural cross-bracing like ribbons drawn taut, crowding out the open water until the cathedral-scale vault is divided into narrow, submerged aisles no wider than a man’s shoulders. Elsewhere, thick mats of submerged moss-analogues, bred for continuous photosynthesis under immersion, coat the turbine housings and pump-jack skirts in deep, velvety blankets of yellow-green pile. The moss does not rot the metal; the metal feeds the moss through microscopic sacrificial anodes designed to dissolve into the water as ionic nutrition.

The density is total. There is no line of sight longer than six or seven paces before the eye meets a bulkhead, an armored power conduit, or a wall of overlapping, spade-shaped fronds. Copper pipes four feet in diameter disappear into hedges of submerged fern whose leaves are nearly two inches thick, turgid with water and heavy with internal starch.

Light comes from two sources, balancing each other into an even, shadowless noon. From high overhead, filtered through thick, laminated glass lenses set into the roof of the pressure vessel, the harsh, white glare of high-altitude daylight enters the water, scattered immediately by the dense suspension of tiny, oxygenated micro-bubbles. From below, set into recessed cast-bronze wells behind wire-glass covers, low-frequency industrial floodlights throw a constant, pale-amber wash upward through the foliage. The green of the plants catches both: their upper surfaces appear silvery and cool, their undersides hot, translucent, and yellow.

It is completely soundless, save for what travels through the bone. The water dampens air-borne acoustics entirely, replacing them with a vast, low-frequency mass of mechanical conduction. If you rest a hand against an intake flange, you feel the slow, monumental revolution of a five-meter impeller somewhere deep in the basement of the tower—a deliberate sixty cycles per minute, rhythmic as a dying pulse. Occasionally, a pneumatic purge-valve seated on a steam collector vents with a muffled, dead *thump* that shudders through the fluid, displacing a curtain of broad leaves by an inch or two before they drift back to absolute rest.

There are no animals. Not a snail, not a water-mite, not a single blind pale shrimp. The water was boiled and irradiated before the intake valves were dogged shut, and the spores that seeded this green labyrinth were chemically washed. It is an ecology of only two kingdoms: steel and vascular plant. Without predators, without grazers, without the rot of terrestrial fungi, the leaves do not tear or decay. They simply swell, growing heavier and broader against their copper wire harnesses until they touch the neighboring pipe, then the next, filling every cubic foot of heated brine with silent, unmoving flesh.

Above the water line—if one could reach the ceiling—there is only a foot of dead space filled with moist nitrogen and the rhythmic hiss of condensation dripping from the ceiling rivets back into the pool. But down here, suspended five fathoms below the surface between a bank of pristine, gray-painted transformers and a wall of emerald kelp-leaves that smells faintly of heated tin and cut lettuce, there is only the thick, warm resistance of the water, the unblinking light, and the slow, hydraulic patience of an engine that has forgotten why it was made to grow.
