The plateau lies miles above the lowlands, yet the air here is strangely thick, warm, and laden with moisture, like the breath of a greenhouse balanced on the crest of a mountain range. The light is full and steady—a broad, pale mountain daylight that filters through an invisible ceiling of high, warm vapor, casting clean-edged, luminous shadows that do not drift.

Geologically, the land is newborn. The mountains here did not wear down into gentle shoulders; they broke upward along colossal fault lines, leaving sheer, glass-sharp cliffs of dark slate and basalt that drop in clean vertical planes. The edges of the precipices are so crisp they look sheared by a wire. Everywhere, the rock shows its raw fractures—prismatic faces, split planes, and stepped ledges where the mineral skin is unpitted by age, still bearing the gloss of deep subterranean pressure.

Yet over this sharp-edged world, an immense, orderly settlement was once laid down, extending for miles across the high shelves and rift benches. 

It is an urban landscape executed entirely with the simplest means. Thousands of wide, rectangular terraces step up the incline, their retaining walls built of cyclopean blocks of basalt, split along their natural grain and dry-fitted without mortar. The scale is vast—long ceremonial avenues thirty paces across, sunken drainage canals carved directly into the bed-rock, and low, flat-topped stone compounds that repeat in disciplined grids across the plateau. The craft is entirely manual, born of stone hammers, wedges, and endless abrasion; you can see the pitted tracks of hand-held pounders along the lintels and the rhythmic grooving where logs were rolled to lever slabs into place. There are no arches, no vaulting, no poured lime—only the sheer, overwhelming weight of fitted rock, raised with monumental patience.

Though the settlement is urban in its extent and coordination, it feels empty of clutter. The structures are widely spaced, set apart by great meadows of level ground and broad stone plazas where nothing stands. There is an expansive emptiness between one sector and the next: an avenue will travel straight for half a kilometer across open, spongy turf before reaching the next cluster of low, roofless pavilions.

Water defines every surface. It does not rain—or if it does, it has finished raining so long ago that the wetness has simply become a permanent condition of the matter here. The stone does not dry. Sheets of cold, clear moisture weep constantly from the vertical fracture lines in the cliffs, sliding in silent films over the rock faces to gather in the hand-cut gutters below. The broad stone slabs of the avenues are slick and dark with water that never drains away entirely, resting in sheets millimeters deep that reflect the pale sky like mirrors. 

Where the water pools in the terrace beds, the vegetation has taken hold with quiet ferocity. It is not a jungle, but a dense, low, heavy growth: waxy-leaved mountain laurels, mats of club moss so thick they spring back underfoot like saturated wool, and long curtains of pale green liverwort that cling to the vertical joints of the stone walls. In the courtyards, dark ferns with fronds as thick and leathery as hide grow in tight, symmetrical rosettes. Everything is swollen with water, glossy, vibrant green, and completely unchewed.

There are no animals. 

No flies hover over the standing pools; no lizards dart across the sunlit basalt; no birds cut across the wide, pale gulf of sky between the valley walls. Without fauna, the plateau lacks the small agitations of life. There is no chirping, no scuttling, no cry carried from a distant crag. 

The silence is massive and acoustic. Because the air is heavy and warm, it carries sound with a strange, flat fidelity, but there is almost nothing to make a sound. The only movement is the slow, continuous glide of water finding the lower terraces. It does not churn into white water or roar; the masonry was designed to divide the flow into hundreds of narrow, shallow rills that drop from step to step with a muted, liquid murmur, like the sound of oil trickling over slate. 

The landscape exists in a fixed, immovable equilibrium. There is no sign of decay: the stones have not tumbled, the walls have not bowed, and the moss does not seem to rot, only to layer upon itself in green permanence. The temperature never drops to frost, nor does the sun burn through the vapor to bake the earth dry. It remains as it is: high, drenched, sharply cleft, and silent, an enormous city of hand-hewn stone resting in perpetual, warm moisture under an unblinking sky.
