It sits at an altitude where air would be too thin to hold heat, yet there is no air here. This is an ocean suspended at the roof of the world, a drowned plateau of planetary scale, lifted miles above the lower mantle by ancient, tectonic masonry. 

Everything here was once laid down with intention, though no machine touched it. The substrate is a woven megalith: an endless, sunken lattice of lignified heartwood and braided, petrified vines, joined together before memory by some extinct instinct of cultivation. Millions of years of cold water have smoothed the corners of these gargantuan structures. The lintels and arches, wide as highways and thick as hills, have rounded into cylindrical, bone-like curves. There is no exposed stone or mud anywhere; every inch of the floor, the walls, and the soaring uprights is made of this ancient, hand-tended, timber-masonry, long dead and hardened to the density of iron, over which an unbroken mantle of living vegetation has laid itself.

The water is nearly freezing, clean, and dense. It fills the basin entirely, moving in slow, deafening gyres. Because the water is continuous and trapped within these immense high-altitude troughs, it conducts sound with brutal efficiency. The landscape is intensely loud: a deep, unchanging roar that lives in the chest rather than the ear. It is the friction of millions of gallons of water shearing against the miles-long timber pylons, mixed with the structural groan of the wood-stone flexing under hydrostatic weight, and the rhythmic, percussive snaps of shrimp-sized organisms that cloud the distances.

Despite the vastness of the architecture, the space feels overwhelmingly empty. The scale is vast—a single bay between two ascending pillars could swallow a mountain range—and the structures are set far apart. A traveler would drift through hundreds of meters of barren, open blackness before the prow of another colossal trellis emerged from the gloom.

Light does not survive here. At this elevation, the surface of the water is perpetually covered by a mat of thick, floating peat that rejects the sun; beneath it, all is ink. The only illumination is a rare, faint cyan exudate from the mosses that coat the timber arches. It does not travel far—merely outlining the massive curves of the beams against the cold void.

Where the woven architecture stands, life is clustered thick. The living vegetation is not green, but a dull, pale umber and translucent white, having adapted to feed entirely on the minerals weeping from the ancient wood and the chemical drift of the currents. Carpeting the pylons are leagues of fibrous, hair-like bryophytes that trail ten feet into the flow, shuddering in the draft. Great bladder-kelps with stalks as thick as ancient trees root into the joins of the masonry, reaching upward into the cold dark until their gas-chambers rupture under pressure, holding them rigidly vertical like the pillars of an unfinished temple.

The fauna is everywhere, yet it belongs to the shadows. Blind, white-skinned things with ribbon-like bodies thread continuously through the bryophyte fleece. Vast schools of small, disc-shaped fish with armored, calcified plates drift in open water, turning in unison like thrown coins, their plates clicking against one another to add a metallic rattle to the roaring draft. Deeper in the hollows of the timber frames, where the wood was long ago hollowed out into chambers and halls, dwell larger, motionless predators—great, soft-mouthed salamanders twenty feet long, their skin like boiled leather, anchored by splayed claws into the ancient joinery, waiting for the current to carry schools into their jaws.

Nothing changes here. There are no seasons beneath this depth; the temperature does not vary by a tenth of a degree from century to century. The current never slacks, and it never accelerates. The dead wood does not rot in this oxygen-poor, icy brine; it only smooths further, grain by grain, into an ever-more-perfect hydrodynamic line. The living mosses grow only as fast as the water shears their tips away, holding the ecosystem in an absolute, cold-locked balance. It is a drowned civilization grown from seed, abandoned before history, now settled into its final, eternal weight.
