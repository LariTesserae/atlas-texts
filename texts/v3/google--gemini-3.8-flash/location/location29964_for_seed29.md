The gap is no wider than the spread of two hands, running diagonally through six feet of newly shattered gray granite. 

It is perched eight thousand feet up on the exposed throat of a mountain pass, but inside the seam, none of the high-altitude weather enters. The cold alpine wind sweeps entirely overhead, sheared away by the iron apron of an ore-drop fifty paces down the ridge. What fills this pocket instead is heat: dry, heavy, and motionless, radiating from the bedrock itself. The rock has been warmed to the consistency of a bread oven by the subterranean vent-pipes of the lower reduction mills, whose massive draft-flues run through the mountain’s spine just beneath this ledge.

The granite surfaces are unweathered. The blast that cracked this fault occurred perhaps six months ago; the fracture faces are viciously crisp, showing bright mica leaves, sharp quartz prisms, and edges that would slice through oilcloth. There has been no water here to soften them—no seep, no rain-drip, no condensation. Any moisture carried in on the high, thin air is baked out instantly by the dry stone. The rock dust from the original blast still sits on every shelf like powdered bone, undisturbed except where bodies have dragged through it.

And the bodies are everywhere.

Within this narrow, dark envelope, space is almost entirely occupied. The cleft is packed from floor to ceiling with flattened, soot-brown dermestid beetles and segmented crickets, layered three and four deep over the stone. They do not scurry; they creep with a glacial, deliberate density, their chitinous plates sliding over one another with a dry, rustling friction like dry corn husks shifted by hand. Their legs—stiff, barbed, and specialized for wedging into shear-lines—interlock so tightly that the rock face appears to be clad in an undulating, segmented armor. They are not blind, but their small compound eyes are redundant in the near-total dark; they navigate entirely by the intense thermal gradient and the profound, relentless vibration of the machinery below.

The sound is massive, though muted by the surrounding mass of the mountain. It is not an ambient noise, but a structural state: the deep, cyclical hammer of piston-driven stamp mills, three levels down, pulsing through the solid stone at seventy-two strokes per minute. Every stroke travels through the granite and directly through the bellies of the insects, a rhythmic, bone-jarring impact that rattles the fine quartz shards in their sockets. Above this low industrial thrum sits the constant whine of an exhaust impeller—the mechanical wind of the vent-line—singing through the micro-fractures like a distant siren.

Air inside the seam smells of parched feldspar, friction-burned grease that has traveled upward through the vents as dry blue vapor, and the faint, sweet, alkaline stench of millions of shed larval husks. These husks, papery and completely desolated of moisture, fill the lower wedges of the crack in drift-lines an inch thick. They never rot. They simply break down under the weight of crawling generations into an organic meal that settles between the sharp points of the gravel.

There is no green life. Nothing germinates in this temperature. The ecology is sustained entirely by what the mountain’s mechanical works shed upward: oil-soot that settles on the lips of the crevice, dried flakes of hide from the heavy conveyor lacing, and the desiccated carcasses of wind-borne moths blown down off the snowfields and killed by the updraft’s radiant heat. When an errant body falls into the lower gap, it is not torn apart in a frenzy; it is covered slowly, methodically, by a dozen heavy brown carapaces that absorb it into their mass through sheer, packed competition.

Light reaches only the outer lip—a dull, leaden smear of high-altitude noon that filters down perhaps four inches before dying on the shoulders of the outer-most insects. Beyond that, the pocket is pitch. 

In this dark, the cycle of the place moves with the heavy, unhurried cadence of heavy industry. When the stamp mills throttle down at the turn of the shift, the vibration eases, and the packed carpet of insects settles slightly into the stone’s recesses, their carapaces cooling by half a degree. When the night-boilers take the head of steam and the long drive-shafts begin their low, oscillating growl, the cleft warms again, and the million small, jointed limbs resume their slow, blind displacement across the unhewn stone.
