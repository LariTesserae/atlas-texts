High on the plateau of Mount Vraun—a landform so worn down by a billion years of wind that it resembles less a summit and more a vast, sun-baked knuckle of ancient quartzite—there is an oblong depression no larger than a ballroom. It sits perched at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, yet the cup itself is warm, cupped like bathwater in the stone. 

The warmth does not come from volcanic vents, but from the relics buried in the silt. Long ago, things engineered under principles that modern mechanics cannot chart were left to sleep here. Three upright pylons of a material that resembles curdled milk or pale, matte jade stand at unequal distances across the hollow. They have no seams, no fasteners, and their surfaces feel neither hot nor cold to the touch, yet the air within three yards of them shivers with an unyielding, silent thermal gradient. They keep this sunken circle at twenty-nine degrees Celsius, day and night, year upon year, while the freezing gales of the high plateau skim harmlessly across the rim fifty feet overhead.

Because the heat is constant and the outside air is arctic, the boundary layer sweats. Fine droplets form in the upper air of the pocket and fall not as rain, but as an intermittent, warm precipitation—tiny, bead-like plinks that strike the broad, waxy leaves below.

The floor of the hollow is not crowded. It has the measured, deliberate composition of a dry garden, though everything within it is supple and green. A thick, resilient moss, the color of wet lime, coats the rounded floor of ancient basalt, softened by millions of seasons of decay into a crumbly loam. Spaced wide apart—so that a person might walk unimpeded from one side of the basin to the other—grow solitary, fleshy ferns whose fronds do not curl outward, but rise vertically in ribbed, emerald pipes, drinking the falling beads of condensation.

It is peaceful to look at from a distance, but to step into the bowl is to be submerged in a wall of sound. 

The fauna does not hide. Here, isolated from the barren tundra that surrounds the peak, life has hyper-concentrated into the warmth. It is overwhelmingly loud. Millions of micro-cicadas, no bigger than apple seeds, cling to the milk-colored pylons in crawling, shifting scabs of black and copper. Their stridulation is not a buzz but a dry, mechanical rattle that vibrates through the bones of the middle ear—a rhythmic, deafening clatter that rises and dips as air currents ripple across their carapaces. 

Beneath them, darting across the empty spans of lime-green moss, move dozens of small, warm-blooded quadrupeds with skin like wet river stone and ears folded back against their skulls. They are nearly blind, navigating entirely by the acoustics of the cicadas' din, snapping them out of the lower air with lightning-fast, bifurcated tongues. In the warm pools that collect in the shallow dips between ancient, worn stone flags, blind translucent crayfish scuttle through the detritus, fighting over fallen husks. The water they paddle through is not mineral runoff; it is subtly alkaline, tasting faintly of ozonated copper, filtered through the hidden nanostructure of the pylons.

Overhead, hovering on blur-fast wings, are things that resemble heavy-bodied hummingbirds, though their feathers are replaced by an iridescent, beetle-like shell that clicks against itself in flight. They do not feed on the ferns; they graze upon the pylons themselves, licking away the thin films of electrostatic oil that the strange machines exude along their southern faces.

The relics do not seem to notice this parasite ecology. Every twenty minutes or so, a sub-audible pulse—a physical displacement of pressure—travels through the soil, causing the ferns to dip in unison and silencing the cicadas for precisely two seconds. In that momentary, shocking lull, the only sound is the gentle, steady *tick-tick-tick* of moisture dripping from the warm air into the moss, and the faint, low-frequency hum of a reactor core whose fuel cycle was designed to outlast the mountain it was buried in. 

Then, a single click starts up on the nearest pylon, another answers from across the gravel, and the roar crashes back in: thick, vibrating, frantic with the work of eating, mating, and dying in an artificial summer ten thousand feet above the dead earth.
