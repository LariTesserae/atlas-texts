The rift is called a basin only by courtesy of its floor, which flattened out when the mountain broke open three or four centuries ago and has not yet had time to decay into gravel. The stone is a dark, cleavage-happy phonolite—clean, angular steps that look as though they were sheared yesterday with an enormous wedge. It sits at eight thousand feet, tucked into the lee of a western needle-peak that keeps it in a slate-blue penumbra for twenty-two hours of the day. The air is cold enough to make the temples ache if taken in too quickly through the nose, yet it never quite freezes; a steady, thermal draft rises through the floor-cracks, smelling faintly of cold well-water and iron filings.

There is very little loose water. No brook runs here. Instead, a dense, slow moisture forms out of nothing upon every chilled surface: beads of glass on the undersides of sills, a glaze over smooth rock-faces that never turns to ice, and an immense, dripping fleece of lungwort, club-moss, and broad-leafed black ferns that seem to drink directly from the breath of the stone. The greenery is fleshy and thick-spined, growing out of the vertical joints rather than topsoil, tumbling downward in heavy, dark aprons that brush the shoulders of anyone walking the lanes.

Between these vertical stone curtains sits the town. It is surprisingly organized—a strict grid of three-story dry-stack towers, linked at the middle levels by stout pedestrian bridges made of split larch lashed with wet rawhide. It has the layout of a dense city-quarter—there are squares, dead-ends, elevated gutters, and tiny, arched communal cisterns—yet it feels vacant of mass, built with wide, empty voids between the towers to allow the cold mountain draft to clear the cellars. The mason-work is crude but intensely deliberate: no mortar, no lime, just the natural rhomboids of the fractured stone fitted with hand-adze precision, pinned here and there with cold-beaten copper spikes that have turned the color of dried parsley.

What fills this small, orderly stone corral is not people—though two or three hundred inhabitants live here, scraping tallow into bone dishes and dressing mountain goats behind heavy felt curtains—but the animals.

They are everywhere, and they are nearly mute.

On the roof-slabs, where the shale has been laid flat to catch the condensation, thousands of white, velvet-skinned marmots—short-legged and triple-jointed—lie packed shoulder to shoulder like cobbles, their ribs working in quick, frantic unison to share body heat. They do not whistle or chirp; their vocal cords seem missing or sealed, and the only sound of their presence is the dry, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of long, keratinous claws shifting against the slate. Above them, nesting in the gaps between the second-floor floorboards and the lintels, are clouds of broad-winged, downy-breasted swifts with four eyes—two forward for the dark lanes, two pointed upward into the mist. They launch themselves continuously in dropping arcs, hundreds every minute, skimming the mossy walls with a faint, velvet *whuff* of displacement that stirs the hair on a man’s neck, taking invisible midges off the wet moss.

The dynamism of the place is frantic, but muffled as if under four layers of wet wool. A perpetual hunt unfolds on the vertical planes. Pale, legless lizards as thick as a forearm slide down the rock-chimneys with the greasy speed of spilled lard, chasing colonies of silver-haired woodlice that scuttle in tight, geometric formations like compass-roses across the copper-sheathed doorframes. If a lizard takes a louse, there is no crunch; the shell is soft as lard-rind, yielding with only a tiny, wet *plink* like a drop of water falling into a bucket of lard.

Down in the lanes, where the phonolite is swept clean by the town’s children using broom-bundles of wiry fern, the space between the houses remains airy and cold. The inhabitants move with their chins tucked into collar-rolls of grey down, their boots soles made of triple-thickness sheep-gut to deaden their steps. They have learned the acoustic economy of the rift: to speak above a wheeze brings thousands of the swifts down in an agitated, blinding blizzard of grey feathers, their four eyes wheeling in silent distress. 

Everything here is touched by this quiet press of breath. The air feels heavy, not with mist, but with the combined respiration of five thousand small lungs working against the cold. In the communal courtyards—paved in pale, newly split flagstones that ring like glass when struck with an iron tool—the only domestic fixture is often a central grease-trough. Into this, the women ladle the fat from the wing-joints of the high-climbing goats; within an hour, the grease is completely covered by a trembling, shifting carpet of tiny, tailless dormice, whose coats are so fine they look like blue smoke. They eat until they are spherical, then roll from the stone edges into the gutters, where the town’s infants gather them by the apron-full like windfallen plums, to tuck into their bed-ticks for warmth during the long, dim hours when the needle-peak blots out the sun entirely.

There is no rust. There is no decay. The high, thin air and the pervasive, chilled damp act together like a preserve. A discarded wooden spade, carved from a single piece of ash, lies in an alleyway between two blind stone gables; a pale, fern-like fungus has mapped its grain, and three species of translucent slugs are grazing the fungus down to the wood, their paths cross-hatching the timber with mother-of-pearl trails. The tool looks as if it were dropped twenty minutes ago, yet the copper nails holding the corner of the adjacent granary have grown long, turquoise beards that reach nearly to the mud.

Every six hours, the wind in the upper gorge reverses. When this happens, a wall of pure, white vapor drops over the upper rim of the basin like milk poured into a dark horn. The lizards retreat into the chinks; the marmots press closer until their spines click; the townspeople pull their hide awnings tight over the window-slits with braided gut cords that creak in the stillness. 

For twenty minutes, while the basin fills to its brim with cold, dense fog, the only thing audible is the collective sound of thousands of tiny claws adjusting their hold on the raw, young stone—a sound like a woman dragging a skirt of stiff silk over wet grass, vast and delicate, before the damp settles back into the moss and leaves the rift in its flat, cold, watchful twilight.
