It is the interior of an enormous, forgotten draft plenum—a vertical concrete riser perhaps sixty paces wide and twice as tall, completely sealed away inside the lower foundation blocks of an antique pumping complex. From the outside world it does not exist, buried beneath layers of structural ballast and seismic footings; inside, however, it is an unbroken, thundering hollow.

The roar is the first thing that defines the space: a sound so total and immemorial that it ceases to feel like noise and becomes an atmospheric pressure. Tens of thousands of gallons of warm industrial condensate plunge endlessly through high-pressure atomizer nozzles set into the distant ceiling ribs. It does not drop as rain, but as a violent, continuous sheet of pulverized white mist and plunging rivulets that smash against the tiered iron scaffolding lining the perimeter. The acoustic energy vibrates the breastbone and teeth. The machinery feeding this cascade is completely obscured, buried somewhere behind tens of feet of reinforced concrete, running at a steady, immutable pitch that hasn't varied its cycle in a century.

Because the chamber is nearly devoid of interior fixtures, the sound has nowhere to absorb. Aside from the heavy, curving concrete walls and a single, rusted catwalk ringing the circumference, the center is a soaring, empty volume of bright, vapor-choked air. Floods of clear, clean-spectrum industrial lighting—unfailing mercury-halide or incandescent banks enclosed in heavy, bronze-bolted marine housings—burn through the mist from every corner, casting an even, daylight-bright glare across the void. There are no shadows, only gradients of saturated, luminous fog.

Every square inch of the substrate is artificial, yet it has lived with warm, running water for so long that the materials have reached their final, post-mechanical geology. The concrete has lost its formwork lines; it has been dissolved, scoured, and smoothed into slick, undulating bellies of grey aggregate, worn as soft to the eye as tidal shale. Heavy steel H-beams have rusted down to stable, black-iron cores, their sharp edges rounded off by decades of soft friction. Copper drainage conduits have bled oxidized verdigris into the masonry, staining vast vertical flutes of peacock green and turquoise that never dry. 

Where the concrete and oxidized metals settle, the green has taken hold with savage, unhurried density. Fed by the heat—a consistent, humid eighty-five degrees—and the unending, nutrient-rich mist, the walls are thick with life. It is not a tangled wilderness of trees or deep soil, but a vertical, hanging biome of astonishing mass. Giant, drooping fronds of ribbon-fern and saturated clumps of staghorn hang from the flanges of steel struts. Liverworts, glistening like wet oilcloth, carpet every horizontal pipe in mats three inches deep. Filmy ferns cling to the vertical concrete faces, completely cloaking the architecture in a pelt of pale, translucent emerald. Long, fibrous water-roots drop straight down through the empty air toward the basin floor like suspended rigging, trailing in the updraft created by the falling water. 

There is an absolute, immutable stability to the place. Nothing rots away completely; the dead plant matter is quickly washed down to the drainage sumps below, leaving only living, perennial green that drinks the constant spray. There are no seasons here, no night and day, no drought, and no flood. The water level in the sump at the base remains fixed to the exact fraction of an inch, balanced perpetually against submerged gravity-overflows that siphon the liquid off into dark subterranean flumes. 

Animal life has almost no foothold in this violence of sound and water. Occasionally, a tiny, pale amphipod or a translucent midge skitters across the sodden root-masses near the catwalk floor, washed down through the supply feeds from some distant open reservoir, but there are no birds, no fish, no frogs. The air belongs purely to the plants, the mist, and the machine.

Standing on the vibrating iron of the catwalk, looking across the bright, empty span of the shaft, one feels the terrifying calm of an environment that has fully solved itself. It is a closed loop of iron, power, and water, operating in total mechanical equilibrium, where life has simply settled into the metal ribs of the work, enduring forever inside an endless, deafening midday shower.
