There is no bedrock beneath the soles, only the floor that was laid down yesterday, and beneath that, the floor laid down during the watch before.

The ground is an endless parquet of wet, unglazed terra-cotta tiles, green timber balks, and packed tallow-cakes, bound together with braided horsehair and cold river-pitch. It does not rest on earth. If one were to pry up a course of the tiles—which are still soft enough to take the impression of a thumb—there would be only another tier of joists, another crisscross of split ash poles, and then an empty, drafty blackness falling away into unseen under-vaults where the builders worked three seasons ago. Everything here has been made by hand, with adzes, wooden mauls, bone wedges, and heavy round stones swung from tripods of lashed birch, yet the work stretches past the eye’s reach into the cold gloom of an indoor basin the size of a province.

It is dim—not pitch-black, but the thick, blue-gray darkness of a vaulted cellar lit only by grease-lamps set miles apart. The dampness does not fall from a sky; it rises from the materials themselves. Tens of thousands of square yards of fresh mortar, slaked with cold ditch-water, are sweating out their lime. The moisture gathers on the underside of overhead trestles and drips in steady, heavy plops onto the shoulders of the scaffolding. Where the wood is new—and nearly all of it is new, smelling of torn sapwood, wet bark, and sour tannins—fine beads of condensation coat the adze-marks like a film of grease. In the sags and troughs between the support ribs, small black puddles collect, no wider than shields, perfectly still until a tremor from the ongoing joinery ripples their surfaces.

There are no trees, no grass, no lichen. The only organic things are dead things worked into utility: boiled hide, dried bladders used to seal joints against the cold draft, bundles of coarse reeds brought from some forgotten border to serve as temporary shim-stock. The air tastes of wet lime, cold fat, and split green oak.

The sound is enormous and near-constant, yet entirely mechanical in the most ancient sense. It is the noise of manual labor multiplied to the scale of a weather system. Somewhere two miles to the north, a bank of counterweighted timber rams is being raised by teams working tread-spokes, their cedar axles screaming without grease before dropping with an earth-shaking *chock* that drives another line of foundation wedges into the lower lattice. Closer by, the sound is a thick, rhythmic chatter: mallet on peg, mallet on peg, the dry *snick* of flint axes squaring tenons, and the wet slap of trowels slapping gray muck into the seams between floorboards. The sheer mass of the framework groans continuously under its own raw, settling weight; timbers seasoning in the damp air crack with the sharpness of pistol shots, sending dry vibrations hummed through the soles of one’s feet.

Because the construction is so recent, nothing has had time to weather or round off. The edges of the beams are sharp enough to shave skin; the mortar snarls with stiff, unwashed animal hair that sticks out of the seams like bristles. It is an embryonic landscape, an unfinished floor being rolled out continuously into the dark by millions of small, repetitive interventions.

Through this forest of struts and uprights, life is scarce and furtive. There are no birds in the upper stays. Occasionally, a pale, blind ferret—bred with broad, paddle-like front paws for scrabbling along slick poles—slips through the lacing of hemp rope, hunting the thumb-sized silverfish that feed on the tallow seals. Once or twice in an hour, a human shape resolves from the gray haze: figures wrapped in greased hide and coarse burlap, their forearms thick as hams from driving wedges, their faces smeared with powdered chalk to keep the damp from rotting their eyebrows. They move without lanterns, navigating by the sound of the drop-hammers and the differing grain of the planks beneath their bare, callous-plated feet. They carry baskets of damp clay, bundles of willow withies, or heavy stone plummets on raw rawhide strings. They do not speak; the clatter of the rams would swallow their voices anyway. They communicate with mallet-raps against the framing—two strikes for *slack*, four for *draw*.

The scale of it feels impossible for the tools at hand, yet the evidence of the tool is in every seam. A valley-sized floor of hand-cut timbers, rising slightly at the perimeter into immense tiered ramps that vanish into the ceiling mist, held together not by iron or sorcery, but by millions of neatly driven wooden pegs, each whittled by someone sitting in the dark with a knife. The whole structure breathes slightly, flexing two feet up and down as the weight of the workforce shifts from one quadrant to another, like a raft of logs adrift on an invisible sea that no one has ever gone down far enough to see.
