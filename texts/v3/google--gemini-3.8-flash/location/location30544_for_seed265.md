The dark here is not a lack of daylight; it is a physical medium. It has thickness, the temperature of an exhaled breath, and the heavy, sweet scent of warm machine oil cut with sulfurous water. There is no horizon to orient by, yet the expanse is unmistakably vast—a subterranean or post-atmospheric floor stretching out for leagues, broken into sharp, recently cleaved terraces.

Every surface is wet. The moisture does not fall as rain, but condenses out of the stagnant heat in fat, lukewarm droplets that form on cold iron, on skin, and along the razor-sharp edges of freshly fractured basalt. The rock underfoot has not had time to soften or weather. Its ridges are keen as broken slate, geometric and fresh, as though a continental plate dropped forty cubits only last season, leaving sheer facets that slice through grease and water alike.

The terrain is crowded, though not with trees. Vegetation is thin and anemic: rubbery, black-brown thallophytes that coat the vertical cracks, and brittle, leafless stalks that grow sideways out of the rock, calcified and hollow. Instead, the density comes from the fauna and the metal.

Every few yards, the dark reveals another edge of industry. These are not ruins in the classical sense, but fragments of an assembly line or processing district that seems to have sheared into existence along with the stone. Massive cast-iron flywheels, forty feet across, sit half-submerged in warm, shallow troughs of mineral brine. They do not turn, or if they do, their revolution is measured in years. Heavy copper busbars run along the floor, thick as a thigh, disappearing into solid blocks of basalt without an entrance hole, the rock molded around the metal as if the two were poured together like milk into tar. Pneumatic pipes, sweating a foul condensate, snake across the low saddles of the rock, occasionally shuddering with a soft, dull *thump* that travels through the soles of one's feet rather than through the air.

And everywhere, pressing against the machines and the stone, is the fauna.

They are packed close, shoulder-to-flank, carapace-to-skin, moving with a patient, untroubled sluggishness. In the absolute pitch, their numbers can only be sensed by the wet displacement of their bodies, the warmth they cast off, and the continuous, low slumping sound of thousands of tons of living mass shifting over iron rails. Many are soft-bodied, heavy as walruses but segmented like blind grubs, their hide tasting of iron rust and salt. They crawl over the dead machinery, wedging themselves into the housings of steam-turbines, their breath small, rhythmic, and moist. Among them, smaller things skitter—many-jointed limbs clicking against cast iron, sliding between the folds of the larger beasts without resistance.

There is a fundamental violation in how space behaves here. At times, the ground tilts abruptly without the balance shifting; a flat shelf of stone will feel, to the muscles, like a vertical descent, though one does not fall. Water pools on the sides of boulders rather than in their hollows, forming vertical lenses of warm liquid that the blind animals swim through horizontally before emerging back into the damp air on the other side. 

Sound behaves erratically. The landscape is mostly quiet—a deep, muffling silence born of moisture and massed flesh—punctuated only by the slow *clack* of a cooling armature or the thick lap of brine against an engine block. Yet a footstep in the water may sound three yards behind where it was placed, or be delayed by several seconds, returning as a dull squelch from inside a sealed boiler. Distances are untrustworthy; a machine that sounds fifty paces off may brush cold grease against your shoulder after two steps. 

The heat does not dissipate. It radiates upward from the floor, trapped beneath some invisible ceiling that feels low despite the openness of the plains. It is the heat of a machine room left running in an abandoned cellar: stale, grease-thickened, and crowded with life that requires no sun, only the slow chemical seep of the water and the electric rot of the buried grid.

Occasionally, an electrical current will arc—not with a flash, for the darkness remains absolute, but with a sudden, violent smell of ozone and a dry snapping noise that sends a ripple through the massed bodies. In response, a hundred thousand soft bellies will contract at once, a communal shudder that presses outward like a slow wave through grease, before settling back into the wet, unhurried press of the dark.
