At twenty-two thousand feet, the air has almost no weight, yet here inside the fracture, it is thick with the grease of tallow-skinned things.

The place is a throat, perhaps fifty paces long and four paces across, torn through the razor-crest of the horn-ridge. It is an infant fissure—the rock has the chalky, razor-bright sharpness of stone that has only seen four or five hundred winters since the fault snapped. Nothing has been rounded. A falling pebble does not bounce so much as shear itself into flakes against the walls of grey syenite, whose edges are as keen as freshly struck bottle-glass. 

Above, the sky is only a hairline of violet, too narrow to cast illumination into the bottom. It is cold—a wet, dull chill that hovers just above freezing, sustained by the cloud-tongues that are driven through the cleft from the windward snowfields. The fog enters as dry mist, hits the hot-slick hide of the inhabitants, and turns instantly to runnels of black condensation that stream down the rock face.

There are no trees, no shrubs, only a slick, skin-colored film of lithophytic yeast that coats the lower six feet of the cleft, fed by urine and the mineral flour sloughing off the raw stone.

And the cleft is packed.

They are called the *kivva*, though no one who named them lives up here now. They are vertebrates, but they have abandoned legs for broad, segmented ribs that function like crampons, tipped in amber-colored keratin. They do not fly. They hang. They overlap like roof shingles from the floor up into the darkness thirty feet overhead, their grey-violet bellies touching the greasy backs of the ones below them. Tens of thousands of them, each the length of an arm, blind, with heavy, tripartite skulls that butt together with dull, wooden clacks when the wind stirs them.

Their breath is the source of the sound. It is not a chorus, but a great, desynchronized piston-stroke: a loud, wet wheeze followed by a metallic suction as their throat-sacs collapse against their ribs. Multiplied by thousands, the sound does not echo; it crowds the ears until the stone itself seems to be vibrating like a loose tooth. The volume is high, but the tempo is glacial. A single lungful takes twenty seconds to draw and thirty to expel. Between breaths, there is only the sound of high-altitude frost tinkling against the rock rim above and the slow, heavy drop of thick grease into the sump-pools at the bottom.

In the lower hollows, where the syenite pinches into a floor of angular gravel, are the marks of people who were here before the cleft settled into its current depth. They did not build walls—there is no room, and stone cannot be piled against the living mass—but they left wedges. Elk-horn spikes, dry as chalk and three fingers thick, have been hammered into the bedding planes with stone mauls to serve as steps or anchors. Some of them are wrapped in coarse, greasy cord made from kivva-gut, stiffened by age into iron-hard loops. There are no iron nails, no soot from torches—light is useless here, and the kivva are sensitive to heat; a torch would cause an avalanche of falling, writhing meat that would crush a man in seconds. 

The human presence was merely that of harvesters: primitive hunters who came up the long couloirs on bone crampons, blind in the dark, feeling for the fat-pads behind the third rib of the torpid creatures, cutting with slate crescent-knives that left clean, straight nicks on the bedrock behind the carcasses. That was long ago, but because the cold is so dry above and the air so sterile within, the severed horns of the kivva left by the butchery still smell of dried suet, tucked into small niches along the floor.

The ecology here does not run on sun. It runs on the high, continuous drift of the jet stream, which hurls trillions of midges, lichen-spores, and dead spiders from the lowlands against the peaks, icing them into the snowfields. The meltwater carries this frozen soup down through the head of the rift, where the kivva filter it through bristled mouths that never open more than a thumb’s width. 

Change does not come from the seasons, which are indistinguishable at this altitude, but from the slow, tectonic creep of the range. Every decade, the fissure closes by half an inch; every three or four decades, an earthquake jars a thousand kivva from their moorings. They drop to the floor without cries, pile three-deep, and slowly, over weeks, re-anchor themselves upon one another’s backs, their small horn-tipped ribs finding purchases in the leathery skin of their kin, knitting back into a single, wall-to-wall mat of damp, breathing grey.

To stand at the base—if one could squeeze between the shuddering wall of flesh and the flinty crag—is to be in a dark, vertical lung. The smell is not rot, but old tallow, cold granite dust, and the sharp, vinegar tang of concentrated ammonia from their skins. The world outside, the vast blue sweep of the continental plateau, does not exist here. There is only the damp granite, the cold air sliding down from the notch, and the steady, deafening groan of sixty tons of warm meat wedged into an iron-hard crack in the roof of the sky.
