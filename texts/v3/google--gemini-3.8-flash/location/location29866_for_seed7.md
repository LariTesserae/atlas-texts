The heat here does not circulate; it hangs like wool. It is thick, stagnant, and smells faintly of vulcanized rubber and wet river gravel. 

We are fifty paces beneath the capstone of an unrecorded drainage shaft, in a vault roughly the size of a parish church. The ceiling is low enough that the warmth presses downward against the skull, radiating from an unseen layer of basalt or buried slag above. The walls are not smoothed by centuries of subterranean seepage; they are jagged, fresh, blasted out with black powder and pneumatic drills only a season or two ago, leaving sharp shelves of fractured shale that could open an arm to the bone. 

Yet, in this nearly total, suffocating blackness, the floor has surrendered completely to green. 

It is an impossible lushness, fed not by sunlight but by the constant, greasy warmth of the floorplates and the thick, intermittent mist that purges from the pressure sleeves. Broad, spade-shaped leaves, pale as tallow and rubbery to the touch, crowd out of the ballast stones. They grow in dense, silent clusters, though they leave wide lanes of emptiness between them—vast flags of sweat-slick iron and bare gravel corridors where nothing has yet taken root. Where they do gather, they are thick enough to muffle an echo: pale fiddleheads uncoiling with agonizing slowness, liverworts coating the flange of a severed steam manifold, and long, fibrous vines that loop themselves mechanically through the teeth of idle drive sprockets.

Without direct light, their green is wrong—a dark, liver-toned emerald, or a chlorotic yellow that seems to drink what little illumination exists. The only visible light comes from the dying embers of three heavy-gauge resistor coils mounted high on the eastern wall, which hum without vibration and glow a sullen, bruised cherry-red. It is just enough to cast long, hard-edged shadows of the leaves across the floor, stretching them into spidery silhouettes against the raw, split stone.

The machinery scattered through the room belongs to a heavy, careless industry. A stationary steam pump, its flywheel eight feet across and counterweighted with pig iron, sits sunk in silt up to its axle. It is dead, yet its iron block remains hot to the touch, sweating bead after bead of clear, distilled condensate that rolls down its oil-black flanks into the moss below. Further in, a battery of open settling tanks holds an inch of lukewarm, mineral-heavy water, perfectly still, reflecting the dull red coils above like twin slits of a furnace door. 

There are no insects here. No beetles crawl among the wet gravel; no blind isopods scuttle under the rusted girders; no mold-gnats hover in the damp drafts. The silence is profound and heavy, broken only every few minutes by the deliberate, weighty impact of a single drop falling forty feet from a hanging pipe into the center of a metal hopper: a clean, ringing *tock* that dies instantly against the wall of thick, fleshy foliage.

Everything moves according to the slow arithmetic of heat transfer. The rock conducts warmth into the roots; the roots pull condensation from the iron; the iron weeps its moisture back into the stones. The leaves do not rustle, for there is no air current down here—only the slow expansion and contraction of the vault itself as the surface miles away shifts through its seasons. A vine may take three months to crawl across the polished tread of an iron gear, its tendrils feeling for the oil-slick grooves, testing the cold metal, finding it warm, and anchoring itself with small, grey discs of sap. 

The air tastes of boiled iron and mint. It is close, womb-like, and sterile of breath, sustained by a mechanical fever that has outlived whatever hand turned the valve.
