The floor of the basin extends for three thousand miles without rising more than fifty feet above its mean datum, but it is not flat; it is woven. 

Underneath everything lies a foundation of cold-rolled steel plate, ribbed and grooved to hold the runners, though the metal has long since oxidized into a matte, lilac-colored patina that yields no dust. Upon this plate grows the dry canopy. It is not an ecosystem that relies on water; the air is parched to zero humidity, kept at a steady, refrigerative chill that smells faintly of pressed flax, ozone, and hot machine lacquer that has cooled down decades ago. The flora here is an anhydrous lineage: its vascular bundles do not carry sap, but an electrostatic tension that draws fine, micro-crystalline resins up through rigid stems of coiled, amber-hardened cellulose. 

Because there is no water, nothing rots. When a frond ceases to cycle its charge, it does not wither; it simply sets, becoming as brittle and translucent as mica, until the reciprocating combs of the floor shear it into neat, square-inch flakes that are swallowed back down into the pneumatic flues.

The light is the light of an unblinking magnesium daylight, cast from an overcast that is not cloud but an endless ceiling of frosted, ribbed industrial glass suspended at an altitude of six miles. The illumination is uniform, shadowless, and nearly total. It eliminates horizon. When one looks into the distance, the landscape does not fade into blue haze or atmospheric gloom; instead, it scales down indefinitely with crisp, architectural precision, until distant gantry towers and million-acre fields of carding-ferns converge into a single, razor-sharp hairline where the white floor meets the white ceiling.

The silence is enormous, almost tactile. A world so full of moving mass should roar, yet the dynamic here is muted to the threshold of hearing. The vast mechanical operations have been engineered—or grown—with tolerances so exact that they slide upon self-lubricating graphite skins. Billions of tons of matter are in continuous transit: miles-wide carpets of stiff, needle-leafed spindle-grass slowly creep sideways across the iron decks, their root-cables engaged with recessed gears that turn once every thirteen hours. You hear only a vast, dry whisper—the sound of an entire library of heavy-stock paper being shuffled simultaneously by velvet gloves. Occasionally, an overhead gantry—its trusses grown from lignified ash-wood wrapped in braided zinc—shifts its track with a muffled, iron *clink* that travels through the marrow of the deck rather than through the thin, dead air.

The plants are indistinguishable from the apparatus that cultivates them, because the apparatus is their skeletal system, and they are its switches. Giant, three-story-tall thistles of stamped brass foil and living pith rise from the junctions of dry conduits. Their leaves are corrugated, edged with fine copper whiskers that comb the air for drifting graphite spores. As the dry weather-fronts move—fronts not of rain or vapor, but of pressure differentials and sweeping charge-differentials that make the skin prickle—these thistles twist on their ball-bearing pedestals, turning their broad, sun-hungry vanes directly into the magnesium glare.

Every square yard is occupied. The density is claustrophobic, yet organized along strict, manufacturing logic that has slipped into natural selection. There are thickets of cold-extruded rod-bamboo that do not sprout branches, but rather die-cast brackets; between these brackets, dense colonies of paper-liverwort have stretched themselves into paper-thin, drum-tight sheets that hum at imperceptible frequencies. Deep beneath the primary foliage, in the three-foot clearance between the steel deck and the lower root-mesh, an understory of dry lichen forms complex, relief-carved friezes of cams and escapements, functional gear-trains carved out of dried mycorrhizal crust that tick forward by microscopic increments as they absorb the ambient light.

Reality suffers small, structural fractures under this weight of over-specified design. Space here does not always resolve its vanishing points correctly. Because the vegetative machines reproduce by exact stamping rather than sexual or sporular variance, thousands of acres present precisely the same cluster of twelve thistle-stalks, the same three bent drive-rods, the same dry, papery rose whose petals align with the screws of an inspection hatch. Walking forward across three miles produces no parallax; the foreground and the middle distance appear to be the same plane, doubled by some mechanical registration error. In certain clearings where the spindle-grass has been harvested down to the painted iron, the shadows fall *toward* the light, cast by an impossible geometry that the dry air fails to correct.

Fauna is nearly absent, having no moisture to sustain flesh, but there are exceptions: small, desiccated things that resemble long-legged beetles, their carapaces formed from folded tracing cloth and needle-punch steel. They do not eat; they carry small, vibrating brass needles in their mandibles, moving with a jerky, clockwork gait from leaf to leaf, sewing the tears in the foliage with single strands of glass-fiber thread. They are maintenance, not life, yet they mate by pressing their hot thorax plates together until a spark jumps between them, leaving behind a small, dry egg that resembles an unthreaded bolt.

The basin’s season is a slow, forty-day cycle of tension and discharge. Currently, it is in the intake phase. The air is cold enough to ache in the teeth, perfectly motionless, while the plants draw the ambient charge of the white sky into their pith. The stalks are swollen with potential; their outer rinds, polished by dry wind, gleam like bone-china. The machines do not stop, but their rhythm slows until a man could stand among the giant, reciprocating looms of wild linen for an hour before seeing the shuttle move six inches to the left. 

Then, miles away, so far that the eye registers it only as a rhythmic flicker against the glass sky, an entire quadrant of steel deck drops three inches into a sub-floor dock. A million tons of dry vegetation fall silent, their switches tripped, waiting for the cold, dry sparks that will travel the continental copper root-mesh to start them moving in the other direction.
