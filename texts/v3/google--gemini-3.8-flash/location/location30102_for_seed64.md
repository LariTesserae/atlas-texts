High in the needle-towers of the upper crust, where the atmosphere thins to a sharp, bone-dry draught, there is a fissure no wider than a carriage house, buried deep within a spine of pale basalt. It sits miles above the lowlands, pinned against the sky, yet it has no view; it is entirely internal, a vertical flue wedged between sheer walls of cold stone and forgotten cast-iron infrastructure. 

The air here has never carried moisture. It smells of powdered zinc, graphite, and cold friction. Despite this absolute drought, the chimney is choked with an impenetrable, dry lushness.

Every surface is smothered in an anhydrous thicket that knows nothing of sap or rain. Sheets of parchment-lichen, thick as boot leather and cold to the touch, cover the basalt in tiered, overlapping scales. From the crevices burst enormous, fan-shaped fronds made of spun silicate and brittle cellulose, stiff as sheet brass, rustling with an incessant, razor-sharp hiss whenever the air shudders. Beneath them, mats of dense, hair-like wire-moss pack the corners and floor, dry as tinder yet thriving, sustained not by water but by the steady drift of microscopic mineral dross and electrostatic charge flowing through the mountain’s veins. It is a jungle of desiccated fiber, silver-grey, ochre, and soot-black, tangled so completely that there is nowhere to step without crushing brittle stalks that snap like twigs underfoot. 

Embedded in this vegetation are the bones of an industrial era that never left. Massive riveted trusses of grey boiler-plate bridge the gap between the rock faces, half-strangled by the dry, ropy vines. Cold steam-pipes the diameter of oak trunks run vertically through the thicket; their joints are sealed with lead and braided hemp, long since petrified. In the center of the pocket, half-sunken in the dry moss, sits the housing of a vast, direct-drive centrifugal exhaust pump. Its iron casing is intact, dark and greasy with oxidized pitch, bolted directly to the mountain’s bedrock with pins as thick as an arm.

The place is deafening, yet nothing moves.

The sound is not born of weather or living voices—there are no insects here, no birds, no breath—but of continuous, mechanical resonance. Deep within the core of the spire, far beneath the floor, ancient turbines or trapped drafts continue to spin with invariant speed. The vibration is immense; it rises through the rock and the iron ribs, expressing itself as an unending, low-frequency roar that rattles the teeth and makes the dry leaves buzz against one another like a billion metallic reeds. The air does not blow; it trembles. The noise is perfectly flat, an immutable drone that has occupied this cavity for centuries without shifting pitch by a single hertz.

The light is a uniform, twilight dimness. There are no cycles of day or night within the flue. High above, through a labyrinth of baffles and iron grates, a faint, slate-colored daylight filters down, stripped of all warmth. It casts dull highlights on the edges of the silica leaves and glints weakly against the grease on the machine housings, but it casts no sharp shadows. It is an ambient, cellar-like glow, cold and perpetual.

Because there is no water, there is no rot. Nothing decays; nothing softens. Fallen fronds from three hundred years ago lie beneath the current growth, as sharp and dry as the day they broke from their joints. The plants do not grow visibly, nor do they die back; they have filled every available centimeter of the gap, pressing against the stone and the ironwork until physical compression halted their expansion. They exist in a state of locked equilibrium—a saturated, motionless biology wedged immovably inside an industrial tomb, cold, bone-dry, and roaring forever in the dark.
