At twenty thousand feet above the drowned lowlands, the air does not thin; it thickens, curdling into a scalding, pressurized soup that tastes of copper and boiled mint. The verticality here is absolute, an immense shelf of dark, basalt-veined schist that rose before the naming of continents and simply stayed, jutting through the clouds like an amputated bone of the earth. Yet it is not cold. The heat is constant and bodily, roughly the temperature of fresh blood, radiating upward from the rock itself as though the core of the planet were venting directly through the pores of this high tableland.

Spread across these impossible precipices is a city that has never seen a wheel, a lever, or a nail. It is vast—stretching across terraces, clefts, and suspended natural buttresses for leagues—built entirely of lime-slurry, hand-tamped alluvial mud, and river-smoothed cobblestone bonded by fibrous sap. Its scale is metropolitan: multi-story towers lean toward one another across narrow, dripping alleys; stepped plazas big enough to assemble armies step up the mountain faces; aqueducts wide as dry docks span thousand-foot voids. But every surface bears the soft, rounded touch of finger-work, of palm-beaten mortar and woven reeds. It is an urbanity without masonry, an architecture of desperate, collective sediment that has settled into the cliffside so long ago that the cliffs and the habitations share the same root systems.

Nothing moves through the avenues. There are no lizards on the lintels, no ticks in the moss, no vultures tracing the violent thermals. The city is empty of breath, but it is not quiet.

The sound is a flat, continuous catastrophe. It is the roar of an oceanic cataract that falls from an unknown lip three miles further up the peak, dropping past the city in a vertical sheet of boiling white froth that never wavers, never alters its frequency, and never shifts an inch with the wind. The sound does not echo; it fills the skull immediately, crushing the ability to imagine silence. It is loud enough to loosen teeth in their sockets, yet because it has remained at precisely this decibel for millennia, the valley has adapted to its pressure. The stone does not shake; it hums with a deep, dull deadness, like a church bell submerged in oil.

Water here does not obey the simple law of descent. It condenses from the heavy, blinding daylight upon every surface at once, forming slick, glassy skins over the mud facades. Walls sweat continuously. Lichen—coarse, orange, and thick as carpet—drinks this perspiration, growing alongside broad, fleshy leaves shaped like human kidneys that hang motionless from the parapets. These plants do not sway. There is no wind, only an immense, stagnant draft that pushes vertically without disturbing a hair. The vegetation has choked the window openings and flooded the ground floors of the tenements, its roots knotting into the mud-brick until building and jungle are a single, saturated loaf.

Space folds in the upper tiers. Looking down an alleyway between two towering tenements of hardened clay, the perspective does not converge at a vanishing point; instead, the far end of the street appears wider and higher than the entrance, opening onto a courtyard that seems to face south from its northern wall. Steps climb upward only to meet themselves from underneath. A stone trough, fed by the sweating rock, flows in a complete, seamless ring, its gray water perpetually circling a central pedestal without a pump or an incline, flat and calm beneath the battering din of the great fall.

The light is broad and indifferent—a flat, high-noon glare filtered through an endless canopy of pale, boiling vapor that never thickens into rain and never breaks into blue. Shadows do not travel; they are stamped directly beneath the eaves and the broad leaves, black and greasy, permanently affixed to the pavement.

Everything is in balance, arrived at through some ancient, unrepeatable exhaustion. The mud does not melt, despite the hot water sheeting down its face; the sap-glue does not rot; the monstrous, spade-headed ferns do not drop their dead frills. The entire expanse—the towers, the bridges, the wet roots clutching the void, the howling wall of white water—exists in a state of violent, stationary completion. It is a city that has been caught in the act of being drowned and roasted at the roof of the world, preserved at the exact instant the logic of the earth gave way, left to shout into the mist with a voice that will never run out of water.
