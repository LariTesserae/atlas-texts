The water does not sit in a basin so much as it occupies an altitude. At this height—where the atmosphere elsewhere would be thin and starved of heat—the liquid forms a massive, self-contained horizon, a clear and sun-saturated lens that drowns an entire upland plateau. 

Looking down through it, the light behaves with an uncommon, piercing clarity. Because there is virtually no terrestrial silt and the water is pure, warmed from below by a slow geotectonic simmer, the sun punches through eighty fathoms without losing its yellow brilliance. The illumination does not fade into blue-gray murk; instead, it cuts in hard, diagonal slats against pale stone and intense, wet greens, illuminating the seafloor with the crispness of an alpine midday.

The terrain beneath is violently young. Great needles and stepped terraces of black hornfels and splintered rhyolite rise toward the surface, their edges razor-keen. There has been no time for surf or tumbling pebbles to round these shoulders. The rocks are clean fractures, sheer geometric planes that split the water like sunken wedges. In some places, fault-scarps plunge vertically down into deeper trenchwork, their faces showing the pale, unweathered scars of stone that parted only centuries ago. 

Clinging to these jagged promontories is an astonishing, crowded weight of growth. It is not kelp in the traditional sense, but something closer to a high-sunlight, sub-aquatic jungle that has adapted to absolute submersion. Fronds with the broad, ribbed architecture of palm leaves rise from crevices on stalks as thick as a wrist, translucent enough that their inner vascular cords glow amber in the downwelling light. Higher up, on the flat tops of submerged plateaus, dense thickets of ribbon-moss form carpets three meters thick, spongy and yielding, their margins trailing off the edges of cliffs like torn green fabric. 

Everything is near-touching. Stems twist around stems; broad floating spathes overlap to create false floors, under which smaller, shade-tolerant filaments—colored in lilac and pale sulfur—spread across the bare rock. The water is so still that delicate structures which would be torn apart in a coastal surf can flourish: paper-thin bracts the size of shields hang motionless in the water column, tilted slightly toward the zenith to catch the unobstructed rays.

The water itself is warm, carrying the gentle, drowsy heat of an enclosed spring. Because it does not circulate with the violence of a planetary ocean, its movement is almost imperceptible. There are no tides here. The only currents are wide, languid plumes of buoyant, mineralized warm water rising from the fissures in the rock, carrying tiny, suspended pearls of gas that do not rush to the surface, but drift upward at the speed of a falling leaf in reverse.

Animal life is an absence that sharpens the silence. Across miles of flooded crags, there are no clouds of small fish, no scuttling armadas of crabs. Occasionally, if one watches a single sunlit clearing between two rhyolite spires for half an hour, a pale ribbon—some flat, blind vermiform creature forty feet long—will ghost across the moss, its fringes undulating with a rhythm so patient it seems less like locomotion and more like an unfolding. Sometimes a translucent siphonophore, glowing with refracted sunlight like a chandelier of blown glass, drifts through the middle depths, anchored to nothing, waiting for nutrients that drift only by chance.

Sound here is stripped down to bone and pressure. There is no roar of breaking swells, because there is no shore within sight—the submerged highland stretches out into shimmering green distance on all sides, bounded only by the horizon of the water’s own surface above. Instead, the ear receives a dense, velvety quiet, punctuated at vast intervals by the sharp, single *clink* of a thermal fracture—a rock face settling in the warmth—or the faint, low-frequency hiss of a deep fissure exhaling hot water through a bed of gravel.

Above, the surface of this inland sea is a taut, unwrinkled skin. From below, looking up through the bright canopy of green and chartreuse, the sky is visible as a brilliant, trembling disk of blue-white light, distorted only by the lazy domes of rising gas. The mountains under the water are climbing, slowly, steadily, pushed from beneath by forces that have barely begun their work, carrying their dense, sun-drenched gardens up into the sky.
