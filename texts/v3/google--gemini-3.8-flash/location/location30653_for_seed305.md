At this altitude, the sun does not warm; it merely bleaches. The light arrives raw through an atmosphere stripped of dust, striking the jagged, razor-crested spine of the cordillera with a flat, diamond glare so total that shadows have the hard, blue edges of glass. These mountains are young, torn upward by some recent, violent buckling of the crust—their faces are fresh faults, unsoftened by centuries, sheer planes of dark basalt and slate that fracture into needle-sharp talus. Yet there is no emptiness here. Despite the freezing air and the lethal altitude, the terrain is packed to suffocation.

The mechanization here was never built. It grew. 

There are no foundations, no rivets, no brickwork, no signs of hands or intentional design; instead, the ecology has evolved the logic of heavy industry directly out of iron-rich marrow and silicate stone. Across every square yard of the crags, dense forests of thick, jointed pipe-reeds—hollow, ribbed like pneumatic tubing, clad in a fibrous, rubbery bark—jostle for space against iron-moss and plate-ferns that unfurl like sheets of cold-rolled tin. Their sap is not watery, but an amber, viscid grease that prevents freezing at forty below, exuding a sharp, paraffin reek that hangs heavy despite the wind. Intermittent flurries of frozen condensation drift down from high banners of mountain fog, melting briefly where the ground is warm, then glazing back into rind-ice over the roots.

Beneath the vegetation, and weaving through it, is the sound: a vast, rhythmic stroke-work. It is deafening, yet it moves with an agonizing, glacial patience. The dynamic here is slow—a relentless, low-cadence revolution of organic flywheels and pneumatic lungs. 

Everywhere, crowded shoulder to shoulder in the narrow valleys and clinging to the vertical terraces, are the fauna. They exist in an overwhelming, claustrophobic press. These are not soft creatures. They are massive, slow-heaving ungulates and arthropodic behemoths whose exoskeletons are thick as boiler plate, segmented with oiled ball-and-socket joints, venting jets of superheated, steam-white breath into the sub-zero glare every forty seconds. Some are the size of locomotives, their carapaces carrying whole colonies of parasitic lever-crabs and mosses that tick and click like escapement mechanisms. They do not run; they cycle. Legs like walking beams lift with a heavy, hydraulic hiss, sink into the shale, and drive the mass forward by inches. 

Because space is so fiercely contested, these beasts grow into one another, brushing sides until their shells polish to a mirror sheen under the cruel sun. Herds of thousands stand wedged in the tectonic saddles, locked flank-to-flank like parked freight cars, chewing through the fibrous metallic reeds with slow, rotary jaws that grind with the sound of ore crushers. When a creature dies, it does not rot; its oils are drained by tube-feeders, its valves are pried open by wedge-beaked scavengers, and within weeks its hollow, ironized ribcage is colonized by moss and pressurized moss-spores, becoming part of the landscape’s living machinery.

There is intermittent water, but it rarely flows free. It travels through the ecology under pressure. You hear it knocking in the subterranean ducts—natural fissures sealed with dense, vulcanized resin by burrowing colonizers—shuddering like a water hammer in an old pipe network. In sheltered depressions between the razor peaks, pools of brine simmer from deep geothermal taps, coated in a thick, iridescent scum of chemical scum and cold-hardy algae that churns with microscopic, self-propelled pistons. The air smells of wet slate, battery acid, crushed pine, and lard.

High above, riding the thermal shear of the peaks, drift creatures that are little more than rigid, rib-braced bellows, miles wide. They hold their altitude by slowly combusting atmospheric hydrogen harvested from the high frost, their translucent bellies flashing like strobe lamps in the bright air, casting immense, ticking shadows across the serrated summits below. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The great herd-clutches move across a single scree slope over the course of decades, shaving the mountain down by iron friction alone, leaving behind polished stone chutes that glisten with grease. It is an unengineered manufacture, an industrial wilderness operating at crushing pressures and freezing cold, roaring to itself beneath a blinding sky that never softens.
