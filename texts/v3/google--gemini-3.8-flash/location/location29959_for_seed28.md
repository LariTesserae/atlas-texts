It is smaller than a city ought to be, but it is undeniably a city. 

The whole thing is caught inside a dry vertical rift, a sunken caliche fissure no wider than a common orchard, yet dropping two hundred spans into the blinding belly of the white benchlands. From above, walking the sun-cracked surface of the salt-pan, you would step over it without knowing, mistaking its mouth for a shadow between two chalk slabs. But drop beneath that upper lip and the world opens into an enormous, evacuated cylinder—a bell-shaped hollow where the air is baked to eighty degrees, bone-white with glare, and filled with a sound so vast and continuous it ceases to feel like noise and becomes an iron weight pressed against the skull.

The sound is wind, but wind that has been trapped and played like a reed. The thermal disparity between the blazing upper flats and the dead-dry floor below pulls a relentless, shrieking downdraft through thousands of natural blowholes in the ceiling. The air does not blow; it screams. It shears through the chamber at gale force, yet there is not a single particle of moisture in it. It is air that has traveled a thousand leagues over dry mineral basin, stripped of every vapor until it licks the skin like a hot rasp.

Clinging to the perimeter of this hollow is an entire metropolis of dry masonry.

Because the floor of the rift is bare, glaring, and uninhabitable—an empty lake of fine talc where nothing can anchor—the inhabitants built sideways. The walls are stepped back in thousands of hand-cut limestone terraces, dry-stacked without mortar, rising tier upon tier like the interior of an amphitheater made for a million souls. There are alleys the width of a forearm, grain-vaults cut into soft gypsum, cantilevered staircases bound with braided sinew and parched hide, and public plazas no larger than a rug, suspended over the drop on poles of petrified salt-cedar. The architecture is dense, layered, ancient, and thoroughly primitive: every lintel bears the gouge-marks of soft copper adzes and fire-hardened flint; every terrace is pinned to the living rock by driven wooden wedges that have baked into the stone until they are indistinguishable from it.

Yet between the walls, the center of the chamber is completely empty. A great, sunlit void of three hundred yards across, filled only with the deafening column of downward-rushing air and the blinding reflection of noon bouncing between whitewashed facades.

And in this vacuum of water, life has gone mad.

It is not green; it is the lushness of parchment, copper, and dry horn. Fastened to every ledge, bursting from every masonry seam and draping across the abandoned ladders, is a suffocating thicket of anhydrous vegetation. It does not drink water; it drinks the wind. Great, cascading mats of beard-grass as brittle as spun glass trail fifty feet into the empty center, vibrating in the gale with a hiss that underlies the roar. There are bladder-ferns whose fronds are dry, inflated sacks of paper-thin cellulose, clacking against one another like gourds; they synthesize their bulk from airborne gypsum dust and the intense, unfiltered sun that pours down the rift at midday. Thickets of sulfur-briar—tangled, thornless, and the color of saffron—choke the lower thoroughfares, their stalks hollow, their interiors filled with fine, running grains of yellow powder that circulate by heat convection instead of sap. It is as thick and rampant as an equatorial jungle, but when you crush it in your fist, it does not weep; it shatters into an aromatic, pepper-scented flour that the roaring wind instantly carries into the white sky.

The city is largely deserted, or perhaps merely cleared for the midday gale. Fauna is almost absent. Occasionally, high in the scaffolds of the dry-reed bridges, one sees the movement of a flat-bodied sun-skink—chalk-white, eyelid-less, its belly plated with horn to skate over the razor-edges of the glass-grass. Once in several hours, a carrion-moth with wings like stiffened linen will launch itself from a high colonnade, caught instantly by the draft, spinning down the funnel like a falling leaf until it catches the stagnant back-eddy near the talc floor and clings to the stone to feed on lichen that feels like rusted iron.

The brightness down here is worse than on the plains above. The limestone of the walls has been ground smooth by wind-borne grit over ten thousand seasons, polished to the luster of tallow. Sunlight enters from the fissure above at a steep rake, hits the northern wall, and splinters into an endless crisscross of reflections, so that the city has no shadows—only varying degrees of glare. It burns the retinas through closed lids. The lime-washed lintels of the primitive stone hovels seem to hum with light, white-hot, vibrating in sympathy with the sound.

The air does not cool. It cycles. A great thermal piston moves through the shaft: every three hours the updraft stalls as the sun passes zenith; the deafening shriek drops to a hollow, organ-pipe moan; the hanging curtains of beard-grass settle into stillness, clicking softly like thousands of dry fingernails; and the choking smell of the saffron briars rises to fill the city with a scent like scorched bread and cinnamon. 

During these brief, ringing silences, from deep inside the honeycombed rock—behind the thousand blind doors of the terrace dwellings—you can hear the dry, manual scrape of copper on stone: the inhabitants, unseen in their deep, dark recesses, patiently chiseling the rock a few grains larger, carving their city deeper into the rib of the earth, waiting for the wind to scream again so they may throw their dust into the hollow.
