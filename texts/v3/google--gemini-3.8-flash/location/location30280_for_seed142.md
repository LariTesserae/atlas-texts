The plateau does not fall off into valleys so much as it sits at the world’s ceiling, a shelf of basalt and polished lead-grey granite lifted so high that the atmosphere has thinned to an unmoving, dry veil. It is warm up here—not a baked, sun-drenched heat, but the deep, insensate warmth of an iron plate left near a buried furnace. The stone radiates a steady, body-like temperature through the soles of anything that walks upon it, never fluctuating by half a degree, day or night. Not that day ever arrives. The sky is an absolute, starless black, a dome of vacant space that absorbs light rather than reflects it, leaving the expanse below it to be mapped almost entirely by heat, touch, and low reverberations.

Eons of imperceptible friction have worn the high shelf flat. If there were once jagged peaks or crags, they have been ground away by some ancient, patient process that has long since stopped; the rock now rolls out for miles in broad, gentle swales of stone, dusted with an ash-fine mineral powder as dry as talc. There is no moisture here—not a droplet of dew, not a trace of steam. The air tastes of scorched zinc, powdered shale, and old grease. 

Rising out of this vast, dark flatness are the works of a heavy, industrial order. They are not clustered tightly, but set far apart with an austere, measured generosity, like monuments on an empty plain. 

Miles of dark space separate colossal blast stoves, cast-iron silos, and gantry towers that reach hundreds of feet into the gloom. These structures are built with massive, rivet-studded plates and thick-flanged girders, their proportions meant for immense pressures and loads that were resolved hundreds of thousands of years ago. The architecture is that of an unending refinery or a city-scale assembly yard, yet it has the solemnity of a tomb. Because there is neither rain nor oxygen-rich humidity, the metal has not rusted into flaking ruin; instead, it has oxidized into a slick, hard hematite skin, dark as plum and completely stable. The ironwork looks less manufactured than crystallized, weathered by epochs into the terrain itself.

Between these monolithic factories run broad causeways of vitrified slag, raised just a few feet above the mineral dust. Overhead, cableways the thickness of tree trunks stretch into the black, sagging with the weight of suspended iron buckets that never move. Nothing rotates; nothing tips. A row of four-story-tall reciprocating engines sits in the open air, their flywheels forty feet across, arrested mid-stroke. The pistons rest silently in their sleeves, sealed by coatings of dry graphite that have kept them smooth for ten thousand centuries.

Sound travels poorly in the thin, dead air. When a sound does happen, it is solitary and small: the dull *tink* of cooling iron shifting a micron against a rivet head, or the faint, papery rustle of dry dust sliding an inch down the flank of a slag-mound. There is no wind. The air does not stir to disturb the fine grey flour that coats the upper surfaces of the beams.

In this pitch darkness, sight is largely useless without a flame, and a flame would reveal only the infinite soot-black surfaces of the frames and the dusty plains between them. Yet the world is not entirely dead. 

Every several hours, something will pass through the open undercarriages of the machinery. They are sparse, solitary things—creatures neither purely animal nor machine, but something born of long, dry stagnation. Some move on many jointed, hair-thin legs of dried keratin and silica, light as thistledown, leaving delicate herringbone tracks in the graphite dust as they search the iron legs for dried grease or microscopic crystals of sulfur. Others are thick, slow-moving segments of segmented scale that lie draped across the warm boilers, drawing the plateau’s eternal heat directly into their carapaces, dormant for decades between a dozen sluggish steps. They make no vocal cries; they possess no lungs to push the dry gas. They communicate, if at all, through the resonant iron itself, tapping a single hard claw against a structural I-beam, sending a pure, ringed pulse through the frame of the city that can be felt through the soles of one's feet three miles away.

The city has settled entirely into its final form. It does not produce anything; it does not consume anything; it does not decay. It is an industrial landscape that has outlived both its purpose and its creators, finally achieving the permanent, unchanging stillness of a geological stratum. The immense machinery sits upon its high, warm plateau like an offering to the dark, holding its breath forever in the quiet, thin air.
