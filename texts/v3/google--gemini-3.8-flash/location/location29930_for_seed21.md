At seven thousand meters above the sea floor, the air should be thin enough to scorch the throat with frost, but here, inside this cleaved shoulder of basalt, it is suffocatingly hot. The air sits heavy, wet, and thick as tallow. 

It is a pocket of land no larger than a sunken stone courtyard or the nave of a roofless chapel, perched out on the tooth of an up-thrust ridge. The rock underfoot is geologically raw, freshly fractured along crystalline shear planes that still hold mirror-bright faces and razored edges; it feels less like an ancient mountain and more like a quarry block dropped yesterday from some impossible ceiling. Yet between these fresh, jagged black edges, water constantly weeps. It does not fall as rain, but sweats straight out of the atmosphere, condensing against the stone in thick, hot beads that trickle down vertical fractures to collect in shallow, steaming cisterns.

The heat is not solar, nor entirely volcanic. In the center of the terrace, half-buried in the shale and slick dark mud, lies a spar of pale, matte-white material—neither ceramic nor metal—curved like a rib bone three meters long. It does not warm the hand if touched, yet the air within three yards of it shimmers violently with mirage. It seems to pump ambient pressure and latent heat into this small notch of the peak, bending the mountain’s cold troposphere away in a persistent, transparent bubble. Where the rib meets the living basalt, the stone has not melted; instead, it has molecularly fused, turning the dark rock into a pale, soapy jade that remains lukewarm to the skin.

Because of this constant, pressurized wash of moisture and tropical heat, vegetation has seized the notch with sudden, desperate speed. It does not form a carpet; the terrain is too young and broken for true soil. Instead, life exists in sparse, massive, solitary architectures. 

Huge, rubbery ferns—the color of bruised plums—sprout directly out of the razor-sharp fissures, their fronds ribbed with translucent channels through which milky sap pulses with an almost muscular rhythm. A single broadleaf vine, thick as a man’s thigh, loops across the cleaved cliff face, anchored by thousands of glass-clear rootlets that seem to dissolve into the rock itself rather than burrowing. Space opens generously between these presences; wide, bare slabs of wet, glassy basalt sit empty between the plants, reflecting the flat, clear daylight of the high sky. The emptiness is deliberate, uncluttered. Each organism has staked out a substantial thermal territory around the fractures where the invisible field hums.

The fauna here is nearly absent, save for things that seem designed rather than born. Occasionally, across the wet obsidian, a creature the size of a wrist moves with mechanical deliberation: something like an elongated chiton, its dorsal plates made of iridescent silica, grazing on the film of pink cyanobacteria that coats the rock. It leaves behind a thin, drying trail of salt. Higher up, suspended in the thermal pocket where the heat meets the high-altitude freeze at the edge of the terrace, small, spherical bladders of biological gas drift like lanterns, anchoring themselves to the cliff face with threads of hair-fine silk when the wind picks up.

It is profoundly quiet. The soundscape is stripped of the roaring wind that ravages the peaks just twenty paces outward; inside the boundary, the air is dead-calm. The only sounds are the steady, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of water droplets falling into deep, stone-lipped slots, the dull hiss of moisture hitting a hot seam of rock, and the wet, expanding sigh of the plum-colored fronds as they unroll toward the noon light. 

Further back against the cliff, where the rock steps upward into a narrow chimney, a second artifact stands. It is a thin, black pylon, barely taller than a walking stick, perfectly square in cross-section. It emits no light, but the water that sheets down the wall behind it diverts around the pylon in mid-air, curving around an invisible hemisphere of force before re-attaching to the stone below. Around its base, the moss is brilliant, synthetic emerald, smelling faintly of ozone and wet river gravel.

The dynamic of the terrace is fast, metabolic, and closed. In the extreme heat and saturated air, leaves sprout, mature, and turn to black, nutritious sludge within the span of days, feeding the slick that nourishes the next generation. It is an incubator balanced on the blade of a knife—a tiny, suffocating tropical garden nailed to the side of a frozen sky, kept alive by engines that do not turn and will not die.
