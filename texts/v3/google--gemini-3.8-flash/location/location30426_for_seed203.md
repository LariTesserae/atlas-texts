The gulf opens downward not into dark, but into an intolerable, freezing brightness.

It is a rift sixty leagues wide and unreckoned in length, sunk miles below the world’s true crust, illuminated from somewhere above by a sheet of cold, incandescent white—an exposed seam of pale, burning mineral or a tear into an atmosphere of pure glare. Because there is no ceiling to catch dust and no floor within human sight, the light travels through the sub-surface void without scattering. It strikes the eye as flat, clinical, and total. There are no hazes to soften distance; things twenty miles off appear as sharp-edged as the knuckles of one’s own hand, only infinitesimally smaller.

The air here is thin, tasting faintly of iron filings and crushed stalks, resting at a steady two degrees below freezing. 

Despite the cold, the living mass is absolute. There is no mineral rock to be seen in the chasm, no granite shelves or basalt pillars. Every feature of the terrain is botanical. Yet it is not a crowded thicket; the space is dominated by an enormous, hollow vacancy. What terrain exists takes the form of colossal, solitary axes of growth: spans of pale, petrified-hard fibrous wood, three or four hundred yards in diameter, that leap across the abyss like suspension bridges stripped of their cables. They run straight, taut as drawn bowstrings, before curving down into the bright white mist of the floorless depths or locking their knuckles into far-off counterparts.

Between these solitary structural ribbons lies five, ten, twenty miles of pure, frozen nothingness. 

The vegetation does not grow by centuries; it grows by seizures. The species here are young, aggressive, and volatile, driven by a biological panic that resembles thermal convection. A bough of fibrous pith, smooth-barked and pale as lard, will lie rigid and frosted for hours in the blinding light; then, triggered by an internal hydraulic surge of sap, it will shudder, scream with the sound of torn silk, and thrust out a fresh, spear-headed shoot sixty paces into the empty air in the span of a single breath. The newly exposed tissues are dark, wet, and steaming with pale condensation that immediately freezes into long, brittle needles of hoarfrost. 

These surges are violently unstable. Stems overreach their own tensile limits in their bid to span the gulf; miles of pale green architecture will suddenly buckle under the load of its own fast-forming ice, fracturing with dry, clean thuds that echo miles across the brightness. When a bough fails, it does not tumble into earth; it falls into the deep white void, cartwheeling slowly through the empty glare until it shrinks to a hair and vanishes.

Moisture exists only along these sudden ruptures. The interior sap of the great chords is saline and resistant to freezing, but where a structural fracture opens, it weeps out in thick, clear beads. These droplets run down the vertical faces of the wood, steaming faintly in the cold, gathering into shallow condensation-pools in the knots and split-grooves before turning into slush. In these shallow, fleeting drains of damp, low mosses—the color of verdigris—scramble for life, living out their cycles in the few hours before the sap seals over and the dry frost reclaims the furrow.

Human presence has the character of grit blown into the seams of a machine. 

High up on the southern flank of an arching rib known only by the rhythm of its vibrations, three huts cling like galls to the bark. They are built of hand-split laths taken from the fibrous outer skin of the arch itself, lashed with wet sinew and pounded flat with river-stones hauled from some long-forgotten surface. The wood does not decay in the cold; it simply blanches and hardens until it can only be notched with flint chiseled against bone. 

The people who live here move with ropes tied about their hips, anchored to crude spruce-pegs hammered into the living cambium. They do not use fire often—the smoke hangs too sharp in the motionless chill—relying instead on fat rendered from the blubber of the pale, jointed things that crawl up from the underside of the trunks during the dynamic surges. These creatures are few, blind, and rubbery, their bellies yellow from scraping the damp cambium of the fractures, moving with the sluggish deliberation of animals that have no natural predators except the violent physics of the plant itself.

In the settlements, language has thinned down to directions and warnings of structural stress. Sound carries with an unsettling, near-mechanical precision through the cold void, but because the gulf is so sparsely furnished, there are few echoes—only the original crack of snapping cellulose or the dry *ping* of a split peg, ringing once and dying instantly in the immense room.

From a ridge of peeled bark at the edge of the nearest habitations, one looks down past one's own boots into thousands of vertical feet of empty illumination. A mile below, two massive root-pillars are currently engaged in a slow-motion collision, forced together by a fresh heave of growth from an invisible wall far to the west. They grind against one another without haste, shedding sheets of translucent frost that drift down like mica shavings through the white glare. There is no soil waiting for them, only the vastness of the light, the cold, and the next sudden, blind reach of the wood.
