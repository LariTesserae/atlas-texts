There is no floor that is not made of pulp and root. The ground does not yield to rock or dirt when dug into; it merely becomes denser, cooler, and wetter, a felted matting of fibrous cords five or ten meters deep, resting upon the drowned plates of an old cast-iron sump floor that hasn't seen the sky in two centuries. 

The air is warm—like the breath inside a laundry—and carries a steady, damp pressure that condenses on every surface faster than it can run off. Droplets form on the underside of broad, ribbed leaves the size of skiffs, hang trembling until they grow heavy as plum stones, and drop into the tangle below with heavy, hollow claps. Everywhere there is water weeping out of wood: trunks that sweat a thin, alkaline brine; swollen knots in the vine-girders that leak warm trickles into the troughs formed by their own bark.

At the center of this enclosure—which spans no more than sixty or seventy paces across, hemmed in by steep, vegetal palisades—sits the carcass of a triple-expansion pumping engine. It is not an abandoned machine in the conventional sense; it has been swallowed so entirely that its iron has become a kind of vascular scaffold. Thick, fibrous creepers, grey-green and hairless like boiled hide, have crept along the rocker arms, insinuated themselves through the packing glands, and forced their way down into the steam cylinders. The machine still runs, but at an agonizing, subterranean tempo. Every three or four minutes, a buried piston strokes with a muffled, concussive *thud* that shakes the root-floor underfoot, causing gallons of warm condensation to rattle down from the overhead canopy in a brief, violent squall. Following each stroke, there is a long, wheezing exhalation—steam venting through pores in the moss that jackets the high-pressure manifold, hissing with the sustained, mechanical fatigue of a locomotive trapped under turf.

The light is daylight, perfectly ordinary and flat, falling through the broken glass of a high, collapsed lantern roof whose iron ribs are now completely webbed with lime-green liverworts. It illuminates a green so saturated it ceases to feel like scenery and becomes an enclosure. There are no clearings, no negative space. The vertical shafts are choked with hanging straps of ficus-like tissue; the horizontal voids are jammed with dense, pale brackets of polypore that have grown together into brittle, shelf-like bridges. Where cast-iron flywheels once spun in open air, they now turn—groaning against immense botanical friction—through dense collars of fibrous lichen that are continually ground to wet meal and continually regenerated by the ambient heat.

Animal life is virtually absent. A fly or a beetle would find nothing here; the plants do not produce nectar or fruit, only endless, structural cellulose, wet leaves that smell of copper filings, and thick, oily gum that traps dust. There are no birds in the upper ribs. The only motion belongs to the plants and the buried steel. Tendrils, thick as an arm and tight as mooring lines, slowly winch themselves shorter over months, drawing cast-iron valve housings closer to the central mass of roots until bolts shear with a gunshot crack that goes unanswered. 

Rust and chlorophyll have formed a joint chemical regime. In the wet rifts where iron lies directly against the cambium, the wood is stained a deep, bruised violet, hardened by iron salts into something that blunts steel chisels. Grease from the old oil cups has been taken up into the sap; the leaves near the machine’s main bearings are slick, blackish-bronze, and water skitters off them in greasy beads that leave shimmering tracks on the pools below.

The noise is not a natural rustle. It is loud, steady, and mechanical: the rhythmic, subterranean thump of the drowned stroke; the grinding scrape of a shaft turning against bark at half a revolution per hour; the persistent, pressurized hiss of wet vapor escaping through bark-wrapped flanges; and the ceaseless, percussive dripping of condensation into condensation. 

In the margins where the old brick retaining walls still push through the vegetation, lime has leached out into long, chalky stalactites that have been engulfed mid-air by ascending roots, sealing them into pale, bone-like knuckles. The place does not expand and does not shrink. It operates within its four walls like an organ in a chest: wet, warm, working constantly, grinding itself away only to rebuild itself out of fiber and wet rust, with no intention of ever stopping.
