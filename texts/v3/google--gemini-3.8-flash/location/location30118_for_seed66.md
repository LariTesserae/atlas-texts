The plateau of Oun sits six thousand meters above the salt-flats, but the air is not thin in the way of mountain tops; it is fat, heavy with moisture carried up the windward cliffs by the thermal plumes that rise off the southern basins. It is warm up here—not the dry, brittle cold one expects of high granite, but a slack, greenhouse warmth that stays trapped beneath a permanent ceiling of high, white, sun-glazed haze. The light that falls through is diffuse, even, and bright enough to make the eyes ache against the bleached lime of the masonry.

There are no trees. There are no weeds in the cracks, no moss, no gray-green scabs of lichen clinging to the shaded sides of the pillars. The world here has never known cellulose. In its place, the city of Oun is constructed entirely of cut basalt, baked clay-tile, pig iron, and the hard, calcified shells of its dead.

The city does not sit upon the plateau; it has become the plateau. Across thousands of square kilometers, the sprawl stretches in unbroken terraces, stepped viaducts, and square-shouldered smelter districts whose chimneys have ceased smoking centuries ago, their flues now clogged with the nests of blind, fat-bodied swallows. The scale is continental. One can walk for three days along the roof-crown of the Great Aqueduct of Saint-Malo and look down on either side into leagues of identical, water-smoothed courtyards, all connected by narrow, dripping sluices.

Water is everywhere, though it rarely rains. It sweats out of the stone. The warm air hits the high, cooled mass of the masonry and condenses into a continuous, greasy film that coats every wall, every handrail, every rung of the iron ladders that descend into the lower vats. It weeps from the joints between the dressed blocks. It collects in gutters six cubits wide, running sluggishly over black river-gravel to turn the waterwheels of the stamping mills. These wheels do not turn quickly. They are four stories tall, built of bolted boiler-plate, turning perhaps once every seven minutes with a low, bone-deep groan that vibrates through the bedrock.

Because there is no plant life to absorb the moisture, the water belongs entirely to the beasts.

The fauna does not merely inhabit the city; it chokes it. The stone surfaces are alive with movement, but it is a slow, heavy, crowding movement. Across the roofs, great herds of flat-skulled carapaced ungulates—creatures the size of oxen, with three-jointed legs and hide like soaked leather—huddle against one another for warmth, their flanks slick with condensate. They do not graze, for there is nothing to graze upon; they sift the constant drift of sulfur-crust and airborne grease that precipitates out of the industrial fog, scraping their broad spade-mouths across the masonry until the brick is polished like jet. 

Above them, the air is thick with wings. Not the nervous, flickering wings of small songbirds, but broad, leathery planes belonging to vast colonies of mast-gulls—white, six-winged carnivores that nest in the decorative cornices and the hollow iron capitals of the gas-line bridges. Their droppings have calcified the façades, turning red-brick warehouses into pale, stalactitic cliffs. Millions of them roost shoulder-to-shoulder, their throats throbbing with a dull, communal croak that drowns out the rumble of the machinery below. When a section of a flock takes flight, the sound is like a carpet being beaten against a cellar floor, a dull *whuff-whuff-whuff* that displaces the heavy air and sends sheets of water splashing from the gutters.

In the streets—which are mostly canals paved with iron slag—the water is deep and warm. Here, the great industrial work continues, though its origins are lost to the inhabitants. Iron still moves through the town. Heavy, steam-driven cable systems, their tallow-greased ropes thick as a man’s thigh, drag iron scows through the locks. The men who work them are gray-skinned and thick-fingered, clad in aprons made from the tanned bellies of the river-worms. They live among the machinery, their stone huts built directly onto the sides of the dry-docks and pump-houses.

The machines do not roar; they throb. The city is mechanized, but the mechanics belong to an old, patient technology. There are no rapid-fire engines, no screaming sirens. Everything moves on massive gears cut from phosphor-bronze, submerged in oil that smells of boiled animal bone. A piston may take forty seconds to complete its stroke, hissing as it draws back against a vacuum seal, releasing a long, warm breath of steam that immediately condenses into droplets on the beards of the teamsters and the chitinous shields of the mud-crabs that scavenge around the drainage pipes.

These crabs—round as bucklers, their legs ending in blunt, peg-like spikes—climb the masonry with the steady persistence of time itself. They are everywhere. A wall that appears black from twenty paces away is found, on closer inspection, to be entirely covered in their shells, all clacking their feeding-palps in the steam-wash of a turbine exhaust. 

Erosion here is not the work of weather, but of bodies. The stairs leading down to the grand basins are not sharp-edged; centuries of claw, hoof, and iron-shod boot have scooped them into shallow, slick-bottomed bowls where water pools two inches deep. The iron balustrades have been buffed down to silver ribbons by the passage of countless leathery flanks. Even the monuments—enormous, blocky statues of forgotten master-founders and water-wardens—have lost their noses and knuckles to the rubbing of the herds, their features rounded until they resemble the boulders in a mountain brook.

The light stays fixed. Through the bright, milky sky, the sun is a broad white disk that casts no distinct shadows, only soft, gray pockets beneath the archways and under the hulls of the beached lighters. It is the light of noon in an iron foundry when the roof-hatches are opened to clear the lime-fumes.

A low, resonant clanging drifts from the deep yards to the west: a bell or a trip-hammer, struck once every quarter-hour to mark the shifting of the sluice-gates. When the sound passes over the rooftops, three million birds do not fly; they merely tilt their heads in unison, their yellow, horizontal pupils tracking the tremor through the mist, before settling their chins back down into their breast-feathers, their wet down drying not at all in the warm, stagnant breath of the high world.
