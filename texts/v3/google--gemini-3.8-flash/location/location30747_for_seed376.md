There is no rock to walk on; the floor of the highland is a packed, compressed fleece of petrified moss and fibrous peat, meters thick, worn into smooth, canyoned contours by a million seasons of cold runoff. The altitude is high enough that the sky retains that deep, hard slate-blue typical of mountain passes, and the air bites—a crisp, dry cold that nips the skin, though down among the growth the atmosphere remains slick with a heavy, sweating condensation. 

Every surface is plant. Trees do not merely grow here; they form the geomorphology. Massive, gray-barked trunks, ribbed like iron flumes, run horizontally for hundreds of yards across the slopes, interlocking to create false plateaus, stepped terraces, and suspended bridges. Below them, in the dim recesses between their girths, hanging curtains of beard-lichen and dense, fibrous tapestries absorb every wandering draft. 

Yet this landscape is violently awake. 

Across the wide shelf of the basin, the flora does not sway—it operates. The dynamic is manic, constant, and strangely mechanical. Immense, lignified galls, tough as tempered steel and the size of steam boilers, shudder and heave in place, driven by internal, pressurized sap-flows that cycle with the erratic rhythm of an unthrottled assembly line. Tough, braided tendrils as thick as steel mooring cables haul themselves through deep slots in the peat, winching colossal burls up the slope only to drop them back into damp chutes with sudden, frantic momentum. Pods with notched, tooth-like edges rotate against one another in tight clutches, stripping away old skin and shredding it into a blizzard of green meal that settles instantly on the damp floor. It is an ecology that behaves like a sprawling, uncontained factory floor—valves opening, massive levers of living wood snapping taut, hydraulic leaves flexing under sudden surges of bitter fluid—all driven by an ancient, hyperactive metabolism.

And yet, it is completely, unnervingly quiet.

The sound is deadened before it can form. The wood is saturated with cold moisture, wet-cured and dense; the cables slide over pads of greasy, lubricating liverworts; the falling debris lands on cushions of sodden felt. When a trunk the size of a railroad tanker groans upward under violent, swelling turgor and cracks its neighboring bough, there is no splintering crash—only the soft, wet *thwip* of shearing fiber, masked instantly by the mountain’s vast stillness. The furious, turbulent upheaval of the valley floor plays out like a silent film: frantic, heavy, churning industry, executed in complete hush.

Through the clear, cold light, the vista extends for miles down the cascading watershed. To the east, where the shelf breaks off toward the lower ranges, the horizon is continually altered by the motion of the mass. A terrace that existed at dawn—crowded with thousands of nodding, cold-blackened succulent blades—buckles and collapses into an underlying cavern of hollowed roots, vanishing beneath an emergent wave of knotted boxwood that surfaces like a surfacing whale, glistening with cold meltwater. 

Animal life here is an afterthought, nearly absent. Occasionally, high in the motionless branches of the dead canopy that crowns the kinetic underlayer, a solitary beetle the size of a fist will cling against the wind, its pale shell crusted with hoarfrost, perfectly still, watching the frantic, soundless churning of the wood below. 

The water comes in brief, cold washes—drifting squalls that drop out of the higher peaks, glaze the thrashing machinery in a brief sheen of ice, and vanish. The machines of wood digest it at once. Conduits swell; the rotational pods speed up, their silent teeth blurring in the midday sun; the great ground-flumes pulse with cold, dark sap, carrying on a labor that has no purpose, no architect, and no end, locked in its own cold, violent equilibrium.
