The stone has been beaten flat for so long that it has forgotten grain and fault. It is a single, uninterrupted slab of dark iron-gneiss, miles wide, ground down by some pre-historic sea until it holds no cliffs, no hills, not even rubble—only a continental pan scored with shallow runnels no deeper than a thumb. Over this expanse, water moves constantly, not in torrents, but in a vast, cold film an inch deep, sliding south-southwest with the patient drift of a glacier that has melted into grease.

The air sits low and heavy, smelling of cold phosphorus and wet slate. It is cool enough to numb bare ankles in minutes, condensing against any raised surface in beaded rills that instantly join the general sheet-flow. There are no plants. Nothing with leaves, nothing with roots that pry; the rock offers no purchase, and the soil was washed off an epoch ago. Instead, the stone is slick with a thin, leathery felt—blackish-olive, rubbery underfoot, composed of colonial amoeboids and calcifying slime that digest the mineral grit suspended in the mist.

What fills this world to bursting is not quietude, but an overwhelming, physical wall of noise. 

The sound is not wind, for the air is sluggish and thick. It is the sound of life without silence: millions of heavy, carapaced animals moving through shallow water, scraping horn against horn, drumming hollow bellies against stone, and rasping their breathing-plates against the damp air.

They are *gull-ticks* or *plate-skimmers*—low, dinner-plate-broad invertebrates with calcified disc-shells the color of lead. They carpet the flats in shifting constellations, hundreds of thousands to a mile, yet spaced loosely enough that the grey stone constantly glints between them. They do not walk; they scull through the sheet-water on dozens of jointed, spatulate oars, and where their serrated undersides brush the polished gneiss, they produce a high, screeching friction that, multiplied across the basin, becomes an unbroken, metallic scream. Beneath that scream sits the thrumming: a wet, rhythmic concussive thud produced by the *bull-worms*—pale, arm-thick segmented burrowers that dwell in the ancient boreholes drilled deep into the rock. When the tide of cold melt swells, they hammer their muscular tail-plugs against the stone to signal the rising floor, turning the bedrock into an acoustic soundboard that vibrates straight through the soles of one's feet.

The light is permanently stalled in the hour after dusk. The sky is an undifferentiated plate of purple-tinted cloud, hung so low it seems to graze the backs of any upright creature. What illumination exists does not come from a sun, but from the low sheen of the water itself, which carries faint, milky streaks of luminescent bacteria flushed out of the deep cracks. In this dim, silvered twilight, shadows do not cast; everything is edged in dull, wet pewter.

Yet people—or something very like them—live here.

Their presence is sparse, marked only by things that will not wash away. Every mile or two, rising like chipped teeth from the mirror of the water, stands a weir: two low arms of stacked, dry-laid gneiss slabs, perhaps two feet high, guiding the plate-skimmers into wedge-shaped traps. Beside these traps sit the shelters. They are simple, low mounds made of the interlocking ribs of colossal filter-feeders that must have drifted across these flats when the water was deeper. Over these ribs, thick sheets of hammered skin—stitched with gut and weighted at the perimeter by river-grooved cobbles—keep out the drizzling mist.

These dwellings do not gather into villages. They stand isolated, solitary outposts tethered to the stone by sheer friction. Outside one, a wooden-handled chisel with a chipped chert head lies submerged in three inches of moving water; near it, a drying rack made of lashed poles holds ribbons of grey meat, dripping steadily into the sheet-flow below. The people who made them wear heavy boots of oiled stomach-lining, tied off at the knee with tendon, and they move across the flats in a wide, sliding skate, their ears plugged with wads of bitumen to spare their brains the agony of the stone’s scream.

Everything here is in motion, but the motion goes nowhere. A school of skimmers churns through a shallow depression, their spiked carapaces clattering like dropped cutlery, chased by long, eel-shaped things that strike from the margins with wet slaps. A geyser of silt-less, near-freezing brine erupts from a vent three feet wide, pulses six times, and subsides, adding its volume to the sheet that drains inexorably toward the unseen rim of the world. 

The air vibrates against the teeth. You can taste the stone in the dampness—a faint, iron-cold tang that settles on the tongue like a wet coin. To stand here is to feel that the world has already happened, that all the mountains have already fallen, that all the forests have already rotted into black paste, and that what remains is simply this: the stone, the water, the deafening friction of things eating things in the dark, and a lone hand-chiseled cairn holding itself against the tide.
