The air here does not move. It has the dry, mineral weight of an attic that has baked beneath an unshielded roof for centuries, yet there is no sky above it—only an overhang of sheared, unpolished basement rock so high that its ceiling creates its own horizon of blackness. It is warm, steadily and uniformly warm, radiating not from a single furnace or vent, but from the dense, unyielding mass of the stone itself. It is a dry, baking heat that pulls every trace of vapor from the skin, leaving the tongue tasting of ground feldspar and chalk.

Beneath the feet, the ground has never been softened by rain or smoothed by river wash. The terrain is young in the manner of a freshly broken plate: vast plains of razor-edged slate, step-faults that drop forty cubits in clean, sheer vertical slices, and immense forests of crystalline basalt columns that have cleaved along pristine hexagonal seams. Everything is sharp enough to draw blood at a careless brush. There are no scree slopes, no mounds of rounded gravel; where rock has fallen, it sits in huge, jagged wedges with edges keen as newly knapped flint. 

The scale is oceanic, but hollow. Sound travels poorly through the dry, static air, muffled by the sheer geometric complexity of the space. Miles of stone prisms interlock, creating vertical labyrinths so densely packed that a traveler must squeeze sideways between sheets of volcanic glass, only to emerge onto a shelf overlooking a void that stretches beyond the throw of any light.

In this dark—a pitch so total it presses behind the eyes—the landscape is inhabited by a dense, unhurried biomass that relies nothing on green matter or sun. There are no leaves, no mosses, no damp molds. Instead, the life here is built of bone-dry chitin, keratinous plates, and fibrous, silica-stiffened flesh. 

Underneath the basalt shelves, thousands of dry-skinned creatures cling in silent clusters. They resemble segmented urns or flattened trilobites the size of shields, their bellies lined with microscopic scrapers that harvest the thin, powdery bloom of inorganic salts and sulfur-crusts that coat the cold fractures. They do not scuttle; they creep by fractions of an inch each day, their carapaces rubbing against the rock with the faint, rhythmic sound of dry leaves sliding over flagstones. Predatory things, long-limbed and spindling like desiccated harvestmen, stalk between the crystal pillars on stilt-like claws tipped with flinty spurs. They have no eyes; their heads are swollen bulbs of thermoreceptive pits and sensory bristles that read the micro-disturbances of the dead-calm air. Because the heat is uniform and absolute, their bodies generate no steam, leave no scent of dampness, and produce no liquid waste. Everything that dies here simply dries, turns brittle, and is ground to dust by the trampling of the next generation.

Signs of intelligence appear, though they are sparse and deeply strange, scattered across this endless, broken floor. 

Here and there, in the lee of monumental fracture-walls, one finds low enclosures built of stacked slate. They are dry-stone works, assembled without mortar, their walls composed of razor-sharp shingles balanced with uncanny precision. Nearby, the rock shows the marks of crude, manual fabrication: deep, repetitive grooves where hard hematite nodules have been used to saw away at softer shale, fashioning shallow basins or crude, heavy picks. 

There are ropeways, too, spanning the narrower crevices—not made of hemp or fiber, but braided from the tough, dried dorsal tendons of the great shield-bugs, fastened to the stone by stone wedges driven deep into the fault-lines. In small clearings cleared of sharp shards, one finds the remnants of tool-making sites: piles of flakes struck from black glass, bone-dry scrapers, and simple hand-hammers formed from heavy iron-rich stones that must have been carried from leagues away. These small occupations are not villages; they feel like transient stations or ancient observation posts, inhabited by tool-users who have mastered only the physics of the lever, the wedge, and the sharp edge, living in the same pitch-black equilibrium as the crawling herds they hunt.

The most unsettling property of the place is its permanence. It exists in an unyielding state of rest. There are no seasons, no drafts of wind, no dripping water to carve canyons over epochs, no earthquakes to upset the delicately stacked slates. The temperature never fluctuates by so much as a fraction of a degree. The warm stone holds its heat without change; the darkness never relents; the silence is broken only by the perpetual, papery rustle of millions of dry legs rasping over clean, unbroken mineral edges. It is a world caught at the very beginning of its geological birth, yet entirely dead to time—vast, sharp, crowded with silent life, and utterly motionless.
