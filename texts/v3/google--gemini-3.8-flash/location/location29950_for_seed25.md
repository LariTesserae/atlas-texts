The floor of the depression lies hundreds of feet below the prevailing rim of the country, spread out across leagues of worn, grey-black dolomite. It is a wide, shallow basin whose slopes have been rounded by immense spans of wind and friction until no sharp edges remain; every ridge is a smooth, heavy knuckle of stone, polished to a dull grease-sheen. There is almost no light. The sky above, if there is a sky, is choked off by a heavy, cold stagnation of fog and high vapor, leaving the valley in a perpetual, soot-colored gloom where sight reaches only a few paces before forms dissolve into silhouette, then into nothing.

Yet the basin does not sleep, and it is never still.

The primary fact of this place is acoustic, long before it is visual. The sound is an absolute, physical pressure that fills the ears until the skull aches: a roaring, multifaceted turbulence composed entirely of biology. It is the sound of millions upon millions of joints clicking, segmented bellies dragging across damp rock, the rasp of rasps, the rhythmic expulsion of air from spiracles the size of sewer pipes, and the wet, slapping tread of uncounted soft-footed things. It sounds like an endless freight train derailing into gravel, layered over an ocean that churns not water, but cartilage and grease.

There is no soil here, and nothing with a leaf has ever taken root. In the crevices where the dolomite splits, there are no mosses, no ferns, not even the pale fuzz of ordinary mold. The world is entirely mineral and animal. What fills the damp air is not the breath of trees, but the vast, collective exhalation of a fauna so dense that it forms its own shifting strata upon the bedrock. 

They are everywhere, stacked and interwoven. In the lowest folds of the valley, where cold condensation collects into shallow, slick-bottomed hollows, the ground itself is invisible. It is carpeted three or four deep in slow, plated crawlers—beasts like broad, flattened shields of blue-grey horn, twelve feet long, possessing dozens of stubby, spade-shaped legs that grind steadily against the stone. They move without apparent destination, grazing upon the slick micro-crust of mineral salts and the dead bodies of their own kind, their mouthparts tucked underneath, working with a wet, steady gnash. 

Above them, balancing on the broad carapaces of the crawlers, move thinner, taller things: spindly tripods and quadrupeds with joints that bend in three directions, their hide the color of cold tallow, entirely hairless and damp to the touch. They possess no eyes—there is nothing here to see—but their snouts are crowned with blooming rosettes of quivering, wet cartilage that taste the chill air for the scent of rupture, shedding, or rot. They are scavengers and harvesters of the living, delicately nipping off the sensory antennae of the larger crawlers or stripping away the ribbons of sloughed, gray skin that hang from the flanks of the herds like wet parchment.

Because there is intermittent moisture—a cool, clinging mist that condenses on cold chitin and drips in heavy, rhythmic patters onto the stone—the entire basin is perpetually slick. Pockets of standing fluid, black and viscous from churned dung, pulverized shell, and cold mineral seepage, sit trapped in worn rock bowls. When the wind moves, which it does in sluggish, irregular drafts, it smells intensely of cold ammonia, copper, crushed insect husk, and the wet, flat odor of living skin.

The erosion here is ancient, but it has not been carved by rivers. The gullies that crease the valley floor are biological troughs, three feet deep and twenty feet wide, worn into solid dolomite by the abrasive bellies of the great herds passing along the exact same paths for thousands of generations. The rock inside these grooves is as smooth as river glass, dark and slippery with a lacquer of biological oil. 

Occasionally, rising out of the gloom like the ribs of sunken ships, are the slow-movers: towering, pillar-legged masses of muscle and bone that stand sixty cubits high, their skin like cracked slate. They stand stationary for weeks in the open flats, barely swaying, their broad heads tilted upward into the cold mist. They are living condensation traps; the moisture of the low sky beads along the intricate, corrugated channels of their backs and drains down toward their mouths in steady, trickling rivulets. A hundred smaller species cling to their flanks like barnacles on a whale, biting into the thick, bloodless hide or fighting for position along the drainage grooves to lap at the runoff before it reaches the ground.

Everything is in motion, yet nothing changes. A massive, sluggish economy of flesh recycling flesh, entirely closed, sustained by the sheer sheer inertia of its own biomass and the trace minerals dissolved in the mist. When one of the great pillar-beasts finally collapses—a dull, thudding earthquake that sends a shockwave through the stone, swallowed almost instantly by the din—there is no period of waiting. The swarm surges. Within hours, the site of the fall is a boiling mound of blind, clicking, gnawing life, hundreds of creatures deep, tearing through hide and cold gristle with a frantic, methodical industry until the smooth, wet dolomite is cleared once more, save for a pale stain that the mist will wash away into the next hollow.

The light never brightens. It remains an ink-stained dusk, cold and damp, where the only orientation is the steepness of the ground beneath one's feet and the direction of the deafening, grinding tide of the herds.
