The stone has not had time to blunt. At four thousand meters above whatever floor this world rests upon, the mountain ridges do not roll; they are raw cleavage planes of black basalt and dark blue slate, sheer as cleaved flint, their edges so keen they split the low clouds into clean ribbons. These summits were not pushed up by slow centuries—they were torn upward recently, geologically speaking, and the fractured facets are still unpolished, dry of dust, crystalline and mirror-flat where the rock broke along its grain.

Yet despite the bitter, near-freezing chill that stiffens the knuckles within minutes, the rock does not stay dry. The air is so thick with pressurized mountain moisture that everything sweats. Water does not fall as rain here; it condenses directly out of the ambient gray light onto every vertical face, welling from invisible pores in the stone, running in clear, sheeted laminates down thousands of feet of sheer cliff. It gathers into thin, high-velocity flumes that slide over precipices without ever hitting bottom, atomizing into the cold draft halfway down.

Between these monumental, blade-like peaks lies an abyss so vast that whole weather systems could rot within it without touching either side. The distance between one wall of rock and the next is eight, ten, twelve miles of sheer atmospheric vacuum. The space is largely empty—not a desert of sand, but a desert of vertical air, a blue-gray volume so enormous that the mind cannot properly map its depth.

Suspended inside this void, anchored directly into the freshly broken faces of the mountain walls, is an urban apparatus of staggering, mechanized ambition.

It is organized like a city, but a city stretched across a canyon so broad that its districts must communicate by high-tension cables thick as old trees. Steel truss frameworks—braced with millions of cold-hammered rivets and coated in a dull, leaden primer—cantilever hundreds of yards out over nothingness. There are streets here: iron-grate avenues that look straight down into two leagues of mist, multi-tiered thoroughfares running along the stone facades, and civic-scale galleries of black-lacquered cast iron. Immense, multi-story turbine halls are bolted directly into the basalt, their intakes fed by the continuous, violent weeping of the cliffs.

Everything runs on water and electricity. Copper buss-bars as broad as sidewalks run along the underside of every catwalk, hummed through with staggering current, their insulator bells caked in verdigris and wet frost. Colossal steam exhaust vents and hydraulic counterweights cycle in place, though there is no smoke—the power is derived from the pure kinetic drop of the mountain’s own sweat, channeled through leagues of bolted penstocks. The architecture is that of a great municipal manufacturing center: warehouses with sawtooth roofs of frosted wire-glass, tiered tenement blocks with rhythmic rows of blank, glazed windows, water-treatment towers like iron cathedrals, and rail terminals whose tracks simply end at the lip of the gulf, pointing out toward the opposite, miles-distant peak.

And yet it is completely, utterly empty of breath. There are no operators in the control pulpits; no birds wheel through the vast gulf between the trusses; no beetles scuttle through the damp bolt-holes; no fish inhabit the cold, iron-lined reservoirs that cling to the rock faces. The fauna parameter is flat zero. The city was built to standard human scale—doors have brass turn-handles at waist height, staircases have standard eleven-inch treads, the control consoles are fitted with Bakelite knobs that fit an ordinary palm—but no shoe has ever worn down the diamond-tread steel of the stairs. 

In place of animals, there is only the vegetation, which thrives in the cold, hyper-oxygenated, endlessly running water. It is not a sprawling jungle, but a heavy, alpine lushness that coats every non-moving surface in inches of saturated green. Enormous liverworts, thick as harness leather and the color of dark pine needles, climb the damp iron columns, encasing the structural steel in cold, spongy sheaths. From the undersides of the suspended streetways, long curtains of pale, beard-like lichen and emerald-tinted mountain moss hang down fifty feet into the empty air, dripping steady beads of condensation into the gulf. In the corners of every iron terrace, where turbine oil has emulsified with the mountain water into an amber sludge, thick mats of cold-tolerant alpine succulents and giant, fleshy saxifrages flourish, their pale leaves rimmed with tiny rimes of ice that never melt and never thicken.

The most terrifying thing about the place is its sound. It is nearly deafening, but it is entirely without variation. It is a massive, multi-tonal, unchanging roar. 

The sound is composed of three elements that have reached an immutable balance: the high-altitude scream of the wind slicing across the sharp basalt crags; the planetary rush of millions of tons of water sliding down iron flumes into turbine rotors the size of roundhouses; and the low, chest-thumping 60-cycle vibration of the dynamos that never falter, never change pitch, and never cycle down. The sheer volume of the place drowns out thought, yet after an hour, the ear ceases to interpret it as noise; it becomes the acoustic atmosphere, a permanent pressure against the skull.

Nothing happens here. The light is that of a clear, pale mid-morning through high cirrus clouds—brilliant enough to see the razor-fine corners of the furthest crags miles away, but utterly lacking in warmth. The sun neither rises nor sets; the shadows cast by the immense truss bridges onto the vertical cliffs do not lengthen by an inch. The water flow does not swell with rain or diminish with drought; the penstocks carry precisely the same tonnage of water from moment to moment, spinning the shafts at an invariant RPM. The cold does not drop into hard frost, nor does it yield to thaw; it remains fixed at precisely that knife-edge where liquid water flows smoothly over stone while leaving a thin, permanent sleeve of glaze on the edges of the iron.

It is an engine of continental scale operating at full, roaring capacity inside an empty sky. It is completely new, pristine, unweathered, yet it feels as though it has stood in this exact microsecond of its operation for an eternity, producing power for an empty grid, lighting streetlamps that illuminate only the wet, beautiful ferns and the sheer, bottomless drop below.
