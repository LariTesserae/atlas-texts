The chamber is bounded by sixteen cylindrical stanchions of cast iron, each sixty feet in diameter, rising out of a lower cloud deck and terminating in an open-latticed dome thousands of meters above the world’s actual crust. Though the entire assembly sits at an altitude where human blood would ordinarily boil or freeze in the thin blue cold, the air here is heavy, warm, and thick with tallow-scented vapor. It is an artificial pocket: a solitary mechanical cradle, roughly the breadth of a city square, perched atop a spindling pylon that vanishes into the abyss below.

Inside this boundary, there is almost nothing. 

The void between the iron pillars is vast, hollow, and dizzying. One can gaze from the lip of a service catwalk across five hundred paces of sheer emptiness before the eye strikes the next structural member—a lonely, vertical conduit sweating brown water into the air. There is no floor, only an open geometric web of rusted catwalks, gusset plates, and counterweight pits that drop straight into the white sky. Yet despite this skeletal sparseness, the life that clings to the iron is aggressively, damply lush.

Ferns with fronds as stiff and thick as vulcanized rubber pack the seams between rivets. Broad-leafed aroids, their veins stained the color of motor oil, wrap themselves around steam-bypass pipes, drinking the constant, tepid condensation that trickles down the flanged joints. Because the iron never cools—warmed from within by subterranean draft-furnaces churning miles below—the plant life grows in a state of suspended, unhurried summer. The mosses are not fragile green velvet, but dense, fibrous mats three feet deep, spongy with oxidized grit and lubricating grease. When steam vents hiss open, releasing deliberate, sighing clouds into the void, the foliage simply trembles, absorbs the moisture, and grows heavier.

The sound is not an ambient noise; it is an absolute medium. It is a deafening, bone-conducting roar that never ceases and never shifts pitch. It comes from the core of the central pylon: the churning of immense, low-velocity drive-shafts, each as wide as a galleon’s hull, grinding through planetary reduction gears to rotate an unseen axle below. The air vibrates so violently that the condensation forming on the iron lattices does not fall in smooth drops, but shivers into a fine, warm mist that drifts sideways across the emptiness. Human speech would be obliterated before it cleared the teeth. 

Yet this thunder belongs to a system that barely moves. A counterweight the size of a tenement slides downward along grease-slick guide rails at the rate of an inch per hour. A bevel gear eighty yards across completes a single revolution with the coming and going of the seasons. There is no frenzy here, only the staggering momentum of iron so massive that centuries of friction have only polished its teeth to a mirror sheen.

In the long stretches of air between the beams, the fauna maintains the life of the void. There are colonial swifts, featherless and clad in leathery, slate-grey hides, that nest inside the hollow bolt-holes of the bracing struts. They do not flap; they launch themselves into the acoustic roar and glide on the thermal updrafts created by the boiler jackets, circling the emptiness in wide, leisurely ellipses. Below them, clambering along the wet undersides of the steel beams, are long-legged, shell-backed crabs that feed on the iron-loving lichens and the oily sludge of the drip-pans. Their movements are so measured they seem part of the metalwork, waiting hours for a single vapor-fly to alight within reach of their blunted pincer-tips.

There is no soil. There has never been an inch of stone or loam up here. If you cut into the root-system of the great climbing figs that strangle the exhaust chimneys, you find only the cold imprint of rolled mill-marks and the foundry stamps of long-forgotten consortiums. The greenery has adapted its chemistry to the metal; roots secrete a faint, alkaline acid that keys them into the grain of the steel, locking the living canopy to the machine so thoroughly that it is impossible to say whether the ferns are holding the rusted braces together, or if the iron is slowly turning the plants into brass.

Light falls into the chamber from the high, open ribs of the dome—clean, indifferent daylight that illuminates the suspended emptiness. It glints off wet cables swinging over the drop. It shows the vast spaces where nothing has been built, the deliberate vacancy left between functional parts to allow the monstrous machinery to exhale without bursting its frame. In the center of this immense clearance, suspended by four diagonal tie-rods that vanish into the mist, hangs a solitary lubrication cistern, dripping warm, yellow fat onto a crown wheel below with the deliberate, patient cadence of a water clock. The drop falls through a hundred feet of clear air, catches the sun for an instant, and strikes the tooth with a microscopic splash lost in the deafening, eternal din.
