The col sits at seven thousand meters, wedged between two blunt horns of black hornblende, where the sky loses its blue and turns the dark, bottomless violet of the upper troposphere. The air is too thin to hold heat, yet the sun here is an unblinking, blinding scald. Every surface facing south-southeast is bleached white with glare; every surface turned away is plunged into ink-dark, razor-edged shadow that hovers thirty degrees below freezing.

It is a pocket of extreme density—no larger than the floorplan of an old parish church, hemmed in by sheer drops into cloud-seas that never quite rise to this level. The floor is not flat, but a crowded forest of vertical stone lamellae: slate and quartz ribbons tilted on end by an ancient tectonic cramp, standing like thousands of filed teeth or the thin pages of an open, rotting book. They stand inches apart, four to six feet high, crowding the space so tightly that a body cannot walk in a straight line, but must sidle and squeeze through cold, slate-lined corridors.

There is not a blade of moss. Not an encrusting lichen has taken hold on these blades; the ultraviolet is too pure, the drying power of the wind too absolute. The rock is utterly bare, mineral and raw, its edges rounded not by water-flow, but by millennia of dry, scouring ice-grit. 

Yet, within this mineral thicket, it is damp.

The dampness is a localized, relentless machine driven by the sun. Thin rime—formed overnight from the breath of moisture that creeps up the windward cliffs—coats the eastern faces of the stone plates. As the solar disc clears the eastern horn, the light does not warm the air; it warms only the dark iron-stone itself. The frost does not have time to melt gently; parts of it sublimate into faint, smoking wisps of vapor that rise into the purple sky, while the rest collapses into cold, greasy beads of liquid that race down the vertical cleavage planes.

These trickles are not left to chance. Embedded throughout the stone maze are small, deliberate interventions of hand-beaten bronze and slate shingles, wedged into the natural fissures. Hand-length gutters of beaten red copper, greened with crusts of carbonate, bridge the gaps between the stone teeth. They catch the rapid morning sweat before it can plummet into the abyss or re-freeze in the shadows. 

The tech is sparse, manual, and patient. Weighted cords woven from dried mountain-gut and coarse mammalian hair hang in the narrowest vertical gaps, acting as drip-guides; surface tension holds the icy droplets to the fibers, directing them down into shallow basins hollowed directly out of the quartz veins with primitive pecking stones. These basins are no larger than a cupped pair of hands, each covered with a loose lid of chipped shale to keep the wind from tearing the water away as vapor. 

The air smells of ozone, pulverized rock, and the wet, metallic flat-taste of stone that has just been washed with melt. 

The dynamics of the place operate on a tight, rapid clock. As shadows shift across the floor, the dampness vanishes almost instantaneously. A stone plate in sunlight will bleed water from every crack; five minutes later, as the needle-shadow of an adjacent spire crosses it, the runoff freezes instantly into a skin of clear, glass-brittle glaze, cracking with an audible *snap* like breaking dry pine needles. 

Aside from these tiny fractures and the occasional hollow *clink* of a droplet hitting a copper channel, the col is intensely quiet. The high-altitude wind is constant, but it has nothing broad to catch against—it merely sifts through the thousands of stone teeth with a low, breathy sibilance, like steam leaking through a distant valve.

Deeper in the maze, near the base of the western horn, the stone ribs have been broken down intentionally to form a low, roofless bivouac or shrine, no larger than a horse stall. The walls are dry-stacked fragments of the same hornblende, packed without mortar, their seams caulked with frozen mud brought up from some vastly lower world. Inside, three heavy copper needles, thick as a wrist and tapered to a bodkin point, are driven deep into the bedrock. They act as thermal pins: their sun-struck tips conduct heat deep into the floor, keeping a single, central basin from freezing solid until mid-afternoon. 

A heavy, pitted stone mallet rests beside this sump, its wooden handle bound tightly with copper wire, gray with age and smooth from palm-grease. There are no bones here, no dropped tools or discarded garments. The presence that made this place is felt only in the tight, balanced economy of the catches: every gutter is pitched precisely, every weight is hung to resist the dominant westerly shear, and the water—sparse, clear, and bitter with dissolved mica—moves through the cold stone network drop by single drop, entirely indifferent to the great, empty light pouring down from above.
