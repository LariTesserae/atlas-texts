At this altitude, the air has the dry, thin bite of crushed brick, though the temperature inside never falls below the heat of a baker’s hearth. 

It is a vaulted redoubt, no wider than the nave of an abbey, perched on the highest needles of an ancient, unnamed upland. Yet nothing of the original rock face remains visible. Every square inch of the floor, the walls, and the ceiling is hand-fitted stone: coarse, uneven slabs of sun-cured sandstone and dry-stacked slate, chiseled with clumsy, blunt adzes countless thousands of years ago. There is no mortar. The pressure of the mountain’s peak and the sheer mass of the stacking hold it together in a dense, suffocating grip.

The interior is choked with construction. It is not an open hall, but a labyrinth packed almost to solid displacement. Thousands of thick, square pillars of rough-cut river stone and baked mud tiles rise from the floor, spaced so tightly that a man could only pass sideways between them, brushing his shoulders against both surfaces at once. Suspended between these pillars are heavy, teardrop-shaped pendulums of carved granite, lashed to the crossbeams with thick, braided sinew and hemp that have long since desiccated into brittle, petrified ironwood. None of them swing. They hang dead, locked in an ancient, calcified equilibrium, jammed against one another like grain in a silo.

The heat does not rise and fall; it is stored deep in the density of the masonry, soaked up from the unrelenting exterior glare and bled uniformly into the enclosed air. It smells of hot shale, dry flour, and old lime. There is not a drop of moisture anywhere. Even the dust has been ground down by aeons of sonic pressure into a fine, talc-like silt that coats the floor in a dead, unmoving layer three inches deep.

And through this tight forest of stone roars an invariant, deafening sound.

It does not gust, drop, or stutter. Outside, an endless, high-altitude jet streams across the peak, forced through thousands of primitive, narrow venting-slits cut into the windward masonry. These slots are angled precisely against interior acoustic channels—hollowed-out stone baffles, dry ceramic tubes, and the hard, resonant bellies of the carved pendulums. The result is a monolithic, continuous drone that fills the chamber to the absolute limit of air’s capacity to carry noise. It is an unvarying, bone-crushing chord, somewhere between the scream of an iron foundry and the roar of a falling mountain, locked permanently at the exact same pitch and volume. 

The sound is so loud that the air itself feels viscous and heavy, pressing against the skin like a warm, vibrating blanket. Yet, remarkably, nothing moves. The vibration is so steady, so perfectly absorbed by the tight-packed stone and the interlocking wedges, that not a single grain of dust dances in the air. The sound does not destroy the room; the room was made purely to contain and stabilize it.

Light enters only as dull, ochre needles through the wind-slits, filtered and dimmed by the depth of the masonry walls until it is merely a brown, twilight haze. In this gloom, the ancient age of the work reveals itself: every edge of every roughly hacked block has been rubbed smooth. Not by water or human hands—neither has been here for millennia—but by the microscopic, ceaseless shearing of air driven by the sound. The granite blocks have the rounded, oily sheen of soapstone; the mud bricks have been polished until they resemble dull leather.

There are no insects, no lichen, no spiders in the crevices. The acoustic violence and the absolute absence of water make biological life impossible. Even the decay of the original rigging has stopped; the ropes are simply dry, carbonized casts of what they once were, frozen in the dry heat. 

Between the forest of pillars, nestled into hand-carved niches at waist-height, are thousands of small, spherical stones of polished hematite. Each sits in a cupped depression just slightly larger than itself, held fast by nothing more than gravity and the tight acoustic choke of the chamber. They do not rattle. They rest in their hollows like heavy dark eyes, absorbing the sound, warm to the touch, having sat in the exact same orientation through entire geological ages.

The ceiling hangs low, bowed inward by the weight of the masonry above it, a ceiling built by people who understood leverage and mass, but nothing of arches or steel. It presses down upon the pillars with an immense, silent force that opposes the deafening scream of the air flutes. 

Nothing will happen here. The wind outside never turns; the vents will not wear away for another million years; the dry stones will not slip their friction. It is a completely fabricated summit, primitive in its craft but infinite in its stillness, holding a deafening scream inside a stone box that refuses to tremble.
