At this altitude, the clouds do not drift overhead; they shear directly through the structural framework, torn to ribbons by hundreds of square kilometers of black iron, galvanized conduit, and dripping cross-bracing. 

It is a basin built entirely across a mountain pass, perhaps five thousand meters long and plunging down through tiered levels of industrial works until the valley floor—if there was ever an unpaved floor—is lost entirely beneath stacked decks of corrugated iron, riveted bridge-work, and concrete cisterns. There is no exposed stone or native soil anywhere. Even the vertical faces of what might have once been granite cliffs have been sheer-faced with bolted steel lagging, weep-holes, and continuous vertical raceways for cabling and heavy piping. 

Yet it is profoundly green. The moisture is perpetual. High mountain cold condenses instantly against the cooled flanks of the machinery, weeping down stanchions in steady, silent sheets. Every surface that can hold an accumulation of silt, coal-ash, and dead biomass has been claimed. Ferns—thick, leathery, dark-leafed species suited to cold mists—burst from the joints between structural I-beams. Liverworts and saturated mats of sphagnum moss cushion the walkways three inches deep, swallowing footfalls and muffling the impact of falling water. Creepers with thick, fibrous stems wind around hydraulic lines, matching them gauge for gauge, their leaves glossy and dripping. 

Despite the sheer mass of quiet foliage, the landscape is in constant, violent motion. The turbulence is mechanical and hydraulic, a restless, unceasing choreography of weight and fluid. Massive cast-iron sluice gates, forty feet across, rise and fall on counterweighted chains to reroute torrents of icy condensation and runoff. Overhead, automated gantries slide through the mist on grease-packed rails, shifting iron cradles filled with aggregate, coal, or dense trays of sodden moss from one tier to the next. The system is forever balancing itself against its own mass and the weight of the water it gathers. A deck will suddenly tilt three degrees on massive ball-and-socket swivels, dumping a lake of trapped rainwater into an adjacent culvert with a deep, subterranean slosh, only to be winched back into equilibrium by groaning pneumatic pistons.

This turbulence produces surprisingly little noise. The atmosphere—cold, thin, and choked with mist—deadens sound within a few dozen yards. The sheer volume of wet green growth acts as an acoustic baffle of extraordinary efficiency. The sudden drop of a ten-ton steel counterweight into its well sounds only like a dull, heavy thump, as though struck through several mattresses; the churning torrents of water channeled through the steel weirs are reduced to a soft, pervasive hiss, like static between radio stations. The great steam vents, wrapped in miles of wet canvas and soaked ivy, exhale only a low, rhythmic sigh that vanishes into the cold draft rushing up the pass.

The light is thin and slate-colored. At midday, it filters weakly through the high fog and the lattice of five or six superimposed levels of overhead decking, casting a dim, uniform twilight over everything. There are no sharp shadows, only gradients of charcoal, zinc-gray, and deep, saturated emerald. Here and there, clusters of incandescent filament bulbs in brass, vapor-tight cages burn with a faint amber pulse, their globes smeared with algae and water-droplets, illuminating nothing beyond the rusted flanges they are bolted to.

Life here has adapted to the metal lattice. Animal life is exceedingly scarce, suppressed by the cold and the endless shifting of the steel floor. Occasionally, a single water pipit or a mountain dipper will dart through the maze of vibrating girders, skimming an inch above an open flume to pluck a caddisfly larva from a seam of submerged rust. In the deep hollows beneath the main turbine housings, where the waste heat of massive dynamos keeps the iron lukewarm, small, colorless slugs navigate the slick coatings of petroleum jelly and wet iron oxide. Otherwise, the machinery and the vegetation share the basin alone.

Erosion here has reached a state of mechanical maturity. The rust does not crumble; it forms a hard, black magnetite crust under the constant cold flush of water, sealed by grease and the humic acid of decaying ferns. The structures have stood long enough for the vegetation to incorporate the steel into its root systems, and for the maintenance systems of the valley—automated, cyclic, relentless—to adapt to the weight of the plants. Girders bow slightly under the load of saturated peat, settle into their brackets, and remain there, steady in their deflection.

Every few minutes, the valley undergoes a localized shudder. A bank of automated valves trips somewhere two miles to the north, a chamber vents its gray mist into the lower tiers, and a chain of steel buckets, each the size of a train car, begins to churn through an angled trench, lifting tons of sodden gravel and wet leaves into an upper separator. It happens almost without a sound: just the soft clink of manganese links underwater, the slow rush of displaced moisture, and the vast, unhurried shivering of thousands of wet fern fronds adjusting to the shift in draft. Then the machinery rests, the clouds close back over the gap, and the water resumes its quiet, dripping descent through the iron floor.
