The plateau sits so high that the sky feels thin and close, yet the air carries an uncharacteristic, resting warmth—a heavy, insulated heat that does not bake the stone, but rather keeps it at the temperature of an animal’s inner flank. 

This is a peneplain shaved down by hundreds of millions of years of wind. There are no jagged crags or precipitous drops in the interior; the mountains that once stood here surrendered their peaks eons ago, leaving behind an undulating table of pinkish-gray gneiss that stretches for leagues. The rock has reached an end-state of erosion. It does not crumble. It has been polished into wide, sweeping domes and broad, shallow basins that curve like the bottom of dried seas, seamless and calm.

Despite the height, it is not arid. Every afternoon, warm, dense mist rises from the lower troughs of the continent, creeping over the lip of the high ground in slow, tidal curls. It does not bring rain so much as a pervasive, grease-like dampness. Water collects in the microscopically shallow depressions of the granite: slick, millimeter-deep lenses of dew that catch the daylight and turn the vast, open expanse into an archipelago of silver mirrors.

Vegetation here has lost the race for space. It exists only as dry, fibrous encrustations—scabs of slate-colored lichen and tiny, rubbery nodules of liverwort that hide in the joint-lines of the rock. The world is not green; it is the color of wet bone, liver, and lead.

Instead, the landscape belongs entirely to the bodies.

Because the plateau offers neither soil nor canopy, life has simply laid itself down upon the stone, layer over layer, until the fauna constitutes the topography itself. For miles in any direction, the granite floor is nearly obscured by an immense, sprawling pavement of things that breathe. 

They are slow, thick-plated, and extraordinarily quiet. The dominant forms are disc-shaped, soft-shelled organisms the size of shield-bosses, overlapping like the shingles of an endless, low roof. They are pale horn, dun, and dull bruised purple. They do not fight for territory; they fuse slightly at the margins, sharing moisture through permeable rims, their carapaces coated in the condensation that glazes the plateau each morning. Beneath them, long-legged, blind detritivores—pale as peeled almond wood—move through the narrow crawlspace between the rock and the shell-layer, tending the communal under-skin, feeding on shed mucus, dead neighbors, and windblown dust.

Further out across the shallow swales, larger things lie half-submerged in the damp. Great, oblong mounds of gray muscle, smooth as river boulders and entirely hairless, rest in tightly packed colonies of thousands. They resemble stranded seals or immense slugs, but their anatomy is older and simpler: they have no distinct faces, only broad, slit-like intake vents that pulse once every few minutes, drawing in the humid air and expelling it with a soft, wet *chuff* that carries only a few yards. They are so closely crowded that when one shifts its bulk to drink from a dew-slick, a ripple of compressed flesh travels half a mile through the herd, taking twenty minutes to die away into stillness.

Above this dense, living floor, the sound is astonishingly faint. With hundreds of thousands of living pounds per acre, there are no calls, no warning shrieks, no courtship songs. The acoustic atmosphere is composed entirely of mass respiration: an enormous, collective murmur of damp valves parting, the faint scrape of chitin on quartzite, and the occasional soft, wet slap of shifting meat. It is a quiet so saturated with presence that it feels louder than wind.

Dotted across this biological plain are the remnants of stone-work, completely unmarred by tools, suggesting hands that simply gathered and leaned what was already loose. Low, circular walls of rounded cobbles, three or four stones high, form corrals that contain nothing. Some of these rings are twenty paces wide, their centers long since invaded by the disc-creatures, who use the slightly sheltered interior to stack themselves three deep. Elsewhere, lone slabs have been stood on end, braced by wedges of white quartz; the stone is deeply polished at hip-height, rubbed smooth by generations of thick-hided bulks dragging their sides against the edges to slough off parasites or old skin. There is no fire here, no ash, no metal. The structures do not suggest dwellings so much as resting perches or current-breaks, built to deflect the creeping damp or the slow drift of the herds.

The light stays level, diffuse, and warm, filtered through an atmosphere that seems slightly thicker than it ought to be at this altitude, held down by the sheer metabolic output of the plain. The world here does not progress toward any evening or crisis; it has found a final, heavy equilibrium. The bedrock will wear no further; the creatures cannot become more numerous without crushing one another; the water arrives as vapor and departs as breath, recycling itself endlessly between the gray stone and the warm, wet skin of the plateau.
