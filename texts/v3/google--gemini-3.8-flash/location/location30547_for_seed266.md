The horizon does not bow down; it rises. At this altitude, seventy leagues above the sunken oceanless basins of the lower crust, the world is a series of stacked tabular massifs so vast that their weather belongs only to them. The air is warm—a dry, baking, mineral heat that radiates from the pale feldspar floors—and completely devoid of moisture. It does not carry the sting of frost that extreme elevation usually demands; instead, it has the calm, dense stillness of a kiln that was banked hours ago and left to cool at its own imperceptible pace.

There are no rivers, nor dry riverbeds. Water has not shaped this place for epochs, if it ever existed at all. The erosion is ancient, smooth, and circular, carved entirely by the steady abrasion of airborne talc and fine-ground mica. The stone is rounded like tallow: pillars of cream, pink granite, and chalk-soft gypsum that taper into butter-smooth knobs, separated by flats of hard, compressed slate that stretch flat and unbroken until they vanish into the heat-shimmer.

Scattered across these shelves are the settlements. They are not temporary camps or survival outposts, but heavy, permanent cities of rust, red brick, and black iron. Their layouts are orderly and loose. A district may consist of three dozen stepped zinc foundry-domes grouped around a shared pneumatic siphon, followed by two miles of empty, swept slate, and then another cluster: long rows of tenements with thick adobe walls, interconnected by elevated iron catwalks and insulated conduits. The chimneys do not puff aggressively; they leak a thin, sluggish grease-smoke that smells of burnt tallow and copper filings, drifting sideways in the faint, unhurried drafts.

Power here is mechanical and quiet. Towering cast-iron A-frames, tethered by braided steel stays, support aerial tramways that reach out into the vast distances between massifs. Their heavy, counterweighted buckets move at the speed of a walking man, carrying raw bauxite, dry asphaltum, and cakes of sulfur from the quarries to the processing rings. Deep within the bedrock, massive flywheels turn on frictionless pressurized bearings of powdered graphite, storing energy generated by the slow, thermal expansion and contraction of the plateau’s basalt ribs. When they turn, the ground does not shake; it hums with a frequency lower than hearing, felt only through the soles of thick-soled leather boots.

Between the urban clusters, the flats are not empty. Life is plentiful, though none of it is green. In the absence of chlorophyll and sap, the plateau’s ecology runs on sulfur-cracking and the metabolism of dry, brittle hydrocarbons. 

Across the open slate move herds of *carillons*—lumbering, six-legged grazers covered in segmented plates of porcelain-hard enamel. They have no mouths in the traditional sense, but broad, scraping rasps beneath their bellies with which they plane away the micro-crystalline crusts that weep from the stone. Behind them, they leave pale trails of crushed powder, which the human gatherers follow with push-brooms to collect industrial scouring lime. Above, hanging motionless on the buoyant, warm air, are kites of translucent cartilage—predatory sheets ten yards wide, ribbed like umbrellas, that lower sticky silk threads to snag the small, blind scorpions and dry-beetles that scurry through the expansion joints of the tramway foundations.

There are also the oil-dogs: thick-set, blind things with skin like vulcanized rubber, living commensally along the edges of the rail yards. They sleep in the warm exhaust vents of the compressor houses, waiting for the maintenance crews to clear the paraffin grease-traps. When the tallow-fat is scraped into the gutters, they fall upon it without sound, snapping their scissor-jaws in a frantic, grease-slick heap, only to disperse back into the crevices of the foundation pillars to digest for days at a time.

A human living here works in deliberate rhythms. The dryness demands conservation of movement; sweat is a failure of pacing, a loss of fluids that can only be replenished by drinking from the heavy, sealed zinc canisters brought up from the deep hydrocarbon cracking stills. In the workshops, machinists in canvas smocks work without oil on their hands, using tallow and graphite cakes to lubricate the gears. Lathes turn slowly, shaving long curls of yellow brass that ring like wind chimes as they fall into wicker baskets. 

The light is broad and yellow-white, completely unfiltered by clouds, casting shadows so sharp and solid they look structural—as if the iron towers were propped up equally by their steel legs and their black geometric counterparts on the ground. 

Far to the south, where the plateau finally shears off into the Great Drop, an aqueduct-sized conduit made of riveted sheet-lead runs across the open desert. It carries no liquid, but compressed air, driven by deep subterranean bellows. Every three miles, an escape valve vents with a patient, rhythmic *chuff*, releasing a puff of white talcum that settles into the lap of a sunning, carillon calf, which does not blink, but merely shifts its plates to let the dust settle between its joints.

Nothing here hurries. The cities do not expand with boomtown urgency, but grow the way crystals layer against a cooled surface: a new iron foundry is riveted to the old one; a fresh tram-cable is spliced with hemp and lead; another generation of blind kites hatches in the high iron trestles, unfurls its sails, and rises silently into the endless, dry, warm blue.
