The plateau is high enough that the horizon falls away on three sides into pale, empty air, yet it does not feel exposed. It is cupped within a shallow, saucer-shaped depression of dark, rounded stone—an amphitheater no larger than the footprint of a village hall, roofed only by the sky. 

Despite the altitude, the air is thick, slack, and distinctly warm. Heat rises through the floor from deep fractures below, turning the rain that falls here into an eternal, lukewarm broth. Water does not rush; it seeps. It weeps from the low basalt lips that ring the clearing, running in continuous, skin-deep films over broad slabs of slate-colored rock that have been softened by centuries of friction into the shapes of sleeping bellies and shoulders. Everywhere, the ground is either submerged by half an inch of tepid, glass-clear runoff or coated in an emerald felt of liverworts so ancient they have formed a smooth, uninterrupted pelt over the rock.

There is a strange, architectural spaciousness here. Though the vegetation is extraordinarily lush, it does not crowd. The plants stand apart from one another, isolated like statuary in a vast, water-paved hall. Great, singular ferns rise at wide intervals, their fronds broader than a man’s outstretched arms, heavy-ribbed and glossy with perpetual condensation. Between them lie wide, vacant corridors of wet, gray pavement where the water reflects the steady, neutral daylight. There are no vines tangling the air, no underbrush choking the knees; each plant commands its own broad circle of light and damp, its roots spreading across the rock like melted wax before finding purchase in a hair-thin fissure.

The silence is almost total. It is broken only by the sound of gravity acting on water: the faint, rhythmic *clink* of a drop falling from a high frond into a stone pocket, the muffled sliding of a sheet of moisture over a mossy incline. There are no birds. There are no frogs calling from the margins of the pools, no water-striders dimpling the film, no midges hovering in the warm vapor. When a leaf dies, it does not rustle; it becomes waterlogged, heavy, and sinks through the warm shallows to lie flat against the dark stone, where it bleaches slowly, over years, undisturbed by larvae or worms.

In the center of the depression, half-drowned in the clearest pool, lie objects that do not belong to the geology. They are scattered, very few, and utterly quiet. A wedge of dense, black dolerite, its edge ground down by patient hands into a perfect, blunt chisel; several smooth river stones, brought up from riverbeds thousands of feet below, showing the percussion rings of deliberate, heavy strikes; a slab of split shale with a trough worn deep into its center by the friction of a pestle. 

Near the southern lip of the basin, wedged tightly into a natural cleft of the living rock, sits a hafted adze. Its handle—carved from some dense, oily heartwood—has not rotted in the warm water, but has swollen and darkened to the color of soot, gripped by the stone as if planted there. Its cordage of plaited bark fiber is intact, stiff with mineral crust. These things were not discarded in flight; they were laid down on clean ledges or set into specific notches with the heavy deliberation of tools placed at the end of a long afternoon’s labor. But the hands that shaped them did not return, and the basin has held them in its slow, warm custody for so long that lime and silica have begun to form delicate, translucent collars around their bases, cementing them to the mountain.

Because there are no grazers, the plants grow into peculiar, monstrously balanced architectures. One low shrub, standing solitary upon a raised table of basalt, produces thick, paddle-shaped leaves that grow in stacked pairs like roof tiles; each leaf has a shallow groove down its spine that channels rainwater precisely into the stem-socket of the leaf below it, a closed, self-hydrating mechanism that needs no wind to function. Without insects, pollination occurs through the water itself: during the hottest hours, when the shallow pools warm slightly, tiny white sacs of seed detach from the lower margins of the stalks and drift across the submerged stone like oil droplets, moving millimeters an hour toward the rock fissures.

The light stays level, diffuse, and white. The warm mist hangs three inches above the stone, an exhalation that neither rises into a cloud nor burns away. It is an established world, closed in its circuit, entirely without urgency. The stone dissolves by microns; the water carries the stone into the ferns; the ferns drop their heavy, water-fattened leaves into the shallows to become stone again. The black adze rests in its crack, its lashing wet and taut, waiting through the centuries for an impact that will never fall.
