The brine here does not freeze, though it sits at three degrees below zero. It is too thick with metal salts for ice, too dense to move quickly, holding its volume against the bottom of a stone-flanked trench with the stubborn inertia of mercury. 

The entire quarter fits within the footprint of an ironworks and the tenements that once fed it—scarcely three hundred paces from the shear-face of the eastern basalt to the silted sill where the tramway cables drop off into black water. But within that pocket, the density is suffocating. It is an urban knot that has swallowed itself: three-tier cast-iron housing blocks, heavy-flanged penstocks, transformer boxes sheathed in green lead, and overhead catwalks crisscrossing like a cat’s cradle of rust. Everything is set on top of everything else, bolted, riveted, and subsequently drowned.

The flooding did not arrive as an accident or a catastrophe; it arrived long enough ago that the architecture has ceased to fight the pressure. Cast-iron columns three feet thick have taken on a skin of black magnetite, hard as flint and slick to the touch, which shields the core from further decay. The iron has found its truce with the saltwater. Where timber lintels once rotted out, the space was filled not by collapse, but by the slow, sedimented packing of boiler slag and silicate dust, forming a composite masonry that is colder and heavier than the stone beneath it.

It is nearly pitch dark. The sun never pierced this depth even before the silt settled, but the dark here is not empty. It has color: an abyssal, copper-leached green that lives only inches from the surfaces of the machinery. This glow is galvanic. The drowned city is an immense, sluggish battery. Dissimilar metals—bronze impeller wheels bolted to wrought frames, zinc sacrificial bars still bolted into the masonry of the boiler pits, lead-jacketed cabling coiled across the alleys—generate an endless, faint electric soup. Along the seams where brass meets cold iron, tiny, star-shaped sparks occasionally detach themselves, rising an inch or two into the black before extinguishing in the brine.

Sound does not travel as air-borne noise would; it travels through the teeth and the soles of the feet as a physical displacement. There are no splashes, no grinding squeals. The water deadens acoustic frequency, reducing the entire dynamic of the district to a low, monolithic thrum—the 0.1 of a world whose heart is a drowned flywheel. Far down in the floor of the machine-hall, beneath four stories of submerged stairwells, something enormous and balanced still turns on sleeve bearings kept slick by seventy years of animal tallow and heavy mineral grease. It moves once every few minutes: a quarter-revolution of a fly-press or a walking beam, shifting tons of brine with a dull, concussive *shuck* that ripples through the sediment on the balconies without lifting a single grain into a cloud.

Around this turning, a pale, narrow ecology has formed. The vegetation belongs to the family of things that do not need the sky: ribbons of liver-brown weed, tough as cured hide, sprout exclusively from the caulked seams of the riveted boiler-plates, feeding on sulfur-reducing bacteria that crust the leaks. They do not sway; the water is too heavy for drift. They simply hang downward like wet horsehair, their tips encrusted with pale rosettes of iron carbonate. 

Of animals, there are almost none that have names. Now and then, a creature the length of a finger—flattened, blind, made of colorless cartilage like an unboiled knuckle—crawls out of an exhaust chimney, grazes the greasy slime from a row of hexagonal nuts, and retreats into the cold housing of a submerged dynamo. They move without hurry. The cold has slowed their pulse to four beats an hour. If two meet along a copper conduit, they do not fight; one simply crawls over the back of the other, its small, clawed paddles ticking silently against the lead casing, before they are lost again in the vertical shadows.

Between the buildings run the street-canyons, barely wide enough for two handcarts to pass. Overhead, where an open sky should be, the tramway trestles have trapped an inverted ceiling of grease: an ancient layer of mineral oil, purged from the hydraulic lines when the water first rose, that floats against the underbelly of the steel plate, clear and amber-toned, preserving the rivets above it in absolute, un-pitted freshness. Looking up, one sees the underside of the industrial floor mirrored upside-down in a slick, still pool of petroleum, trapped against the stone ceiling five fathoms down.

The work of the place continues, though there is no one to take the tally. The water rises three inches during the cold cycle, forced up through the floor grates by some distant hydraulic head, and then sinks three inches as the salt-sludge cools and contracts. This tiny, unyielding breath pumps bellows that are twenty feet across, buried in the masonry walls; they intake the icy water and expel it through banks of copper cooling-fins, keeping the trench several degrees colder than the surrounding sea. 

A heavy, grease-bitten silence holds the street. A wrench dropped from an upper staging eighty years ago does not rest on the cobbles; it hangs suspended halfway down an alley, caught in a dense, gelatinous lens of super-saline brine that has settled between the tenements like a pane of plate glass, waiting for the salt to dilute enough to let it fall the final six feet.
