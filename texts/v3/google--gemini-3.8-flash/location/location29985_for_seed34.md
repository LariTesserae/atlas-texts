The plateau sits at an elevation where the sky feels thinned of dust, holding a hard, luminous clarity even as low-hanging banks of cloud drag their bellies across the roofs. 

From the lip of any terrace, the city extends so far in every direction that it generates its own minor weather. To the east, miles away across a sea of tiered, slate-colored masonry, a localized downpour falls in a straight, sheer curtain of grey, silvered by the high sun. Elsewhere, the stone flats steam faintly where the moisture is drawn up by the wind, creating creeping fingers of ground-fog that weave through the lower lanes and vanish over the drops.

It is an urban continuum constructed entirely of dark, fine-grained granodiorite and river-smoothed basalt, fitted without mortar. The masonry is dense and massive: colossal lintels bridging gaps between windowless four-story blocks, narrow alleys paved with flags five feet thick, stepped streets climbing the natural gradient of the mountain shelf in broad, shallow flights. There are no iron hinges, no pulleys, no wheels or conduits of spun metal; every door is a slab set in a deep, ground-stone socket, every roof a heavy corbel of overlapping flags weighted against the uplift of the high-altitude winds. The density is relentless. Structures lean into one another for support, row upon row of tight stone cells, stepped platforms, and parapets, their edges blunted slightly by centuries of rain, yet standing in total structural balance. It is a city made of mountains rearranged, retaining the mountain’s permanence.

Water is the restless principle here. The highlands above feed it continuously through hairline fissures and ancient, gravity-fed flumes cut directly into the bedrock beneath the foundations. Water weeps from the joints between cyclopean blocks; it slicks the risers of the great staircases and fills sunken courtyards with black, sky-reflecting pools that spill over notched sills into the streets below. The whole city is a drainage network, an intentional labyrinth of channels, cisterns, and stepped spillways. Long, shallow gutters run down the center of every thoroughfare, carrying cold, clear run-off toward the outer terraces, where it drops away in long, fraying tails of spray into the abyss.

Despite the wetness, plant life has gained little ground. The altitude is too punishing, the cold air too austere, the surfaces too hard. There are no trees, no shrubs, no weeds cracking the paving flags. What vegetation exists is thin and closely pressed: dark, gelatinous films of algae lining the wet chutes; pale green and rust-orange crustose lichens that have mapped out concentric territories on the dry upper faces of the monuments; and, here and there in the deep recesses of unlit lintels, a sparse fur of damp liverwort that drinks the perpetual spray. 

The sound of this vast place is nearly nothing. The air carries an extraordinary quiet—a silence so dense it seems to possess mass of its own. It is not an absolute deadness, but a profound acoustic vacuum in which the only textures are the faint, multiple rhythms of falling water: the soft *plip* of drops falling fifty feet from a gutter into a deep reservoir; the thin, shearing whisper of sheet-drainage sliding over polished flagstones; the far-off, hollow boom of an aqueduct emptying into a cavernous sub-street basin. Occasionally, the wind catches the corner of a thousand stone angles at once and produces a flat, breathy sigh, then settles back into stillness.

Fauna is rare, almost an anomaly. Now and then, a small, blunt-headed lizard, the color of wet shale, slips into a horizontal joint to avoid the shadow of a passing cloud. High above, solitary swifts with stiff, swept-back wings cut arcs through the cold light, hunting whatever microscopic midges hatch from the cisterns, their flight entirely soundless from the ground. There are no packs of animals, no calls, no signs of recent habitation by anything warm-blooded.

Yet the environment does not feel dead; it feels rigorously engaged in its own mechanics. The dynamic here is hydrological and atmospheric. The city breathes through the passage of clouds through its corridors. In the morning, the stone is cold, weeping condensation that forms rills along every chiseled groove; by midday, the high-altitude sun strikes the platforms, warming the wet stone until the lanes are filled with shimmering updrafts; by late afternoon, mountain squalls roll over the rim of the world, scouring the plazas clean and filling the great holding tanks to the brim. The water arrives, runs its course through miles of carved stone, and departs into the air or over the edge, while the city remains—settled, patient, and vast under the pale, empty blue.
