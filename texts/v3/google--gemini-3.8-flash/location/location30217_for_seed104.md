The ocean does not begin at the base of the world; it rests upon its shoulders.

Here, at an altitude where air ought to be thin and cold enough to shatter lung-tissue, there is instead a warm, amber-tinted sea. It sits upon a planetary tableland so ancient that every spire, crest, and tooth of stone has been filed down into vast, undulating whalebacks of granite and gray schist. There are no cliffs left, only leagues-long slopes that rise and fall by inches across horizons so broad the curve of the planet is visible through the liquid gloom. 

The water is not salt, nor is it sweet. It is thick, soft, and faintly alkali, kept at the temperature of fresh milk by a subterranean pulse that radiates through the stone floor. It does not crash or foam. It has no true surface in the familiar sense; looking upward through the twilight, the amber water merely pales into a glowing bronze mist, where the liquid layer thins into an endless, saturated troposphere. The boundary between sea and cloud is not a line, but a hundred-meter transition of foam-webs and buoyant steam. Because of this, the light never arrives directly. It filters down as a heavy, golden-green luminescence, casting shadows without edges and lending every submerged object the soft, blurred silhouette of an old memory.

The vegetation here has had several geological ages to solve the problem of living in a warm, weightless bath. It is astonishingly dense, choked with continuous, rising architectures of bronze and viridian pulp. From the smooth basalt plains grow ribbon-barks—colossal, hollow stems as thick as towers, rising straight toward the upper steam without branches, tethered to the bedrock by root-systems that have melted into the stone through slow chemical digestion. Between these trunks drift curtains of maiden-moss: translucent, pale green tapestries forty feet long that hang suspended by internal pockets of warm methane. They don’t root; they weave through one another, creating vast, floating marshes at mid-depth, so tightly felted that they form false floors upon which silt and decaying biomass settle in quiet, yards-thick cushions. 

Beneath these floating meadow-mats, the water is dark, warm, and entirely still. If one disturbs the moss, it does not tear so much as yield, releasing small, slow-rising pearls of aromatic gas that smell faintly of bruised mint and hot shale.

The reality here is loose at the seams. It is an upland that should not hold a cup of moisture, yet holds an ocean. At the geographic margins of the tableland, where the mountain shelf finally ends, the water does not pour downward into the lowlands in a cataract. Instead, it curves. The water’s edge clings to the lip of the rock in an impossible, bulging meniscus kilometers high, bending the light of the lower sky into prismatic crescents. From below, looking up from the dry, distant deserts of the world, one would see what appears to be a bruised, shimmering wall of blue glass resting atop the highest peaks, weeping only a fine, perpetual drizzle that evaporates long before it hits the ground. Within the plateau itself, currents run contrary to any discernible gradient; warm rivers of denser, iron-black water snake across the lake-floor, cutting deep trenches through the softer green-silt, occasionally looping over themselves like twisted braids before lifting into the middle depths and flowing horizontally through the open water without dispersing.

Movement is constant, but it is the movement of a massive, slowly digesting belly. The thermal columns rise from the bedrock in plumes a mile wide, carrying rafts of detritus and floating seed-pods the size of coracles toward the upper light. When they reach the interface where the water turns to steam, they cool, collapse, and sink back down in a slow, pale snow of vegetable lint that takes days to touch bottom.

Of living animals, there is almost no sign. Now and then, something large and pale—a creature like an unrolled scroll of cartilage, blind and finless—undulates through the ribbon-bark forest, rippling its margins with a motion so slow it can only be detected against the grid of the trunks. These solitary beings do not hunt; they seem to browse upon the microbial coats that film the stone, or simply filter the warm liquid through bellows-like slits along their sides. They produce no sound. 

The quiet of the plateau is absolute and physical. It is not an absence of sound, but an active deadening. Water under this high, strange pressure conducts vibrations too swiftly for echo or timbre to survive; a stone rolled down a granite flank produces only a sudden, dull thump that resonates directly in the marrow of the sternum, followed by an immediate, vacuum-like silence. The rustle of miles of floating moss rubbing against itself is swallowed whole.

Scattered across the floor of this high ocean are the works of something that understood the water before it came, or perhaps built them while the stone was still dry. They are not cities, nor temples. They are solitary, circular pavements of dressed, greenish stone, forty or fifty paces across, set flush into the primordial bedrock. Most are buried beneath half a fathom of silken silt, their outlines revealed only by the distinct ring of plum-colored ferns that favor the mineral leaching from the mortar. In two or three places, enormous lintels of water-smoothed conglomerate bridge the space between natural granite knuckles, their surfaces carved with shallow, thumb-wide channels that run in spirals toward central drain-holes. When the deep thermal rivers sweep over these pavements, they draw small vortices of clear, silt-free water down through the holes, creating soft, dimpling depressions on the sea floor that stay open for centuries, breathing in and out with the long, tectonic heave of the high ground. 

Nothing rusts here. The lack of air and the alkaline chemistry of the warm broth preserves everything in an eternal, soft-edged present. A seed dropped a thousand years ago drifts along the bottom beside a seed dropped this morning, both plump, both waiting for a season that does not exist in this drowned sky.
