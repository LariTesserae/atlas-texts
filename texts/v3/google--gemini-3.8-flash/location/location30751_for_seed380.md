It is called the Sump-Nave, though no one who named it comes down here anymore. 

The chamber is shaped like a vaulted parish church, perhaps sixty paces from the dry end—where the brickwork steps up into a blind bulkhead—to the drowned sluice gates at the southern curve. The ceiling is ribbed with segmented cast-iron arches that have oxidized into a dull, velvety orange-black, each bolt-head rounded over by eighty years of lime bloom and condensed boiler-sweat. The air is cool—not freezing, but the persistent, dead-cellar cool that finds the joints through wool—and so thick with water that breathing feels like drinking through a felt cloth. Every surface runs. If you hold a palm against the vitrified brick of the wall, you do not feel moisture as a film; you feel the brick exhaling a cold, steady grease that tastes faintly of saltpetre and whale tallow.

The floor is not floor, but a drowned terrace of flagstones submerged under six to twelve inches of dead-flat, mineralized water. It rarely ripples. The water does not smell foul; it smells ancient, alkaline, and metallic, like water that has rested inside a galvanized bucket for thirty summers. At long intervals—perhaps every forty minutes—there is a soft, wet *thung* from somewhere beyond the brickwork, deep in the city’s lower ribs, and the water level rises by the thickness of a coin, pauses, and settles back down through unseen weep-holes.

The space is largely empty of furniture, possessing the sparse, echoing geometry of an engine house stripped of its tenders. What remains are the fixtures that could not be pried out: three gargantuan beam-housings rising out of the water like the stelae of a drowned wharf. Their iron skin is two inches thick, pitted and scalloped by decades of soft-water drip until the metal resembles sea-sponge. One of them still works, if "work" describes a movement so patient it is nearly imperceptible: its walking-beam, thirty feet of counterweighted iron truss, tilts three degrees to the south across the span of an afternoon, sighs through a pack of soaked hemp gaskets, and spends the night returning to the level. 

Because of this rhythm, the fauna does not fear it. The life here is dense, small, and colorless.

Thousands of blind river-lice—flat as shillings, porcelain-white, with legs like spun glass—graze the rust-fur that coats the submerged machinery. They cling in clusters so tight they look from a distance like patches of white lichen or damp curd. Where the copper cooling-sleeves dip into the black pool, the water is slightly warmer, perhaps two degrees above the surrounding scum, and here the lice give way to colonies of spindle-leeches: semi-translucent, six-inch ribbons that do not swim, but anchor one suction-mouth to the hot metal and wave their tails slowly in the draft created by the beam's descent. 

In the high, dry corners of the iron ribs, where the brick is merely wet rather than running, there are birds—or things that were once starlings, dropped down the ventilating shafts three generations back and adapted to the eternal twilight. They have lost their purple sheen; their feathers are gray and greasy as wet cobwebs, their eyes enlarged into dark, convex beads that take in the dimness without blinking. They do not sing. They communicate through clicks—short, dry percussions made with the tongue against the bill that echo off the ironwork like clock-escapements. They eat the grease-crust that oozes from the dead gearboxes, and the small, blanched crickets that tumble continuously from the weeping drainage grates forty feet above.

Light does not enter from windows. High up, where the ceiling meets the foundation stones of whatever street-grade works sit above, there are five narrow slots—coal-chutes or pressure-relief flues—capped with perforated iron plates. Through them falls a pale, lead-colored rinse: the light of an overcast city, sifted through road-grit, soot-sacks, and three feet of greasy cobbles. It does not illuminate the floor; it simply forms gray columns through the suspended moisture, revealing the lazy, spiral drift of lint, iron dust, and fungal spores.

The flora is discreet, knowing its place in the dark. It does not form green carpets. Instead, there are liverworts the color of cold grease that follow the mortar-lines like black veins, and a strange, rubbery polyp that grows exclusively upon the lead-caulked seams of the high-pressure pipes. This growth is pale amber, semi-firm, and smells of turnip skins; it absorbs the slow-leaking tallow from the joints and, over decades, hardens into a substance indistinguishable from gutta-percha.

Everything here operates under a contract of exhaustion. The machines were built by an empire of high pressure and soot to tear coal out of the strata, but down here, abandoned to the seepage, they have been adopted by the basement ecology. An iron balance-wheel, twelve feet across, sits half-choked in a drift of silt and iron shavings; its hollow spokes have become the warren for a species of dwarf eel that is utterly white, lacks nostrils, and spends its twenty-year life cycle browsing the velvet rust off the teeth of the gear. When the walking-beam groans through its hourly three-inch stroke, a single, heavy bubble of sour air—trapped under the cylinder piston since the year the boilers went cold—rises to the surface of the pool, parts the duckweed-like scum of louse shells, and breaks with a sound like a wet boot coming out of clay.

Then the silence returns, laid over the vast, calm, cool basin, until the dripping from the ceiling finds the exact rhythm of the iron once again.
