The floor extends without curvature until the atmosphere itself softens it into a pale, heat-bleached haze. 

It is entirely artificial, yet entirely vacant of machinery. The substrate is a pale, unglazed ceramic—or an extraordinarily fine-grained, hydraulic lime-stone cast in situ—laid down in massive, interlocking polygonal plates that measure hundreds of paces across. The joints between them are razor-sharp and unrounded; there has not been enough weather or passage of time to chamfer the edges or scour down the faint, parallel trowel-marks left during whatever immense pouring formed them. It is brand-new land, geologically speaking, or perhaps even mechanically finished only yesterday, though whatever hands or apparatus laid the plates are absent, leaving only the sheer, deliberate inertia of the floor.

The scale is oceanic. Because the terrace is so level, so uninterrupted, and covers thousands of square leagues, it produces its own localized meteorological drift. The sun overhead is standard, hard, and yellow-white, driving surface temperatures up until the ceramic radiates a dry, heavy heat that hovers two feet above the deck. 

Yet the system is not dry. Every several miles, the perfectly planar floor dips by mere centimeters into vast, shallow catchments—depressions designed to collect runoff, though there are no pipes or grates, only gentle, graded slopes that guide water by gravity alone. The moisture comes from the daily thermal cycle. By midday, the heat off the vast expanse pulls air inland from some unseen margin, lifting humid drafts that condense rapidly into high, white-bottomed cloud sheets. When they break, they release heavy, warm, brief squalls—sudden downpours that drop inches of rain in ten minutes, drumming loudly against the bare stone.

The water does not sink in, for the stone is dense and impermeable. It sheets across the expanse in a mirror-thin film, hissing as it meets the sun-warmed material, filling the air with a faint, clean mineral steam that smells of hot dust and slaked lime. The sheets of water slide slowly toward the shallow sumps, gathering in glassy, perfectly still pans no deeper than an ankle, reflecting the towering cumulus above. Long after the rain moves on, the surface remains slick, damp, and evaporating, caught in a perpetual balance between violent solar drying and sudden precipitation.

Life has barely noticed the place yet. In the seams between the massive plates—hairline gaps packed with coarse, unwashed sand—a few airborne spores have taken root. They are small things: pale, fleshy pioneer succulents no larger than knuckles, and flat crusts of olive-drab lichen that spread along the joints like damp chalk. There are no trees, no shrubs, no grass, and across a march of three miles one might only see four or five of these tiny green nodes clinging to the grout. 

There are no animals. No insects hum over the pools; no birds wheel under the rain clouds. The air is too clean, the ground too sterile, having only recently cured. 

Because of this, the soundscape is reduced to bare physics. There is the slow, rhythmic *clink* and *tick* of the ceramic slabs shifting against one another as they expand in the midday glare and contract beneath the cold deluge of a squall. There is the flat, continuous hiss of rain hitting flat surface over a front five miles wide, advancing like a wall across the pavers. And between these events, there is an absolute, deadening silence, amplified by the sheer horizon, broken only by the faint, low-frequency whisper of wind moving over an earth that offers it nothing to snag upon.

Every structural element suggests purpose, but none suggests habitation. There are low, stepped berms that run straight for leagues, perhaps intended to divide water flow, and massive, flat-topped monoliths of the same poured white material rising ten feet above the grade at intervals of a day’s walk—unadorned, unperforated, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across the damp tiles. They are not ruins, because they have never been broken; they are simply the beginning of something that was prepared on an unimaginable scale, damp and warm and unpopulated, waiting in the sunlight for its wear to begin.
