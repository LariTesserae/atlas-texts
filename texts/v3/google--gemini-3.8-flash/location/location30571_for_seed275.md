There is no stone here, and there is no damp. 

The floor of the basin—stretching out for miles beneath an unseen vaulted ceiling—is a continuous, interlocked expanse of dry heartwood. It is not petrified; it is biologically alive, but its sap is not aqueous. The xylem runs with heavy, clear terpenes and cold silicone-esters that smell faintly of turpentine and desiccated peel. Because there is no water, there is no rot. The colossal, square-cut timber flats and the pale swells of root-mass have the crisp, knife-edged grain of freshly planed ash. Sawdust drifts across the grain in bone-dry drifts, pooling against the curbings of long-dead expansion joints like dry salt, whispering whenever the draft shifts.

The scale is vast—a hollow rift deep within the world’s lower mantle, so wide that the curvature of the timber plain fades into a cold, pale haze. Suspended beneath the far overhead tangle of structural root-struts are arrays of industrial floodlamps: harsh, cold halide lanterns mounted on copper girders, bathing the whole basin in a flat, even twilight that feels like an overcast noon. It is cool down here, hovering around the chill of an unheated cellar, preserved and dry enough to make the skin pull tight over the knuckles.

The vegetation *is* the geology. Titanic trunks, each as thick as a tenement block, do not grow upward from soil; they grow horizontally from each other, splitting and fusing in an ongoing, embryonic architecture. They have only just arrived at these dimensions; the edges of the cambium are raw, swollen, and immaculate, showing none of the softening, slumping, or fungal pockmarking of old growth. Here and there, a fault line is simply a rupture in the grain, a clean splintering half a mile long where the fibers have sheared under immense dry tension, leaving bristles of white, hair-fine wood standing thirty feet high, unweathered and sharp as glass.

Set across this fibrous landscape is a distributed, geometric city. It does not crowd together. Following some austere industrial logic, the settlements are arranged in broad, sparse parcels: three-story rectangular blocks of mortised timber and black structural steel, anchored directly into the structural grain with five-foot iron lag bolts. Between these clusters lie vast, empty squares—dry clearances hundreds of yards wide where nothing is built, crossed only by elevated rail lines that run on trestles of pale, freshly turned dowels. 

Engines operate with an eerie, velvet discretion. Without water to boil for steam, the industry here relies on pneumatic ether-loops and high-torque mechanical linkages. Long, jointed drive-shafts, thick as ships’ masts, spin steadily on grease-packed bronze pillow blocks, transferring rotary motion across miles of timber plain from distant turbine halls. The noise they make is shockingly small: the cavernous bulk of the dry cellulose absorbs and deadens sound almost immediately. A punch-press slamming through sheet resin three hundred yards away registers only as a dull, thumping pulse felt through the soles of one’s boots. The rest is an immense, dry silence, occasionally scored by the rhythmic *shuck-shuck* of automated scraper-sleds clearing sawdust from the rail tracks.

Life moves through the clearance in quick, scuttling flourishes. Without water, fauna has evolved armor of chitin and lacquered paper. Great, many-jointed weevils with shells the color of scorched sugar browse along the edges of the active cambium, their mandibles clipping ribbons of sweet, dry pith with the sound of a tailor’s shears. They are hunted by dry-lunged, lizard-like courser-beasts whose skin has the texture of coarse sand-cast iron; they leave no tracks on the polished wood floor, their claws tipped with rubbery pads that allow them to sprint across the slick grain at terrifying speeds before vanishing into the hollow fissures of the lower root-tiers.

In the center of the regional clearance stands a sublimation refinery. It is built entirely of interlocking tongue-and-groove laths, bolted to steel trusses that crane into the lower canopy. Through banks of finned aluminum tubes, the plant vents plumes of cold, dry nitrogen, condensing heavier air-borne oils into blocks of translucent, amber-colored resin that slide down roller-beds to waiting freight-flats. A team of operators in rubberized aprons and breather masks move along the platform; they work with heavy wooden mallets and iron wedges, splitting the cooled cakes of resin with sharp, echoing cracks that carry briefly before being swallowed by the soft, porous walls.

Above them, the sky is not sky, but the underside of an immense canopy of bleached, leafless bracts that flex on twenty-minute cycles, opening and closing like the gills of a stranded fish to pump the cool, desert-dry atmosphere through the cavern. When they contract, a crisp, resinous wind rolls down across the timber plain, lifting paper-thin curls of shavings into the air, swirling them around the silent factory facades, and settling them again onto the pale, unrotting grain.
