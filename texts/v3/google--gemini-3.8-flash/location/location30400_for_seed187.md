The chamber is roughly eighty yards long, thirty wide, and shaped like the dry dock of a great ship, but its walls and floor are composed entirely of dead-hard, living wood. 

There is no rock here. The geology of the place is root. Over centuries so vast that the original trees have lost their separate identities, their basal buttresses have swollen, flattened against one another, and fused into a seamless timber bowl. The grain runs horizontally in bands five feet thick, deeply furrowed and polished by centuries of cool condensation until it feels like wet, cold saddle-leather underfoot. The walls rise sixty feet to a vaulted aperture that opens cleanly to an overcast sky, admitting a flat, pale daylight that fills the volume without shadows.

Between the timber walls, the space is mostly empty. There is almost no undergrowth, no saplings, no soil loose enough to harbor brush. The floor is an immense expanse of dark, polished root-plank, scarred by drainage runnels that were worn into the fiber long ago and have since petrified into smooth troughs. A vast pocket of cool, still air sits in this clearing, scented cleanly of tannic acid, wet heartwood, and engine oil.

Set into the far western curve of the root-wall, deeply bedded into the grain as if it were cast in place, sits an industrial reciprocating beam engine. It is not an artifact left to rust; it is running. Two massive cast-iron frames, coated in decades of dark tallow and coal-tar varnish, flank a twenty-foot flywheel whose lower rim disappears into a sunken trough of damp mulch. An iron walking-beam, six paces long and scarred by thousands of seasons of grease, tilts and drops with a slow, deliberate cadence—twelve strokes every minute. It runs on an unseen head of water channeled deep behind the wood, its intake burbling through massive lead pipes wrapped in tarred hemp, which are cinched to the root-wall with iron bands.

Because of this mechanism, the hollow is extraordinarily loud. The sound does not dissipate into soft forest litter; it strikes the hardened timber walls and bounds back across the clearing. Every stroke of the counterweight lands with a heavy, concussive *tonk* that vibrates in the knees, overlaid with the continuous, dry clatter of bronze bushings, the wet lash of the belt drive, and the dull, rhythmic hiss of a high-pressure gland blowing cold vapor into the air. 

Yet the machine is not alone in making noise. In the miles of hollow cavities formed where the timber plates meet, thousands of small, slate-grey swifts have built their cup-nests out of saliva and wood-shavings. They wheel through the cold daylight above the machine in dense, boiling rings, their dry calls rising into a continuous metallic shrieking that competes with the brass bearings. Wherever the engine’s lubricating grease has pooled in the runnels of the floor, thousands of hard-shelled carabus beetles, broad as a man’s palm, feed in dense clusters on the tallow, entirely indifferent to the hammer of the piston twenty feet above them.

The moisture in the chamber is light but perpetual. It does not rain inside, but the air is saturated to the point where iron sweats and the timber remains permanently slick. Drops form on the undersides of the great overhead limbs that span the top of the pit, falling sixty feet to snap sharply against the wooden pavement. Puddles never collect into ponds; the timber itself absorbs the excess, swelling to retain a permanent, damp resilience that rejects rot through sheer, ancient density.

Nothing in this hollow changes. The engine operates under a hydrostatic equilibrium that has not varied within living memory; its strokes do not speed when the rains fall, nor do they slacken during dry spells. The root-walls have reached their final, stationary mass, having long since crowded out every competing species until the basin became entirely itself—a self-enclosed, mechanical grove where industrial steel and biological bulk have achieved a cold, deafening truce.
