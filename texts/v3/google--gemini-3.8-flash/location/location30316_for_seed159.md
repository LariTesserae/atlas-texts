The chasm is young enough that its walls have not yet learned how to fall apart smoothly. There are no scree slopes worn to sand, no rounded alcoves or smoothed drainages; the rock is all violent cleave, fresh-snapped shale, and greasy basalt that smells of a split furnace. It sits just beneath the skin of the upper world, an open-roofed trench several miles wide and sinking down into a warm, sulfurous trough. Sunlight doesn't filter down so much as it crashes through the long, ripped-open ceiling in hard, parallel shafts, bouncing off the quartz-grit in the stone until the entire trough is filled with a blinding, flat glare. 

It is humid, but not with rain. Moisture here is an exhalation from the stone itself—warm condensation that bleeds out of hairline fractures, runs down vertical sheets of razor-edged slate, and gathers in shallow, steaming gutters that never stay still long enough to form lakes. 

Nothing green has had the time or the chemistry to take hold. There are no leaves, no ferns, no pale threads of moss. The only thing approaching flora is a slick, grease-colored biofilm that coats the rock in sheets of liver-brown and dull zinc-yellow, feeding directly on the iron-rich damp. It offers no resistance to the foot; it simply shears away like wet lard under pressure.

Yet the trench is choked with movement. It does not look like an ecosystem so much as an open wound overrun by its own cellular panic. 

The floor of the rift is an undulating, multi-tiered mass of animal life—primarily heavy, blind, pale-shelled detritivores and their long-legged herders. They are everywhere, stacked three or four deep in the damp defiles: creatures the size of swine with jointed, ivory-white carapaces, shoving blindly against one another to scrape the mineral slime off the rock with chisel-shaped mouthparts. They climb over their own dead, their shell-plates grinding together with a sound like hundreds of shovel blades being dragged across broken gravel. Between them dart smaller, whip-tailed things—soft-bellied, pale as lard, and feverishly quick—snatching at the flakes of grease and shell-dust kicked up by the larger herds.

Because the rock is so young, it shifts constantly. Minor seismic tremors run through the rift every twenty minutes, knocking slabs of loose stone down the walls. Each fall pulps dozens of the crawling things below; the herd does not scatter, but instantly folds inward upon the impact site, a frantic, roiling boil of mandibles and limbs fighting to ingest the crushed meat before the heat dries it out. Overhead, where the sun-slabs intersect the steam, pale predatory flyers—skin-winged, without feathers, their rib cages clearly visible through translucent vellum skin—wheel in tight, frantic spirals, dropping like dropped plummets into the churn, then dragging themselves back up the sheer rock faces using hooked thumb-claws.

Humanity, or whatever passes for it here, exists only at the margins of this boiling trough, clinging to the embryonic cliffs like parasites on a hide. 

Their constructions are temporary, frantic, and made almost entirely of the fauna itself. They have driven wedges of soft, cold-hammered native copper into the fresh cracks of the basalt, anchoring thick, greasy ropes braided from the intestine and dorsal sinew of the great crawlers. From these hang crude platforms of lashed rib-bones and stretched hide, swinging thirty feet above the churning floor. The people who work them are caked in grease to ward off the steam-scald and the bitter rock-dust; they work with long poles tipped with fire-hardened horn or chipped volcanic glass, gaffing the heavy white carapaces out of the crush below, hauling them up by hand-cranks made of pegged timbers.

Down on the floors, small hunting parties move behind tall, wicker-and-bone mantlets, using them like shields against the blind, crushing mass of the herds. They don’t hunt so much as butcher in place. When they wedge a crawler onto its back, they work with terrifying speed using broad flint adzes, peeling the soft belly-plates away while the creature's dozens of legs are still paddling against the bright, humid air. If they linger for more than a few minutes, the surrounding herd will simply walk over them, pushed forward by the sheer, brainless weight of the millions behind.

The air smells of vinegar, hot wet bone, and the dry, flinty stink of crushed rock. The noise is constant, rhythmic, and vast: the dull *choc-choc-choc* of millions of chitinous legs tapping the bedrock, the wet sucking sound of the biofilm being cleared by millions of plates, the occasional sharp crack of an over-stressed shell, and the thin, whistle-screams of the flyers diving through the heat-shimmer. 

Nothing stays in place. The landscape has no permanent paths, because a ridge of slate that stands fifty feet high in the morning will be gnawed clean of its soft seams and undermined by the sheer friction of the herd by dusk, collapsing into a new flat of broken rock that the swarms immediately surge over, covering it like a white, churning wave.
