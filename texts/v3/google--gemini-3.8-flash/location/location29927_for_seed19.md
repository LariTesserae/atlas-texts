The plateau sits at an altitude where the sky loses its moisture and its gradient, holding a steady, bleached cerulean from horizon to horizon. It has existed in this state long enough for the peaks to have forgotten their jaggedness; the mountains here are not sharp teeth but massive, ground-down knuckles of white dolomite and grey slate, planed smooth by an atmosphere that has long since ceased to blow with any force. There is no dust in the air because there is nothing left soft enough to crumble.

At first glance, from a rise, the landscape appears to be a field of pale, rolling dunes, or perhaps an ocean of dry coral bleached under an indifferent noon. But it is neither. The terrain is covered, three or four layers deep, in animals.

They do not move. They do not die, nor do they breathe in any way that displaces a feather or expands a ribcage. They carpet the high shelves, the wide dry washes, and the broad, smoothed saddlebacks between the summits. There are ungulate forms with hides like dry kidskin and eyes like polished jet, standing flank-to-flank, their long, spiraled horns knit together in an unbroken lattice that stretches for miles. Beneath their hooves, wedged into the spaces between their ankles, are smaller things: rodent-like creatures with scales of translucent mica, clusters of wingless birds resting their chins on one another’s rumps, and heavy, multi-jointed arthropods the color of unglazed clay, folded neatly into their own carapaces.

They are so dense that the actual stone of the mountain is rarely visible. To walk here—if one were to attempt it—would mean stepping upon the flat of a haunch, the nape of a cold neck, the broad, flat plate of a tortoiseshell made of pressed talc. They do not flinch. Their body temperature is precisely that of the rock: cool, around fifty degrees, constant day and night.

There is no water. The creatures do not thirst; their bodies contain no fluids to evaporate. If a skin is pierced with a sharp flint, there is no blood, only a pale, dry pith, like the interior of an elderberry stem or compressed chalk. They exist in an absolute, time-invariant equilibrium. A creature at the bottom of the crush, pinned beneath three larger beasts for ten thousand years, shows no sign of distress or suffocation; its eye remains glossy, reflecting the pale sky, its mouth slightly parted over blunt, dry teeth.

In places, the density breaks geometry. Where the numbers become too great for the surface area of the plateau, reality has quietly buckled to accommodate them. In a shallow depression no wider than a threshing floor, one can look down and see twelve thousand sheep-sized forms packed into a space that should hold fifty, their limbs interpenetrating without resistance, a horn passing through another animal’s flank like smoke through a screen, both solid, both firm to the touch, occupying the same parcel of space in perfect, mutual tolerance.

Plant life is almost entirely absent, reduced to rare, wire-thin lichens that do not grow so much as persist. They trace the seams where dolomite meets shale, black and brittle as carbonized thread, totally indifferent to the sun.

Scattered across this biological crust are the faint remnants of human passage. They are not cities or villages, but the low, dry-laid walls of nomadic shepherds who must have passed through before the world froze its logic. These walls are made of slate slabs, stacked without mortar, often utilizing the backs of frozen beasts as foundation stones. In some shelters, a small hearth of blackened river-stones sits empty, the ash turned to hard crust eons ago. Here and there, a crude hand-axe of knapped obsidian or a scraper fashioned from a broad rib lies where it was dropped on a flat rock. Sometimes, someone has carved a sign into the horn of a sleeping creature—a series of neat, parallel tally marks, made with a copper chisel, the cuts still bright and uncorroded in the dry, sterile chill.

The silence is vast and total. It is not an ambient hush, but an acoustic void. Without wind, without foliage to rustle, without the wet clicking of tongues or the scrape of claw on stone, sound has nothing to generate it except an intruder’s own pulse. When a footstep falls on a shelf of slate, the *clink* is startlingly brief, swallowed instantly by the millions of woolly, leathery, and feathered bodies that absorb every vibration like acoustic foam. If one brushes against an antler, it produces only the dull, dry *tock* of hard wood.

The light does not shift. The sun appears fixed at an angle that casts short, pale shadows into the crevices between bellies and thighs. It neither warms the stones nor sinks toward evening. The landscape does not await a thaw, or an awakening, or a judgment. It has simply reached the end of its permutations, an endless congress of living matter that has traded the inconvenience of time for the permanence of stone.
