The horizon does not rise or fall; it slides. 

Across an expanse the width of an inland sea, the earth sits low and ground down, an ancient slate-and-iron depression where the crust has long ceased to fracture and has instead settled into an uninterrupted, reciprocal grinding. There is no soil here, and nothing with a stem or a leaf has ever taken root. The floor is an immense pavement of native nickel-iron and densified graphite, milled by eons of sheer lateral movement until it holds the dark, oily polish of an engine bed.

It is bitterly cold—a wet, marrow-chilling freeze that hovers just above the point where brine hardens. Water is everywhere, but it behaves under strange, heavy compulsions. It sheets across the dark plate in endless, millimeter-thin films, driven by vibrations that run through the stone like a rapid, irregular pulse. It weeps from the seams between the rock plates, drawn upward by capillary forces so intense that low basalt lips seem to sweat black, mineral-dense slush against the pull of gravity. The air is saturated with a drenching, aerosolized grease: a cold mist of pulverized water and naturally occurring hydrocarbons that settles on every exposed surface in minutes, leaving everything shining, black, and slick.

Above, the sky is the color of a tarnished zinc bucket, low-hanging and dense with iron-tinted vapor. Light filters through it thinly, casting no sharp shadows, only a universal, greyscale sheen that catches on the wet edges of the moving flats.

Though there are no ruins, no foundations, and no sign that any sentient hand ever turned a spade or raised a wall, the territory functions with the deafening, organized violence of a heavy fabrication yard. The geology itself has crystallized into kinematic pairs. Great shelves of slate, sixty feet across and rounded at their borders like ancient river stones, cycle back and forth on natural beds of wet, spherical iron concretions. Deep faults do not shudder and wait; they pump. A quarter-mile to the west, a slab of gabbro the thickness of a hill rises three feet into the freezing air with a wet, sucking gasp, pauses at the top of its stroke, and drops with an impact that sets the shallow water leaping into fine, freezing spray. 

The sound is immense. It is not the roar of an avalanche, which expends itself, but the rhythmic, bone-deep thrum of an unpeopled turbine hall. Iron groans against iron; hydraulic channels carved deep within the sub-floor hiss as millions of gallons of frigid sludge are forced through pinhole apertures in the stone; the rhythmic, flat *clack-slide-clack* of miles of interlocked shale linkages echoes from the grey distance until the sound ceases to be noise and becomes an ambient pressure inside the ears.

Nothing is empty. The basin is choked with life, packed into every crevice, joint, and moving surface until the rock seems furred with meat. 

These are not animals that walk or fly. They are blind, low-slung, and clad in thick, flexible carapaces the color of wet lead. Millions of them crowd the reciprocating terraces, jostling shoulder-to-shoulder in the cold wash, their bellies pressed tight to the moving plates. They have no eyes; they navigate by the torque and frequency of the floor beneath them. Some, broad and flat as foundry ladles, wedge their heads directly into the sheared margins between sliding slabs, straining the continuous gout of grease-rich foam that erupts from the gaps with specialized, vibrating mouthparts. Others, segmented like massive drills, burrow endlessly through the slurry of rock powder and cold tallow that accumulates in the runoff channels, their carapaces polished to a mirror shine by the constant grit.

They do not flee the moving earth; they synchronize with it. A herd of several thousand smooth-backed, walrus-sized entities lies packed flank-to-flank upon a sheet of granite that shifts thirty feet south every twelve seconds. When the stone reaches the end of its throw and slams into the opposing ledge with a dull, wet report, the creatures do not flinch; they breathe out in unison, releasing plumes of freezing, fishy steam that blend instantly into the mist, then shuffle their limbs in time with the stroke, grazing on the black bacterial films that smear across the friction-faces before the next cycle begins.

Reality here has traded its usual organic drift for an exhausting, physical literalism. Causality does not branch; it links. The wind does not blow by thermal gradients alone, but is hauled across the flats by the pneumatic draft of subterranean hollows that draw in and expel the regional mist in vast, rhythmic sighs. Small, oily pools of water, trapped between counter-rotating stone drums, do not reflect the dull sky; they churn into thick, grey emulsions that rotate with the precise, stepped gear-ratios of an astronomical clock, though the teeth are only water-worn basalt nodules.

Further out, where the mist thickens into an impenetrable iron-fog, the scale remains stubbornly vast and crowded. There is no high ground to escape to; there are only lower flats, more plates, the endless, greasy churn of horizontal motion, and the deafening, indifferent clatter of an ecosystem that operates as a closed stroke, running cold, wet, and full, with no operator at the levers and nowhere for the mind to rest.
