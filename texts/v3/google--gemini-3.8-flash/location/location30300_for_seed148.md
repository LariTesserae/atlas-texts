The space is enclosed, no larger than an old railway terminal or a drowned district six blocks deep, yet it feels profoundly, unnervingly hollow. It is mostly cold draft and black air. Between its walls, immense distances of sheer nothingness hang suspended, interrupted only by sparse, skeletal networks of construction that stretch from the darkness above to the darkness below.

Everything here has the crisp, untooled perfection of something that finished forming only moments ago. There is no grit, no settled dust, no rust on the iron-hard fibers, no rounding of corners. The edges of the beams and facades are knife-sharp, freshly extruded from whatever womb or logic synthesized them. 

The architecture is entirely botanical, yet it has organized itself into the dense, pragmatic vernacular of a nineteenth-century manufacturing quarter. Tenements, boiler houses, and multi-tier viaducts are rendered in pure, hyper-compressed lignum: iron-wood so dense it rings like cast steel when struck, though it remains wet and cool to the touch. Facades of tall row-houses climb into the dark, their exterior walls built of interlocking, square root-blocks whose seams are still weeping clear, amber resin. Fire escapes are woven from stiffened bramble with thorns smoothed into structural rivets. There are chimneys—soaring, fluted stalks fifty feet high—that do not belch smoke, but rhythmically exhale plumes of pale, refrigerated steam that condense instantly against the ceiling and fall back through the dark in an intermittent, quiet drizzle.

Because of the void between things, the settlement feels like a city built for ghosts inside a cathedral-sized lung. A suspension bridge made of braided lianas—each cable thick as a boiler and taut under thousands of tons of tension—leaps across a three-hundred-foot gulf of absolute black, carrying a set of narrow wooden rail tracks that connect nothing to nothing. Below it, there is only draft: a slow, rising current of damp, cellar-chilled air that smells of crushed ferns and cold engine oil.

The machinery is alive and functioning, operating with a muffled, organic syncopation. Deep inside the husk of what appears to be a three-story textile mill, massive flywheels of petrified cambium spin silently upon axes of hard sap-wood. They are turned not by combustion, but by vascular pressure—vast subterranean bulbs far below expanding and contracting with pneumatic force, pumping thick, milky sap through translucent conduit pipes that run along the curbs like exposed veins. The sound of this industry is remarkably subdued: a rhythmic, wet *shuck-shuck* of wooden pistons, the hiss of low-pressure moisture escaping through leaf-valves, and the soft, continuous patter of condensation striking the boardwalks.

In the dark, life is everywhere, moving through the empty air and along the damp facades. 

They are not conventional animals. Thousands of small, pale things—segmented like isopods but light as dried papyrus—scuttle along the underside of the viaducts in unbroken, scurrying ribbons. Long-limbed, blind mantises with skin like peeled willow wand cling upside down to the telephone-like tendrils strung between the rooftops, their raptorial forelegs twitching in the drafts, snatching flying midges that are little more than floating, bioluminescent sparks. In the cavernous empty spaces between the buildings, larger shapes drift slowly: membranous, bat-like kites that do not flap, but steer themselves through the rising industrial thermals, their bellies bristling with hundreds of fine, hair-like cilia that comb the damp air for airborne spores. 

The reality here is frayed at its seams, caught in a loop of impossible mimicry. The city seems to have dreamed an industrial revolution without understanding what human beings were, or perhaps it grew from the memory of a blueprint dropped into fertile primordial soup. Gravity behaves erratically near the larger mills; a drop of water falling from an eave will sometimes veer sideways, drawn toward the mass of an idling cellulose dynamo, circling it like a tiny moon before striking its polished drum. 

Looking down a long, straight avenue bordered by gas-lamp posts—whose glass mantles are actually thin, translucent fruit bulbs holding pockets of cold, phosphorescent gas—the street does not vanish toward a horizon. Instead, after two hundred yards of pristine, damp cobblestones (each stone a living, pressurized gall growing in neat rows from the bedrock), the avenue gently twists ninety degrees upward, running flush along a sheer vertical wall until it disappears into the wet gloom overhead. The lanterns along this vertical stretch burn just as steadily, their pale yellow light throwing long, stationary shadows of empty balconies into the cold, silent air.

Nothing has ever lived in these rooms. The windows are single panes of mica, perfectly clear, looking into vacant interiors floored with pale, unblemished pith. There are no doors, only arches where doors should have been, through which the cool, damp drafts circulate without resistance. Yet inside these empty chambers, tiny gearworks of green reed continue to turn, driving miniature spindles that spin delicate, useless threads of silken cellulose into the pitch-black air, letting them drift out the open sashes to float across the chasm like spider silk, catching on the thorns of the viaducts below.
