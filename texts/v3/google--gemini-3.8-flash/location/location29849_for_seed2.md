The water here does not touch earth. The floor, three hundred fathoms down, is an expanse of interlocking, vibrated-concrete caissons—tens of thousands of them, cold and slate-gray, laid out in unbroken rectilinear miles. Every seam is sealed with thick, black bituminous mastic that still smells faintly of petroleum when the bottom currents sheer close. There is no silt. There are no sandbars or ancient shale shelves. It is a fabricated basin so wide that its internal hydrology produces its own Coriolis deflections, yet every square foot of its perimeter is bounded by engineered sheer faces of steel-reinforced aggregate.

It is brightly, mechanically illuminated. From an unseen ceiling leagues above, or perhaps from buoyant gantry networks anchored mid-water, banks of high-output metal-halide and quartz lamps burn with an unblinking, greenish-white intensity. Their beams do not fade into an abyssal twilight; they crisscross through miles of high-clarity brine, casting hard-edged, overlapping shadows of structural lattice-work down into the depths. The water is clean, intensely cold, and tinted pale turquoise by the halogen wash, clear enough that an intake tower four miles distant appears sharp and distinct, shorn of mystery, its yellow safety striping and zinc sacrificial anodes visible across the void.

Despite its vastness, the space is mostly empty. Between the monumental works of engineering lie vast gulfs of dead volume—clean, sterile water under immense hydrostatic pressure, through which nothing passes for miles except the vibration of displaced tons. A single structural column might rise from the floor like an upended factory, sixty meters in diameter, braced by tubular steel struts thick enough to drive a locomotive through, and then there will be nothing else: just three leagues of vacant, brightly lit cold before the next riser appears. 

The sound is immense, ceaseless, and impossible to escape. Water carries acoustic energy without mercy; the churning of subterranean axial-flow pumps miles away arrives not as a rumble, but as a rigid, rhythmic hammering that vibrates the bone. Low-frequency thrums—the displacement of millions of gallons per minute through slotted steel intake grates—reverberate off the hard concrete faces. Every few minutes, miles to the south, an automated pressure-relief valve cycles open: a hydraulic crack like an artillery shot, followed by a long, boiling hiss as high-pressure brine vents into an adjacent chamber, setting up standing acoustic waves that make the light beams tremble.

Because the system is young, the structures have not yet softened. There are no heavy incrustations of barnacles, no deep carpets of sea-fans or thickets of kelp. The steel edges of the girder arrays are still sharp enough to cut neoprene; the form-tie holes in the poured concrete still show the ring-marks of their plastic plugs. Only a faint, velvet-thin biofilm has begun to take hold on the sun-lamp facing surfaces—microscopic diatoms and short, pale-green filament algae that flutter frantically in the localized current-jets caused by cooling-water discharges.

Yet the open water is alive. Millions of silver-sided pelagic fish—escaped from some initial stocking cycle or deliberately introduced to balance the chemistry—move through the emptiness in tight, synchronized spheres. Drawn to the immense heat thrown off by the turbine housings and the glare of the suspended lantern arrays, these shoals drift across the structural voids like smoke. They bank and turn around the sheer concrete faces with military precision, hunted from below by dense packs of spiny dogfish and fast, blunt-nosed squid that dart between the cross-bracing of the pylons. When a school passes through a beam of white floodlight, the water flashes like shattered mirrors; seconds later, they are swallowed again by the pale, cold blue of the open expanse.

Every process here is active, deliberate, and governed by machinery that operates at geological scale. Enormous underwater flumes, their mouths protected by trash-racks forty feet high, swallow surface water to feed lower generation bays, creating powerful downdrafts that bend the schooling fish into long ribbons. Deep thermal vents—steel-sleeved conduits ejecting warm, desalted coolant—send pale, shimmering thermal plumes wobbling upward toward the surface, warping the geometric sightlines of the submerged infrastructure. 

It is an ocean without a coast, completely deliberate, smelling of ozone, cold brine, and hydraulic fluid, running at full load under the glare of artificial daylight.
