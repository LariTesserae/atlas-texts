It is entirely black. Not the velvet darkness of an open night sky, but the blind, heavy pressure of stone packed tight around stone, miles above sea level, deep within the fractured crown of an immense mountain massif. The air does not move. It is eighty-five degrees, perhaps ninety, dense and humid as an open mouth, suspended at absolute equilibrium. The dew point has been met everywhere, perpetually: every cubic inch of atmosphere is choked with moisture that has nowhere to go and no breeze to carry it, so it rests upon itself.

The scale is tiny, an enclosed nodule or pocket no larger than the bed of a cart, wedged between two shifting plates of young volcanic rock that met and stalled. 

Because it is so high, one would expect the cold of altitude, but the mountain’s roots are still hot, venting ancient warmth upward through capillaries of split basalt. And because it is hidden away from weather—shielded from wind, sun, and seasonal freeze—there is no erosion here. None. The stone is not smoothed by centuries of running water; it has never known the scour of sand or the fracture of ice. Instead, the geology here is in a state of raw, infant arrival. It is an embryonic landscape, just precipitating out of the warm, mineral-rich soup that seeps through the ceiling fissures.

The surfaces are sharp, delicate, and unfinished. Millions of hair-fine needles of aragonite and white gypsum sprout from the walls, crowding toward one another in the dark like the bristles of an uncombed pelt. In other places, the rock has bubbled into botryoidal clusters—globes of pale, newly birthed chalcedony that are slick, wet, and chemically warm to the touch. They have not had time to harden into inert bedrock; they are soft, almost gelatinous at the crust, a mineral skin forming over slow geothermal sweat. 

The space is crowded to the point of suffocation. You cannot extend an arm without snapping a lattice of crystals as brittle as blown sugar. Stalactites do not hang as thick, ancient icicles here; they are hollow, translucent straws, thousands of them, each no thicker than a grass stem, dangling down in dense, tangled curtains that nearly touch the floor. Through their centers, water passes cell by cell, falling not with a splash, but with a flat, rhythmic *tick* onto the sodden floor beneath.

The sound is deadened. The sheer density of mineral fuzz, hanging filigrees, and the heavy, saturated air acts as an acoustic sponge. A snapping crystal makes only a dry, smothered pop that dies within inches. The only enduring noise is the faint, collective percolation of a thousand slow leaks: a tiny, warm seepage that neither rises nor falls in volume, an invariant percussion that has maintained the exact same tempo for centuries.

Yet, this pocket is not utterly untouched. 

Wedged near what might be considered the floor—a shelf of layered, wet travertine that tastes faintly of sulfur and iron—are three flat river stones that do not belong to this mountain’s geology. They were carried up from the valleys below, smoothed by some distant torrent, and brought into this absolute dark. Beside them, jammed into a newly forming fissure to keep the crystal curtains from sealing an exit, is a wedge of fire-hardened yew. It is so waterlogged it has turned the color of bruised plum, its grain swollen tight, its surface bound by a coil of twisted hemp cord. The cord is entirely encrusted in a sparkling sleeve of calcite, turned to stone where it sits, preserved mid-decay by the mineral deposition that is slowly eating the space alive.

On one wall, where the aragonite needles have not yet managed to knit, there are four horizontal grooves gouged into the soft tuff. They were made by a scraper of knapped chert; a stray flake of grey flint still lies beneath them, half-drowned in a pool of lukewarm lime-water. The markings are not decorative. They appear functional, the brute, desperate tally of someone who worked by touch alone, feeling for the grain of the rock in an airless dark, perhaps checking how quickly the stone was closing in around them before they backed out through the squeeze-hole.

Life here is almost absent, but not entirely. Along the edge of a shallow, weeping depression where the condensation collects, there is a pale, greasy scum—a primitive biofilm of iron-oxidizing bacteria that forms a fibrous, rust-colored skin over the rock. It produces a faint, flat smell like wet flour and copper pennies. Deep within the lattice of gypsum straws, something very small and without pigment shifts: an eyeless troglobitic amphipod, translucent as ice, with antennae three times the length of its body. It moves with a glacial, stuttering economy of motion, stepping from needle to needle without breaking them, grazing on the bacteriological slime that coats the stone. It has never seen light, has no organs for it, and lives in a steady-state universe where warmth and wetness are the only two facts that exist.

Nothing shifts. There are no drafts to disturb the water-straws. No sun rises outside to alter the pressure within the stone. If an hour passes, or three hundred years, the space remains at eighty-five degrees, weeping warm moisture out of its pores, steadily filling its own small hollow with needles of fresh white mineral, slowly cementing the ancient wedge of yew and the flint flake into the growing bone of the mountain.
