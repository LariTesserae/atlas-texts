The basin is no larger than a village square, sunken slightly into the worn-down root of an ancient continent. The rock here has surrendered its angles over a billion years; there are no cliffs, only low, swelling whalebacks of pinkish-gray granite, buffed smooth by unimaginable cycles of water until they resemble sleeping knuckles under the sun. 

It is very bright—a fierce, white-gold illumination that seems to originate not just from the overhead sky, but from the sheen of the stone itself. A persistent film of water, barely three millimeters deep, sheets over the granite from unseen fissures, sun-warmed and mineral-heavy, smelling faintly of petrichor, warmed sap, and distilled copper. It does not pool into stagnant mud; it flows, laminar and glassy, slipping toward a series of shallow, circular drains that were not carved by water.

Lurking flush with the eroded stone are the rings. They look like pale jade or condensed paraffin, completely unmarred by the immense age of the surrounding rock, though the bedrock has relaxed around them like flesh around an old ring. They do not hum, but where they sit, the physics of the water changes: the sheet breaks into thousands of perfect, quivering pearls that roll uphill across the rock, or hover two inches above the surface in stable, rotating necklaces before dropping back into the flow. Occasionally, the air above a ring will bend light so sharply that one sees a glimpse of a different sky—colder, purple-tinted—before the geometry straightens out again with a soft, suctioning *pop*.

Despite the sparse arrangement—wide, empty yards of glistening granite stretch between features—the vegetation that does take hold is intensely, aggressively lush. It grows from narrow, straight-edged grooves in the rock, suggesting ancient conduits now choked with soil. There are cabbage-thick succulents with translucent fronds that bleed cool, lemon-scented condensation down their stems; low, broad ferns whose leaves are the deep, wet green of river moss, each frond swollen with sap and twitching lazily in rhythm with some underground pulse. These plants stand alone, like sculptures on a polished terrace, taking up immense volume with their fat, sprawling forms, yet separated from one another by yards of clean, naked, sun-struck stone.

And through this sunlit, streaming emptiness moves an almost terrifying press of life.

The fauna is everywhere, dense as silt in a rivermouth, but it is eerie in its utter lack of voice. There are no calls, no warning shrieks, no scraping stridulations. Millions of creatures occupy the hollow, yet the prevailing sound is only the gentle, rhythmic lap of water over granite and the wet, velvet brush of bodies passing over bodies.

Across the pink rock, beneath the hovering water beads, carpets of ribbon-flat worms—wide as human palms, banded in cadmium yellow and ink-black—migrate toward the drains. They glide on cilia with zero friction, thousands of them overlapping like shingles on a roof, yet parting seamlessly around the stems of the ferns. In the air, hovering in the intense glare, are clouds of creatures that are neither insects nor birds: thumb-sized spheres of clear, gelatinous tissue propelled by rapidly fluttering fringes of golden hair. They drift in the thousands, so thick in places they cast shifting, cellular shadows on the wet stone, occasionally colliding with a soft, wet *plink* before drifting apart.

Perched on the edges of the jade-like rings are larger things: pale, six-limbed hunters that look like hairless foxes sculpted from lard. They have no eyes, only broad, saucer-shaped depressions on their brows that glow with faint bioluminescence. They do not pounce; they simply extend long, needle-thin tongues of blue cartilage into the swarms of golden floaters, reeling them in with hydraulic smoothness. When one feeds, its translucent ribs expand, revealing the churn of internal organs that glow like embers in the bright daylight. 

The heat is steady, like a green-house mid-afternoon, causing the sheet of water to steam in thin, twisting wands of vapor that vanish almost instantly into the clear air. The ecosystem is in constant, rapid digestion. Dead floaters drop into the water film and are dissolved by microscopic agencies within seconds, leaving only faint oil slicks that the yellow ribbons eagerly lick from the rock. The jade rings gently circulate the warmth, creating thermal drafts that lift the drifting clouds of life into the bright, silent air, only for them to settle, breed, and fall again.

It is a place that feels simultaneously prehistoric and far past the end of history: an ancient, flattened altar where advanced, forgotten machinery has settled into the mud and now quietly, tirelessly, feeds an endless, voiceless tide of flesh.
