There is no moisture here, not even the phantom slickness of a cavern wall or the greasy dew of subterranean condensation. The air tastes of pulverized cinnamon, toasted flint, and the parched, alkaline flour of dried pollen. Yet it is not a dead desert; it is choked, suffocatingly alive, miles beneath the outer crust in a great vaulted rift that runs for horizons unseen, lit by the steady, unblinking glare of chalk-white mineral veins that cross the high vaults like frozen lightning. The illumination is clear, flat, and daylight-strong, casting short, hard-edged shadows over a landscape that is made almost entirely of combustible, fibrous life.

The ground is not rock. The rock was swallowed long ago, or perhaps it cracked open only yesterday and was immediately overwhelmed. The substrate is a roaring, creaking tangle of desiccated marrow-wood: colossal, hollow trunks the width of cathedrals that do not suck water from the deep, but pump volatile aromatic gases and powdery dry resins through porous, silica-sheathed walls. These trunks weave into one another, buckling upward into undulating hills and sheer bluffs of interwoven wicker and petrified bark. Because there is no water to smooth an edge or carve a gully, nothing here has ever been eroded. Fractures are needle-sharp; broken boughs splinter into forests of bayonets; the terrain has the jagged, violent freshness of a fracture that occurred three seconds ago, frozen mid-shatter and immediately overgrown.

Every square inch of this rift is fought for, crushed, and regenerated with an explosive, feverish hyperactivity. Plants here do not unfurl slowly; they ratchet, snap, and detonate into maturity. Great segmented stalks—ribbed like the trachea of a titan—telescope upward twenty paces in an hour, their growth driven by compressed dry vapors that hiss audibly through microscopic tracheoles. Their leaves are not soft or green; they are vast, stiff sheets of lacquered amber, clicking like shingles in the hot, updrafting air. When a bloom matures, it does not open gently: its dry pod splits with the crack of a field gun, showering entire acres in blinding drifts of iridescent, flour-fine spores that settle over the ravines like dry yellow snow.

The noise is absolute and terrifying. It is not an ambient drone, but an unbroken, deafening physical impact—a roar constructed from trillions of tiny, kinetic violences. There is the endless, dry thunder of bursting seed-casings; the rhythmic, piston-like thudding of growing root-pumps hammering their way through calcified plant matter; and, drowning out even the botanical frenzy, the fauna.

The animals here have bypassed blood and flesh as a wet world knows them. They are creatures of horn, chitin, spun silk, and dry friction. Swarms of flat, hand-sized beetles with polished, mirror-bright carapaces sheet over the slopes like liquid brass, their millions of claws clicking against the silica-wood with the sound of a hail of needles falling on glass. Flying things—broad-winged mantises made of dry, papery pith and gossamer—strike the updrafts in clouds so thick they cast undulating eclipses across the glowing vault. In the canopy of woven vines, segmented burrowers the length of galleons shear through the dry wood with rasping, counter-rotating jaws, their digestive mills grinding the dead fiber into fine, combustible dust that hangs suspended in the warm, motionless heat. The acoustic pressure is so immense it shakes the diaphragm; stridulations rub against stridulations until the air feels solid, an abrasive wall of sound that leaves the ears ringing with phantom static.

Human presence, or the presence of something clever and desperate enough to share its shape, exists only as a parasitic fringe. High above the churn, clinging to the lee of massive, petrified galls, are small clusters of habitation. They are primitive, precarious cages lashed together with stripped tendrils and caulked with dry gum. There is no fire here—to strike a spark in this desiccated, resin-blooming furnace would be to vaporize the valley in a mile-wide firestorm—so the people work by the steady white glare of the ceiling veins, shaping the hard wood with chipped flint gouges, heavy obsidian scrapers, and bone rasps.

Their ladders are cut directly into the living bark of the ribbed stalks, notched hastily because the wood grows so fast it deforms the rungs within days. Suspended footbridges of braided husk span the chasms, vibrating violently in the mechanical wind generated by the swarm-wings below. On narrow, scraped shelves, small groups of hunters in woven-bark armor and goggles made of shaved horn wait with hooked spears, waiting to pin one of the fat, wax-heavy burrowers when it breaches the crust to vent its dry belly.

Below their hanging hamlets, the canyon floor is a boiling churn of creation and self-cannibalization. An ivory-white pillar of woven reeds will heave itself sixty cubits into the air, crowned with a crest of razor-tipped, dry fans; within an hour, the weight of a million climbing seed-grubs will cause it to groan, stress-fracture with a series of sonic booms, and topple into the underbrush, only for three new spires to spear upward through its shattered carcass before the dust has even settled. 

It is an ecology running at terminal velocity, without a single drop of grease or rain to ease the friction. Everything grinds against everything else, baking in its own metabolic warmth, unweathered, raw, and roaring.
