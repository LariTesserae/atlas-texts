It is flat because it has had time to become flat. There are no mountains here, not even the memory of a ridge; five billion years of friction have planed this continent down to a single, continuous slate that lies barely an inch beneath the skin of an endless, boiling soup. 

Yet it is not empty. The density is total. There is no air between things because there is almost no true air left, only an aerosolized, lukewarm brine packed so tightly with particulate, suspended silts, and tumbling mineral-curds that moving an arm through it would feel like dragging a spade through wet cornmeal. It is warm—the fever-heat of a sick beast’s mouth—and dim, illuminated only by a bruised, butter-colored twilight that bleeds through miles of vertical suspension without ever locating a sun.

The sound is not a storm; it is the noise of geometry failing under hydrostatic pressure. It is a deafening, white-roaring shriek, accompanied by the planetary-scale thrum of billions of tons of slurry sliding over slick, polished basalt at high velocity. The sound enters through the soles of the feet, vibrates the teeth in their sockets, and strips away the capacity for internal monologue within minutes. It is a roar without rhythm, sustained and flatline, like an ocean falling down an elevator shaft that never ends.

Across this vast, horizonless low, the turbulence does not travel—it churns in place. Reality here has worn thin at the edges, worn down by the sheer age of the stone below. The water does not obey the clean division of states: it flash-condenses into heavy, grey sheets that strike the floor like lead plating, then immediately boils backward into towering gyres of mustard-colored vapor without ever changing temperature. Columns of water, dense as wet clay, sprout upward out of the floor like instantaneous trunks, spin until they whistle, and collapse back into the froth. In places, the water-tension is so severe that it forms brittle, glass-hard skins across the surface of the torrent; you can see ancient, drowned debris skittering across these temporary ice-hot panes before they fracture with the sound of snapping artillery.

Vegetation has no purchase here, except for the hair. In the shallow lee of sunken shelves, clinging to the polished bedrock, grows a black, greasy turf—not moss, not kelp, but something like long-stranded mammalian pelt that feeds directly on the friction of the water. It never fruits, never roots deeply; it simply weeps and shreds, regenerating from its own torn ribbons as quickly as the silt flays it bare. Fauna is almost conceptual: occasionally, something blind, pale, and ribbed like an accordion will breach the yellow froth, tumble fifty yards in the lateral wind of spray, and be pulverized against a standing wave of mud before it can draw a breath. 

And yet, it is inhabited by the dead labor of something very small. 

Every half-mile, rising just inches above the scouring tide, are the stubs. They are made of a green-black, unquarried soapstone, ground so smooth by eons of grit that they look like wet thumbs poking through the froth. They are built—there are clear, rhythmic indentations where thumbs or pegs were pressed into the material before it cured, faint spiral channels cut to guide the brine, and low, semi-circular dams that try, futilely, to shelter small, square cisterns. They are non-mechanical, ancient beyond the calculation of dust, the leavings of a species that had stone and hands and nothing else, built at the very twilight of the world when the waters first began to thicken. Most of them are choked with the greasy black hair-weed or filled with dense, settled sludge that has begun to bake into a dull, grey tallow.

There is no room in this landscape for horizon. The sky is five feet above the floor, made of a revolving ceiling of greasy foam-clouds that drag across the surface of the shallows like an overturned mattress. Everything is touching everything else. The water is full of stone; the air is full of water; the sound is full of weight; the ancient rock below is so saturated that it hums, soft and yielding, under the immense, chaotic weight of the churn.

If you stood there, you would not drown in the conventional sense. You would be incorporated. The dim, warm soup would pack your nostrils with fine, gray flour; the roar would shake the calcium from your joints; and the ancient, flat world would draw you down into its endless, frictionless scrape, polishing your bones until they were as low and featureless as the bedrock that holds it all up.
