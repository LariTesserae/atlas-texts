The floor of the basin is an ancient, polished wood that does not rot. It spans perhaps three hundred yards from rim to rim—the scale of a grand civic plaza or an open-roofed cathedral nave—sunken low into the regional drainage, where the warm air settles and pools like grease. Millions of years of wet traffic and chemical weathering have planed every angle into a soft, bevelled curve. There is no raw stone or bare soil anywhere; the bedrock itself was long ago replaced by a deep, calcified peat that petrified, softened again, and was overwritten by a single, contiguous vascular mat. Walking across it is like crossing a pavement of smooth, waterlogged horn.

Water here is everywhere, warm and persistent, but it does not simply sit in puddles. Engineered surface tensions—sub-molecular grains woven directly into the lignin of the floor—herd the moisture into paper-thin sheets that race across the open flats at high speeds, gliding like oiled film across a kitchen table. They converge in shallow municipal gutters that hiss with thermal steam. The air carries the sharp, sweet smell of distilled sap, wet copper, and old river-bottom mud. It is deeply warm, a constant greenhouse stew that keeps the skin slick and beads continuously upon the few upright surfaces.

Despite the fact that the entire landscape is composed of living plant tissue, the area feels strikingly sparse, even lonely. The density is kept deliberately low: wide, empty swathes of grey-green, rubbery decking stretch out between solitary presences. There are no thickets or tangled canopies. Instead, the vegetation manifests as isolated civic architecture. A hundred yards to the north stands a single, hollow municipal tower, sixty feet tall, grown entirely from braided iron-wood. Its exterior is smooth, almost vitreous, yet you can watch its grain twist and re-knot itself in real time. 

The turbulence of the site is kinetic and architectural rather than weather-driven. The structures here are caught in an endless, rapid metabolic spasm. The tower is not finished, and never will be; it designs and dismantles itself along invisible, hyper-dimensional stress vectors. A grand overhanging gallery will rapidly knit itself outward from the trunk—thick tendrils of cambium shooting across the void at the speed of a crawling snake, fusing into cellular vaults and balustrades—only to be starved of sap twenty minutes later. The rejected mass then dissolves into a wet, curdled froth that sloughs off and splatters onto the polished floor below, immediately washed away by the racing film of water, while a new archway begins to fruit on the opposite face. 

This is city infrastructure functioning through an incomprehensible biological calculus. Physics within the precinct is subtly steered: gravity here has a hydraulic grain to it. Runoff does not merely drain downhill; ribbons of warm, tea-colored water run sideways along horizontal wall-seams, or suddenly leap across six-foot gaps of open air to feed hanging, bladder-like streetlamps that glow with a steady, daylight-bright luciferin. The sound is loud and relentlessly mechanical, though entirely fleshy: a rhythmic, churning cavitation like a ship’s propeller turning underwater, punctuated by the wet *thwack* of collapsing balconies, the high-pressure whistle of xylem conduits purging steam, and the violent, tearing snap of rapid cellulose expansion.

Life thrives in the margins of this hyperactive architecture. Long, pale things—somewhere between mantids and river eels—cling to the weeping flanks of the shifting towers with suctioned claspers, their translucent bellies swollen with amber runoff. They dart out in lightning arcs to scoop up the sloughed-off architectural pulp before it liquefies. Overhead, where the warm steam collects against the unseen containment field that caps the basin, dense flocks of small, chitinous birds circle continuously. They do not flap; they ride the rising thermal plumes from the drainage gutters, screaming in flat, metallic registers that echo sharply off the naked wooden floor.

At the center of the clearing lies what might once have been a market square or a council ring, now eroded down into a broad, saucer-shaped depression. Its lip is ringed by low, rounded stumps of ancient timber-structures, worn smooth by eons of water and footfall until they look like rotted teeth. From the center of the depression, the floor periodically pulses, bulging upward like an inhaling chest, then flattening out again to send a concentric ripple through the thin sheet of surface water. For a few seconds, the water is thrown into a standing vibration—a lattice of tiny, spitting pyramids—before the underlying lattice clears its throat, the hiss of suction resumes, and the vast, wet timber plaza empties out once more into immaculate, roaring space.
