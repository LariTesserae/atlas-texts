The void here is not sky, and it is not space; it is simply an absence between ribs. 

At this altitude, the air has the thin, iron taste of an uninsulated blade. It rushes without obstacle across leagues of nothingness, but when it meets the framework, it screams. The framework is wood, though to call it wood is to understate its load: miles-long beams of pale, twisted lignum, thick as mountain ridges, arching out of the freezing lower dark and locking together in violent, splintered knuckles. There is no stone here, or if there is, it lies five leagues below, drowned under accumulated centuries of shed husks. The plant is the mountain. The plant is the tectonic plate. Yet it is mostly empty air—a desert of sheer drop, an architecture of sparse, colossal struts suspended over a gulf so deep that falling would grant a man time to starve before he struck the root-floor.

Because the rift is young, the wood is raw. It did not grow through slow, ringed patience; it erupted. Great green-white spars have thrust upward through sheer hydraulic pressure, their surfaces torn by vertical fissures wide enough to swallow longhouses. The edges of these ruptures are still ragged, sharp as sheared bone, weeping a clear, bitter gum that congeals in the frost before it can run an arm’s length. The sap smells of crushed hemlock and freezing vinegar. Where two of these immense members cross and grind against one another in the crosswinds, the sound is like the hull of an iron vessel buckling against pack ice—a wet, deafening groan that travels through the grain for thirty miles, shaking the mosses into loose, powdery drifts.

The light never truly arrives. Above, a permanent ceiling of high, dry storm-clouds seals away the celestial bodies, filtering the day down to the blue-grey gloom of a slate quarry at dusk. Below, the dark is total. Between them lies this cold, whistling lattice, perpetually dampened by the condensation that blooms whenever a low-pressure draft drags clouds through the open spars. 

The moisture does not gather in pools; it cannot rest on vertical wood. Instead, it beads in heavy, clear pearls along the under-ribs, trembling in the gale until it breaks free and drops into the dark as a short, whistling rain. Everywhere there is the trickle of this run-off: water navigating grooved channels in the bark, falling through empty space in long, silver ribbons that are blown apart into mist before they fall three hundred feet.

Life here clings to the lee sides of the uprights. 

It is abundant, but it is small and hurried, constantly aware of the sheer drop. Colonies of blind, pale-winged kites—leathery creatures no larger than a child’s palm, with ribbed wings like dry leaves—roost by the millions in the deep splinter-caves of the joints. When a grinding tremor rolls through the wood, they erupt in a dense, rattling cloud, swept instantly sideways by the wind, crying out in short, dry clicks that map the emptiness around them before they fight their way back to the bark with hook-tipped thumbs. Below them, scouring the damp channels, are leggy, grease-furred beasts that move with the twitching vigilance of weasels, their claws curved like sickles to bite deep into the fibrous skin of the runner-vines. They hunt the fat white slugs that feed on the gum-weeps, ripping them from the timber with blunt teeth while the spray of freezing sleet mats their bellies.

Human presence exists only in the crotches of the great trusses, where two or three massive members have collided and splintered into an accidental floor. 

These camps are nothing more than bundles of rawhide and woven rush, lashed directly into the deep fissures of the grain with hempen cables thick as a forearm. The shelters are shaped like inverted hulls to shed the wind, held down by heavy pegs of hardened scrub-root driven into the wood with stone mauls. There is no metal here to speak of; tools are chipped chert, ground bone, and the dense, petrified heartwood salvaged from older, dead struts that have dried into black flint. 

The people who live here do not walk; they crawl, hooked by three limbs to safety-lines of braided sinew, their knees and elbows calloused into yellow plates of leather. They burn the sap in shallow dishes of hollowed bark, producing a flat, smoky yellow flame that stinks of turpentine and barely warms the chin of the person tending it. Their clothes are heavy, greasy layers of kite-skin, sewn feather-side inward with the gut of the bark-weasels. They do not speak into the wind; they communicate through a vocabulary of heavy, rhythmic thumps struck against the trunk with wooden billets, the vibrations traveling through the miles of interconnected lignum to tell of incoming squalls, structural fractures, or the drift of kite-flocks through the lower reaches.

The motion of the whole place is ceaseless. It is an active world, but its activity is the violent, slow flex of a structure too vast for its own materials. At noon, if such a concept holds in the grey twilight, the thermal draft rises from the warm, rotting depths leagues below, and the wood warms slightly, sighing as it expands. This expansion is not gentle; it comes in gunshot-cracks as tension releases along the faults. A spar forty paces across will twitch three feet to the north with a sound like a cannonade, shearing the safety-ropes of any camp not anchored in the dead wood.

Then the night-chill drops from the cloud-ceiling like lead. The moisture on the bark glazes over in an instant, becoming an invisible skin of ice that turns every pathway into an abyss. The kites retreat deep into the pith-hollows; the hunters retreat into their hides, shivering together in the damp, oily dark, listening to the flute-tones of the gale blowing across the mouths of million-foot gaps. 

Around them, above them, and far into the miles of vacant cold beneath their dangling feet, the great ribs continue to swell, to split, and to brace themselves against the nothing.
