The ridge sits at eight thousand feet, where the atmosphere thins to a cold, dry glass, but the basin itself never dries out. It is a dropped amphitheater, no larger than five or six city blocks across, cradled between three bald, utterly planar summits that were planed flat by glaciers that vanished before the continents finished drifting. Everything here has reached its terminal shape. The granite has no sharp corners left; every ledge is rounded like a kneecap, worn down through hundreds of millions of years of grit and freeze-thaw until the stone looks less like geology and more like melted bone settled in a pan.

Yet it is built. It possesses the distinct, deliberate orthography of an urban grid, though not a single chisel mark or dowel hole exists to prove a hand was involved. 

Rising from the floor of the basin are eighteen massive, hollow pillars of layered travertine and siltstone, spaced at rigid eighty-yard intervals. They stand forty to sixty feet high, slightly conical, with thick, buttressed skirts that merge into a pavement of hexagonal mudstone flags. They look like the ventilation stacks of an underground metropolis or the municipal silos of a culture that had no metal. In truth, they are the fossilized chimney-nests of a blind, colonial crustacean that died out during the Devonian, back when this shelf was a brackish marsh at sea level. Tectonic uplift carried the entire township upward over three hundred million years, freezing it, sanding it down with grit-bearing gales, until only these smooth, hollow megaliths remained, standing like ruined tenement towers in the mountain air.

Because they are hollow and pierced with spiraling respiratory galleries, the site is deafening. 

The wind off the northern ice plains strikes the lip of the amphitheater and drops directly into the open tops of the stacks. It does not whistle; it thrums. It produces a massive, percussive vibration—a chord of low C, E-flat, and an abrasive, microtonal buzz that rattles the cartilage in the jaw. As the wind pulses across the ridges, the towers answer each other in staggered antiphony, creating an acoustic density so thick that talking is useless and footsteps against the stones cannot be heard at all. You do not hear the wind itself; you hear the architecture of an extinct swamp shouting in the cold.

Despite the near-freezing chill, life here is thick, low, and wet. 

The valley catches cloud decks that ride low over the crests. Dense plumes of high-altitude mist tear through the grid every thirty minutes, dumping a sudden, ice-cold dew across every horizontal surface before the sharp sun breaks back through, illuminating the droplets until the entire basin sparkles like shattered mica. This constant pulse of moisture feeds a shockingly lush floor. 

Between the massive, solitary pillars—separated by broad, empty avenues of bald flagstone—the earth is choked with a deep mattress of copper-colored bryophytes, fat-lobed liverworts, and velvet-black mountain moss that grows three feet thick. Where the stone skirts of the chimneys meet the ground, rings of giant, fleshy saxifrage with leaves like cold bacon thrive in the spray, blooming with tiny, dull-yellow flowers that have no scent. Long, fibrous beards of pale gray lichen hang from the windward baffles of the towers, streaming horizontally in the draft like horsehair, stiff with frost on their outer edges and dripping melted condensation at their roots.

It is empty in the way an unpeopled cathedral is empty. The distance between each monolith is generous, wide, and clean; you could drive a team of horses down the moss-bordered avenues without brushing a wall. There is no clutter. The ancient age of the place has swept away all rubble, gravel, and talus; the wind simply hoovers the floor and casts the dust over the southern rim. What remains are only the monumental columns, the wet green lanes, and the sky.

Living things with blood are almost entirely absent. Once or twice a day, a solitary slate-blue raptor, short-winged and broad-tailed, will plummet into the mouth of a chimney to rest out of the gale, hunting the tiny, translucent spiders that spin triangular webs across the internal air-flutes. Other than these, nothing walks the streets. The motion here belongs entirely to the fog, the vibrating moss beds that shudder in resonance with the bass drone of the towers, and the cold water that perpetually sheets down the eastern faces of the stone, running in clear, shallow braids through the joints of the pavement before vanishing into natural drainage vaults deep below the floor.

At midday, the sun is fierce, blindingly white against the worn pale stone, casting razor-edged shadows across the bryophyte plains. The contrast is severe: freezing gale-force drafts that smell of wet slate, accompanied by the bright, unmitigated glare of high elevation, wrapped in a sound so heavy and relentless it feels like standing inside the drum of a running mill. It is a city that never had citizens, polished by eternity into an enduring, functional organ, still playing the atmosphere long after the sea that conceived it has turned to dust.
