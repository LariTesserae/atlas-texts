At eight thousand feet, the plateau does not open into sky so much as it bristles against it. The terrain is a forest made entirely of stone, though to call it a forest is to invoke a softness it refuses: it is a labyrinth of vertically fractured quartz-diorite and dense, iron-stained slate, cleaved into millions of upright needles, blades, and square-edged flutes that stand packed shoulder to shoulder. There is almost no horizontal ground. The spacing between the pillars is rarely wider than a torso, often narrower than a hand, stretching across miles of high, ridged plateau like the teeth of an immense, upward-facing comb.

Because of the altitude, the air is thin, dry, and cold to inhale, but the landscape itself is saturated. A permanent cloud base sits just below the rim of the plateau, driven upward by high thermal lift, where it strikes the frozen mineral mass, condenses instantly upon the sun-warmed stone summits, and runs downward in perpetual, unbroken sheets. Water does not pool; it skins every surface. It slides down the vertical facets of the pillars in glossy laminas, weeping from blind joints and microscopic cracks, filling the narrow interstices with falling veils. 

The light here is terrifyingly clear. High-altitude sunlight, unscattered by dust or smog, hits the wet rock at a severe angle, turning the entire plateau into an engine of glare. Every vertical edge acts as a prism or a mirror. The feldspar and quartz inclusions flash silver, white, and cold amber; the wet faces of dark slate reflect the blue of the deep, empty zenith with an intensity that burns the eyes. There are no shadows that offer relief, only high-contrast slits of black where the stone needles stand so tightly pressed together that even noon cannot penetrate more than a few yards down.

What fills the air entirely, wiping out thought, is the sound. It is a monolithic, crushing roar that never wavers. It does not possess the irregular thud of surf or the gusting whistle of mountain wind; it is the compound resonance of ten million distinct trickles, drips, and gushing rivulets dropping through resonance chambers of variable pitch, merged into a solid acoustic wall. The stone flutes act as pipes. The wind, which blows constantly across the plateau from the west, shears across the tops of the narrow fissures, exciting the air columns within them like a pipe organ the size of a province. The ground hums through the soles of the feet—a low, bone-vibrating fundamental drone over which the hiss and crash of falling water chatters in an unbroken white noise.

There is not a cell of green. No lichen stains the stone orange or grey; no moss cushions the seams; no spore finds purchase on the slick, perpetually flushed faces of the rock. The stone has been washed clean for millennia, eroded to an equilibrium where the rate of dissolution matches the slow, tectonic uplift of the plateau. It is a purely lithic world, rinsed bare of soil, seed, and decay. 

Yet it is not completely dead. In the deep, cold fissures, where the sun never strikes and the water runs in slick, perpetual torrents over black rock, life exists as an occasional, almost accidental presence. Rare, pale things cling in the dark: blind, leathery creatures resembling flattened hellbenders or colossal stonefly nymphs, their bodies pressed flush against the wet facets to avoid being swept down into the abyss. They have no pigment, appearing the color of wet river-pearl or curdled milk, and their claws are tipped with hard, calcified hooks that fit the microscopic grain of the diorite. They do not eat plants, for there are none; they graze upon the thin, invisible chemotrophic biofilms that form where iron-rich water seeps out of the living fault lines, or they wait for the carcasses of exhausted high-migrating insects blown off course by the valley drafts and drowned against the wet spires.

Farther across the expanse, the pillars vary slightly in height—some rising fifty feet into the glare, others sheared off flat at waist-height by ancient glacial action—creating a stepped, jagged topography that seems to roll like a frozen, petrified sea. The geometry is relentless. You could walk for days along the few natural ridges where the needles have fused into continuous, narrow fins, and find no change in character: always the blinding reflection off running water, the bitter chill of the stone against the burning brilliance of the air, the cold spray blowing off the flutes, and the deafening, immutable roar of an earth that has solved itself into permanence.
