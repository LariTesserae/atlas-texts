The noise arrives before the eyes can adjust to the scale of the trench. It is a physical weight, a continuous, thunderous roar that fills the chest cavity and vibrates the marrow of the legs. Far down the axis of the rift—miles into the dimness, set into the sheer vertical walls of unweathered stone—hang subterranean ventilation arrays and pneumatic intake shafts four stories tall. Their steel blades, spinning in the freezing air, draw millions of cubic feet of cold atmosphere through the deep cut, churning a deafening, monotone bellow that bounces off the basalt facets without losing an ounce of force.

The temperature hovers just at the freezing threshold, dry enough in the main draft to preserve the razor-sharp edges of the blasted rock, but weeping where the stone meets the floor. Here, sixty fathoms beneath the surface plateau, the rift opens into a valley-wide corridor that stretches out of sight. It is a freshly opened wound in the crust; the rock faces are clean, sheer fractures, unrounded by ages of water or wind, showing crisp, planar cleavage that catches the weak industrial light in sharp geometric gleams.

Yet the stone floor is almost entirely hidden. 

The substrate underfoot is not gravel or scree, but a dense, cold-hardened mattress of pale vegetation several feet thick. It is an all-encompassing vegetative stratum: fibrous taproots as thick as iron hawsers weaving over one another, topped by a dense, springy turf of cold-tolerant liverworts, fibrous peat-lichen, and stiff, rubbery mosses that thrive in the near-dark. The plants do not grow tall; instead, they have become the terrain itself. They blanket the sharp floor of the excavation in a seamless, pale green and ash-white carpet that yields only slightly under heavy boots. Where the rock drops off into clean ledges, the vegetal carpet spills over like frozen porridge, clinging to the vertical breaks with millions of microscopic holdfasts.

Moisture exists only as condensation and intermittent seepage. The air driven by the fans is dry and biting, but against the colder faces of the rock, it deposits beads of water that gather into sluggish trickles. These runnels do not form rivers; the spongy plant-mat drinks them immediately, swelling with dampness and smelling faintly of turned earth, iron filings, and cold starch. In the slight hollows where the roots buckle, shallow puddles of clear, ice-cold water sit trapped, reflecting the sparse chain of work-lights strung overhead.

The human presence here is industrial, utilitarian, and widely scattered. Every half-mile or so, a single steel-framed gantry or an isolated corrugated shack perches on heavy iron pilings driven directly through the root-mass into the bedrock below. Heavy, black vulcanized power cables, slack and sagging, run in straight lines along the trench, bound to iron pitons driven into the sharp rock. Occasionally, the rusted skeleton of a tracked transport or an idling diesel compressor sits silent and unattended, dusted with pale spores and rime, its engine block cold to the touch. The machines belong to an era of aggressive, mechanized extraction that seems to have paused or slowed to an imperceptible crawl. A single sodium fixture, caged in yellowed wire, hums under the eves of a pumping shack, casting a dull amber cone over a small patch of frosted moss before surrendering to the vast, gray twilight of the canyon.

There is little animal life capable of enduring both the freezing draft and the obliterating noise. Sometimes, high in the fractured ceiling where the exhaust heat from electric cables pools, a solitary cave pipistrelle or a gray carrion beetle crawls across the stone, its movements stiff and deliberate. Down on the carpet, pale, wingless soil-hoppers occasionally scatter from beneath a lifted heel, vanishing into the deep, fibrous grain of the root-turf. 

Everything here happens slowly, beneath the cover of the violent sound. The plants do not rapidly unfurl; they build themselves cell by cell over decades of steady, refrigerated respiration, mineralizing the basalt beneath them and solidifying the canyon floor into a continuous living deck. The steel rusts at a crawl, slowed by the cold. The blades of the distant fans turn without pausing, wearing down their heavy brass bearings by microns each year, maintaining a steady, deafening gale that keeps the trench free of stagnant gases, cold, vast, and waiting.
