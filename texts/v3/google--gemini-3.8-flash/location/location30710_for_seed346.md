The district sits in a cold, clean depression, roughly the size of a collegiate quadrangle or a sunken municipal exchange, but its boundaries are so heavily packed with architectural mass that the sky above feels less like an atmosphere and more like an unpainted ceiling. 

It is very new. The edges of the structural members are mathematically acute—there are no rounded bevels of wind-scour, no foot-worn hollows in the thresholds, no grain where lime or carbon has settled into an oxidized crust. Everything has the immaculate, slightly repulsive sharpness of a render that has only just dropped into the material register. Yet it is an urban space: a tangle of three-story domiciles, conduit-risers, and deep, dry loggias that should belong to an old, congested port city, except that the walls are formed from a single, continuous pour of pale, glass-dense feldspar that conducts neither sound nor heat.

The temperature stays hovering just above freezing, around the threshold where the moisture in the air hesitates between suspension and frost. A thin, greasy sweat coats the vertical planes. This moisture does not fall from above; it gathers along invisible seams where the logic of the local space-time fails to resolve its decimal points. You can watch it form: a bead of clear, hard water will appear in mid-air, four inches off the surface of a blank lintel, wobble as if resting on an invisible shelf of high-density vacuum, and then fall sideways to strike the glass-stone with a faint *tink*.

Because the tech here operates at a scale where physical law is treated as a modular interface rather than a constant, the buildings do not always respect their neighbors' volumes. On the north side of the lane, a five-story tenement block intersects a low administrative portico. They do not crush or pierce one another; they simply occupy the same three hundred cubic meters simultaneously. From the street, one sees both the fluted columns of the portico and the utilitarian bunk-slots of the tenement flickering at the rate of a slow eyelid. When the light strikes them at an angle of forty degrees, you can see the interior furniture of the fifth floor resting quietly within the solid marble of the lower architrave.

A sparse, stiff vegetation has taken root in the few errors where the ground-plane failed to close. These are not weeds in the organic sense, but small, bristle-like stems of grey cellulose that grow along the lines of residual magnetic leakage. They resemble dried moss or the stiff hair that grows on pigskin, cold to the touch and brittle as dried fishbone. Between these bristles, in the gutters where the condensation collects into black, still pools, occasional things move. They are not rats or beetles; they are small, five-legged forms of polished horn that turn over slowly, their motions dragging through the chill air like clockwork running down in grease. They appear to have no mouths, only a series of small, pale indentations along their undersides where they absorb the bitter alkaline sheen of the runoff.

Light is abundant, clear, and neutral—the sort of daylight one expects from an overcast noon in high latitudes, though there are no clouds to be seen, only a flat, grey-blue luminescence that comes from every direction at once, casting almost no shadows under the eaves. Under this exposure, the weirdness of the architecture is painfully legible.

Down one alleyway, which should terminate against the retaining wall of the lower terrace, the pavement continues forward into a perspective that recedes too quickly. A single stride covers what looks like twenty paces; a second stride brings the traveler against an invisible wall that sounds like slate when tapped with a knuckle, though the visual corridor stretches away for half a mile of identical, clean-edged doorways. The sound here is muffled, suppressed by the sheer density of solid objects that have no grain to vibrate. The city does not hum; it ticks, occasionally, with the sound of cold contraction, or lets out a soft, flat *slap* when a sheet of condensate slides down fifty feet of pristine vitreous masonry.

The rules of occupation are unresolved. In several of the ground-level cells, where low platforms suggest beds or data terminals, the air has curdled into a dense, translucent jelly that smells slightly of ozone and raw flour. This substance is slowly curing, hardening from the perimeter inward at a rate of an inch each day. It preserves whatever fell into it during the initial deployment: an aluminum measure, a stray spool of blue optical thread, and the lower half of an unidentifiable jointed instrument that continues to pulse with a tiny, regular red spark beneath the rind of synthetic amber.

A traveler feels an odd pressure in the teeth here—the sensation of two distinct altitudes being calculated across the same set of nerves. The horizon is low, bounded by the square parapets of the upper tier, yet when you look directly into the corners where three planes meet, your eye finds a fourth plane that slopes *inward* toward an origin point that cannot be placed. 

Nothing is rotting, because nothing has lived long enough to die, but the whole arrangement feels suspended in an early, unfinished state of assembly—a city-quarter that was called down into reality by an intellect that lost interest midway through the compilation, leaving the stone un-eroded, the moisture trapped in its odd lateral courses, and the cold air waiting for an instruction to move.
