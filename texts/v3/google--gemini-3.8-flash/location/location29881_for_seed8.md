The land has long since forgotten how to rise. Here, on a continent-wide shield of Precambrian basement rock, geology has run out of ambition; the mountains were ground to meal five hundred million years ago, their roots dissolved by warm rains and carried out to a sea that no longer has an edge to fall into. What remains is a peneplain—a horizontal universe elevated perhaps ten or twelve feet above the regional water table, stretching out in every direction until the low, pale arc of the sky meets the wet floor of the world.

It is wide, open, and drenched.

Water does not carve this ground into ravines or canyons, because there is no gradient steep enough to lend water anger. Instead, it lies in sheets. Vast, skin-deep expanses of warm, tea-colored water slide across ancient, whale-backed pavements of granite and iron-rich laterite. The flow is almost imperceptible—a continent-scale seepage, moving at the speed of a drifting leaf, spreading over square miles of red and gray stone that has been polished slick by eons of sheet-wash. In the shallows, the water is lukewarm to the touch, heated through by a steady, milky-white sun that hangs in a high ceiling of humid haze. 

The light is broad and shadowless. It bounces upward from the thousands of acres of standing film, so that the horizon vibrates in a double glare: the sky above, and the sky held upon the stone below.

Because the rock is old and chemically stripped of nearly every nutrient, life does not riot in thick green walls. It holds back, sparse and calculated. Between the great flat expanses of bare, sun-warmed stone, small islands of coarse white sand have gathered in shallow dips. Here, vegetation survives in low-density constellations: tufts of wiry, jointed reeds that rattle like dry parchment; patches of rust-colored moss clinging to the margins of the shallows; and single, isolated dwarf shrubs whose roots have insinuated themselves into hairline fractures that were sealed when the planet was young. There are no forests, no canopies, no walls of brush. You can look across twenty miles of this country and see only the occasional low crown of a gnarled, leathery-leaved shrub, standing solitary in the middle of a shining pool, spaced hundreds of paces from its nearest neighbor.

Yet the emptiness is deceptive. If the plant life is muted, the place belongs entirely to water-borne creatures and the heat-quickened air, and it is overwhelmingly, deafeningly loud.

The sound is a physical presence. It presses against the skull: a continuous, high-amplitude drone generated by billions of stridulating insects. Millions of water-striders and heavy-bodied, metallic-winged insects skim the shallows or cling to the exposed tips of the reeds, their friction-calls interlocking into an unbroken, vibrating roar that sounds like high-voltage machinery submerged in milk. Underneath that high, abrasive hiss lies a deeper, percussive pulse—the croaking, bubbling, and throat-sac resonance of countless small amphibians that thrive in the endless warmth of the mud. Water splashes intermittently in broad, sudden twitches as lungfish or flattened, armored mud-bottom feeders shuffle across the submerged stone saddles, shifting from one shallow basin to another. 

Flocks of long-legged wading birds, pale as salt, move through the distance like slow white needles. They do not hurry. Their cries—harsh, wooden clatters that carry for miles across the flat—puncture the insect roar only to be absorbed into the damp expanse.

The air smells of oxidized iron, wet quartz, crushed reed-flesh, and the sweet, fermenting tang of warm algal slime baking on damp stone. There is no decay of the violent, rotting sort; the water is too shallow, too constantly sun-cleansed, and the nutrients too quickly claimed. 

Nothing changes quickly here. There are no sudden floods, because the basin is already full, and no droughts that can fully dry the subterranean bedrock sponges that bleed moisture upward day and night. The sun arcs from one side of the flat plate to the other, dragging the pale reflection of the clouds across miles of lukewarm glass, while the stone slowly cools a few degrees into the dusk, releasing its trapped warmth back into a low, buzzing mist that smells of the very beginning of the world.
