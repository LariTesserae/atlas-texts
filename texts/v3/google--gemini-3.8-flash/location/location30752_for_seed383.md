The rift floor sits two hundred fathoms below the continental shelf, illuminated by an unblinking, blinding glare. Far above, the rift’s ceiling is a skylight of fracture-cracked selenite and milky halite, catching the noon-state of an exterior sun and diffusing it down through the chasm without shadows. It is an optic shock: radiant, flat, and hard as bleached bone, yet the air in the basin never warms past the chill of a wet cellar.

There is no soil and nothing with a stem or leaf. The bedrock is ancient, rounded limestone and water-shaved tonalite, rubbed down by forgotten epochs of flow until the edges of every terrace and ledge curve like old teeth. It is a post-fluvial geography, settled into its ultimate, unbudging rest. Moisture here does not fall as rain; it breathes out of the stone. A cold, mineral perspiration beads on the undersides of overhangs, collects into hairline rills, and slips across worn flags to sit in shallow, saucer-shaped depressions before sinking back into the limestone pores.

Across this vast, low-lying expanse sits a city of preposterous horizontal spread. 

It is not an abandoned ruin, though its bones are archaic beyond memory. The settlement is constructed entirely of dry-stacked river rock, cleaved slate, and mortar mixed from lime and crushed shell. The walls rarely rise higher than a man’s chest, but they define a sprawling, deliberate urban geometry: long, sweeping boulevards eighty strides wide, circular amphitheatres with terraced seating for thousands, compartmentalized granaries, and endless, low-slung dwelling complexes that undulate across the basin floor. There are no arches, no forged lintels, no timber beams. Everything is balanced weight, friction, and fitted flat-stone, worn down by centuries of sand-scour and skin-contact until the masonry looks half-melted, indistinguishable from the floor it rests upon. 

Despite the grand urban footprint, the city is mostly emptiness. Vast, bare stone plazas separate the quarters, white and vacant under the milky light, swept clean of detritus. 

The quiet here is heavy, almost physical. The rift air possesses an acoustic viscosity—perhaps from the sheer weight of the atmosphere pooling in the deep, or the damping texture of the fine, salt-laced silt that coats the lower crevices—that swallows resonance before it can carry. A dropped slab makes only a dull, deadened thud; a shout dies thirty feet out, smothered into nothing.

Yet within this profound, dead silence, the landscape churns with manic, frantic motion.

The city is caught in a state of ceaseless, turbulent agitation. Down the broad, silent avenues, hundreds of thousands of pale, knuckle-sized scarabs and soft-bellied blind crabs run in boiling, cross-directional currents. They pour over the low limestone walls like spilled water, tumbling, righting themselves instantly, and scrambling forward in desperate, twitching errand. Above them, skimming inches over the stone terraces, wheel flocks of membranous, four-winged things—creatures the color of raw lard, entirely mute, with needle-fine beaks that snatch the skittering fauna from the cobbles. Their flight is not graceful; it is erratic, violent, and jagged, an endless mid-air collision of wings that beat without producing a hum, snapping their bodies sideways in turbulent drafts that drop unpredictably from the salt-ceiling.

Among this ecological frenzy move the builders. 

They are small-statured, thick-necked figures clad in skins of cured, oiled eel-hide, moving with the rapid, jerky economy of ants on an overturned rock. Their industry is relentless and seemingly without central design. In complete, eerie silence, work-gangs of fifty or sixty tear down a low terrace wall with crude horn wedges and stone mallets, only to run the components—stone by polished stone—three hundred yards across a glaring white plaza to raise another wall identical to the first. They work at a sprint. Hands fly, fingers bound in gut-wrappings deftly nestling smooth tonalite pebbles into gaps; mortar is slung from bladder-pouches with flat bone trowels; ropes of braided sinew are hauled in frantic unison. They do not pause to wipe the sweat that mingles with the chasm’s condensation on their brows. 

They pass one another in the wide thoroughfares without slowing, shoulder to shoulder, carrying baskets of chalk-grit or dragging heavy, rounded sledges loaded with river-shale. No signals are shouted. No horns sound. There is only the mute, frantic churning of hands and feet against the ancient, dampened stone.

Where the city meets a depression in the basin floor, the turbulence reaches a fever pitch. Here, the subterranean perspiration collects into a broad, skin-deep lake that never ripples from wind, but shivers continuously under the churning bodies within it. Millions of translucent, legless amphipods churn the shallow water into a cold froth, feeding on invisible carpets of subterranean bacteria. Primitive stone sluices, laid out in intricate concentric spirals, channel this agitated, milky fluid through the center of the district. The inhabitants wade directly through it, thrusting coarse willow-less wicker traps into the churn, hauling up squirming masses of white flesh, dumping them into cisterns, and immediately resetting their traps in an unbroken, hyperactive loop.

The light overhead does not dim. It remains frozen at noon—a sterile, omnidirectional glare that bleaches the color out of the shale and turns the pooling water into blinding sheets of mercury. Under this frozen sky, the contrast is absolute: an architecture that has reached the end of time, utterly eroded and immutable, inhabited by a feverish, wordless kinetic storm that never sleeps, never rests, and never makes a sound.
