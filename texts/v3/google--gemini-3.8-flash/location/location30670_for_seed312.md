The peaks here are barely out of their upheaval. Their ribs are still raw, sharp-edged, shattered rather than worn down, breaking the pale blue of a high-altitude sky with long, splintered crenellations of slate, basalt, and dark diorite. It is cold enough that moisture does not flow so much as cling: it sits as a fine, greasy glaze of rime on northern facets, or gathers in the early morning as a dense, freezing dew that beads along hair and stone before vanishing into the dry, thin air. There are no great rivers here, only the seepage of cold rock—damp clefts where moss-like crusts and soot-colored lichens take hold, and stone cisterns chiseled by hand to catch the slow, grey weep of the shale.

Yet within this high, breath-stealing altitude, spanning an entire fault-line valley that stretches beyond the curvature of sight, lies a city of staggering, crowded proportion.

It is built entirely without mortar or iron. It is an urban sprawl of titanic dry-stone masonry, terraces upon terraces stacked ten, twenty, thirty tiers deep into the mountain flanks, holding back hundreds of thousands of tons of fractured scree. The architecture is low, thick-walled, and dense, fashioned from angular slabs hammered from the bedrock with harder river-stones. Streets are not wide avenues but steep, narrow flights of flagstones, shallowly graded so that four-legged beasts may walk them without slipping. Houses lean into houses, sharing thick retaining walls, their roofs thatched with coarse, pale tussock-grasses gathered from the lower fringes of the plateau, bound tightly with braided leather thongs and weighted down against the mountain gales with heavy, river-worn boulders.

Because of the thinness of the air and the biting chill, the scale of life here is intensely huddled, dense with bodies and materials. Between the stone dwellings, the alleys are choked with animals. High-altitude stock—heavy, barrel-chested beasts with double coats of matted wool, broad cloven hooves, and flat, patient eyes—stand packed shoulder-to-shoulder in sunken stone pens and along the ramparts. Thousands of them murmur and shift, their breath rising in continuous, pale plumes that blend with the grey smoke of dried-dung fires. There are smaller animals, too: fat, short-eared rodents with thick, slate-grey fur that dart through the weep-holes in the stone terraces; colonies of blue-breasted mountain swifts nesting in the crevices under every lintel; and pale, heavy-feathered eagles that roost openly upon the roof beams, tame enough to hop from ledge to ledge, tolerated for the vermin they pluck from the granaries.

The light is harsh, direct, and clear. Lacking the heavy mantle of lower-atmosphere haze, the sun cuts across the mountain ridges with razor clarity, dividing every alley and courtyard into two absolute domains: blinding, diamond-hard brightness and pitch-black, frost-retaining shadow. Where the light strikes, the stone warms just enough to release a faint, mineral smell—flint, cold water, and animal grease. In the shade, the ice never melts, and the mud between the cobbles remains hard as iron, preserving the footprints of men and sheep for seasons at a time.

Despite the concentration of hundreds of thousands of souls living tier upon tier along forty miles of mountain spine, the soundscape is curiously subdued. The cold dampens everything. Sound carries far in the thin air, yet it arrives thin and clipped, stripped of echo. You do not hear the roaring hum of a lowland city; there are no wagons, for the wheel has never found purchase on these steps, and no iron clatter of smithies. Instead, there is the dry, rhythmic clack of bone needles against wooden frames; the soft, perpetual scrape of stone hoes clearing frost from narrow kitchen-shelves of soil; the shuffling rustle of countless animals shifting on their bedding; and the hollow, percussive *thwack* of men dressing slate with rounded mauls of green serpentine. When people speak, they speak in low, breath-conserving registers, their mouths covered by thick woolen cowls.

Water is a precious, carefully husbanded presence. Rain is rare, arriving only as sudden, biting squalls of graupel that bounce like seed-pearls across the slate before being swept into stone gutters. More often, the dampness is collected from the clouds themselves, which drag their bellies through the upper tiers of the city during the afternoon stillness. The stone walls are intentionally stepped and grooved to catch the condense-run, channeling the grey trickles into deep, covered pits dug into the permafrost, where the water stays pure, chilled, and black as glass.

Nothing happens quickly here. Change is measured by the gradual settling of terrace walls under the weight of centuries of talus, or the slow accumulation of yellow soot against the lintels of ancient hearths. The people move with a deliberate, slow-lunged grace, their chests deep, their skin wind-burnt to the color of dry leather. They wear layered cloaks of coarse, undyed felt—greasy with sheep-lanolin to turn away the damp—fastened with long pins carved from the leg bones of mountain birds.

Looking outward from the highest wall of the upper district, the sheer vastness of the geography swallows even this enormous settlement. The mountain range extends in infinite repetitions of slate-grey, ochre, and lead, ridge after razor ridge marching into the blue horizon like the vertebrae of a buried world. Clouds hang below the city’s lower gates, white and flat as salt flats, hiding the forgotten lowlands beneath a sea of vapor. Above, the summits remain untouched, barren and clean, where the raw rock was broken only yesterday in the earth’s time, glittering faintly with frost under a sun that gives light, but never warmth.
