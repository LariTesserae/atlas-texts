The plateau does not meet the sky so much as it occupies the same altitude as the weather’s origins. It is broad enough that the curvature of the world drops away beneath it in three directions, while in the fourth, the terrain rises into a series of rounded, worn-down stumps of granite—mountains that have spent half a billion years being ground flat by wind and ice until they resemble the sleeping bellies of whales. 

The air is bitter and thin, tasting of iron shavings and cold well-water. It hovers just above freezing, enough to turn a man’s breath into immediate steam, yet it does not snow; instead, a persistent, beaded dampness condenses directly onto surfaces out of the pale daylight. Every lintel, every leaf, and every pebble is filmed with a grease of cold water that never quite collects into brooks, but sits in pooling depressions, sweating silently in the grey glare of a noon that seems fixed in place.

Across these hundreds of miles of slate-colored tableland sit the towns. 

They are undeniably cities, laid out with deliberate civic intent, yet they are separated from one another by days of absolute emptiness. They were raised by hands that possessed nothing sharper than chipped chert and cold-hammered native copper. The architecture is cyclopean and low: dry-stacked basalt boulders the size of hay-wains, fitted together with such punishing labor that the joints are tight enough to shear a hair. Long, sunken avenues run between these windowless bastions. Broad ceremonial plazas, paved with river cobbles brought up from some abyss long since vanished, stretch out toward horizons that fail to behave correctly.

Here, the masonry bears the stamp of primitive tools—the pecked surfaces of granite, the gouges left by bone wedges, the charred ends of roof timbers as thick as masts—yet the geometry it encloses has detached itself from the ground.

A street will begin between two rows of stone storehouses, straight and level, paved with flat flagstones that glisten with seepage. As one walks along it, the buildings on the right do not diminish with distance; they grow larger, rearing up toward the sky until their coping-stones block out the zenith, while the buildings on the left dwindle to the size of thimbles, though both remain precisely three paces apart. If you step from the right curb to the left, your boots do not cross the gutter; your legs pass through a seam where the weight of your own body abruptly doubles, and the perspective re-inverts, the miniature stones suddenly swelling beneath you while the wall behind you collapses into a tiny, distant smudge.

The people who built this understood the seam. The stone is carved with shallow, rhythmic notches at the thresholds—notches cut with stone adzes, marking where one may safely put a heel before the horizontal plane folds ninety degrees into an updraft.

Between these stone settlements, the high plains are not barren; they are smothered in an absurd, quiet lushness. It is not a forest, for trees cannot endure the altitude, but a crawling, muscular carpet of cold-climate flora that thrives on the stones’ condensation. Liverworts as thick as mattresses roll across the tundra, their lobes pale sea-green and greasy to the touch. Giant, spongy mosses swell into mounds twenty feet high, their interiors warm and fermenting, breathing out faint plumes of vapor into the chill. Vast ferns, their fronds stiffened with a natural wax against the frost, uncurl along the megalithic walls with a wet, audible rustling. They grow swiftly, visibly—an inch every hour, swelling and dying in overlapping generational cycles, their decaying fronds forming a black, sterile peat that coats the bases of the stone towers.

Yet there is nothing to eat them. 

No beetle burrows into the rotting moss. No slug trails silver across the cold, damp basalt. No bird circles the updrafts of the broken horizon. The silence is immense, broken only by the dynamic, mechanical operations of the earth itself: the low, gravelly grinding of the foundations as the city blocks slowly slide across the plateau’s surface like rafts on grease, and the perpetual, wet slurring of the plants expanding over the stones.

At the edge of one district, where hundreds of small circular dwellings cluster like barnacles around a terraced acropolis, a set of broad stairs leads down into the rock. The steps are worn deep by bare feet—thousands of generations of barefoot transit, wearing down basalt by fractions of a millimeter. But halfway down the flight, the stairs cease to lead downward. The stone simply continues upward into the open, pale sky, so that anyone descending finds themselves walking out into the high air, their weight resting against nothing, their clothes soaked by the low clouds blowing through their knees. 

Below that impossible staircase, visible through tears in the cloud-bank, the landscape does not fall away to plains or sea. Instead, one looks down onto the very plateau one stands upon, viewed from an impossible height: the same rounded mountains, the same moss-blanketed urban ribs, moving slowly across the surface of the world like the tectonic plates of an engine that forgot its purpose, churning in the cold, wet light of a sky that has no sun, only an even, omnidirectional brightness that leaves no shadow anywhere.
