At this altitude, the horizon does not settle into a line; it frays along the grain. 

The plateau is an expanse of cold, petrified cambium, five thousand meters above any known sea. It is flat not because it was planed by glaciers, but because it has grown to its absolute limit of vertical tension and can now only widen, peeling off sideways into the dry, thin air. There is no stone here. If you bore through the crust for three miles, you will find only compressed growth rings—bands of red-brown, iron-dense heartwood laid down before the atmosphere had oxygen, alternating with sheets of cold, gray cellulose as hard as vulcanized rubber. The ground yields by fractions of a millimeter under a heavy boot, damp and faintly resinous, weeping a clear, bitter water wherever the compression becomes too great.

Spread across this flat, ancient grain is the settlement of Ochs-and-Tallow: an industrial district whose city limits are measured not in streets, but in the stroke-lengths of horizontal push-rods. 

The architecture is sparse, deliberate, and vast. A single processing house might occupy four acres, constructed from structural timbers so immense they must be kept under constant hydraulic tension via external turnbuckles to prevent their fibers from uncoiling. Between these structures lie kilometers of nothing—bare, shaved planes of black timber-shelf, glistening with condensation that gathers during the midday thaw, crossed only by single-track iron gantries and reciprocating drive-shafts that run on oiled lignum-vitae bearings.

The noise is not a roar; it is a mechanical cadence so deep and pervasive it replaces the pulse in the ears. It is the sound of ten thousand reciprocating saws with teeth the size of church pews, working unattended at the edges of the district, and the rhythmic, hollow *clack-drub* of the arterial steam networks. High-pressure steam, generated in subterranean boilers that burn the dry pith of dead layers below, is piped across the emptiness through jacketed copper lines. Where the lines leak, the steam does not rise; the air is too thin and the barometric pressure too unstable, so the white vapor rolls along the wooden floor like sheep, smelling of sulfur and wet elderberries.

The light is broad, pale, and without charity. It comes through a high, uniform ceiling of thin cloud that seems to hang only twenty feet above the highest smokestacks, casting no shadows, illuminating the rust on the iron boilers and the pale, fibrous fuzz that sprouts on every surface not washed down with kerosene twice daily.

Here, the botanical is not an ecology that occupies the land; it is the physical law of the land. It behaves with an invasive, mechanical logic that has begun to warp the geometry of the works. 

Where the heavy stamping mills have stood for more than a century, their foundations have been digested and re-uttered by the floor. An iron flywheel six yards wide no longer turns on its axle; the axle has grown knots, bifurcated, and rooted into the pedestal, while the cast-iron spokes have developed green, vascular channels that carry cold oil from the sump to the ceiling trusses. The engine still runs, but its timing has drifted from the mathematics of gears to the slow, erratic twitching of a dying taproot. When it misfires, the shockwave travels through the solid floor for miles, kicking up small fountains of stagnant tannin-water from the seams between the boards.

Distances across the open areas are untrustworthy. The wood has an orientation—a prevailing nap—and walking against the grain takes three times as long as walking with it, though the ground appears level to an engineer’s spirit. A surveyor looking through a theodolite across the three-mile gap between the Sulphite Works and the Billet Yard will watch the far chimney split into three identical chimneys, each smoking in a different direction, because the light is being refracted along the micro-fissures of the living air-cells that bleed from the timber-shelf into the sky.

Nothing lives here that has blood. Occasionally, between the long tracks of the gantry cranes, something small and gray will skitter—not an animal, but an offshoot, an ambulatory gall formed of woven birch-wire and ball-bearings that has dropped from a grease-cup and rolls until its wind-up tension is spent. 

Over everything lies a perpetual, shivering damp. It is not wet enough for rot—the altitude is too cold, the air too antiseptic with creosote—but it is wet enough that iron tools left on an anvil overnight will be glued to the metal by morning with a fine, orange skin of lichenous rust. 

At the northern margin of the district, the wood simply ends. It does not drop off into a cliff; rather, the grain becomes loose, fibrous, and un-joined, turning into a sea of vertical bristles eighty feet high, vibrating with the collective torque of the machines behind them. They hiss together against the dry wind like the bristles of an enormous broom sweeping the bottom of the cloud, waiting for the industrial lines to advance another yard, to lay down the rails, to drive the wedges, and to plane the world out flat once more.
