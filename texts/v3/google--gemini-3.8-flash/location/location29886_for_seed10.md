The plateau sits so high that the air has lost its thickness, yet it carries the weight of a constant, freezing drizzle—water so cold it borders on slush, weeping out of the atmosphere onto a surface that is neither stone nor soil. The bedrock died hundreds of millions of years ago, ground down by uncounted epochs of ice and scouring rain until it was planed into an endless, low-slung curvature. In its place, the world is floored entirely by a fibrous, black-green stratum: living matter compressed through deep time into the density of cast iron, sponge-wet at the top, slick with glacial grease, and vast. 

There are no buildings here, nothing raised by hands, yet the plateau operates with the deafening, unlubricated violence of an open-air foundry. 

The vegetation did not stop at being flora; it evolved into mechanism, or perhaps physics rotted out from underneath it and left only the memory of work. Across miles of empty, gray-lit steppe—sparse stretches where nothing stands except the shivering horizon—isolated colossal structures rise like lone blast furnaces carved from bog-iron and knotted yew. They are single plants, centuries old and half a mile apart, their interior xylem conduits pressurized to bursting. Deep within their trunks, where sap meets naturally occurring sulfuric acids and pockets of cold peat-gas, combustion occurs. 

It is not gentle growth. It is heavy, industrial metabolism. Massive, jointed taproots as thick as river barges piston in and out of the ground with slow, concussive thuds that shake the water out of the mire for leagues around. Flywheels of lignified ironwood, thirty yards across and caked with weeping liverwort, spin on bearings of slick, secreted lipids, shrieking in the wind as they cast off fine sprays of freezing sap. Thick plumes of greasy white vapor erupt from natural exhaust flues atop the canopy, coughing steam into the dim, perpetual twilight, only to condense instantly and fall back as freezing rain.

The scale of the place is wide and bleak. You can look out across forty miles of open plateau and see only five or six of these churning macro-stems, their black silhouettes spaced far apart in the pale fog, pumping against the skyline like the derricks of a drowned oilfield. But between them, the emptiness is deceptive.

The ground itself is a hydraulic nervous system. Underneath the rubbery turf, pressurized conduits pulse and shiver. Where the internal pressure peaks, the peat ruptures, shooting jets of scalding, iron-rich water sixty cubits into the freezing mountain air, where it crystallizes into brown slush mid-fall and rattles down like gravel. Reality does not hold its lines straight here; the sheer kinetic output of these biological engines seems to warp the geometry of the plateau. In the hollows between the pumping towers, distances telescope unpredictably. You might watch a bank of fog roll across what looks to be twenty paces of flat lichen, only for the fog to sink into an impossible fold—a valley that exists only laterally, its floor vibrating with the hum of high-voltage galvanic sap arcing between subterranean roots.

The air smells of crushed pine needles, machine oil, raw ozone, and stale peat. It is cold enough to turn breath to frost instantly, yet the peat beneath your feet trembles with hot, feverish friction.

Life thrives in this frantic, mechanized swamp, though it has adapted to the violence. Enormous, blind-eyed arthropods, their carapaces coated in dense downy moss to keep out the chill, cling to the thrashing joints of the piston-trees, scythe-like mouthparts scraping away the boiling fats that leak from the bark. Flocks of leathery, long-winged things—neither bat nor gull, their bellies stained with rust—wheel through the low-hanging exhaust clouds in their thousands, riding the violent thermal updrafts caused by the steam vents. They feed on aerial plankton: billions of microscopic spores and floating grease-droplets kicked into the turbulent sky by the pistons. Down in the wet crevices, where cold water rushes in violent, black flumes across the ironwood roots, pale, eel-like amphibians churn the slurry, feeding on the electrical drift that arcs from the roots into the saturated ground. The water itself glows with a faint, dying blue luminescence, sparked by static discharge and extinguished by the cold.

Nothing stays settled. The dynamic is one of perpetual, storm-like production. A piston-tree will hammer itself to pieces over the course of three days, its fibrous struts tearing free under the load, shattering into colossal splinters that rain down onto the marsh; within hours, a dozen fast-growing tap-spindles erupt from the wreckage, spinning and swelling, drinking the deluge of icy rain, hungry to take up the rhythm again. 

Sound does not travel normally. The concussive *whump* of the root-pistons travels through the dense, waterlogged floor faster than through the thin air, striking your knees and teeth before the sound reaches your ears—a rhythmic, subterranean heartbeat, laid over by the high, metallic scream of the wind tearing through rotating wooden turbines, the hissing of steam vents, and the shrieking of millions of feeding birds overhead. 

Above it all, the light never truly arrives. The sky remains an unmoving ceiling of cold zinc, dim and heavy, lit from below only by the erratic orange flashes of peat-gas combusting in the tree hollows and the dull blue snap of static across the mud. It is an ancient world that has flattened all its mountains into a single endless shelf, worn out its dirt, cast aside its quietude, and set its own wild, mindless marrow to work.
