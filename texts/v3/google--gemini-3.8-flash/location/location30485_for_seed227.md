The air inside the sink is colder than the plateau above it by ten degrees, and it does not move. It sits like well-water in a stone jar, trapped beneath an overhanging lid of limestone and hemlock roots that cuts off the sky to a thin, violet seam sixty feet overhead. 

What fills the space is sound. It does not come from falling water—there is only a cold sweat on the stone, gathering in the furrows of old runnels and falling drop by drop into gravel—but from somewhere beneath the floor. A blind river runs through an unvented syphon forty yards deeper down, forced through a constriction in the bedrock at massive pressure. The rock beneath your boots is not merely vibrating; it is shouting. The sound is a blunt, continuous roar, flat and physical, striking the breastbone and teeth before the ear can parse it as noise. Because the room is an enclosed dome, three hundred paces across and nearly devoid of rubble, the acoustic reflections fold over themselves until there is no direction to the noise: it is a solid condition of the chamber, like its chill or its darkness. One has to lean against the wall to feel where the sound is hardest, but even four paces out into the gravel, the hollow interior acts as a resonating drum.

The chamber is mostly empty volume. The floor slopes gently toward a dry central depression lined with small, gray pebbles of chert and river-rounded quartz, laid down when the water table was higher, perhaps three thousand years ago. There are no scree piles, no fallen boulders; the limestone roof was scoured clean before the lower drainage captured the flow, leaving a vast, arched ceiling that is smooth as a scooped marrow bone. 

Around the low perimeter, where the condensation from the ceiling drips consistently upon the ledges, there is a dense, cold greenness. It does not have the yellow vigor of sunlit growth. It is made of flat, overlapping scales of liverwort, *Marchantia* that has grown thick and leathery in the twilight, alongside broad cushions of an uncommonly dark, blackish moss that thrives on the constant wash of bicarbonate-rich moisture. These mats grow several inches thick, hanging off the rims of the natural benches like cold, sodden felt. A few species of delicate, hair-stemmed spleenworts lodge in the hair-line fissures where the lime has softened into grease. They are still; the wind does not reach down here, and the plant life has adapted to the violent acoustic trembling of the rock by growing low, glued to the stone by dense carpets of rhizoids.

On the southern ledge, just above the reach of the floor’s damp perimeter, are the traces of old work. 

They are small and widely separated. Two baulks of split oak, blackened by damp and age until they resemble coal, are wedged between the rock floor and a natural horizontal shelf. They were trimmed with a broad-bitted axe; the facet-marks are coarse and short, the work of someone working in tight quarters without room to swing. One of the timbers has three square holes driven through its heart with a hot iron punch, each hole containing the broken-off stub of an oak trunnel. Nearby, laid directly on the bare rock, rests a dry-stone crib—no larger than an unrolled blanket—filled with fist-sized nodules of dark brown limonite. The iron ore is cold to the touch and slick with a microscopic grease of iron-oxidizing bacteria. A few paces further, someone had bored a series of shallow holes into the bedrock using a hollow drill and sand; the cores have been snapped out, leaving small, perfectly circular wells that hold half an inch of clear, mineralized water.

Nothing lives here that relies on sight. If you take a light to the gravel beds, there are long intervals of nothing—dozens of paces of clean, damp stone—before you find the pale, transparent casing of an amphipod, or a single harvestman with legs as fine as cobwebs, suspended motionless against the damp curve of the wall. These creatures do not scurry when the stone quakes; the tremor is their constant ground. Once or twice in an hour, a pale slug, blind and nearly four inches long, will cross a stretch of liverwort, leaving a track that remains silver in the dimness for days.

The light at midday is never more than an iron-colored dusk. It enters entirely from the north, where the shelf above has parted slightly along a joint plane, but by the time it reaches the floor it has been diffused by three layers of wet ferns and the broad, hanging leaves of mountain maple that crowd the lip above. It reveals only the shapes of things: the great curving ribs of the eroded limestone, the vastness of the empty air between the walls, and the matte, light-drinking green of the floor-mats. 

The erosion here is done with its hurry. The ceiling does not drop chips; the water does not carve fresh grooves. The room has found a geometry that resists the cold and the vibration. A drop of water forms at the tip of an ancient, rounded boss of flowstone, hangs for three minutes until its weight overcomes the tension of the cold air, and falls thirty feet into the chert. The strike of the drop is utterly lost in the roar from below, vanished into the general thunder that has hollowed out the hill and left this high room behind it, quiet to the eye, violent to the ribs, and dark.
