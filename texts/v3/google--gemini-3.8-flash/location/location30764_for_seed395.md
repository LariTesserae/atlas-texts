Across hundreds of miles of sunken plain, the earth is covered in a contiguous, bone-pale metropolis that was never planned and never designed by a thinking mind. 

It is constructed of cast calcium, dried salivary cement, fine alluvial grit, and biological resins, rising from the floor of an ancient salt-flat that has not held standing water in ten thousand seasons. The sheer scale is continental: a labyrinthine sprawl of spires, perforated domes, layered colonnades, and buttressed ramps that reaches out to every horizon, so vast that morning light creates thermal wind-fronts across its roofs, shifting dust in broad, silent waves between its districts.

Yet it is entirely fresh. There is almost no erosion here. The cornices and pinnacles have crisp, micro-crystalline edges; the galleries and layered facades bear the fresh, concentric ridges of creatures that have only just troweled them into shape within the hour. Where wind or age might wear away a fluted parapet, a thousands-strong column of blind, eight-legged workers immediately layers new, wet secretion over the gap, curing instantly in the baking heat into a stone-hard mineral paste. The landscape does not decay; it builds faster than entropy can smooth it.

Beneath the warm, clear sunlight—unfiltered by cloud or humidity—the city is packed tight. There are no plazas or open fields. Every square foot of ground is built upon, built through, or tunneled beneath. Between towering flutes that rise forty feet into the dry air, narrow corridors wind in dizzying complexity, spanned by arched bridges and flying buttresses of layered clay. It resembles a coral reef dried out, scaled up a hundredfold, and colonized by the architecture of giant wasps and social termites, yet on a civic, imperial scale.

Life here has decoupled itself from open moisture. Water is not found in pools or streams; it exists only as bound hydration locked within the cellular tissues of the plants and the blood of the builders. In every terrace and vertical slit of the masonry, vegetation has rooted in extraordinary density. These are stiff, xerophytic plants: columnar euphorbias with spines like copper needles, sprawling mats of powdery blue lichens, and thick, cork-barked creepers that bind the architectural spires together. They have fleshy, wax-sealed lobes and deep, wire-thin root networks that penetrate into the cool chalk-rock miles down. Their sap is thick as grease, bitter and mineral-rich. Some terraces are choked with dense stands of gray-leaved spindle-trees that produce no flowers, only hard, dry husks that crack in the heat to release winged, papery seeds.

Movement is constant, yet it takes place in an uncanny, profound quiet. 

Billions of inhabitants crawl, scuttle, and climb through the streets. They are social arthropods and soft-scaled reptiles, ranging in size from the width of a fingernail to creatures the mass of draft oxen that haul blocks of sun-dried resin along the broad viaducts. They communicate not with vocal cords, but with chemical trails, the brushing of antennae, and the low-frequency vibrations of their shells against the pavement. From a high vantage, the view across the spires is a riot of ceaseless industry—endless streams of workers pouring out of triangular portals, foraging through the gray scrub, tending to the nursery-hollows in the lower towers—but the only sound is an oceanic, papery murmur. It is the dry hiss of claws over calcified dust, the dry clack of a mandrel, the faint tick of drying resin setting under the sun.

The air smells of warm chalk, sun-baked wax, and the dry, peppered scent of dried foliage. There is no rot here, because there is no moisture to fuel decay; dead leaves and shed carapaces are simply gathered, masticated, and bound into the mortar of the next tier of arches. 

Because the landscape is so low and flat, the city defines its own topography. Districts form distinct biological zones: some areas, thick with resinous vines, are dark green and amber, buzzing with silent, felt-winged pollinators; other regions are sheer, white, vertical canyons dedicated to drying brood-combs, where the sun reflects with blinding intensity off fresh, crystalline walls. 

Below the inhabited crust, within the endless foundations that have swallowed the original soil, the temperature remains steady and cool. There, in the dim, powdery depths of the under-city, the foundations merge seamlessly into the raw geology of the basin, unworked and ancient, waiting to be honeycombed, reinforced, and raised toward the dry, brilliant sky.
