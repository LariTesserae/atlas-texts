The roar is not a single note; it is a solid, crushing volume that replaces hearing with vibration in the skull. It is the sound of thousands of tons of warm brine colliding with itself inside a space too small to hold it, a relentless, concussive cavitation that rattles the teeth and vibrates the marrow of the ribs. 

Here, at an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, there is no air at all. Instead, an amphitheater of black, razor-edged stone—a cirque fractured so recently that its broken facets still glint like freshly cleaved flint—is filled to its brim with a dense, tepid sea. The liquid does not flow over the jagged rim to plunge down the mountain slopes. At the threshold where the rock ends and the sky begins, the water simply shears against the open void, held upright by an invisible, trembling skin that flexes and bulges outward against the atmosphere like an overfilled drop of oil.

The scale is that of a sunken cathedral, perhaps two hundred yards across, ringed by sheer walls of volcanic basalt that show none of the rounded soft curves of water wear. The edges of the shelves are viciously sharp, capable of splitting flesh or canvas with the slightest brush. Down inside this basin, the light is reduced to an ash-colored gloom—a dim, slate-hued dusk that filters down through thick suspensions of gray grit and churned lather. 

The heat is oppressive. It is not the chill of alpine snows, but a heavy, feverish warmth that leaks from the floor of the fracture, smelling faintly of sulfur, wet slate, and raw copper. Thick plumes of boiling water erupt from fissures in the floor, but they do not rise to an escape; they are immediately caught in a vicious, horizontal gyre that spins the entire basin into a vortex of boiling, blinding foam. The turbulence is erratic, almost organic: great bells of vacuum tear open within the fluid mass, remain empty for a heartbeat—revealing glimpses of naked, jagged crag—and then implode with cracks that sound like cannon fire.

Scattered across the drowned shelves are the remains of low, dry-stacked stone structures. They are not ancient ruins worn smooth by centuries, but crude shelters or altars built only days or weeks before the basin flooded, still showing the pale chalk marks of their quarrying. Some of these masonry blocks have been ripped loose by the current, drifting slowly through the mid-water like dead livestock, their mass buoyed by strange densities and salt layers that form and dissolve within minutes. 

Vegetation is nearly absent, reduced to sparse, rubbery filaments of rust-colored moss clinging desperately into the hairline fractures of the rock. They don't wave gently; they are whipped flat against the stone, shredded by the violent friction of the water until only their black root-knots remain. 

Nothing swims here with intention. Occasionally, tumbling through the murk like a discarded rag, something pale and segmented is visible—a blind, soft-shelled organism three feet long, with translucent legs twitching against the churning draft, born from some deeper fissure and pulverized against the obsidian walls within minutes of arrival.

Above, if one looks through the boiling, distorted lens of the water’s boundary, the gray sky outside is visible, upside-down and spinning, while the roar inside the basin grinds on—an endless, blind engine of pressure, heat, and shattered glass, suspended in the sky where clouds ought to be.
