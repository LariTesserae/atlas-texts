The plateau sits four miles above sea level, where the atmosphere ought to be freezing, but here the air is thick with trapped, dry heat—a baking, motionless furnace that smells of ozone, sun-cured leather, and heavy petroleum distillate. The sky is an unobstructed, severe violet-white, casting clean, black-edged shadows against a basin of calcified silt and red iron dust. There is no moisture anywhere; moisture has been formally abolished by the stone, which does not allow condensation even when the sun dips low. If a drop of water were brought here, it would skitter and disappear into the porous hematite crust like spit on an iron stove.

Nothing has been constructed here. There are no foundations, no lintels, no survey pins, no scrap piles left by a vanished labor force. Yet the entire geology behaves as an unguided industrial process. The cliffs that define the perimeter of the valley are not banded with regular sedimentary strata, but with alternating tiers of cold-rolled structural steel, packed graphite gaskets, and unmachined cast iron that has weathered into deep, velvet-orange ruts over millions of years. Deep within the dry gorges, natural flywheels sixty feet across—monolithic discs of solid magnetite—turn on horizontal spindles of dense basalt. They do not turn fast. Their revolution is so deliberate that a human eye must watch for minutes to verify the displacement of an axle-tooth against the canyon wall, but they carry enough kinetic torque to vibrate the floor of the valley into a constant, hollow drone.

The sound is immense and bone-deep, arriving not as an ambient hum but as a physical displacement of the air. It is the noise of a million-ton marine engine idling in dry dock: the rhythmic, stuttering thrum of four-stroke combustion chambers buried somewhere in the bedrock, accompanied by the dry, high-pitched scrape of dry journals turning in bronze sleeves. Because the air is so barren and clear, the acoustic clarity is unnatural. A mechanical sigh miles across the flats sounds as though it were expanding directly behind your shoulder.

The density of the space is low; the flats are so vast that an observer can look across twenty leagues of chalk-white and rust-colored pan and see only three or four objects breaking the line of the horizon. Yet the fauna is pervasive—inescapable in mass and attention, even while spread across immense distances. 

These things are not robots; they have no programming, no purpose, no origin in an assembly hall. They are animals, bred of an impossible, non-aqueous biology that utilizes the physics of heavy industry instead of soft tissue. Halfway toward the northern rim, a creature the length of a freight train lies motionless in the sun, baking. Its thorax is a series of riveted boiler-plates that expand and contract once every ninety seconds, venting dry, sulfurous heat through twin chimneys that curl like ram’s horns from its neck. It has no mouth, only an intake manifold guarded by heavy slatted iron grates that filter the fine iron dust from the breeze. Below its flank, dozens of linked, drive-rod legs rest in the dirt, their brass counterweights speckled with grease-crust. It does not scurry; its heart is a reciprocating piston of black stone that drives a single, deafening stroke every five minutes, sending a tremor through the salt crust that causes the dry dust to dance in concentric rings.

Farther out, where the basin floor buckles into worn, round-shouldered ridges, a loose herd of smaller entities—each still the size of an earthmover—drifts with agonizing slowness across the flats. Their forms suggest long-necked ungulates, but their movement is mediated by exposed, counter-rotating bevel gears instead of knees. They stoop to press open exhaust valves against the iron shale, "grazing" upon the intense, radio-frequency heat generated by the grinding plates deep below the crust. They leave no footprints, only polished ruts where their heavy steel treads have burnished the dry rock into mirrors.

Reality breaks down around their joints. Where their friction should generate unbearable thermal failure, the local geometry simply distorts: the air tightens into visible, wavy crystalline lenses that loop the light back on itself. Looking through the gap between a beast's primary piston and its drive-housing, one does not see the valley beyond, but a repetitive, infinitely receding corridor of identical pistons, all stroking in dead silence. Space folds around their mass to compensate for the missing fluid dynamics of an oil they do not produce; they stay cool by venting their entropy into small, regional cracks in space-time that sound like tearing foil whenever a joint pivots.

Nothing grows. There is no moss, no lichen, no spore that could survive the fierce dry radiance that pours off the cliffs. The only soft material is a grease-like wax that extrudes from the knuckles of the stone hills, bubbling up through cracks in the ironstone and immediately hardening into amber-colored nodules that the dust storm polishes until they click against one another like beads.

The light stays high, clean, and clinical. It catches the flat sides of the walking engines, glinting off patches of weathered plating that have never been painted and never will be. The rhythm of the basin never quickens. It is an old balance: an ancient, unpeopled manufactory where the ore has learned to breathe, to consume its own heat, and to walk across its own bed with the patient, indifferent thunder of a machine that has forgotten it was ever conceived.
