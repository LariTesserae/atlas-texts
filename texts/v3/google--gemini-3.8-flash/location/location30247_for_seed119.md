It is an under-hall, built entirely by hands that had only timber wedges, river-sand abrasives, and soft bronze adzes, yet it was built to the scale of an emptied reservoir. Every foot of it is floor, wall, or ceiling; nowhere does living earth or native bedrock break the surface. The material is a thick, dark-red earthenware tile, square-baked and fitted with tongue-and-groove joints that have, over centuries of moisture and slow shifting, settled into a supple, watertight skin. The room is perhaps two hundred paces long and eighty paces wide, vaulted with a low, barrel-sprung ceiling that sits heavy and close over the emptiness.

Because it was designed to hold or channel something long forgotten, the center of the hall is a wide, shallow scoop—a dish of tiled emptiness. There are almost no columns, no dividing partitions, no furniture, and no rubbish. The density of physical structure within the room is near zero: a vast, cold lens of humid air suspended over a single, gently concave plane of glazed terracotta.

Yet it is not quiet, and it is far from dry.

Water arrives from the high ceiling joints in a rhythm so constant it has ceased to be a series of drops and become an organ-pipe hiss, interspersed with heavy, flat slaps where condensation gathers on the crown of the tiles and lets go. The air is cool—the temperature of spring mud—and thick with the smell of wet clay, river marrow, and ancient, waterlogged brick. A film of grey water, rarely more than an ankle deep, sheets over the tiled floor, sliding in slow, thin ripples toward a circular bronze drain-grate at the center. The bronze has oxidized into a furry turquoise paste, but the slots remain open, swallowing the skin of water with a perpetual, guttural throat-rattle that echoes off the curved roof.

Because of the acoustics of the tiles and the tight curve of the barrel vault, every sound is multiplied and sharpened. A drop sounds like a small hammer striking slate. The air is never resting; it roars softly, an unceasing, white-noise churning of trickles, slaps, clatters, and gurgles.

Plant life cannot maintain a hold here. There is no soil, and the light is a perpetual, tallow-colored dusk that filters down only through eight high, square venting shafts that rise to the surface somewhere far above. In the wash of this faint light, nothing green survives. There are only grey crusts of slime mold clinging to the grooved tile joints, and a pale, rubbery fungus that spreads like damp leather along the baseboards where the floor curves up to meet the walls.

In this hollow, wet stone gut, however, the animals are thick and in continuous motion.

They do not come from the ceiling shafts; they live here, adapted entirely to the hard, unyielding tile. Chief among them are the pale, long-limbed river-crawlers—amphibians the size of dogs, with leathery, scaleless skin the color of skimmed milk. Their bellies are flat, and their wide, spade-shaped toes are tipped with horn pads that give them traction on the slick, algae-greased ceramic. They run in erratic, skittering bursts across the open floor, their claw-pads clicking like castanets against the baked mud-surfaces. They hunt by vibration; having no eyes, their broad snouts are covered in short, trembling whiskers that brush the shallow sheet of water, sensing the pulse of the drain-swirl or the thrash of prey.

The prey comes through the ceiling vents: not from the light, but washed down the ceramic conduit pipes during the rains above. Grubs, drowned mice, grease-globs, beetles, and thick ribbons of black earth-worms drop fifty feet through the vertical clay flues, striking the shallow water with soft, fleshy thuds. 

The moment a lump of organic matter hits the tiles, the hall detonates.

The clicking of the crawlers rises from a dozen points across the emptiness. They sprint, their hind legs kicking up fans of grey water that splash against the tiled ceiling. They collide in the center, snarling with a sound like tearing linen, rolling over each other in the two inches of running water. Above them, nesting in the shallow corbels where the wall meets the roof, are colonies of scissor-tailed swifts. The birds never touch the floor; they dive horizontally through the mist, their wings cutting through the heavy air with low, whistling whirs, snatching up the water-spiders and midges that hatch in the drains before snapping back up into the dry brick shadows of the eaves.

The erosion here is peculiar because it is mechanical and repetitive, not natural. The tiles directly beneath the eight drop-shafts have not broken, but they have been worn into smooth, shallow bowls—circular depressions where millions of falling water drops and carcasses have polished away the glaze and exposed the gritty, pale core of the ancient earthenware. The bronze rim of the central drain has been ground down by the bellies of the crawlers sliding across it century after century, until the metal is thin and curved like an old spoon.

Nothing changes here, yet nothing sits still. The water moves continuously from the ceiling joints, sheets across the wide terracotta floor, and vanishes into the floor’s iron heart. The crawlers rest with their sides pressed together against the curved walls, their white flanks heaving in unison, their skin kept moist by the fog that collects in the corners, until the next heavy drop falls from above. When it hits, the room re-arms itself: a single hollow knock, the immediate hiss of claws on tile, the quick flutter of wings in the upper dark, and the deep, hollow, resonant booming of an engine built entirely of baked mud, cold run-off, and blind skin.
