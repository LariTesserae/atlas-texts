The rock here has had every corner taken off it by four hundred million years of wind. What remains at this height is not jagged, but swollen and blunt: immense, rounded knuckles of ironstone and grey porphyry that rise above the cloud-shelf like old teeth worn down to the nerve. At eight thousand paces above sea level, the air is thin enough to make the temples throb, yet it does not bite; a persistent, stagnant warmth hangs trapped in the leeward scoops of the stone, heated by the sheer thermal mass of the mountain and sealed beneath a bronze-tinted haze that never quite clears.

In one of these scoops—a concave south-facing hollow no wider across than a modest village square—sits a settlement that seems less built than excreted into the cracks.

There is no running water. The deep rock has not known a spring since the continent was flat. The inhabitants survive on the grey dew that skins the cold faces of the northern rock before dawn, scraped off with spatulas of beaten horn, and the bitter, gel-thick sap of the *palo-seco*—a dwarf succulent with skin like boiled leather that mats every horizontal ledge. Despite the arid stone, the vegetation is surprisingly dense, choked into every seam: tough, silver-leaved broom with woody roots that can split granite over centuries, cushions of hair-moss so dry they crunch under the heel like dried bread yet remain green at their hearts, and trailing curtains of fibrous, air-feeding bearded nettle that hang ten yards down from the upper rims, swinging not with wind, but with the weight of nesting things.

Because moisture is scarce, life here does not waste itself on rot; things dry, polish, and abide. The architecture is an accretion of dried caliche, baked clay blocks, and unmortared river-cobbles brought up from basins that dried up before human memory began. Dwellings climb three and four tiers up the parabolic curve of the cliff, their roofs serving as the footways and threshing floors of the tier above. The timber used for lintels is petrified drift-log or ancient, twisted juniper harvested from dead groves down the ridge—wood so dense and dark it blunts simple iron chisels and must be scored and split with wet wedges of harder stone. Doors are rare; in their place hang heavy curtains woven from the split fibers of the *palo-seco*, grease-rubbed to keep out the powdery grit that constantly sifts down from the summit.

The air inside the hollow is close, smelling of rancid seed-oil, dry droppings, musk, and the faint, sweetish odor of stone that has baked in the sun for an eternity. The light is weak. The hollow faces away from the sun's direct path, and the sky above is an unvarying, milky amber, bruised at the edges by distant electrical storms that never drop rain on these peaks. It is a world lived in half-tones: umber, ash-grey, tallow-yellow, and the dull, oxidised green of drought-adapted scrub.

Yet for all its dryness, the place is neither still nor quiet. The scoop acts as a parabolic ear, catching and multiplying every rattle. It is relentlessly, rhythmically loud. Tens of thousands of small, spine-tailed swiftlets nest in the honeycomb of holes drill-bored into the sandstone layers by ancient sandstorms. Their chatter is a continuous, dry rattling, like dry peas shaken in a bladder, punctuated by the synchronized *whuff* of their wings as flocks of five hundred drop together into the drop-off to catch the thermals. Between their cries runs the clicking of sun-beetles—hard-cased, finger-sized scavengers that graze the moss mats—and the shrill, mechanical whistle of rock-badgers that live in the lower terrace walls, kept semi-tame by the inhabitants for their thick, woolly coats and salt-rich dung.

Human presence is jammed tight against this animal flurry. In the narrow gaps between houses—lanes so narrow two broad men must turn their shoulders to pass—women with skin darkened to the shade of cured tobacco sit on stone stools, their fingers moving over hand-spindles, working badger hair into cordage. Children with dusty foreheads drag flat sledges of dried hides loaded with dung-cakes, the sole fuel for the bread-ovens. Every square cubit of space is negotiated: a goat-pen constructed beneath the ramp of a stairway; a row of narrow, flat-bottomed seed jars tucked into a natural groove of the basalt; three rows of prickly greens growing out of cracked pottery bowls lashed to the exterior roof-beams. 

The movement here is slow, dictated by the altitude and the scarcity of grease. Men do not run. Even the dogs—thin, hairless, sand-colored creatures bred to catch the lizards that scuttle across the warm masonry—hunt by creeping, freezing for minutes at a time before striking with a single, dry snap of the jaw. Life here does not lurch or develop; it grinds along a level plane. Tools are maintained until the metal is a paper-thin sliver, then cold-hammered into needles. The houses are not torn down; when a wall buckles under the weight of the mountain's slow settling, the inhabitants simply pack the fissure with clay and hair, lay three more courses of stone along its base to act as a buttress, and live inside the newly cramped space.

Below the lip of the scoop lies a straight, sickening fall of two thousand yards down into the white wool of the lower inversion layer. Nothing comes up from down there except the vultures—broad-winged, grey-headed scavengers that circle with their legs hanging down, riding the chimney of warm air that spills eternally out of the village’s throat. They do not flap. They merely tilt their wings a fraction of an inch to match the slow, circular breathing of the mountain, floating as long and as patiently as the dust.
