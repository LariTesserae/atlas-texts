It exists inside the root-meat of something that has not bothered to finish naming itself, eighty yards beneath the loam, in a pocket that shouldn't have an inside. 

The scale of the chamber is no larger than a quiet attic room, perhaps twelve paces across from one fibrous bulk to the other, but the space between the surfaces is almost total void. There is no dust in the air. There are no loose grains of dirt. The floor, ceiling, and curving perimeter are an unbroken envelope of translucent, newly divided plant-flesh: wet, white-green pith that has split open under internal pressure, leaving its interior utterly vacant. It is not an excavation. It is an intercellular tear—a sudden, clean rupture in the tissue of a macro-tuber that has swollen so fast its center could not keep up with its skin. The walls are sharp-edged, crystalline with freshly broken cellulose, smelling aggressively of sheared cucumber, alkaline starch, and hot sap.

The warmth is heavy and interior, like the air inside a freshly baked loaf of bread or the mouth of an ox. It carries a low, persistent humidity—not a mist, but a damp skin of sweat that beads directly onto the green walls and hangs there in perfectly spherical beads, vibrating with the pulse of the surrounding organ.

Above, or in the direction that currently functions as *above*, there is no rock, no sky, no ceiling of roots. There is only a smooth, arched vault of cellular columns so tightly packed they form a single living lens. Through this lens pours a daylight that has no business being here: full, hard, flat noon-light, slightly yellowed by chloroplasts, refracted down through hundreds of feet of translucent taproot from some distant, sun-struck meadow above. It strikes the interior without diffusing. Because the air in the chamber is entirely clear of particulate, the light does not show its beams; it simply lands on the floor in razor-sharp geometrical panes, throwing shadows so hard they look like gouges in the floor.

The sound is immense. It is not an echo, because the fibrous walls are soft enough to deaden any bounce, but a direct, mechanical transmission from the walls themselves: a deep, wet roaring, like a river forced through a bronze pipe. Every few seconds, a sharp *thwack* cracks through the room—the violent snap of water columns breaking under negative pressure inside the xylem, followed by the hiss of cavitation bubbles collapsing in the wall. The floor shudders upward with each pulse. It is like standing in the crankcase of a green, hydraulic engine running at full displacement.

And yet, it is nearly empty.

The only thing occupying the middle of the floor is a tripod of lashed pith-stakes, dried until they have turned grey and stiff as bone. They are bound at the crown with stripped bast fiber using a knotted hitch that is simple, coarse, and plainly human. Hanging from the center of this tripod, six inches off the trembling, glistening floor, is a wedge of river-slate that has been worked by hand—chipped along one margin to create an edge, though it has never been ground smooth. The stone has no business down here; it was carried in. It hangs motionless, while the world around it vibrates so fiercely that the moisture on the walls runs in lateral zig-zags, ignoring gravity.

Here, gravity is untrustworthy. The center of mass is not down in the earth, but distributed unevenly through the colossal mass of the surrounding organism. As you move toward the edges of the room, your weight tilts outward, drawing you toward the walls as if the vegetative tissue were a lead cliff pulling on your marrow. To cross the room is to walk uphill in every direction away from the center, though the floor appears level to the eye.

There is almost nothing alive in here besides the container itself. Once in an hour, a pale thing—a blind silverfish with legs like glass needles, an inch long—darts from a fissure in the cambium, races across the light-pool, and vanishes into another rupture. It does not linger; the air is too loud, the vibration too violent for small lives.

The plant is growing while you watch it. The tear that forms the room is healing, but healing the way an explosion cools: with sudden, jerky contractions. A new structural rib, pale as lard and thick as an arm, pushes out from the eastern flank with a groan like a mooring rope under storm strain. It stretches across the void toward the opposite wall, bridging the gap in minutes, dripping clear, tasteless mucilage as it extends. The light flickers as the overhead tissues shift their orientation toward an invisible sun, the yellow tint deepening to sulfur, then brightening to a bleached, salt-white glare that makes the eyes ache.

The silence of an empty room has been replaced here by the absolute opposite: an empty room that is screaming with the labor of its own expansion. You could sit on the floor, perfectly dry if you avoid the weeping seams, bathed in the noon of a sky you cannot see, watching the bone-handled slate swing an inch to the left, then an inch to the right, as the root shifts its great shoulders against the bedrock of the world.
