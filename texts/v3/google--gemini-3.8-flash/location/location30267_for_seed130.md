There is no soil here, nor has there ever been a stone that was not poured or forged, but time has treated the iron floor exactly as it treats slate. 

Miles of cold, riveted plate-steel stretch out into the dark, worn down to a gentle, sickening flatness. The seams where the great slabs meet are no longer sharp; aeons of greasy, pressurized condensation have rounded their edges into the likeness of slick river-boulders. In places where the traffic of titanic things once rolled, the decking is dished into shallow basins—hundreds of yards across—filled with an inch of black water that smells faintly of kerosene and tallow.

Above, there is no sky, only the underbelly of a higher shelf of the machine, suspended so far up in the gloom that it reveals itself solely through what it drops. A heavy, tepid rain falls continuously, not in clean drops, but in wide, flapping sheets condensed against the cold overhead conduits. It strikes the floor with the sound of lead shot.

And it is loud. The world does not whisper; it shrieks and thrums. The noise is a physical medium, dense as mud. Somewhere beyond the low horizon, past the gentle humps of collapsed generator halls, monumental reciprocating pumps still hammer against the planet’s crust. It is a slow, bone-breaking cadence—one concussive strike every four seconds—that sets the oily water shivering in concentric rings across every pooled square mile. Beneath that percussive impact sits the higher, constant screech of high-pressure vapor venting through corroded brass governors: a wall of white noise so total that the air itself feels hard to breathe, packed tight with friction and pressure.

Yet, between these monuments of mechanical rot, the landscape is empty. You can walk for an hour along a drainage channel—cut fifty feet wide through fused slag and zinc-treated beams—and pass nothing more than the occasional stub of a sheared-off pylon. These uprights, eight feet thick and rusted down to porous, spongy orange pillars, resemble rotten sea stacks jutting from an iron beach.

Life exists only where the water gathers enough copper and dissolved sulfur to sustain something pale and simple. In the seams between deck-plates, where rust has expanded and lifted the steel into jagged lips, there are thick mats of felted, ash-colored bryophytes. They do not need sun; they drink the chemical grease that sweats from the expansion joints and feed on the faint, dull heat radiating from the dead transformers below. Sometimes, trailing out of an overflow weir like wet hair, a dark, rubbery weed grows in long ribbons, undulating frantically in the current of hot water that sluices toward the sump-pits. It has no leaves, only bladders of foul gas that keep it afloat on the black surface.

Nothing walks or flies. Even the scavengers died out when the lubricants hardened into tar. There are only the cycles of the floor itself. 

Every few hours, a shudder runs horizontally through the decking. It is not an earthquake, but a purge: some distant automatic valve, blind and ancient, opening deep in the lower decks. The standing pools suddenly drain with a desperate, sucking roar, pulled down through perforated floor-drains into the guts of the system. For a few minutes, the vast iron plain lies bare, glistening like the hide of a wet whale, venting steam into the chilled air until the rain from above begins the filling again. 

The darkness is nearly complete, save for where the friction of the surviving shafts turns an axle dull cherry-red in the distance, casting a greasy, copper-colored smudge against the mist. It is cold enough to see your breath, though the vapor from your mouth is instantly torn apart and swallowed by the downpour of the machines. Everything is made, everything is dying, and none of it will ever stop running.
