The cradle sits at seven thousand paces above the plains, locked inside a split in the mountain’s crown. The rock here has not yet learned how to crumble. Its edges are still sharp enough to flay leather: clean, violent fracture-faces of dark schist and iron-streaked quartz that were hoisted out of the earth’s gut by some recent tectonic heave and have not had the centuries needed to round their shoulders. There is virtually no soil, only the pulverized stone-dust of the uplift, grey and gritty as emery.

Yet it is soaked, almost amphibious. The cold air—thin, sharp, carrying the faint metallic tang of snowmelt—condenses endlessly against the dark plates of stone. Water weeps from hairline cracks in the sheer cliffs, running down the glassy facets in broad, quivering sheets that break into spray before they hit the ground. It does not pool; it sheets downward into the central throat of the rift, a drop so deep the eye cannot find the floor, only a blue-black gradient of mist.

Because the rock forms a nearly perfect elliptical enclosure—scarcely larger than a great cathedral, perhaps three hundred paces from rim to rim—the acoustics are monstrous. The sound of water is not a trickle; it is a solid, battering roar, an immense white vibration that shakes the marrow of the teeth. High mountain wind shears across the top of the cleft like a bow across a cello, tuning the roar into a deep, mechanical drone that never alters. To speak here requires leaning into an ear and shouting against the roar of the water and the singing granite.

Clinging to the perimeter of this roaring void is an entire town, constructed with the methodical desperation of termites. 

It is layered vertically, a city of scaffolds, dry-stone ledges, and split pine. The builders have no mortar and no iron bolts. They have used what was thrown up by the fracture: heavy slabs of greenstone stacked into lintels and battered retaining walls, pinned into the mountain’s raw crevices with fire-hardened oak pegs. Above these stone shelves rise multi-tiered galleries of pale, peeled larch, lashed together with miles of braided rawhide and oiled grass cord. The timber is clean and pale, smelling of sap, while the ropes are dark and slick with grease to keep them from rotting in the mist. 

Though the architecture possesses the dense, deliberate organization of an ancient city—alleys mapped along stone shelves, communal hearth-pockets carved into dead-end fissures, stepped causeways zigzagging past multi-story communal halls—the space feels strikingly hollow. The vast center of the rift is completely unoccupied. Between the northern tier of platforms and the southern terraces lies an emptiness of two hundred paces of free air, crossed only by single, swaying catenaries of twisted hemp that sag into the gloom. Nothing moves across the center; the volume of the place is mostly void, an abyss of cold light and spray, framed by the intricate human fretwork against the cliffs.

Light here has the scouring intensity of high elevation. When the sun clears the crags, it does not warm; it simply illuminates with surgical violence. The wet surfaces of the schist act as mirrors, throwing blinding, moving glares across the timber underbellies. Every drop of water clinging to the moss sparkles like quartz. 

And the moss is everywhere. Despite the youth of the rock, vegetation has invaded with a strange, primeval speed. Spores carried by the mountain draft have found the constant condensation and built thick, spongy carpets of peat-green and rust-red liverwort across every horizontal surface. Ferns with fronds as stiff as horn emerge directly from the raw cracks, their undersides caked with black spore-dust. Long tendrils of pale, hair-like water-lichen drape from the timber joints, trailing three or four arm-lengths down toward the chasm, heavy with liquid weight. There is no decay here, only soaking life fastened to sterile, newly cleaved minerals.

Animal life cannot easily endure the noise or the verticality. A rare species of pale, leggy salamander creeps along the joints where the lashed pine meets the wet rock, feeding on invisible blooms of slime. Very occasionally, a crow or mountain chough will drop past the lip of the canyon, only to be tossed upward on the brutal draft, beating hard to escape the acoustic trap of the chasm before the stone walls batter its ears.

Movement in the settlement is patient, heavy, and slow. The timber walkways are perpetually wet, rimed with a slick biofilm that requires a flat-footed, deliberate tread. A single load of dry firewood or salt fish takes half a day to winch from the lower platforms to the high storage rifts via wooden drums turned by hand-spokes. The inhabitants do not hurry; they move with the sluggish equilibrium of creatures living inside a bell that is constantly being struck. They communicate with hand-signs—curved palms, tapped wrists, fingers dropped like water—because the voice is useless against the drumming of the cataract below.

Everything is suspended in this state: the stone is brand-new, unweathered, raw as a freshly broken tooth; the wooden city is dense, intricate, and painstakingly maintained by primitive tools; the vegetation is ancient and suffocating in its wet richness; and between them all lies the enormous, cold, roaring emptiness of the canyon, waiting for the mountain to finish what it started.
