It is a cleft hung so high in the spine of the world that the atmosphere has the thin, hard bite of an edge, yet the air is perpetually drenched. 

The space itself is small—a vertical chimney or amphitheater perhaps three hundred paces across—but it opens directly into a void so clean and bottomless that the sense of interior density drops to zero. Between the two sheer, fluted walls of black hornblende, there is nothing. Just thousands of vertical feet of empty, sunlit air, framed by stone that has stood long enough to be worn silky and groove-ribbed by rain and sleet. It is a chamber defined almost entirely by its absence of mass: two opposing cliffs clutching a great column of hollow daylight.

Yet the margins are weeping. A massive, permanent cloud-tier boils up from the lowlands far below, forced up the chimney by high-altitude pressure differentials. As this vapor hits the freezing lip of the upper ridge, it collapses into liquid instantly, not as rain, but as a torrential, horizontal sweating. The rock faces do not simply drip; they run in continuous, quivering sheets that break into spray whenever the gusts catch them. 

Because of this constant wash, the verticality is smothered in a startling, cold lushness. Heavy hanging mats of copper-red liverworts and pale, fleshy ferns cling to every bedding plane. There are broad-leafed saxifrages with translucent veins, their roots driven deep into ancient hairline fractures, swelling until they burst the stone. Long beards of a rubbery, lichenous moss hang three fathoms down into the empty air, dripping steady beads into the abyss, glistening like blown glass in the hard, unshielded daylight.

The sound is immense. It does not pause. The primary tone is the roar of the updraft tearing through the slit—a hollow, organ-pipe bass that rattles the sternum—layered over the sharp, percussive slapping of water whipped against stone. Above that din rises the frantic, high-pitched screeching of thousands of needle-tailed prions. 

These birds nest in the thousands within the undercut flutings of the rock. They are small, slate-grey, and built for violent air, launching themselves into the void by dropping like stones before opening their narrow, scissor-like wings to snap upward on the thermal. The empty air between the cliffs is crisscrossed every second by dozens of them, hunting the pale, wingless insects that crawl through the sodden moss, or tumbling in territorial skirmishes. Larger shapes—square-winged mountain kites—hang motionless in the throat of the chasm, tipped slightly on their axes, watching the flurry below. Now and then one folds its wings and falls like an iron wedge, striking a prion in mid-air with a wet, wooden *clack* that echoes between the walls, before hauling itself back into the updraft on laboring strokes.

The place is not entirely wild, but what has been built here is quiet, manual, and subservient to the precipice. 

Tucked into a horizontal fissure fifty feet above a sheer run of moss is a single, low-walled shelter of dry-stacked slate, chinked with sod and tallow. It has no mortar, no square corners; it follows the natural bend of the ledge like a swallowed tooth. To reach it, someone long ago pecked a sequence of shallow, cup-shaped footholds into the vertical black face using hard riverstones or soft-metal gouges. Beside these steps, anchored to a thick ring of hammered, cold-worked copper wedged into a joint, hangs a heavy rope braided from the split hides of mountain goats. It swings loose in the draft, its lower end frayed and darkened by spray, tapping out a rhythmic *thud-thud* against the rock.

A few yards from the hut, wooden drying racks—lashed together with stripped birch withes and pinned with bone pegs—are lashed flat to the stone. Upon them, split prions are pinned out to cure in the cold wind, their fat rendering slowly in the thin, fierce light, protected from scavengers by thorn-screens tied down with sinew. 

There is an eerie, physical tension to the place. You stand in an ecosystem that is miniature, almost domestic in its physical boundaries—you can see every nest, every dripping cushion of moss from one side to the other—yet it is suspended over an abyss so vast that it makes the gut clench. The light is blindingly ordinary, clean mountain midday, illuminating the violent speed of the birds, the relentless churning of the moisture, and the patient, ancient fatigue of the stone holding it all up.
