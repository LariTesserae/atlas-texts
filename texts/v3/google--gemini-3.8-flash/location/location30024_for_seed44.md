The heat in the canal basin does not move; it sits at the level of the lower walkways, held down by a continuous ceiling of woven ficus limbs and the iron framework of the gantry cranes. It is an enclosed, pocket-sized industrial precinct—scarcely two hundred paces from the intake gates on the tidal slough to the brick face of the compressor house—yet it contains the density of an entire municipal works.

Everything here is either made of cast iron, wet timber, or root mass, and after ninety years in the warm damp, the distinctions between them have softened into a single load-bearing substance.

At the center of the pocket is the pump pit. It is sunk twelve feet below the local water table, lined with dark, glazed engineering brick that stays permanently slick with lime-wash and green diatomaceous slime. Down in the sump, two massive, low-speed horizontal beam engines turn with a rhythmic, wet *thunk-slosh, thunk-slosh*. They do not turn fast; their flywheels, fifteen feet in diameter and counterweighted with lead pigs, slice through their lower troughs with a measured, ponderous churn that sends rhythmic ripples across the surface of the bilge. The sound is heavy and percussive—a low, tooth-vibrating thrum mingled with the shrill, steady hiss of steam weeping past packing glands and the wet splashing of warm condensation falling from overhead rafters.

Above the pit, the vegetation is not merely an overgrowth; it has become the principal structural support. Long ago, the corrugated iron roofing rusted through and was cleared away, leaving only the structural trusses. Tropical ironwoods, water-hickories, and aggressive river figs took seed in the damp soot accumulated on the flanges. Over decades, their roots traveled down the stanchions, casing the six-inch H-beams in living timber that is now four times thicker than the metal it swallows. You can see rivets peering out from beneath bark like old buckshot. In several places, a main steam header—jacketed in moldering magnesium pipe-wrap—is cradled directly in the lap of a swollen root-knee, the living wood scorched dry and polished dark by years of contact with the lagging.

The water is everywhere, yet it rarely falls as clean rain. Instead, the river air pushes into the hollow, hits the boiling breath of the engine discharge, and drops out of suspension immediately. It beads on the broad, leathery leaves of the wild taro that choke the overflow ditches; it runs in continuous, trembling cords down the brass sight-glasses and grease-cups of the machinery; it pools on the stamped-steel diamond plate of the catwalks, deep enough to submerge a boot sole. The smell is a thick, inescapable compound of mineral cylinder oil, decomposing swamp peat, river sulfur, and the sweet, yeasty scent of warm starch from an adjacent, half-seen fermenting house.

Despite the heavy mechanical churn, animal life is scarce. The continuous vibration keeps birds from nesting in the rafters, though an occasional small green tree frog clings to the unheated copper cold-water leads, its skin the exact yellow-gray of oxidized brass. Beyond that, the life belongs entirely to the plant kingdom: shelf fungi thick as iron brackets protruding from spruce-wood sluice boards; hair-fine moss filling every thread of every exposed bolt; and the thick, spongy carpets of duckweed and water-fern that skin the cooling ponds until the water looks like lawn until a pressure valve trips and boils a hole through the green.

Light enters this space sideways. Because the canopy is so thick, the direct sun never strikes the floor of the precinct; instead, the daylight comes through the low archways where the drainage culverts empty into the outer bayou, bouncing off the gray-brown water and illuminating the undersides of the walkways with a pale, steady sheen. It is bright enough to read the pressure dials—most of which are crusted around their bezels with green verdigris—but free of sharp shadow. Everything exists in a heavy, even twilight.

Human intervention here is slow, rhythmic, and minimal. An engineer occasionally steps onto the upper gantry—wooden clogs loud on the iron grids—to turn a four-spoke brass wheel with an iron wrench, relieving pressure in an auxiliary line, or to swab mutton tallow over a crosshead guide. The tools are kept in open wooden boxes where they lie drowned in neat’s-foot oil to keep them from pitting overnight. The work is not urgent; it has the long, patient cadence of a drainage system that cannot be allowed to stop, but which has already outlived the men who surveyed its foundation.

The walls of the brick buildings have long since bowed outward under the lateral thrust of the root systems, but they do not fall; the roots that wedge the bricks apart also hold them in a vice-like, fibrous basket. Down along the sludge drains, where the warm water runs milky with lime, the current is only an inch deep, moving with the sluggish, thick dignity of grease over stone, patient and continuous, while behind the lime-spotted masonry, the great engines push their pistons back and forth through the steam, indifferent to the moss growing into their valve chests.
