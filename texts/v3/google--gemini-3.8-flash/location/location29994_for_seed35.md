The air inside the vault has never known a cloud, a drop of dew, or the passage of a human lung that was not instantly parched by its contact. It is seventy meters across—the footprint of a small municipal cistern or an assembly bay—and roughly thirty meters from its raw floor to its segmented iron rafters. It sits two hundred cubits below the basalt crust, cut into a formation of dense, charcoal-hued pyroxenite whose fracture faces are so clean and sharp they catch the dim, red-shifted light like newly struck flint. Nothing here has been rounded by moisture. There are no stalactites, no mud banks, no smoothing of steps. The breaks are crisp, boxy, and violently immediate, as though the room were hollowed out by hydraulic wedging only hours ago, though its machinery has been turning without hesitation for centuries.

The heat is dry and uniform: thirty-eight degrees Celsius, radiating upward from the rock bed and outward from the unjacketed housings of three colossal walking-beam engines that dominate the floor. 

These engines are not steam-driven. They operate on an anhydrous thermal cycle, circulating a heavy, closed-circuit fluorocarbon that smells faintly of charred bone and warm slate. Their massive iron rocker arms, each sixty tons of untreated pig iron, rise and fall with an unyielding, pneumatic regularity. There is no hiss of exhaust, no condensate spitting from gaskets; only the rhythmic, concussive *thrum-chock* of solid-state valves and the dry screech of needle bearings packed with powdered graphite. The sound is enormous and completely flat. It does not echo so much as it occupies the volume of the space, pressing against the eardrums like warm felt, identical in every corner of the enclosure.

Suspended across the ceiling and spanning the clefts in the sharp rock are iron gantries, rusted only by trace oxygen before the atmosphere settled into its permanent, parched nitrogen-argon balance. Between these beams hangs a sparse, skeletal flora that owes nothing to chlorophyll. It grows in brittle, wire-like tendrils—stiff, copper-colored filaments that sprout directly from the shear lines of the stone and the seams of cold-rolled steel. These are dry moss analogs whose cellular walls are reinforced with silicon dioxide and organo-lead polymers; they do not bend, they snap with the sound of breaking needles if disturbed. They produce no fruit, no nectar, only dry, hexagonal spores that drift like talcum through the convection plumes above the engine manifolds.

Because there is no water to limit them, the fauna exist in a fever of dry metabolism. They are everywhere, carpeted over the machinery, nested within the lattice-work of the girders, and wedge-packed into the razor-edged crevices of the quarry walls. 

They are small, segmented creatures, the size of river crabs or flattened knuckles, clad in dark, iridescent carapaces of sintered apatite and slate-gray chitin. They have no soft tongues or mucous membranes; their breathing apertures are covered with fine wire-gauze filters that shudder with rapid, dry clicks. Millions of them crowd the rocker arms, riding the thirty-meter arcs of the iron beams without losing their grip, using sharp, hardened spurs to graze the graphite grease directly off the journals. When an engine limb descends, thousands of these creatures are swept through the wake of the downward stroke, their hard shells clattering against the metal like a shovel scraping across gravel.

Above them, cling-bats—creatures with wings of thin, dry bladder-leather stretched over bone rods that clatter like bamboo—hang in dense, vibrating rows from the valve pipes. They do not drink. Their internal systems derive what pressure they need from a viscous, sulfur-rich lipid that never turns to vapor at this chamber’s temperature. They feed continuously on the graphite-scavengers, shearing through their shells with scissor-jaws that make a sharp, metallic *snip-snip-snip* that sits just above the bass throb of the walking beams.

Light is scarce, cast almost entirely by the dull cherry-red glow of the engine bases—where internal friction keeps the heavy cast-iron beds at a constant low incandescence—and by long, dust-caked vacuum tubes bolted along the support pillars. These tubes emit a jaundiced, low-frequency hum and an amber flicker that never brightens and never fails. There are no shadows that move of their own accord; every shadow is tethered to the eternal swing of the steel rods and the constant, shivering mass of shells beneath them.

This place has no days. It has no seasons, no weather, and no decline. The bearings do not wear down because the lubricant is dry mineral dust, continuously recirculated and ground finer by the pressure of the steel races until it behaves as a super-fluid. The dead among the fauna are not decomposed by bacteria—for no common rot can survive this total absence of humidity—but are instead systematically dismantled by their kin, their carapaces crushed between flat, toothless grinding plates to replenish their own armored hulls, their dry grease reabsorbed. 

The air tastes of ground slate, hot graphite, and ozone. It never clears. A fine, glittering silt of pulverized shell and quartz dust hangs suspended in the thermal drafts between the floor and the roof, drifting through the amber rays like snow that will never touch the ground, circling down only to be caught in the heat of a crank-pin and lifted again, precisely as it did yesterday, precisely as it will do until the iron itself forgets how to hold its shape.
