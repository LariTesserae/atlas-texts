The noise arrives before the eye can settle on any single boundary: a catastrophic, hydraulic roar, like a thousand dry docks being flooded with warm starch. It is not thunder or wind, but the sound of wood exceeding its own tensile strength. Millions of vascular bundles are pumping under pressure so severe that the stems hiss and whine, a shrill, multi-toned shriek of cambium expanding against bark that splits down the grain with the report of artillery. 

This is a city that has never seen a trowel, a plumb line, or a brick, yet it holds the staggering, dense geometry of an imperial metropolis. It rises from a low, basin-shaped plain where the bedrock has been completely submerged beneath a churning carpet of living root-plates. The architecture is not overgrown; it is *grown*. Giant, squared trunks of an unrecognizable lignified order shoot upward in vertical colonnades, their bark smooth and grey as wet poured concrete, self-grafting into clean lintels, flying buttresses, and tiered arcades sixty spans high. They mimic civic design with the feverish, blind precision of a crystal or a cancer. 

Streets exist here—vast, paved avenues formed by interlocking ironwood tiles that grow flush against one another, their seams oozing a dry, fragrant amber gum that seals them flat. These roads do not rest. They creep. Driven by the furious cell division at their margins, the avenues roll slowly forward, carrying balconies of knotted wisteria and colossal, hollowed-stem tenements along like rafts on an unyielding, green conveyor. Where two such thoroughfares collide, there are no collisions of stone, but a grinding, squealing heave: walls of living ash and boxwood buckle into the sky, tearing open rooms whose ceilings are woven from interlocking wicker reeds, spilling empty chambers of fibrous air into the glaring light.

The light is bright and hard, pouring down through a sky that seems scrubbed clean of clouds, though the air itself is clotted with a warm, suspended vapor. The sun strikes the upper canopies—great, flat sheets of broadleaf that act as roofs, awnings, and terraces—turning them into translucent panes of chartreuse and gold. Beneath these green glass ceilings, the light is saturated, heavy, and hot. It is not wet like a bog; there are no ponds, no sluggish creeks. Instead, water exists only as an internal fuel, drawn up from vast subterranean depths and blasted through the capillaries of the place. The only dampness is the slick, constant condensation that bleeds through leaf-pores: a fine, oily sweat that coats the ramparts, keeps the wood from combusting under its own friction, and leaves puddles the size of coins in the corners of root-stairs.

Nothing has had time to rot. The edges here are young, cruel, and sharp. When an eight-story fluted reed-tower splits open under the strain of its own internal sap-head, the fracture leaves spear-points of yellow wood as keen as freshly flaked flint. There is no topsoil, no loam, no layer of fallen mulch; any leaf that drops is seized by the aggressive, carpeting runners of the floor-vines and dissolved within hours, folded back into the vascular churn.

There are no birds in the galleries. There are no rodents in the hollow floorboards, no beetles in the bark. The silence of animal life makes the mechanical violence of the flora even more deafening. The place performs a pantomime of habitation without inhabitants. In the interiors of the great banyan-citadels, chambers open into chambers: living partitions of tightly braided osier form corridors, vestibules, and deep, spiral wells that resemble staircases, though they twist at angles that defy gravity, folding back through ceilings that are simultaneously floors. 

Because the reality of the growth has detached from seasonal logic, space folds under the weight of its own speed. A courtyard forty yards wide might, over the space of an afternoon, choke itself into a solid pillar of interlocked heartwood, its central fountain—a thick, spouting taproot that gushes bitter, clear water—strangled by its own rim until the fountain becomes an obelisk, and the obelisk splits into four soaring arches that reach outward to weld themselves to the neighboring tenements. 

The heat inside the denser quarters is suffocating—a fermenting, biological bake that smells of raw sugar, bruised mint, and the sharp, alkaline tang of green sap. Looking out from the upper tiers of an arcade that was likely formed only three dawns ago, the vista is terrifying in its scale: miles of emerald towers, green-roofed plazas, and terraces of shuddering, vibrating foliage stretching toward a flat, pale horizon. The whole basin twitches and hums with a sickening, mechanical vitality. It is a metropolis designed by a nervous system that does not possess a brain, building its walls higher and higher simply because the pressure within the soil will not let it stop.
