The descent ends not in darkness, but in a flare of white-chalk brilliance so total that for several minutes the eyes register only geometry without color: a circular lip above, sheer and cleanly sheared, and below it, an amphitheater of bleached calc-rock hollowed out like a dry lung.

It is perhaps ninety paces across and forty deep—a collapsed dome or ancient solution pocket whose ceiling surrendered so long ago that the debris pile at its heart has been weathered into a smooth, low mound, nearly flush with the basin floor. There is almost nothing inside it. The volume of warm, still air dominates everything; walking across the flag-like floor of silted gypsum feels like crossing an unroofed temple between the clearing of the morning mist and the arrival of an afternoon that never quite breaks into wind.

Yet it is damp. The moisture does not pool or flow; it has no bed to follow. Instead, it lives as a microscopic film over the vertical fluting of the perimeter walls. The limestone there has been dissolved by millennia of dew-fall into long, organ-pipe grooves that hold moisture in their concave shadows. Every few minutes, a drop gathers sufficient mass near the rim, slips from a lip of weathered stone, and falls thirty cubits straight down, catching the high noon light like a flung needle before striking the bare stone with a solitary, resonant *tink*. 

The temperature is that of an unbaked loaf: steady, blood-warm, sheltered from the gusts that scour the flat scrub-country above. The light bouncing off the pale walls strips away shadows, illuminating every crack and grain from three directions at once, so that objects in the hollow appear suspended, weightless, and slightly hyper-real.

Despite the near-total vacancy of the central floor, life is dense along the vertical perimeter, adapted to the verticality and the blinding clarity. Small, flat-bodied skinks—nearly colorless, their ribs visible through paper-thin scales—lie pressed tight against the warmest limestone ribbons, basking without closing their eyes. They feed on colonies of silk-spinning midges that hang in geometric webs strung across the narrower erosion flutes. These webs are not spiral traps but single, taut chords of grease-slicked thread that vibrate when touched by the rising warm air. High up, where the light is fiercest, swallow-like birds with bifurcated tails nest in tiny natural pockets, their plumage dusty and sun-faded to the color of dry oats. Their chirps are infrequent and sharp, instantly swallowed by the immense, quiet dome of empty air.

The ground underfoot is clean, scoured by evaporation rather than current. In the few places where condensation drips predictably day after day, small, saucer-sized depressions have been worn into the stone. In one corner, leaning against the pale wall where the shadow stays longest, are signs of human hands—deliberate, simple, and ancient. 

A vertical series of hand-pecked notches runs up a six-foot step in the rock, their rims rounded by the pressure of calloused palms and bare insteps. Beside them, resting in a dry niche out of the drip-line, sits a cluster of heavy river cobbles—foreign to this chalk basin—with their ends bruised from knapping, and several broad, fan-shaped scrapers of chert, white with patina. There are no ashes, no bones, no pottery. Only the tools, left lying on a flat stone ledge as if set aside between hours of work, and three dried gourd-rinds, brittle as eggshells, each cut across the shoulder to form a water-scoop and banded with braided hair to keep the dry edges from splitting.

Toward the center, the silence is so heavy that the scurrying of a skink forty yards away across a sheet of chalk sounds like dry paper dragged over sand. Sparse, leathery tufts of salt-grass grow only where the perimeter walls meet the floor, their roots wedged into hairline fractures, drinking the run-off from the rock sweat. They don't wave; there is no draft to move them, only the slow, upward crawl of shimmering heat that makes the blue circle of the sky above seem to pulse faintly, like an opening through which something patient and indifferent looks down.
