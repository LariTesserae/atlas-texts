The water here is not an intrusion or an aftermath; it is the atmosphere, heavy and body-warm, carrying the mineral taste of fresh sulfur and crushed basalt. We are miles above the continental plains, perched on the crown of a colossal upthrust ridge that nevertheless fails to break the surface of an endless, tea-colored ocean. To look upward is to see only a bronze dimness that thickens into olive, where light arrives not as beams, but as a slow, bruised diffusion that has lost all memory of the sky.

Because the ridge is geologically newborn—heaved upward by a mantle-pulse that only just finished cooling—there are no sandbars, no rounded boulders, no gravel beds. The floor is an unbroken expanse of black, vitreous obsidian and bulging pillow-lava. Its edges are still razor-sharp, fracturing in clean, shell-shaped flakes that have not yet had their teeth worn down by sand or surf. Every crevice is distinct, a pristine black wound in the rock, venting faint, rippling mirages of thermal brine that wobble into the cold ambient water like poured syrup. 

Yet despite the raw, untamed youth of the stone, the ridge is choked with life. It is not delicate coral life, but a dense, fleshy vegetation adapted to warmth and mineral broth. Vast curtains of amber kelp, their fronds as thick and leathery as wet hide, anchor directly into the volcanic crust with fibrous, claw-like holdfasts. They rise seventy feet toward the dim ceiling of the water, tethered upward by internal gas bladders that creak softly against one another in the faint, idling swell. Beneath them, covering the pitch-black stone like a greasy pelt, are carpets of maroon felt-algae and pale, tuberous sea-succulents whose pale lobes expand and contract to breathe in the hydrothermal heat.

Within this drowned mountain forest sits the settlement. 

It is arranged along a stepped series of tectonic shelves—a town constructed entirely in the middle distance between seabed and void. Without metal or kilns, the architecture relies on tension, weight, and the natural physics of buoyancy. Thousands of hollowed-out pumice blocks, porous and desperate to rise, have been lashed together into domes and wide, inverted terraces; they strain upward against braided cordage made from beaten kelp fibers. These cords are anchored down into the sharp volcanic floor by heavy ballast baskets of greenstone, wedged tight into the fresh fissures of the basalt.

The result is an upside-down city of suspension: hanging avenues of woven reed-mesh that serve as walkways, swaying gently underfoot, strung from anchor-crag to anchor-crag across sixty-foot abysses. Lower chambers are little more than sunken stone cribs, piled high with uncemented slabs of vitreous lava, roofed over with thick layers of woven sea-hide to keep out the slow, siltless drift. The settlement stretches wide across the saddle of the ridge—layered, crowded, home to hundreds of tethered cells that nudge each other like anchored coracles. Thin, pale grease-lanterns—fed by rendered plant oils—glow faintly inside some of the lower lattice-works, casting dull, chartreuse rings onto the surrounding black stone.

There is very little motion. The water moves not with the rush of tides, but with the massive, patient breathing of a deep convective cell: a long, slow glide that pushes the kelp fifty yards to the northeast over the course of three hours, then lets it rest, then draws it back. 

Fauna is nearly absent, or else so slow as to seem part of the geology. Occasionally, out in the dim olive expanse beyond the ridge's edge, a broad, pale disc—some cartilaginous drifter the size of a threshing floor—will slide past, undulating with a languid, rib-less ripple before vanishing into the murky green. Smaller things are rare: small, blind glass-shrimps that pick methodically along the raw basalt edges, their shells clicking against the obsidian with a sound like dropped needles.

Sound carries through the bath-warm brine with strange, heavy fidelity, but it has little to say. The loudest noise is the settlement itself: the ceaseless, low-register groaning of thousands of stressed hemp-and-kelp tethers pulling against the rocks, an orchestral creak of damp hemp and wood flexing under hydrostatic load. Underneath that, deep within the floor that has only just finished arriving, there is the hollow, intermittent *tink-tink-thung* of cooling glass as the basalt continues to settle, fractures propagating micro-inches through the mountain’s brand-new bones. 

The heat of the floor rises against the body like steam in a closed room, trapping the settlement in a perpetual, stagnant dusk—a city built of wet rope and fresh glass, moored in the dark at the top of the world.
