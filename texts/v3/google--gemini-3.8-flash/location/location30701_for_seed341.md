The air inside the Sump-Basin does not circulate; it cycles. It climbs against the black iron flanks of the central transformer towers, cools slightly against the high, soot-varnished concrete vaults three hundred feet above, and falls back down as a heavy, scalding drizzle. The rain tastes of sulfur and tallow. It falls onto broad, flat acres of submerged basalt paving where water sits perpetually four inches deep, skinning over with a film of rainbow-colored mineral grease that parts and seals itself again like grease on soup.

It is an urban space, undeniably municipal, but built on the scale of a cathedral’s sunken crypt, walled in by five-story retaining bulwarks of rust-scaled iron plates. Once, this was a power-switching yard and water-distribution terminal for a larger district overhead, but over decades the lower floor has sunk into its own silt. There is wide space here—broad, empty expanses of dead pavement stretching between the monumental iron works. The installations do not crowd one another; they sit isolated in the shallow water, twenty or thirty yards apart, like black iron monuments stranded on an indoor mudflat: squat boiler houses with their roofs caved in, banks of massive ceramic insulators as tall as pine trees, and four-legged steel gantry pylons whose lower braces have dissolved into red lace under the constant wash of hot drainage.

The temperature holds steady at blood-heat, pushed higher by the rhythmic venting of steam from deep subterranean conduits. Somewhere below the flags, massive reciprocating engines are still stroking. Every nineteen seconds, a concussion travels through the water and the stone underfoot—a heavy, hollow *thung* that ripples the surface and jars the marrow of the shinbones. Following it comes the scream: high-pressure steam purging from an array of vertical pipes mounted along the southern retaining wall. The sound is deafening, white noise that obliterates thought for six seconds out of every twenty, leaving behind only the heavy, wet clack of mechanical check-valves and the steady roar of waterfalls where the upper city’s gutters breach the ceiling and pour straight down into the floor.

Yet, despite the scalding mist and the machine-rumble, the place is overwhelmingly green. 

It is not an accidental green of weeds in pavement, but a thick, deliberate, runaway botanical colonization that has adapted entirely to the heat and the oil. Broad-leafed aroids, their stalks as thick as a man’s thigh and mottled like boiled liver, grow directly out of the expansion joints in the flagstones. They do not need soil; their roots are red-tipped and naked, spreading across the wet stone like networks of veins, drinking the runoff from the turbine drains. Enormous ferns, pale chartreuse from the lack of true sunlight, hang from the steel gantries in clumps the size of haystacks, their roots wrapped tightly around high-voltage cables as if seeking the warmth of the current.

There are no birds in the gantries. There are no rats in the conduit breaches, no roaches in the silt, not even the drone of gnats over the warm puddles. The water is too hot, too laden with mineral salts and heavy lubricants to support them, and the concussion of the steam valves would burst an insect’s air-sacs. The only movement belongs to the water, the pistons, and the plants.

The plants move with an unsettling, mechanical speed. The scald-reeds, which grow in pale, bamboo-like thickets around the cooling ponds, have joints that swell with steam pressure; when the hot purge passes, they creak audibly, their pale tips uncurling inches in an afternoon to catch the vapor. Moss here does not creep; it swells, puffing up into bright, spongy cushions along the steam lines until its own weight pulls it loose, splashing heavily into the soup below where it dissolves into a fibrous scum.

Light exists only as a low, rust-colored twilight. Far above, where the ceiling arches meet, narrow slits open to the overcast sky of the city above, but the daylight that filters down is smothered by the rising exhaust and stained yellow by iron deposits on the glass. What little functional illumination remains comes from submerged inspection lamps: heavy bronze bells bolted into the flooded streets, their thick lenses glowing with a dull, steady amber from under four inches of moving water. They light the roots of the aroids from below, casting ribs of yellow shadow up into the steam.

Everything is wet; nothing is clean. The moisture is not clear rain, but an emulsion that leaves a velvet silt on whatever it touches. The iron is eroding, but slowly, having found a kind of calcified balance with the acid water; thick encrustations of green copper-scale and gray lime armor the pipes against further decay. 

At the center of the yard, where four flooded tram-lanes converge at a circular turntable, a single steam manifold stands eighty feet high, shaped like a cluster of fluted church organs. It is entirely covered in a pelt of pale, weeping liverwort that drinks from its sweating joints. As the pressure mounts below, the liverwort shivers, its fronds vibrating against the iron skin. When the relief valve opens, the blast of white heat passes straight through the leaves; they do not burn, but turn translucent for an instant, drinking the vapor, shaking violently in the roar, before sagging back into the heavy, dripping dimness to wait for the next stroke.
