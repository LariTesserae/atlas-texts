The rift sits miles above sea level, where the atmosphere ought to be thin and freezing, but instead the air is thick, warm, and sweet with the smell of scorched tallow and hot copper. 

It is a young geology, violently raw. The peaks have not had time to blunt themselves against the weather. They rise as catastrophic black needles and sheared-off block-faults, their facets mirror-bright and razor-edged, cleaved from some deep mantle layer only yesterday in geologic time. Yet the stone itself does not behave strictly like basalt or granite. Strata of pitted cast-iron run directly through the cliffs like sedimentary bands, interfolded with cold-rolled sheets of raw zinc and veins of coarse, vulcanized amber. There are no mortar joints, no rivets, no signs of assembly. The metallurgy is strictly tectonic: an industrial geology that erupted whole, unbuilt and unguided, cooling into spontaneous geometries of cams, flywheel crags, and colossal blind manifolds that plunge thousands of feet into the gorge.

Because the mountains are so high and so wide—spanning a rift-system vast enough to generate its own trapped, cyclical climate—the low sun rarely reaches the floors of the troughs. The light is a perpetual copper gloom, twilight filtered through miles of greasy, high-altitude inversion haze. 

Moisture here is stealthy. It does not fall as rain, but condenses in sudden, warm beads along the chilled iron overhangs, running down the razor-crests in blackish, mineral-rich rivulets that taste of galvanized tin and sap. Where the water pools in narrow, jagged depressions, mosses of incredible density take root. They are lush, swollen liverworts and thickets of ferns that do not branch so much as loop and coil like insulated wire. Their fronds are dark bottle-green, rubbery and hot to the touch, exuding a slow, rhythmic heat as they compost their own rapidly dying underlayers. 

Despite the sheer kinetic turmoil of the place, it is unnervingly quiet. 

The turbulence is immense, but it moves without the deafening screech one would expect of grinding metal and collapsing cliffs. Whole mountain faces shift in absolute silence. A cliff-sized cluster of interlocking gears, carved out of porous basalt, will turn three agonizingly slow degrees, crushing hundreds of yards of fresh forest into a warm, fragrant paste, and produce only a soft, wet *chuff*, like an exhalation through flannel. Slopes shear away and re-fuse in the span of an afternoon, not sliding in scree, but rearranging their internal logic: a vertical drop-off suddenly becomes a stepped tier of reciprocating terraces, each pumping slowly up and down on sleeves of pressurized steam, utterly devoid of purpose, silent save for the hiss of warm condensation on cooling iron.

Reality here suffers from an exhaustion of categories. The boundary between mechanism, mineral, and organism has liquefied. 

In the hollows beneath the lunging rock-pistons, life is frantic and thick. Small, blind things, warm-blooded and shelled in paper-thin mica, swarm across the moss in their millions. They have the segmented legs of crustaceans and the soft, palpitating throats of doves. They feed on the greasy condensation and on each other, laying eggs that look like brass bearings directly into the fissures of the living rock. Above them, drifting through the twilight between the spires, are vast, slow-swimming mantas of fibrous, vascular leather. They are buoyed by internal bladders of warm methane, trailing miles of nervous tissue that occasionally brush against the iron crags, setting off long, silent arcs of pale blue static that ripple down the mountain faces.

Nothing here is old. If you break open a boulder, the interior is still organizing itself—cellular lattices crystallizing into iron struts, soft meat hardening into slate, cold gears of pyrite dissolving back into yellow slime before they can finish their first revolution. 

It is a machine that is also a womb, or a mountain range trying frantically to invent physics by trial and error. The air grows warmer as you climb higher, contrary to all atmospheric sense, heated by the friction of a million unguided parts sliding past one another in the dark. Vast expanses of pristine, wet fern-forest are carried upward on rising slabs of shale, hoisted toward the suffocating clouds, while just beneath them, subterranean exhaust vents exhale long, rhythmic plumes of odorless white steam that curtain the jagged horizon, muffling the world into an immense, twitching hush.
