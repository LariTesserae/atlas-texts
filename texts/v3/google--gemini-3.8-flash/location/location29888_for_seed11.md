The sky is not empty, but the space it occupies is. 

At this altitude, where the atmosphere should be thin and freezing, the air is instead thick, humid, and furiously warm—driven upward from some vast, invisible thermal floor miles below. What remains of the solid world is reduced to isolated, colossal monoliths: spires of ancient, honeycombed granite so severely eroded they no longer have peaks, only rounded, polished domes and fluted vertical fins. They stand miles apart from one another, like the rotted pilings of a wharf built for a vanished continent. Between them lies pure drop: an ocean of empty blue and racing vapor, dropping five, six miles down into a milky sea of lower cloud where the roots of the world are hidden.

The sound is the first thing that unmakes a mind here. It is deafening, absolute, and physical. 

It is not an ambient gust, but a continuous, tearing roar produced by atmospheric slipstreams moving at the speed of falling water. The air shears horizontally between the monoliths, colliding with their ancient flanks to split into thundering vortexes. Millennia of high-pressure winds have drilled hollows and tunnels entirely through the rock, turning each spire into an unearthly pipe organ. The stone screams. It drones in sub-audible rumbles that vibrate through the marrow of the shinbones, and it whistles in shrieking overtones that shred conversation into instant silence. To communicate here, one does not speak; one gestures, or leans a forehead directly against another’s skull to pass vibration through bone.

Yet despite the violence of the air, the rock is green. Incredibly, impossibly lush.

The wind carries an endless supply of high-velocity condensation—mist particles atomized by the speed of the current. Because the air is warm, life does not freeze; it grips. Every surface in the lee of the wind, and every sheltered groove, is carpeted in a deep, rubbery pelt of bryophytes, bulbous air-orchids, and heavy, braided lianas that grow flush against the stone like living rebar. These plants do not have delicate leaves; their foliage is leathery, oil-slicked, and teardrop-shaped to minimize drag, weeping constant runoff from their tips. They drink the roaring damp. In the interior fractures of the spires—canyons no wider than a man’s reach that cut hundreds of feet into the stone—the growth is so thick it bridges the gaps, forming bouncy, suspended floors of roots and damp humus suspended over sheer infinity.

Animal life is sparse, almost nonexistent, unable to contest the fury of the air. Occasionally, in the swirling dead zones behind the largest buttresses, one catches sight of an aeroplankton swarm: pale, translucent disk-creatures the size of saucers, drifting in gyres until an errant gust catches them, snapping them into the slipstream at projectile speed. 

Human trace is minuscule, lost in the sheer cubic mileage of air. 

Here and there, along the rim of a rounded cliff, are the works of hand-tools. Holes chiseled into the weathered stone with stone-tipped drills, plugged with charred wooden pegs that swell tight with the moisture. From these pegs hang ancient, braided cordages made from stripped liana bark, weighted down by river stones that were hauled up from some lost bottom ages ago. These lines support tiny, basket-like shelters—blind-slung cradles of woven wattle and cured hides that tremble constantly in the buffeting draft. They are primitive anchorages, shelters built by people who must navigate this terrain with their bellies pressed flat to the moss, tethered three times over to ringbolts driven into deep fissures. 

There is an eerie permanence to the landscape's chaos. The storm never lands, and it never clears. The sun climbs through pale, high-altitude cirrus, casting clear, hard, natural light across the scene, illuminating the staggering geometry: a green-streaked red pillar, half a mile broad, plunging straight into nothingness, surrounded by four miles of vibrating, howling void, then another pillar, then another, dwindling toward a horizon that bends slightly with the curvature of the planet.

To look across it is to feel the sensation of being cast out of the world while still touching its bones. The wind hammers the chest, warm and smelling of river-silt and bruised, peppery leaves, trying with frantic, mechanical persistence to peel the moss from the rock, and the watcher from the moss.
