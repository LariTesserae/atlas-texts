The water is not sea, though it tastes of copper and dissolved iron; it is the sealed, dark contents of an intake cavern built into the bottom-most tier of an old hydroelectric complex or municipal pump-battery that has spent two centuries drowning under its own supply. 

The floor is entirely sheet steel, half-inch plates once riveted to criss-crossed I-beams, now bowed and softened by uninterrupted immersion. There is no bedrock here, no silt gathered from riverbeds, only the steady fallout of iron oxide: a reddish-gray flour that covers the tread of catwalks, coats the heavy flanges of isolation valves, and lies four inches deep in the dead corners where the current cannot reach.

It is loud. Water holds sound close and moves it fast. From somewhere three bulkheads back, through pipes four yards wide, a massive low-frequency surge repeats every nine seconds—a dry, bronchial shudder that rattles through the teeth before it hits the ears. It is not an engine dying, but an engine that cannot be turned off, some slow centrifugal impeller still sloshing a dead head of cold water back and forth against a shut gate. Around this central pulse are higher, sharper noises: the constant, needle-like hiss of high-pressure leakage spraying from a failed packing gland seventy feet up near the ceiling, and the dull, hollow *clink-tonk* of heavy steel chains swinging on their pins as the backwash catches them.

The scale is modest—perhaps ninety yards from the intake screens to the southern blast door, sixty yards across—yet the darkness makes it feel cavernous until an obstacle drifts out of the green gloom. Visibility does not exceed five paces. The only illumination is a pale, milky seepage from an emergency deck-run high above, where a string of sealed accumulator lanterns still glimmers with an amber, sour phosphorescence, casting long, diagonal ribbons through the murk. 

The water is cool—about nine degrees—and singularly clear where it moves quickly, though heavy with suspended flakes of rust that resemble burnt paper. Where it stagnates in the machine pits, it turns greasy and thick. 

Life has taken this box without altering its geometry. There are almost no plants; the light is too thin for bladder-kelp or river ribbons, leaving only a dark, greasy fur of manganese-eating bacteria that slicks every handrail and ladder-rung. But animals are everywhere, moving with the careless familiarity of things born in a cellar. Small, blunt-headed sculpins sit by the dozens along the horizontal edges of the crane-rails, their pectoral fins splayed like small leather fans against the corroded rivets. In the mid-water, between the vertical columns that support the crane gantry, schools of pale, short-bodied bream circle in loose, mechanical wheels, flashing silver-dull bellies whenever they tilt to graze the bacterial scum from the plate joints. They have no natural predators here except for large, thick-bodied burbot that lie wedged into the drainage gutters beneath the valve actuators, their pale chins resting on the lip of the concrete curbs, waiting for the current to wash stunned fry into their jaws.

Everything made of metal has lost its edge. The erosion here is not the work of sand or surf, but the slower, total chemical surrender of iron to stagnant freshwater. The hex-heads of bolts have rounded into swollen iron acorns; the sharp corners of machine beds have exfoliated into brittle pastry-crusts of rust that break away in tea-saucer slabs if brushed by a diver’s knee. A lathe, bolted to a work platform above the sumps, has swollen into a single, seamless mound of reddish scab, its chuck-jaws cemented shut around a bit that dissolved sixty years ago.

Overhead, the roof is a low barrel vault of reinforced concrete, mapped with fine, dripping calcifications. Slender white soda-straw stalactites, no thicker than wheat stalks, hang three feet down through the water, their tips wavering slightly in the draft caused by the subterranean intake. 

Nothing changes here, yet nothing is still. The cycle is purely hydraulic: the steady intake draw, the hollow clatter of an iron check-valve settling into its seat, the schooling fish drifting up toward the lantern glow to pick at the crust of a girder, and the silt settling endlessly onto iron that has forgotten what grease smells like.
