The water here sits at the crest of the world, a deep, cold, boundless plateau-sea held up against the sky by nothing but the towering bulk of itself. It is a high-altitude ocean, thousands of fathoms deep, yet lifted miles above whatever bedrock or lowlands might exist below. The medium is not cloudy or turbid; it is a cold, pale aquamarine, exceptionally clear, illuminated by a full, steady daylight that pierces down through hundreds of meters without wavering. 

Despite the vastness of the water, the true substrate is vegetable. The basin, the floor, the verticality of this realm are formed entirely of a single, ancient, living mass—a fibrous, petrified-green cellulose that has thickened over geological epochs into shelves, trenches, and colossal, fluted pillars. There is no stone here; what looks like basalt is simply heartwood pressurized under millions of tons of cold brine, dark and glossy as vulcanized rubber. 

Yet it does not behave like ordinary forest or reef. It operates as heavy industry. 

Because nothing has been constructed here—there are no ruins, no bolts, no hulls, no weld-lines—the mechanization belongs entirely to the botanical anatomy. The flora has evolved the architecture of scaled, brute-force engineering. Rising out of the blue emptiness every five or six miles are gargantuan, vertical engine-trunks, four hundred yards thick. Within their translucent, ribbed outer layers, giant vascular pistons—solid cylinders of dense lignified tissue—slide up and down within smooth, mucilage-lined bores, moving with the measured, immutable cadence of marine diesels. 

They draw the cold, dense brine through massive intake louvers woven from rigid reed-bone, forcing it through internal electrolytic cambiums. The water discharges through deep venturis at the base of the pillars in continuous, perfectly laminar jets. The scale of it produces a pervasive, immense sound: a synchronized, planetary thrum. It is not an ambient noise but a structural weight—the low, bass rumble of thousands of subterranean strokes occurring at an unvarying frequency, accompanied by the wet, deafening hiss of high-pressure displacement. The vibration is so uniform it does not shake the water so much as define its tension; a diver’s ribcage would feel the pulse not as a shock, but as a permanent condition of gravity.

The sparseness is essential to the place. Between the great engine-pillars lie immense gulfs of vacant water. One can look through miles of cool, lime-tinted clarity and see only two or three of the dark, working columns rising straight toward the upper light, perfectly vertical, unswayed by current because the currents are entirely manufactured by their own strokes. In these clear intervals, the empty water feels vaulted, almost architectural in its discipline.

Power moves through this sea not by wire, but through continuous, taut cables of copper-rich vascular vine, thick as tree trunks, suspended in mid-water. They span the miles between the engine-spires, hanging in mathematically perfect catenaries. They crackle faintly under the water with a pale, silent bioluminescence, carrying hundreds of thousands of organic amperes between the pumping towers. Small, harmless discharges of natural galvanic current leak into the cold sea, creating tiny, steady streams of electrolysis bubbles—hydrogen and oxygen rising in dead-straight, beaded lines like plumb bobs made of air.

The fauna here are secondary inhabitants of an engine room. They are abundant, but they do not wander; they have settled into the steady tolerances of the machinery. Schools of long, pale, blade-shaped organisms with segmented, chitinous carapaces hang motionless in the laminar discharge jets, their mouths pinned open against the rush to filter out the expelled sap-lipids. Other creatures, blind and armored like brass boiler fittings, crawl over the intake grills, scraping away the fibrous slag that sloughs off the working pistons. They do not hunt or mate in frenzies; their metabolisms are locked to the constant voltage of the vines and the unwavering stroke of the wood-iron pumps.

Above, the surface of the sea is flat. It is not an ocean of waves. The high-altitude air meets the water without wind, and because the internal circulation of the world is entirely closed-loop and self-regulating, the surface is as flat and rigid as plate glass, pierced only by the domed heads of the engine-pillars, which vent cool, pure oxygen into the upper atmosphere in steady, vertical plumes of white mist. The daylight remains clean and noon-still, shining down through the surface with a neutral, unshifting clarity that illuminates the highest tiers of the machinery and fades evenly into the deep, oceanic floor.

There is no succession here. There is no season, no rot, no growth that outpaces the perpetual wear of the mechanical fibers. Where a piston sleeve erodes, the cambium secretes an exact replacement layer of slick, mineralized wood-resin, curing in seconds against the cold brine. The balance is total, established so long ago that the concept of an origin has been ground away. The system does not advance toward a goal, nor does it run down. It merely strokes, pumps, and hums at pitch—an untended, living factory running at peak capacity, entirely indifferent to the sky above or the depths below.
