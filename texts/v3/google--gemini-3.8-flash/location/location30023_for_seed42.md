At this altitude, the water does not have the murk of the lowlands. It is cold, heavy, and startlingly clear—a pale, turquoise-tinted bath filling a high alpine cirque whose granite rims breach the water far above like broken teeth. This is a submerged town, settled inside a hanging tarn two thousand meters up, completely drowned under sixty fathoms of slow, snow-fed water.

The architecture does not sit upon stone, for the floor of the cirque is buried under a deep, living carpet. Colossal water-mosses and thick, pale-stemmed charophytes have grown here for so many centuries that they have formed an elastic peat-reef tens of meters thick. This plant matter is the geology here: spongy, fibrous, yielding yet indestructible in the freezing oxygen-poor depths. 

Anchored into this green mat is a tight, intricate urban fabric, built entirely by hand.

It is small in territory—no wider than a few city blocks, an amphitheater-scale community—yet densely organized. Layer upon layer of dwellings, storehouses, and walkways rise into the water column. The construction relies on heavy mountain timbers—ancient larch and fir felled on dry peaks above and towed down through submerged passes with stone counterweights. These beams do not float away; they are lashed with boiled rawhide, sealed with pitch, and pinned down by perforated ballast stones carved from dark slate. The streets are not paved roads, but vertical and horizontal corridors bounded by woven wicker screens and latticework of water-hardened pine.

Because it is underwater, the city uses three dimensions with the logic of a crowded cliffside village. Homes are inverted cradles or rounded, bell-shaped wicker frames lined inside with multiple layers of bladder-wrack and tallow-impregnated felt, holding small captive envelopes of breathable air for their inhabitants, or simply serving as flooded quarters for people who live half-gilled or tethered to slow air-reeds. Walkways are broad, slack rope-bridges made of braided sedge, suspended between tall structural pilings that are themselves living trunks of water-ironwood, pruned over generations to grow in rigid, load-bearing grids. 

Despite the urban density of the buildings, the settlement is not crowded; it breathes. Broad, empty shafts of pale water cut between the structural clusters. Sunken plazas are simply wide, still voids of pure blue, devoid of timber, where the green-black bed of the tarn lies undisturbed below. Through these sparse intervals, the high mountain sun drives down in straight, hard pillars of silver light, illuminating the silt-free space with a crisp, cathedral clarity. 

The sound here is immediate, vast, and deafeningly loud.

Water carries noise with brutal efficiency. The basin acts as a sounding box for the entire mountain. From far above, the ceaseless thrashing of alpine gales against the surface descends not as wind, but as a continuous, low-frequency roar that vibrates in the chest like a church organ. Against this drone, the city makes its own clatter: the groan of green larch joints flexing against current; the dull *tock-tock-tock* of wooden mallets driving wedges into new fish-traps; the wet, metallic slap of signal stones struck together by divers communicating between tiers. Every snap of a dried stem, every shifted cobble echoes five times across the granite walls of the drowned cirque.

Fauna fills every pocket of the infrastructure. The timbers are encrusted with cold-water mollusks the size of thumbs, their shells black and ridged like slate. Great schools of high-lake char—pale-bellied, vermilion-spotted fish with milky, wide-set eyes—drift through the open walkways like flocks of sparrows in a courtyard, parting slowly around the braided ropes and settling into the eaves of the dwellings. Long, eel-like loaches slip through the wicker partitions, grazing on the white film of bacteria that coats the woodwork. In the darker clearances beneath the town's lower floor, massive blind crabs pick through the anchor-lines, their carapaces covered in hair-fine moss.

There is almost no swift movement here. The current within the bowl is nearly imperceptible—a glacial displacement so patient that suspended needles of larch take an hour to sink ten paces. The cold arrests decay. A timber cut three hundred years ago still smells of resin when a diver cuts into it with a flint chisel; the dead weed does not rot, it slowly hardens, compressed under the weight of the water into a dark, fibrous lignite that the builders carve like soft soapstone.

Living here is an exercise in tension and balance. The entire settlement strains toward the sky, pulled upward by the natural buoyancy of its dry-sealed chambers and wood, yet held fast to the ancient moss-bed by tons of river-ground slate. It hangs in the middle depths of the cold lake: a complex, hand-made honeycomb of wood and reed, rattling with sound, washed in cold sun, utterly still against the granite belly of the peak.
