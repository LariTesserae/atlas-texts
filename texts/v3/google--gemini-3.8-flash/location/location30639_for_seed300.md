The basin is no larger than a dry-dock or the footprint of a small parish church, set deep into a shelf of pale, lime-shot mudstone. Its walls are scooped and hollowed by centuries of scouring, curved like the inside of a beef bone, smooth to the touch and dripping constantly with cold, alkaline water that leaches out of horizontal fissures in the rock. The air smells of wet slate, tallow, and raw petroleum.

At the floor of this depression, packed edge to edge, is an apparatus that has forgotten its original intention but never lost its momentum. 

It is an intensely crowded tangle of low-slung machinery, sunken into thirty inches of gray, milky water. Drive-shafts as thick as a man’s thigh cross one another at oblique angles, layered four or five tiers deep. Pitted iron rocker-beams pivot on bronze pins that have worn into loose, egg-shaped sleeves, yet they plunge and rise with a furious, unceasing agitation. Eccentric wheels, green with copper oxidation and black with heavy grease, spin just beneath the surface of the wash, throwing up sheets of fine, cold spray that hang in the daylight like an illuminated curtain. 

Yet for all this frantic agitation, the basin is remarkably quiet. 

The water here is heavy, rich in suspended clay and vegetable waxes that deaden shock; the bearings are water-lubricated or packed with cakes of tallow that yield silently under load. Metal strikes metal only through thick films of slime. What should be an ear-splitting machine shop produces only a soft, dense churn—a sound like a dog lapping milk from a stone floor, multiplied across hundreds of linkages, mingled with the faint, wet *shuck-slap* of flat leather belts running through troughs of mineral oil. The rock walls do not echo; their porous, sponge-eaten surface drinks the vibration and converts it into the weeping of more water.

The light falls from a high, pale overcast—diffuse, even, entirely ordinary daylight that illuminates every wet bolt-head, every scored cylinder, and the raw edges of broken cast-iron plates without drama. 

Things here are being endlessly redistributed. The system is violently dynamic, caught in a perpetual cycle of mechanical sorting and self-destruction. A set of perforated copper pans violently sifts gravel against a bank of counterweights; as soon as a pan fills to a certain measure, a trip-hammer drops, dumping the load into a churning wooden hopper, which immediately feeds an Archimedes screw driven by a drowned turbine. The water boils with gray foam where hydraulic jacks lift and drop stone blocks that have been squared off, worn down by friction into rounded loaves, and ground down to pebbles within the span of a few weeks. The architecture of the machine is continually remaking its own bed: new channels are cut through the soft bedrock overnight by the concentrated discharge of high-pressure brass nozzles, causing entire clusters of rocker-arms to tilt, slip their cogs, and find new, unplanned purchase against neighboring gears.

Vegetation has little purchase in the churn. There are no trees, no reeds. Only a thin, rubbery lichen the color of a wet boot heels the damp stone above the splash line, and in the calmer eddies behind rusted flywheel guards, mats of pale, stringy water-mold trail like unraveling fleece, feeding on the animal fat used to grease the sliders.

Life makes only brief, accidental intrusions. Occasionally, a pale, blunt-headed salamander—nearly translucent, its gills branched like red fern—will be carried over the upper lip of the rock by the seepage, tumble through the spray, and dart with manic agility between the thrashing rods. They seem drawn to the warmth generated by friction in the submerged thrust-bearings, clinging with splayed toes to the housing until a shift in the hydraulic cycle dislodges them and flings them toward the low-end drains. Once, a crow or a gull might drop in, attracted by the tallow, only to be clipped by the frantic, silent sweep of an iron counter-balance and pulled under the froth.

Human craft is everywhere, but human presence is long gone. The structures that remain are fragmentary: an ashlar bridge that carried pipes now broken off at the stubs; a timber control hut whose roof has rotted through, leaving only a skeletal frame over an iron lever bank whose handles are worn smooth by vanished palms. The levers still shift on their own, tugged by submerged chains connected to floats that bob violently in the surge.

At the eastern wall, the water finds its exit through three low, arched sluices cut directly into the limestone. The iron grates that once barred them have bowed outward under pressure, bent into smooth parabolas. Through these gaps, the water vanishes into a dark, horizontal void, moving fast and without an echo, taking with it a continuous slurry of ground stone and grease, while the frantic, muffled engine above continues to churn its shallow pond, endlessly busy, cold to the marrow, and perfectly alone.
