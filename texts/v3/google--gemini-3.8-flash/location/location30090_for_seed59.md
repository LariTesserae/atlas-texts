The hollow sits in a cleft of the high cordillera, a notched shelf of granite no wider than an amphitheater, entirely swallowed by the flesh of living wood. Cold cloud-water drags constantly across its upper rim, condensing against a colossal, multi-trunked fig-cypress hybrid that fills the entire basin like a plug of braided muscle. There is virtually no exposed stone; where the rock wall exists, it is muffled under three feet of water-logged liverworts, spongy clubmoss, and ancient root-casings that have calcified into grey gutters. 

It is cold here—the chill of wet fleece and melting hail—yet the air is utterly choked with breath, heat, and motion. 

The settlement, known simply as the Rung, is built not *on* the tree, but *out of* its perpetual renewal. Hundreds of small, rounded huts made of bent osier, woven rushes, and scraped rawhide are lashed directly into the crotches of the limbs, stacked four and five deep like the mud-daubed nests of cliff swallows. Walkways are not sawn timber, but split-bamboo hurdles pinned to the living bark with fire-hardened hardwood stakes. The carpentry is entirely manual: stone-adze marks notch the handrails; cordage made of braided nettle-fiber binds the ladders, slick with green slime and continually tightened against the surging growth of the wood. 

Water does not simply rain here; it cycles with violent immediacy. Runoff from the upper ridges plunges over the moss-shelves in twin cataracts, their roar bouncing off the rock walls until the basin hums like an iron bell. Flumes hollowed from hemlock logs divert this icy flow straight through the tiers of huts, rattling through communal troughs, driving primitive wooden trip-hammers that pulp bark for fiber, and spilling over the walkways into the abyss below in endless, fraying sheets of spray. Everything is soaked to its marrow. The inhabitants—cloaked in heavy grease-rubbed skins and matted wool—move across the ropes with the rapid, careless agility of creatures who have never known a dry footing.

And the living things are everywhere, deafeningly present. The hollow is a biological choke-point, a sheltered cauldron in an otherwise barren high-altitude desert. Tens of thousands of birds nest in the underside of the wicker huts and inside the hollow cores of the buttresses: slate-throated hummers that buzz like hurled stones through the mist; colonies of high-altitude parakeets that scream in rolling waves from the canopy every time the cloud-cover tears open; and heavy, blue-wattled guans that crash through the bromeliad gardens, shaking down showers of frigid water onto the alleys below. 

Beneath the footways, the root-wells churn with animal life. Blind, pale crayfish scuttle through the submerged moss carpets, harvested by children wielding notched bone spears. Long-tailed mountain weasels, semi-domesticated and half-wild, race through the thong-lashings of the dwellings, hunting the fat, leaf-eating rodents that gnaw at the city’s wicker walls. The air is thick with midges that rise off the falls in smoke-like pillars, pursued by broad-winged nightjars that wheel through the spray even in the glare of the noon sun.

The light is sharp, upland daylight—white and thin, filtered through tearing scraps of fog. For twenty minutes the entire gorge will be blinded by blinding, glare-filled vapour, only for a downdraft to scour the basin clear, exposing the tiered sprawl of thatched roofs, the smoking communal hearth-braziers lined with river-pebbles, and the dizzying drop beneath the lowest suspension bridge, where the roots dangle into blue air like frayed anchor lines.

There is no stillness in the place. When the wind funnels through the cleft, the great tree groans—a deep, subterranean creak that resonates through the floorboards of every hut—and the woven bridges sway in violent, arcing rhythms. The human populace lives in the pitch of this constant agitation: shouting over the thunder of the falls, repairing fraying ladders with green withies, hauling up basket-traps heavy with river-mollusks, throwing back swarms of cliff-wasps with smoldering bundles of bitter moss. 

It is an ancient site, worn into a rut of pure biological momentum. The beams of the oldest dwellings have been swallowed entirely by the tree’s secondary cambium, their door-frames now oval apertures of smooth, bark-lined wood that people stoop to crawl through. The refuse—bones, fibrous meal, broken slate tools—drops straight through the slatted floors to fertilize the roots below, feeding the very mass that holds the settlement above the void. It smells of tannic acid, wet feathers, cold river-silt, and the pungent musk of an entire ecosystem compressed into a single, roaring fissure.
