The floor of the cradle has not yet learned how to crumble.

It sits high—elevated on a sheer, truncated spur of the upper cordillera, perhaps three hundred paces across, shaped like a shallow amphitheater whose back wall climbs into a perpetual ceiling of warm, graphite-colored cloud. The rock underfoot is obsidian-dark and brand-new, an embryonic floor extruded so recently by whatever churns beneath that its fracture planes still carry the lethal, molecular sharpness of freshly cleaved flint. There is no sand here. There is no silt, no rounded gravel, no soil. The steps between terraces are raw crystallographic faults, clean as guillotine cuts, unsoftened by even an hour of windblown decay.

Yet it is soaked. A thick, maternal warmth hangs in the air, a greenhouse fug smelling of boiling minerals, wet zinc, and raw albumen. The cloud ceiling does not rain so much as it constantly collapses against the terrain; droplets form directly upon every exposed edge, swelling and slipping in endless, frictionless sheets down the black glass. It weeps from fissures that have no depth, bleeding clear, lukewarm water that gathers in razor-lipped pools without ever cutting a channel.

The space is largely open—sparsely furnished by nature, vast in its internal silences of shape—but loud with a relentless, mechanical and biological cadence.

Down the center of this high shelf stand three low, asymmetrical pavilions. They are not ruins, though they appear partial; they look like architectural thoughts caught midway through crystallization. Their material is neither stone nor alloy, but a stabilized, milky force-matrix that feels dense as lead when touched, though light passes through it with a slow, amber refraction. From their lintels, thick threads of an impossible geometry hang downward, motionless despite the drafts: solid light spun so thin it rings when water strikes it, a high, glass-chiming drone that underpins the louder racket of the basin.

The noise is vast. It comes first from the throat of a tilted, spire-like artifact half-embedded in the back wall—a post-physical conduit whose housing shivers at a frequency that vibrates the marrow in your shins. It is not burning fuel; it is folding the local vacuum, shedding a side-product of deep, acoustic heat that causes the mountain to rumble like a purring cat. Steam screams as it jets from the hairline gaps between the conduit’s casing and the raw stone.

Beneath that mechanical roar is the wet, manic thrashing of the animals.

They have found this embryonic terrace and filled its sparse geometry to bursting. Because there is virtually no plant life—save for pale, grease-thick films of chemotrophic slime and sparse, wire-like filaments of orange turf that sprout directly from the glass fractures—the ecology bypasses the botanical entirely. The fauna feed on the field, on the water, on each other.

Sleek, blind organisms the size of otters, plated in flexible, pearlescent horn that does not scrape against the obsidian, course in frantic packs through the shallow spillways. They move with an oily, lateral whip of their spines, diving into the boiling-warm pools to tear at mats of synthetic protein that slough off the conduit's lower ribs. Above them, nesting in the lintels of the milky pavilions, are colonies of soft-bodied flyers. They possess no feathers; their wings are ribbed membranes of smoky, translucent tissue that dry and stiffen in the updrafts before going limp again when they plunge back into the condensation. They chatter in deafening, saw-tooth rasps, dropping down to spear the blind swimmers with barbed, calcified tongues, then hauling them up to the glass ledges where the newborn rock drinks their pale, alkaline blood.

Everything moves. The landscape has no patience for stillness. A terrace forty feet wide suddenly shudders with a dry, glassy *clack*, dropping three inches along a newly forged slip-face, sending a sheet of steaming water washing across the boots. The creatures barely flinch; they ride the settling stone, their claws adapted to find grip in microscopic lattice-flaws.

The light is heavy, suppressed, an eternal late dusk filtered through the warm mist, but the technology provides its own dull, rhythmic luminescence. Every forty seconds, a wave of pale violet phosphorescence pulses outward from the central pavilion—not from a lamp, but as a temporary change in the refractive index of the air itself. For the three seconds the wave lasts, the cascading water appears to freeze into suspended ropes of violet beads, the flying swarm hangs static against the grey sky, and the black, embryonic stone below turns entirely transparent, revealing the deep, cyclopean conduits of the machine running miles into the mountain core, pulsing with cold, deliberate intellect.

Then the index snaps back. The water falls with a loud, unified slap against the flats; the birds shriek; the warm mist rolls in again, thick enough to taste, blanketing the newborn stone before it can even begin to weather.
