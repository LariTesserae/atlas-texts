The horizon sits low and unnervingly crisp, broken only by the sheer, unchiseled geometry of a high mineral tableland. Up here, the air has neither dampness nor haze; the sunlight strikes with the clean, flat brilliance of an arc-lamp suspended over an empty stage. There is no dust to scatter it, no moisture to soften its fall. Every edge—every drop-off, spine, and flat—is resolved with immaculate focus for miles in every direction.

The terrain beneath is not stone as it is usually understood, but raw metallurgical geology, fresh and unweathered. It has the look of an aborted foundry floor or a tectonic plate cast directly from molten stock and abandoned before the slag could be cleared. Vast planar expanses of dark, cold-rolled hematite and slate-gray phosphor bronze stretch out flat, broken by perfectly straight fracture lines. Because water has never touched these surfaces, there are no gullies, no rounded river stones, no alluvial fans. The cleavage faces are razor-bright, their crystalline facets catching the sun in blinding, instantaneous flashes. It is embryonic rock—unworked by the slow scythe of rain or frost, retaining the precise, severe angles of its initial cooling.

Despite the absolute lack of moisture, life occupies this high floor, and it is strangely, impeccably lush. 

It does not resemble the green flesh of earthly plants. Rooted directly into the cold seams of the alloy plain are sprawling, dense thickets of anhydrous flora—broad-fronded canopies made of laminated mica, spun-basalt filaments, and flexible, oil-rich carbon tubules. They grow in immense, isolated groves separated by half-leagues of bare, mirror-bright ground. Where a grove does take hold, it is opulent: tiered umbrellas of ribbed brass-foil spread wide to collect the thermal radiance; understories of fibrous, soot-black reeds crowd together in dense tangles, their stems ribbed like heavy electrical conduit. They bleed no sap if broken, only a dry, fragrant graphite dust or an amber resin that cures instantly in the dry air. 

These plants do not drink; they conduct. Deep taproots of native silver and lead drill straight down into the warm, unquenched core of the plateau, drawing up galvanic current and subtle chemical vapors. They sit in a state of continuous, sun-drenched equilibrium, neither growing rapidly nor dying back. Without seasons, without the rot of moisture or the grazing of animals, the groves remain fixed in their lush maturity, suspended in an eternal, heavy noon.

The scale is vast and hollow. Between the thickets, the plateau is mostly void—acres of unblemished, planed mineral where nothing stands, nothing moves, and nothing is buried. Looking out across the valley shelf, one sees five or six massive, solitary clusters of this metallic forest, standing like dark, intricate islands on a field of dark iron, and beyond them, only the rim of the world, dropping off into an azure abyss. 

There are no ruins here. No one has built a furnace, dug a trench, or laid a road. Yet the entire landscape bears the imprint of an industrial logic: the natural basalt towers have stepped cooling fins like transformer housings; the veins of native copper form tight, regular spirals on the flats, resembling the stator coils of massive dynamos; the soil—where it exists—is a uniform gravel of zinc pellets and crushed quartz. It is an ecology that has arrived at industrial form through pure abiotic chemistry, executing the mechanics of an engine without the intervention of a hand.

Nothing moves. There are no birds, no beetles scuttling through the mica mulch, no spores drifting on the draft. The air is warm, dry as baked slate, and entirely motionless. 

The sound is not an absolute void, but something more deliberate: a profound, low-frequency hum that rises through the soles of one’s feet rather than the ear—the steady, invariant transmission of thermal load across miles of unjointed metal. Very rarely, an expansion seam somewhere five miles out will yield a millimeter to the solar heat, releasing a single, clean *ping* that rings through the clear air like a struck bell, lingers without echo, and dissolves back into the silence.
