The ledge sits five thousand cubits above the basin floor, a shelf of pitted basalt no wider than a fairground, cupped in the throat of an extinct volcanic flue. The air up here is hot, arid, and violently alive. There is not a single drop of moisture in it; to open one’s mouth is to feel the tongue dry instantly to the texture of salted leather. 

Yet the shelf is smothered in growth. 

Because there is no water, the vegetation does not rot, swell, or wilt. It is a forest of dry silica, spun glass, and stiff, lacquered cellulose. Stems rise from deep fissures in the stone like bundles of copper wire, splitting into broad, fan-shaped fronds that feel like brittle vellum or mica. Great thickets of marrow-cane—hollow, pale yellow tubes as thick as a man’s torso—thrust twenty feet into the dim bronze air. They do not drink; their roots are acid-dripping drills that dissolve the basalt directly for phosphorus and heavy metals, feeding on the dry gale that pours continually out of the upper troposphere. When the plants bloom, they do not produce petals; they shed sheets of gossamer-thin, translucent sulfur shavings that tear free in the wind and race across the stone like driven sparks.

The light never clears. The sky is an impenetrable haze of pulverized pumice and vaporized resin, turning the sun into a blind, brass disc that casts long, diffused shadows. Despite the murk, the shelf is warm—heated from below by the lingering breath of the mountain’s core, and from above by the sheer friction of the atmosphere tearing across the summit. 

The sound is overwhelming. The marrow-cane is perforated by thousands of natural bore-holes; as the gales rip through the thickets, the grove screams like a damaged pipe organ, a shifting chord of shrieks, low bass drones, and hollow whistles. Beneath that music lies the endless, dry clatter of the forest itself: millions of rigid leaves slapping together with the sound of falling playing cards, and the percussion of seed-casings—hard, fist-sized pods of compressed chalk—exploding under the sun’s dry heat to sling their contents against the canyon walls like grapeshot.

Life here has a manic, frantic rhythm. The animals never rest because they do not sleep in water-soft bodies; they are creatures of chitin, scale, and dry joint. Across the bare basalt between the wire-groves scuttle herds of dust-striders—creatures like six-legged tortoises carapaced in polished slate, their claws tipped with iron-rich horn that clicks sharply against the rock. They graze furiously on the sulfur-shavings before the wind can sweep them over the abyss. Above them, fighting the churning updrafts, flocks of ribbon-tail gliders weave between the cane stalks. Their bodies are light as balsa, covered in a down of spun quartz, snap-turning through the shrieking air on four broad, leathery planes to snatch mid-air mites that drift in the static storm. Everything moves at high speed, driven by the hyper-oxygenated, dry gale and the sheer precarity of the drop below.

Between three massive pillars of weathered rock, human hands have left a mark, though simple and worn. 

There are low, circular bivouacs built of dry-stacked basalt slabs, mortared only with boiled insect-resins that have hardened into a dark, amber pitch. Their roofs are not thatch, but layers of heavy glider-hides pegged to the rock with bone wedges, lashed down against the gale by cords braided from the tough core-fibers of the marrow-cane. A solitary wind-baffle—a zig-zag wall of slate shingles set in rough wooden frames—stands near the ledge’s lip, directing the fiercest gusts away from a line of hand-turned stone querns. Scattered around the shelters lie primitive tools: scrapers made of sharp-edged flint tied to marrow-cane handles with dried sinew, broad shallow baskets of woven wire-grass used to sift the wind-blown chalk, and hammers of unworked volcanic bomb-stones, their grips rubbed smooth by greasy palms. There is no fire here; friction and the mountain’s breath provide all the warmth required, and any spark would turn the paper-dry thickets into a furnace in seconds.

The landscape is never still for an instant. A sudden downdraft strikes the center of the terrace: a clump of mica-ferns shatters with the sound of breaking mirrors, their glittering shards blasted sideways into the cliff face, instantly cleared to make way for a fresh knot of marrow-shoots already levering themselves out of the rock-cracks below. Two horned striders clash over a mound of wind-drifted sulfur dust, their shells knocking like stone mallets until the losing beast is flipped, scrambled by the gale, and blown violently over the precipice into the brown void beneath. 

The wind howls through the hollow reeds, shifting an octave higher as the brass sun sinks behind the dust, and the dry, screaming forest simply tightens its fibers against the storm, clattering and growing faster in the dark.
