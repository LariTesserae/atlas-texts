High on the shoulder of the limestone massif, tucked behind a slip-fault that sheared off millennia ago, lies a cleft no wider than forty paces and perhaps ninety paces long. It has no sky; the two walls of gray, dissolve-pitted stone lean together eighty feet overhead, their lips interlocked like the teeth of a worn zipper, sealed with plugs of compressed sod, ancient detritus, and the perpetual drainage of the alpine ridge above.

It is cold here, held at a permanent four or five degrees above freezing, but it does not freeze. The air is so thick with moisture that it ceases to feel like air and takes on the weight of an exhalation. Water does not fall as rain; it weeps. It sweats from every hairline fissure in the rock, runs in slick, grease-clear sheets down fluted stone runnels that were smoothed into soft concave gutters eons ago, and gathers into shallow, lip-full basins of travertine that never spill over their rims with any violence, only slide continuously into the peat below. 

The darkness is nearly complete. To an eye unadjusted, it is black; after hours, it resolves into faint, phosphorescent gradients—the sickly, cold-tea luminescence of wet limestone, the dull foxfire of wood rot, and the occasional pale glint of an eye reflecting nothing more than the bioluminescent smear of a crushed snail. 

Despite the gloom, the cleft is choked with growth. This is not the green of sunlit leaves, but a lushness of shade-flesh: heavy, water-logged liverworts that hang in aprons two feet thick, smelling of river bottom and cellar floor; pale, ghost-white clubmosses that trail like drenched hair; and a velvet turf of black-green bryophytes so densely woven and saturated that stepping on it would be like treading on a waterlogged mattress of pelts. The vegetation does not compete; it has long since filled every cubic inch of volume, mounting upon itself until the stone walls are entirely furred, their sharp corners softened into undulating, rounded swellings of wet vegetable pulp.

And inside this saturated felt, the animals live.

They are present in numbers that defy ordinary ecology, packed into the cleft like grain in a bin. They are small, heavy-bodied, and slow. Most are blind or pin-eyed, their faces dominated by quivering whisker-pads and blunt, rubbery snouts. There are thousands of them—thick-furred rodents the size of loaves, short-tailed burrowers with paddle-shaped claws, and slick, rubber-skinned salamanders as long as an arm that lie wedged into the crevices three deep, their bellies pressed together for communal heat. They do not scurry; they heave and shift. To reach out a hand in the dark would be to touch, inevitably, a wet back of grease-sheened fur, a cool rib cage rising and falling, or the blunt nose of a mole-thing snuffling patiently against one's knuckle.

Because there is no wind, no direct sun, and no change of season within the rift, there is no migration, no breeding season, no dormancy. The life here exists in a locked, frictionless cycle that has run so long its edges are completely round. The animals feed on the endless sheets of gelatinous fungus that break down the peat, on the vast mats of white cave-cress that root in the dung-slurry, and on each other’s spent carcasses, which are drawn down into the sod by pale, blind necrophores before they can ever foul the air. 

Deeper back, where the floor rises into a bench of siltstone that stays slightly drier than the runnels, there are signs of an ancient, unchanging industry. It is not human, or perhaps it is the remnant of something that forgot how to be human ten thousand generations back. Scattered among the living bodies are the tools: heavy, smooth river cobbles—stones that could only have been carried up from the valleys below when the cleft was open to the air—each with a thumb-sized depression worn into its center from endless, repetitive pounding. Small piles of cracked, calcified snail shells lie beside these anvil-stones, swept into neat, low dams to redirect the trickling water away from communal sleeping depressions. 

Sometimes, one of the larger creatures—a pale, broad-shouldered beast with forearms like an otter and long, spatulate fingers—will sit in the darkness, lift a flat cobble with both hands, and bring it down upon a thick-shelled water beetle with a single, practiced, hollow *clack*. It does not look at what it is doing. Its paws work by touch alone, peeling the bitter black wing-cases and offering the soft fat inside to the small, shivering young that cling to its throat. The tool is placed back down in the exact groove in the rock from which it was lifted, slick with grease and palm-sweat that has impregnated the stone for centuries.

The sound of the place is astonishingly low, smothered by the tons of wet moss and the cushion of living bodies. It is a dense, muffled murmur: the liquid *glop* of water dropping into deep sediment, the persistent, low-frequency hum of thousands of tiny hearts, the rhythmic scritch of blunt nails against soft silt, and the collective, humid sigh of moist lungs drawing in the cold, wet air. 

Nothing changes here. The water seeps at the exact speed it evaporates into the porous rock. The moss grows only as fast as it is cropped and trampled into peat. If an earthquake shook the mountain, the deep, elastic mattress of mud, roots, and sleeping bodies would absorb the shock without a tear. It is an equilibrium so settled that it resembles a clockwork organ, continuing to play its one wet, dark chord high above the valleys, entirely indifferent to whether the sun has risen or the world below still exists.
