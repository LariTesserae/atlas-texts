The light up here has teeth. At this altitude—where the troposphere thins until the zenith bruises into indigo—there is no haze to cushion the sun. Everything is struck by an unremitting, white-hot glare that carries no true heat, only a fierce, scouring brilliance that makes the eyes ache behind closed lids. Yet the air itself remains stubbornly cool, sharp as chipped flint, smelling faintly of ozone, dry feathers, and cold stone.

It is a world of vertical ribs. Long ago, the softer margins of this continental spine were eaten away, leaving behind a field of colossal, freestanding granite towers—pinnacles that plunge downward thousands of feet into a floor that is entirely invisible, forever swallowed by a flat, motionless plateau of lower cloud. The erosion is ancient, settled; these towers have stood in their current profiles for hundreds of thousands of years. Their flanks are not raw rubble but are ground smooth, flute-grooved, and pitted by an eternity of airborne grit until they resemble petrified marrow.

The sound is immense and entirely stationary. It does not rise and fall like the surf; it does not gust like a storm. It is a flat, continuous, deafening roar—the sound of an unbroken planetary jet shearing across the vertical labyrinth. The wind does not stop. It has likely not paused for a second in ten thousand years. It blows with such constant, invariant pressure that you can lean out over a two-mile void and rest your chest against the air, supported by the sheer mass of the current. The sound fills every cavity of the skull, vibrating the teeth, a white noise so massive and permanent that within an hour the brain ceases trying to interpret it as noise and accepts it as a physical texture, like gravity or atmospheric pressure.

Because the wind is invariant, the water here has learned to exist as a steady-state friction. There are no rivers, no rainstorms. Instead, the wind carries a fine, microscopic dampness combed out of unseen cloud-decks hundreds of leagues to the west. This mist is driven violently against the windward prows of the granite pillars, condensing instantly into thin, glassy sheaths of moisture. It does not run far; it trickles through hair-thin fractures, collecting in small, circular potholes no wider than a bowl, gouged into the rock by ancient pebble-drills. These cold, translucent pools are entirely still, protected from the blast by precise lip-curvatures in the stone. They sit throughout the pillars like quiet eyes, never freezing in the high-intensity sun, never boiling away, perpetually replenished at the exact rate they evaporate.

Vegetation has surrendered the open rock. The only plants are crustose lichens the color of dried liver, welded so tightly to the granite they seem dyed into the crystal, and a species of tough, rubbery alpine felt that wedges itself deep inside the lee-side fissures. These mats do not have leaves; they are spongy, grey-green cushions that absorb the aerosol mist and shelter micro-ecosystems of minute, six-legged springtails that crawl over the damp stone in absolute silence beneath the roar.

Yet the void is not empty of life; it is extraordinarily full. 

The air between the spires is dense with bodies. Not ground animals, for there is almost no horizontal ground to stand upon, but an immense, permanent biomass of aeronauts. Thousands of pale, narrow-winged creatures—neither strictly birds nor bats, but something with the stiff, leathery flight surfaces of pterosaurs and the downy underbellies of storm petrels—hang motionless in the air-columns. They do not flap. To flap in this gale would be to break a wing. Instead, they lock their joints, angling their bodies by fractions of a millimeter to ride the standing shockwaves formed where the wind splits against the stone. 

They fill the yawning chasms between the towers in stratified tiers: thousands upon thousands of them, hanging suspended like dust motes caught in a cathedral sunbeam, their pale bellies dazzling white in the ultraviolet glare. They nest in the honeycombed lee of the spires, crowding shoulder to shoulder in the circular eroded hollows. Because the sound is deafening, they do not sing or cry; they communicate through subtle twitches of long, ribbon-like tail plumes and the rhythmic blinking of nictitating membranes that flash yellow in the raw light. Their guano coats the downwind faces of the pillars in thick, chalky streaks, feeding colonies of nitrogen-hungry mosses that glow vivid orange in the shadows.

Human presence here is almost non-existent, reduced to the absolute minimum required to observe the void without dying. There are no shelters—no wall could stand against the shear. But in the deep, narrow cracks where the wind is baffled down to a bearable draft, there are small, unmistakable traces of manual passage. Wedged into a cleft high above an abyss sits a piton made of carved ibex horn, lashed with twisted strips of sinew that have dried into amber-colored wire. From it hangs a short lanyard made of knotted gut, holding a single, hollowed-out bird bone drilled with finger-holes: a wind-whistle, left there to vibrate in the draft. It does not play a tune; it produces a single, shrill, unending harmonic that slips into the seams of the great roar, marking that a thinking hand once anchored itself here, rested in the deafening light, took a mouthful of water from a granite cup, and moved on along the edge of the sky.
