The shelf sits at nearly eight thousand feet, suspended on the volcanic shoulder of a ridge that catches the trade winds from both oceans. Down below, the world is cold, thin, and scourged by upland wind, but here a thermal fault line runs beneath the basalt floor, keeping the bedrock steadily blood-warm to the touch. The heat rises through cracks in the stone to meet the descending alpine mist, producing a standing, sunlit greenhouse no larger than a city block—a natural amphitheater enclosed on three sides by amphitheater-curved cliffs of fluted, weeping basalt, and open on the fourth to a sheer drop that looks out over a sea of bright white cloud.

Because the rock is warm, the constant mountain rain never freezes and rarely pools into stillness. It sheets, hisses, and drips. The air is brilliant and saturated, filled with the sharp, clean light of high altitude passing through vapor that refuses to consolidate into fog. 

The vegetation is luxuriant, but it does not form a jungle. It is lush in scale and health, yet curiously sparse in arrangement. Between the specimens, wide flagstones of reddish-black volcanic pavement lie exposed, washed smooth by centuries of low-viscosity mineral water. There is immense room to breathe between the presences that live here. 

Chief among them are the great cistern-cabbage trees—perhaps nine or ten of them scattered across the terrace like solitary columns. Each rises on a single, ringed trunk of jade-colored wood as thick as a wine vat, terminating in an immense, architectural rosette of fleshy, cup-shaped leaves. A single leaf is the size of a fishing dory, stiff, translucent, and ribbed with pale veins that pulse faintly with the slow hydrostatic pressures of the plant’s interior. These rosettes catch the perpetual mountain condensation and the runoff from the cliffs, holding tons of warm, iron-rich water in their crowns. 

Every ten or twelve minutes, one of the higher leaves sags under the weight of its own catch, tilts with a heavy, rubbery groan, and discharges fifty gallons of water all at once into the stone basin below. 

This is the source of the noise. The terrace is remarkably loud, not with the chaos of a storm, but with an echoing, percussive rhythm. The semicircular cliff behind the ledge acts as an acoustic dish, amplifying the clatter of sheets of water slapping against the stone, the subterranean gurgle of drainage fissures swallowing streams, and the hollow, booming *thump* of the great leaves snapping back into position after tipping their loads. It sounds like an untended forge, or a primitive mill running empty at full flood.

Animal life is nearly absent, deterred by the altitude and the sheer acoustic violence of the chamber. There are no birds. The only mobile creatures are small, semitransparent isopods that crawl across the undersides of the leaves, grazing on microscopic red algae that stain the dripping mineral surfaces like streaks of dried arterial blood.

At the center of the terrace, where three drainage channels converge toward the cliff’s edge, there are faint, unmistakable signs of handcraft, though they are old enough to have settled completely into the geology. The rim of the stone floor has been chipped away with harder stone—pecked into a series of six shallow steps that lead down into a sunken trough. Beside this trough sits a pestle made of a dense river cobble that does not belong to this mountain; its base is stained dark from some oily root that was ground here decades, or perhaps centuries, ago. Tied to the bole of the nearest cistern-tree, roughly chest-high, is a weathered loop of braided grass, grayed by sun and mineral encrustation, holding a split gourd that has long since calcified into something resembling heavy porcelain.

Whoever stood here did not build a home; they came to harvest something that only this heat, this altitude, and this slow hydraulic patience can produce. 

The dynamic of the terrace is one of heavy, deliberate equilibrium. The rock does not crack further; it has achieved its balance with the heat from below and the water from above. The great rosettes grow by millimeters a year, their wood dense as ivory under the relentless mountain sun. Water arrives from the sky, warms against the dark basalt, rises as steam, hits the cooler rock face, condenses, fills the plants, falls with a crash, and spills over the terrace edge into the void—falling thousands of feet before it can even reach the valleys below, turning into a fine, warm spray that vanishes into the lower air long before it hits the ground.
