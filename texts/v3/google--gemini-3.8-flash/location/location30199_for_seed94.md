The elevation does not declare itself through sharpness. At this altitude—thousands of meters above the low basins, where the air should reasonably be thin and starved—the rock has had time to forget its teeth. The cordillera is vast beyond the sweep of a single horizon, rolling in monumental, whale-backed massifs that have worn themselves into steady equilibrium over tens of millions of years. It produces its own atmospheric cells: towering banks of pewter-colored vapor that drift through broad saddles, break apart into cold drizzle, and re-condense within the hour.

The cold is immediate, clinging, and wet. It sits around four or five degrees above freezing, biting through moisture rather than wind. Water is everywhere, yet it rarely falls as a deluge; it weeps. It beads across every lip of stone, hangs suspended in the needle-furred leaves of stunted podocarps, and runs in silent, laminate sheets over rounded granite sills. The earth does not drain so much as it slumps under its own saturation. 

Despite the cold and the height, there is no bare rock. The plateau is choked, packed edge to edge in a dense, competitive sprawl of life. Mats of peat and rust-red sphagnum are three meters deep in places, vibrating slightly underfoot like waterlogged mattresses. Above the moss rise thickets of black-barked dwarf trees, their boughs woven so tightly together that one could walk across their crowns without touching the ground. Bromeliad-like rosettes, tough as cured leather and filled with ice-rimmed rainwater, lodge into every fork. There is no empty space; life here acts like expanding foam, filling every niche, crevice, and saddle until the terrain appears upholstered.

Yet there is a distinct, heavy grain to the materials of this wild place, an odd mechanical logic embedded in its biology and geology. There are no walls, no roads, not a single squared timber or chipped lintel to suggest a builder has ever stood here. But the plateau sits atop a rare, ancient seam of native metals—raw bands of elemental copper, iron-rich bog ores, and deep strata of bitumen-bearing shale—and the ecosystem has organized itself around these veins like an unguided industry.

The mud in the depressions does not smell of rot; it smells of cold machine oil, wet coal, and ozone. Seeping springs bubble up through the peat carrying slicks of iridescent, natural lubricants that coat the stones in a greasy, protective film. Certain reed-like grasses have absorbed the high mineral content, their hollow stems sheathed in silica and iron, clinking like loose hardware when the wind passes through them. In the broader valley floors, geothermal steam fissures vent directly into deep, water-filled hollows within the granite. Because of the weight of the water above them, they do not erupt as wild geysers; instead, they operate as natural pneumatic pistons, releasing subterranean pressure in steady, cyclical strokes. The peat shelf rises an inch, falls an inch, sighs out a breath of scalding, metallic mist, and settles. 

The sound of all this is astonishingly subdued. The sheer density of the vegetation and the continuous, low-lying mist act as an acoustic sponge, deadening any sharp noise within fifty paces. The piston-vents do not roar; they produce only a heavy, subterranean *thump-hiss* that travels through the soles of the feet rather than the ears. What remains is a profound, dripping silence, punctuated by the soft, rhythmic clicking of the iron-grass and the flat *plop* of heavy condensed droplets falling from canopy to marsh.

Fauna is abundant, but it shares the quiet, heavy discipline of the terrain. Low-slung, broad-bodied ungulates with dense, oiled fleece move in deliberate files along the contour lines of the slopes. They feed on the galvanic mosses that grow around the copper outcrops—mosses that hold a continuous, faint electrical charge from the earth's natural currents, faintly glowing with a dim bioluminescence in the deep shade. In the branches above, armored lizards with overlapping, slate-gray scutes navigate the canopy with agonizing slowness, their claws clicking dully against the sodden wood. Nothing calls out. Communication among the high-tier animals happens through chemical traces left in the oily runoff, or via the low-frequency vibrations that shudder continuously through the saturated turf.

When the cloud breaks, the light is surprisingly clean, pale gold and flat, striking the vast, rounded shoulders of the range. For a few miles in every direction, the landscape glints: the metallic sheen of the mineralized reeds, the wet, polished black of the dwarf wood, and the silver threads of a thousand tiny cataracts all spilling toward the distant, unseen rim of the plateau. Then the pressure shifts, another shelf of high montane air rolls inward from the west, and the valley is swallowed again in cold vapor, ticking and breathing in its own rhythmic, unpeopled labor.
