The frost does not melt here; it is absorbed. At four thousand meters, where the ridge should break into barren slate and ice-scoured shale, there is instead a basin the width of a parish church, filled to its rim with an architecture of living tissue. 

It is bitterly cold, hovering just a fraction above freezing during the bright hours and dropping into hard frost by sundown. The wetness does not come from rainfall, which falls rarely and only as sleet, but from the low cloud that rakes across the pass without pause. The mist pushes through the streets in gray, woolly tongues, snagging on the corners of things, wetting every exposed surface until the structures sweat and drip into the lower gutters.

The entire place is an urban fabric built without stone, without mortar, and without cutting a single trunk. The masonry is a dense, peat-hardened basketwork of high-altitude osier, dwarf alder, and a species of black, resinous birch whose boughs have been guided over centuries to fuse into load-bearing vaults, floor joists, and lintels. Where two branches meet, they graft under pressure, leaving heavy, knobby knuckles of scar tissue that act as corbels. The walls between these frames are thick curtains of living liverworts and felted alpine clubmoss, three or four hand-spans deep. They are moist to the touch, dense as damp sod, and smell faintly of wet iron and cold tannin. 

Because the space in the ravine is narrow—scarcely two hundred paces from the shear-cliff wall to the western scree—the settlement has grown upward in tiers. Alleys run beneath low, springing arches of rooted wood. Overheads are webbed with narrow gangways of braided runners that bounce faintly underfoot. Nothing here is flat. The ground is a deep, springy mattress of compressed root-felt and fallen needles that yields two inches beneath a boot, muffling all footsteps to a soft, wet thud. Beneath that felt lies the cold rock, fractured and young, with sharp, raw edges of split quartzite that occasionally push up through the floor of a cellar like gray teeth, not yet worn smooth by water or time.

It is laid out with an undeniable urban density. There are distinct tenements, multi-family stacks where the rooms are hollow chambers formed inside the living, swollen trunks of hollowed-out mound-willows. There are public conduits: long, fluted troughs grown from split cedar-shoots that route the condensing cloud-water down from the high canopies into public basins shaped like fat, moss-lined cisterns. Windows are rare, usually just gaps in the woven bough-screens stuffed with thin panes of beaten animal fat or trans-sheer sheets of bladder-kelp brought up from some distant coast, rattling slightly as the alpine wind hits them.

Light rarely penetrates to the lowest tier. The air there is stagnant and smells of cold cellarage and wet loam. The only illumination comes from the dim, bluish daylight filtering down through five layers of branch-decks, turned emerald where it passes through translucent carpets of wet sphagnum. In the higher walkways, near the level where the basin meets the mountain wind, the light is gray and glaring, flat and shadowless against the rime that forms along the northern lip of every bough.

The cold keeps decay at bay. Leaves do not rot so much as they mummify under the steady glaze of ice-melt, gradually cementing into the substrate. There is no open flame—the resinous heartwood would catch and burn the entire precinct to ash in an hour—so the interiors are warmed only by the tight packing of bodies and the slow, latent heat of the compost-cores buried deep beneath the thoroughfares, where heaps of compressed moss and kitchen scraps ferment at an almost imperceptible pace, venting faint, pale wisps of steam through hollow reed-chimneys into the freezing lanes.

Animal life is sparse and secretive. A traveler might catch the dry click of a raven’s bill high up in the anchor-pines that tie the settlement to the mountain wall, or see the fat, white shape of an ermine slipping between the double-walls of a woven granary. Once or twice in an hour, a pale moth, broad-winged and hairy as wool, flutters out from a seam in the moss, bewildered by the mist, only to freeze against a lintel, its legs stiffening into the wood before it can beat its wings again.

The only continuous sounds are the sounds of weight and wet: the leathery creak of the framing-boughs as the cloud-wind leans against the upper ridge, the steady, syncopated *clink-tock* of condensed water falling from the ceiling-girders into the lower cisterns, and the soft, pneumatic sigh of the mist being dragged through millions of wet needle-clusters. It is an organized hive, silent, tightly fastened against the mountain’s throat, neither dying nor flowering, holding its breath against the frost.
