At an altitude where the air should be thin and scouring, the ravine is instead choked with a heavy, cold soup of suspended mist. It is a hanging rift no wider than a cathedral’s nave, wedged between two sheared faces of black basalt so young their fracture lines are still razor-edged, snapping cleanly against the gloom without the soft rounding of rain or frost. Nothing here has had time to weather, yet nothing here is beginning.

The water does not fall from above; it weeps out of the rock face along microscopic, pressurized fissures, keeping every vertical surface slick, black, and glass-bright. It gathers in a film that does not trickle downward, but clings in a state of high-surface-tension equilibrium, cold as well-water, smelling faintly of sulfur and raw machine oil.

There are no buildings, no bolts, no evidence of human labor or deliberate assembly. Yet the rift is deeply, furiously mechanized. 

Projecting directly from the living stone are massive, reciprocating arms of native iron and greased slate. They are not bolted to the mountain; the strata of the crags simply twist, re-crystallizing into seamless crankshafts, eccentric cams, and great, grooved flywheels eight paces across that spin with an immaculate, terrifying silence through the damp fern-masses. They turn at an absolute, unvarying tempo. There is no friction, no heat, no grit ground into their tolerances; they are geological formations that happen to be high-pressure internal-combustion linkages, breathing cold air and weeping distilled petroleum from glands of dark, velvety moss.

The sound is pervasive and rhythmic: the subterranean thrum of three-stroke cycles firing through the stone, the wet *shlick-clack* of counterweights rising through dripping thickets of liverwort, and the steady, lung-filling hiss of steam released from natural vents that behave exactly like exhaust manifolds. It is loud enough to vibrate in the teeth, yet completely steady—a flat, unvarying acoustic ceiling under which everything else must live.

And life is everywhere, thriving in the spray and the grease.

Dense carpets of broad, blue-black ferns crowd the ledges, their fronds glistening with an oily sheen that beads water into perfect, heavy pearls. Between the churning iron linkages, long tendrils of pale, tuberous vines hang like heavy cabling, their pods pulsing in sync with the stroke of the basalt pistons. The flora does not rot; leaves that drop into the sumps of natural lubricant remain intact, suspended forever in the clear, cold fluid.

The animals here do not flee the movement; they are part of its tolerances. Swarms of small, beetle-like organisms with shells of burnished zinc crawl over the cold journal bearings, licking away the condensate and leaving behind trails of graphite slime that keep the rock-iron joints frictionless. They exist in thousands, clinging to the wet undersides of connecting rods as they plunge and rise through curtains of spray. In the narrow crevices where the steam exhausts into the cold, blind salamanders with translucent gills press their bodies against the warm brass veins of the rock, their throats vibrating at the exact frequency of the engine's central shaft.

Above, the light is fixed at the dim grey of a late November twilight—a flat, shadowless exposure that never shifts. There is no morning here, no dusk, no change in season. A broken patch of sky far above the rift's sheared lip shows clouds that do not drift; they are locked in a curdled, motionless sprawl, lit from within by the dull, copper-colored static of an atmosphere permanently ionized by the ravine’s relentless, purposeless output.

Nothing in this place consumes fuel. The iron rods do not wear down against their bushings; the moss does not overgrow the mechanical journals; the water neither floods the ravine nor dries out under the steam. It is an impossible machine that is also a natural valley, an ecosystem that has crystallized into an industrial stroke, holding itself in an iron-hard, dripping stillness through the sheer violence of its eternal motion.
