The stone shelf sits at seven thousand feet, pinched between two dull shoulders of gneiss where the mountain forgets to keep climbing and levels out into a broad, shallow terrace. 

It is small—no wider than a bowshot from the cliff face to the drop-off, and perhaps three times that in length—yet it has been laid out with the methodical rigor of an ancient city quarter. There are alleys barely two shoulders wide, paved with flat-cleaved river flagstones that must have been hauled up the gorge centuries ago on human backs. There are stepped thresholds, communal hearth-circles sunken into the granite, and rows of single-room dwellings built from unmortared, dressed basalt. Every wall is plumb, double-skinned, and backfilled with dry gravel. The tools that made this place were simple: stone mauls, split-timber wedges, fire-softened rock, and perhaps the edge of a soft copper chisel that left rounded, bruising scars in the grain of the darker blocks.

The air is cool, thin, and remarkably still. It tastes of cold rock and the wet iron smell of old well-water. High above, the sky is a clean, pale daylight that doesn't burn, casting sharp-edged, slate-gray shadows into the courtyards without ever warming the stone beneath them. 

Moisture here does not come as a storm; it simply exists in the joints of the world. Clouds drag their bellies across the edge of the shelf in the early hours, leaving behind a fine, uniform film of condensation that glazes the northeast side of every upright stone. It gathers in deliberate, carved channels—narrow rills no deeper than a finger, grooved into the lintels and the courtyard paving—guiding the sweat of the morning down into small, rectangular cisterns cut directly into the bedrock. These basins are dark, calm, and perpetually three-quarters full. The water does not run out, nor does it spill over; it sits at an exact, glassy equilibrium, disturbed only when a drop breaks loose from a rock overhang with a dull, wooden *tink*.

Vegetation survives only where it cannot be dislodged. It does not sprawl. In the hair-cracks between the dry-stacked rocks, there are tight cushions of pale blue-green lichen, crustose and brittle to the touch, and tufts of a stiff, wiry sedge whose rootlets have mineralized into the fissures. No trees grow on the shelf; the wind, when it passes, has nothing to rustle. A single patch of dwarf willow, no taller than a boot and flattened entirely against the stones of the western retaining wall, has remained precisely four spans wide for as long as the masonry has stood.

The town is empty of crowd, but it is not a ruin. A ruin suggests collapse, the work of weeds prying apart stone, the sag of rotted beams. Here, there are no beams; the roofs were corbelled inward, stone overlapping stone until a single heavy capstone sealed the vault. Nothing has fallen. The erosion has long since finished its conversation with the stone: the corners are blunted, the flags are polished smooth by feet that vanished an age ago, and the sand that gathered in the leeward corners of the doors has compacted into hard, gray crusts that no longer drift.

Sound does not travel far, but it retains its shape. If a boot scrapes the flags, the noise clips off sharply against the basalt faces, swallowed immediately by the empty doorways and the open sky beyond the rim. There are few living things to make noise. A solitary, slate-colored lizard occasionally shifts its position along the warm edge of a drainage curb, its belly dragging with a faint, papery hiss across the grit, then pauses for an hour without blinking. High above, a hawk might circle on a flat thermal, but it does not cry out; down here, the only continuous sound is the faint, rhythmic seepage of high-altitude moisture settling into the stones.

It is a place frozen not in ice, but in a completed architecture. The people who built it cut deep enough into the terrace that their works became part of the mountain's geology. Nothing is deteriorating because there is nothing left to rot; nothing is being added because the plan of the terrace was finished before the first mortarless roof was closed. The day stays at a cool, constant noon, held between the pale mountain sky and the damp, immovable basalt.
