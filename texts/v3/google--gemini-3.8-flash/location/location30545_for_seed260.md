The roar does not come from wind, because the air is so thin and parched that it barely has the body to carry a breeze. The noise is structural. It is the sound of sheer, unjoined matter being extruded upward through an unfinished threshold of geometry: a single, crushing, tectonic chord that stays at the exact threshold where hearing ceases to be an auditory sense and becomes an ache in the marrow of the teeth. It is deafening in the way a mountain range would be deafening if it were being shoved through an iron die at the speed of fingernail growth. It never pauses, never modulates, and never echoes, because there are no soft banks or water-smoothed hollows to cushion it.

Everything here stands at a violent, stratospheric altitude, so high that the ceiling of the world is not blue or black, but a cold, bruised ash-violet. The horizon is broken into staggering, vertical splinters. These are not peaks carved by weather; weather has never touched them. They are newly minted spires of raw, unweathered olivine, slate-gray volcanic glass, and plates of dense, white mineral resembling unpolished porcelain. Their edges are mathematical, razor-hard, and completely unblunted. Because there has never been rain, frost-heave, or running water to scrape them down, they look embryonic, like the crystalline scaffolding of a planet abandoned halfway through its first cooling.

Between these monumental knives of stone, wide gulfs drop into gray obscurity. Yet across these sheer surfaces, life grows with an uncanny, dry lushness.

It is vegetation that has never known a drop of moisture. It does not possess sap, juice, or cell-water. Instead, the flora is constructed of brittle carbon braids, powdered sulfur velvet, and flexible, gossamer sheets of mica that peel outward like broad hosta leaves. In the fissures of the fresh rock, massive ferns of woven graphite unfurl in thick, dense swathes, their fronds overlapping in heavy cascades that look soft from a distance but clatter like tin plate when they brush together. Vast, tumbling tapestries of an ivory-colored, hair-like lichen choke the gullies, suspended on nothing, nourished entirely by the electrostatic friction of the deafening hum and the faint, dry dust of crushed talc. It is luxuriant, sprawling, and utterly desiccated; snap a stalk, and it breaks with the dry snap of fired terracotta, releasing an odorless puff of pale, glittering meal.

Reality here has not quite set. It remains half-cured, like drying glue.

In certain sectors between the spires, perspective misbehaves. A pillar that appears miles away will cast a sharp, diamond-edged shadow directly onto a ledge only three paces in front of you. Gravity has small, local disagreements with itself: near the lip of the eastern drop-offs, the pull of the ground slants forty degrees outward, toward the empty sky, so that the dry ferns grow horizontally into the void, their heavy frills pointing toward a cold, absent center of mass. The light is dim, coming not from a discrete sun, but from a dull, glowing seam where two tectonic facets of the sky fail to meet. It casts no true warmth, only a flat, cool illumination that leaves the deeper chasms choked with ink-black, three-dimensional shadows that do not move with the hours.

Human presence has touched this place, but only with the barest, most desperate simplicity.

Tucked into an overhanging shelf of freshly cleaved basalt sits a low-roofed shelter. It is made of dry-stacked slate shards, wedged tight without clay or mortar, held firm only by gravity and the precise, manual chipping of its stones. There are no chimneys, no hearths; there is nothing to burn and no draft to carry smoke. Anchored into the rock beside it are a series of thick wooden wedges, pale and bone-dry, hammered into virgin fissures with river-smoothed cobblestones that must have been carried from some forgotten, ancient world where water once existed. 

From one of these wedges hangs a long, braided descent-cord made of the graphite-fern fibers. It drops down, down through thousands of feet of dead, dry air, dangling into the dim roar of the lower shelf. At its end swings an empty hook of hammered horn. A short distance away, small, primitive markers—cairns of three or four raw, razor-cornered stones balanced with impossible precision—trace a path along a ledge that narrows until it becomes a single, knife-thin line of quartz. There are no footprints. The ground is too hard to take an impression, too young to have accumulated a crust of dirt.

There are no birds in the canyons. There are no beetles in the graphite thickets, no nematodes in the dry mineral meal, no wings, no bellies, no breath. The only movement is the agonizingly slow emergence of the world itself: a spire of pale stone that, over the course of three generations, might lengthen by the width of a thumb, widening a crack in the shelf with a sound like a distant, iron bell.

The cold is quiet, but the air is not. The deafening hum of the planet’s slow, unfinished birth fills every lungful of dry, chalky air, vibrating the tiny bones of the inner ear until the silence of the void feels like a memory that belongs to someone else entirely.
