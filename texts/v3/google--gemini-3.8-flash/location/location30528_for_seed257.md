The basin sits at an altitude where air would be thin and dry, but there is no air here; there is only the water, held in a massive, enclosed cistern of hand-fitted megaliths perched upon a mountain shoulder. 

It is utterly submerged. From floor to the vaulted, corbeled ceiling forty feet above, the chamber is brimmed with warm, heavy water. The heat does not boil or roil; it seeps through the floor slabs from some deep, buried volcanic vein, keeping the liquid at the temperature of fresh blood or sun-baked silt. Because of the elevation and the sealed stone envelope, the hydrostatic pressure is gentle, an enveloping, tepid womb that has not drained in ten thousand years.

There is no sunlight. The masonry above is thick, sealed with bitumen and heavy clay, keeping out the sky. The darkness is near-absolute, diluted only by the faint, milky bioluminescence shed by creatures that have never seen a leaf.

Nothing here photosynthesizes. The kingdom of plants does not exist in this basin—no ribbon-weeds, no suspended algae, no green slime upon the stone. In their absence, the surfaces are coated in a pale, living pelt of animals. Encrusting sponges, ivory and sulfur-yellow, have spread across the chisel-marks of the walls until the rectilinear stone looks organic, cushioned, and blunted. Millions of minute hydroids and freshwater bryozoans feather out into the dark, their translucent tentacles combing the stillness for detritus. Where a diver might expect moss, there are only carpet-forming tubeworms with crowns of white hair, opening and closing in rhythmic, silent sighs.

The construction is entirely artificial, though its age has softened its geometry into something resembling a limestone cave. Every inch was once hewn with stone hammers and soft metal chisels. Immense rectangular pillars—eight paces thick at the base—march down the length of the hall, supporting low, stepped arches. But water has lived against the rock for so long that the edges have slumped. The stone has dissolved grain by grain into the warm broth, smoothing the lintels into swollen, bone-like shapes. The floor is paved with vast slabs of dark basalt, grooved with drainage channels that have not tasted air since the first dynasty of stone-masons laid them by hand. There is no natural bed; beneath every layer of silt is dressed flagstone.

Despite the blackness, the space is crowded. Life has packed itself into every cubic foot with the density of an overstocked pond. Blind, pale fish—heavy-bellied and whisker-mouthed—drift between the pillars like suspended logs, their fins occasionally brushing the stonework with the dry rasp of sharkskin. Thousands of porcelain-white isopods and blind crawfish clamber over one another along the stepped bases of the columns, their legs ticking against the rock in faint, brittle rhythms. From the crevices where ancient mortar has washed away, fat, ribbon-shaped leeches and blind eels extend into the current, swaying in unison toward the slight warmth rising from the paving stones.

Movement here is infinitesimally slow. Convection lifts the warm water toward the ceiling in lazy, majestic plumes that take an hour to cross the width of the hall, drifting down again along the cooler perimeter walls. Silt, which is nothing more than powdered stone and the molted carapaces of small animals, falls like a permanent, imperceptible snow that never settles deep because the floor-dwellers perpetually sift it through their gills and legs.

The acoustic landscape is nearly dead. Sound travels fast and flat in the dense warmth, robbed of echo. There is only the low, ambient thrum of water molecules shifting against stone, the occasional faint *click* of a claw upon basalt, and the soft, fleshy displacement of a fish turning in the dark. 

It is a monument that has completely forgotten its makers. Built with primitive hands to hold a mountain reservoir, it has outlived the civilization that hauled its stones, outlived the sun, and settled into a warm, eternal, crowded twilight—a drowning hall kept alive solely by the quiet persistence of blind flesh touching ancient stone.
