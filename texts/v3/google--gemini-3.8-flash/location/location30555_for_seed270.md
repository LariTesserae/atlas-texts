The chamber is no larger than the foundation pit of a heavy machine, perhaps forty paces long and twelve wide, sunk six feet below the working floor of some long-outmoded sorting house. Above, an overhead ceiling of slotted steel grating admits a steady, neutral daylight—cool and grey, completely flat, illuminating everything with the indifferent clarity of late morning.

Down here, it is cold and entirely without water. Moisture has not touched these surfaces in generations; the air carries no vapor, only the sharp, alkaline smell of pulverized slate, graphite flakes, and the dry, musk-scented dust of sheds. 

The floor was once stepped concrete and cast iron, but time and friction have undone its edges. Over decades of constant scuffing and shifting weight, the metal has worn down to satin-smooth concavities. Boltheads have been ground into gentle, shiny domes like silver river stones. Where heavy structural beams meet the low walls, the angles have slumped into smooth, liquid-like fillets, eroded not by rain or acid, but by the endless abrasion of dry carapaces rubbing against iron.

It is an environment wholly surrendered to fauna. There is not a sprout, not a smear of lichen, not even the dried crust of moss. Yet every square inch of the floor is occupied, shifting, and noisy. 

The creatures are dry-bodied arthropods, varying from the size of a pumpkin seed to the length of a man’s forearm. They are built for a world of zero humidity: their shells are thick, chalky slate-grey and dull brass, coated in a powdery, waxy bloom that seals in their internal fluids. Some resemble flattened woodlice, their segments overlapping with the precision of plate armor; others are long-legged and spindle-thin, moving with a jerky, clockwork cadence over the backs of the slower masses below. They do not burrow—there is nothing soft enough to dig into—so they stack. In the corners and hollows where the iron frame meets the wall, they form living drifts four or five inches deep, a boiling mulch of legs, antennae, and clicking jaws.

The sound is immense and continuous. It is not the buzz of a hive or the hum of a swamp, but the heavy, rasping hiss of dry beans poured into an iron chute, doubled and looped endlessly. Millions of tiny, needle-sharp claws scrape against polished metal. Beneath this dry clatter runs a low, rhythmic vibration from overhead: an enormous eccentric cam, driven by a slow, central engine elsewhere in the works, still turns on a cold brass journal every eight seconds. Each rotation sends a dull *clunk-shudder* through the masonry, and each time it thumps, the carpet of creatures flinches in unison, producing a sharp, papery *snick* that travels down the pit like a wave.

They feed on a closed, violent economy of friction and detritus. High on the iron walls, thick, fossilized ropes of tallow and graphite lubricant still ooze microscopically from old pillow blocks, hardening into grey stalactites before they can drip. The larger, broad-headed crawlers plane this material away with hardened mandibles, rasping down to bare, gleaming steel. Lower down, the swift predators—pale, six-eyed creatures with jaws like wire-snippers—patrol the drifts, slicing through the thinner bellies of the mill-beetles. Nothing rots. When an animal dies, its fluids are drawn out by the parched air within minutes; its hollow shell is trodden upon, fractured, and gradually pulverized by millions of passing feet into the fine, grey talc that carpets the depressions in the iron.

The daylight from the grating does not shift much. Now and then, a shadow falls through the bars—the silhouette of a crane hook drifting along an overhead rail, or a heavy cable sagging as it takes tension—and the light chops into slats. The creatures do not flee the light; they are indifferent to it, having lived under its cold, functional wash for so long that their eyes, where they have them, are milky and focused only on the nearest inch of movement. 

It is an ecology that has reached its final, unyielding density: cool, iron-hard, totally dehydrated, and relentlessly alive within the belly of a tool that forgot to stop.
