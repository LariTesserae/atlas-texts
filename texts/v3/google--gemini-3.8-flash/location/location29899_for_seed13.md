The light falls from a steep, unmoving angle, perhaps two hours past the zenith, casting blue-gray shadows that do not lengthen, shorten, or soften at their edges. Because the light does not travel, the landscape exists in a perpetual afternoon—crisp, pale gold on the flat surfaces of the rock, cold slate-gray on the leeward planes.

This is a high plateau, broken open along clean, unweathered fault lines. The stone is young: fractured dolomite and dark flint with surfaces so newly exposed their fracture points are still glassy and keen. There is virtually no soil, yet vegetation thrives with a quiet, stubborn thickness. Dense cushions of wet, pale-green moss carpet the shelves and horizontal ledges, pooling moisture from the air like sponges. Low alpine scrub, tight-leafed rhododendrons with waxy crimson bell-blossoms, and dwarf rowan cling to the stone wherever a split provides a seam. Every leaf holds a single, heavy bead of condensation that never falls, reflecting the fixed white disc of the sky.

Spread across this high rift is an immense, sparsely inhabited city built by hand. 

It is an urban landscape executed entirely in the logic of dry-stacked stone, untooled timber, and rawhide. Though the technological level is that of the chisel, the wedge, and the counterweight, the scale is civic, spanning several miles of broken ridge. Colossal terraces step down into the ravines, their retaining walls built of cyclopean blocks fit together so closely that a knife blade cannot enter, yet clearly shaped by hammer-stones rather than saws. Long avenues paved with massive, rough-hewn flagstones stretch between distant stone compounds, interrupted by vast empty squares where the native limestone simply crops out through the pavement. 

The architecture does not crowd itself. There are hundreds of yards of mossy shale between one great stone hall and the next. High watch-towers—narrow, square, built of slate shingles pinned with bone—rise from isolated crags, connected to the lower terraces by dizzying spans of braided goat-hair rope and split-pine decking. Because the city is built across sheer fissures, many of the thoroughfares are aqueducts and ramps, engineered to funnel the thin, intermittent veins of meltwater that seep from the higher cliffs. 

Water here does not rush; it trickles through precisely chiseled stone gutters, pooling in cisterns lined with river clay. It is constantly damp underfoot. The air smells of wet slate, pine gum, and the cold tallow used to seal the timber joins. The temperature remains indefinitely hovering just a few degrees above freezing—a thin, biting cold that keeps the lungs awake, but never turns the water to ice.

Life is everywhere in the city, but it moves without urgency. 

Herds of pale, long-limbed mountain ibex graze upon the terrace-top mosses, their cloven hooves making sharp, hollow clicks against the masonry. They rest in the shadow of the great open colonnades, unbothered by the human inhabitants who pass them carrying split-ash baskets or bundles of cured reed. In the sheer faces of the unweathered cliffs that flank the city, thousands of swallow-like birds nest in holes pecked directly into the softer veins of chalk. Their flight is astonishingly silent; they wheel in their hundreds through the gaps between the towers, diving down into the ravines to snatch midges that hover in static, shimmering clouds above the damp gutters.

The stillness of the place is not dead, but absolute. There is no weather in motion. The wind does not gust; it exists only as a continuous, gentle draught that smells faintly of high snowfields, drifting down the ravines at a uniform pace, never lifting a single dead leaf from the flagstones. Sound travels immense distances through the thin atmosphere, yet it arrives drained of clatter—the distant thud of a wooden mallet against an oak peg sounds muted, like a heartbeat heard through wool. A goat’s bleat carries for a mile across an empty amphitheater and disappears into the clefts without an echo.

Because the erosion has not yet had time to round the world, everything is sharp. The edge of a stair is as straight as a plumb line; the vertical faces of the mountains look like they were split with an adze only weeks ago. The people who live here build constantly, but their buildings do not age. The timber does not rot in the cold, stable dampness; it cures into a hard, gray horn. Moss covers the north-facing walls of the megaliths, but it does not creep southward; its boundary on the stone is as neat and permanent as an ink line drawn along the edge of the light.

In the center of the largest expanse—a sunken basin surrounded by tiered stone benches that could hold thousands—a single pool of deep, black water sits level with the stone lip. It has no inlet and no outlet, yet it remains perfectly clear, reflecting the unmoving sky and the high, pale walls of the upper redoubts. Small, blind silverfish hang motionless beneath its surface, suspended in the cold water like needles in glass, waiting beside the ancient, hand-hewn steps that lead down into the dark.
