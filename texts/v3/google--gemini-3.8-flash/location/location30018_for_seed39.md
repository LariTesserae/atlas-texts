The rock has not yet learned how to crumble. Its edges are still factory-fresh, clean as freshly struck flint, sheared off in enormous, unweathered planes of black gabbro and iron-banded argillite. There are no scree fans rounded by centuries of frost-wedging, no soft apron of topsoil, no rolling shoulders. The mountain is an embryonic thrust, a vertical tear in the crust forced upward so recently in the geologic ledger that the fracture faces still hold their glossy, razor-bright margins.

It sits at the ragged edge of the troposphere, where the air is thin enough to sting the throat and numb the gums within minutes. The cold here does not bite with wind; the atmosphere is curiously still, settling at a heavy, leaden damp just above freezing. Moisture clings to everything. It does not fall as rain, but arrives as a dense, perpetual drift of steam and aerosolized water that rides the updrafts from the lowlands, condensing immediately upon contact with the frigid stone. The vertical faces weep constantly. Ribbons of cold water run down sheer, unblemished slabs, pooling in hairline fissures and dripping into the gray void below with a faint, rhythmic tick-tick-tick that carries across hundreds of yards of empty air.

The silence is deep, but it is not natural silence. Beneath the quiet splash of water and the rare crack of settling stone lies a vast, pervasive sub-audible tremor—a low-frequency vibration that travels through the marrow of the rock itself. Miles down, submerged beneath an impenetrable ocean of white-gray inversion fog, a continental industrial complex is running. Its rhythm is palpable: the dull, synchronized pulse of heavy reciprocal engines, the cyclic thrum of high-pressure cooling circuits, and the faint, high-tension sizzle of megavolt transmission corridors crossing the lower valleys. None of it is visible. The mountain shelf remains entirely untouched by hand, steel, or foundation. There is not a single bolt drilled into the rock, not a trail cut into the ledges, not a scrap of discarded wire. The uplift is completely virgin, wild and inaccessible, yet it is utterly steeped in the exhaust of human production. 

The clouds that bathe these crags are not born of oceanic weather fronts; they are the cooling-tower plumes and steam-scrubber discharge of the lowlands, pushed skyward by chimney convection. The water that slicks the dark cliffs tastes faintly of copper, lime, and ozone. 

Between the massive, vertical walls, the space is enormous and predominantly empty. Great gulfs of dim, sulfurous light open between one promontory and the next, separated by thousand-foot drops into milk-colored mist. The light rarely changes; the sun is a pale, smudged nickel disk somewhere far above the murk, casting no distinct shadows. 

Despite the cold and the raw, newly fractured terrain, life has colonized every damp cleft with frantic speed. It is not plant life that leads the way—vegetation is sparse, limited to greasy encrustations of black and yolk-yellow lichens, along with occasional tufts of fibrous, silica-stiff moss wedged into drainage cracks where mineral runoff concentrates. 

Instead, the fauna dominates the emptiness. Thousands of high-altitude cliff-swallows and soot-colored prions wheel through the chasm in silent, coordinated bands. They make use of the artificial thermals rising from the unseen heat sinks below, spiraling up through the fog to hunt the clouds of midges and cold-hardy crane flies that hatch in the condensation pools. On the broader, terraced shelves of raw stone, colonies of mountain marmots and thick-coated alpine voles move across the bare rock, darting between slab-falls. They forage along the wet seams, feeding on the insect drifts and the mineral-rich crusts left by the weeping industrial water. Higher still, on ledges no wider than a handspan, pale mountain ungulates stand motionless in the gloom, their hooves chipped and scarred by the razor-edged stone, chewing rhythmically while watching the fog churn in the abyss.

There is a strange, delicate momentum to the place. It is not decaying; it is forming. The rock is coming into being under the influence of moisture and stress, fracturing along its crystal lattices, while the ecosystem above feeds greedily upon the damp, heated breath of an invisible world running at full throttle far below. Stones occasionally dislodge—not from decay, but from internal pressure—shearing off with the dry, sharp report of a gunshot and falling into the silence, down toward the engines.
