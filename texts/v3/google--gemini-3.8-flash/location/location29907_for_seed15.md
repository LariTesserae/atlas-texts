The light does not filter so much as strike. Midday falls straight through a wide, unglazed aperture overhead, piercing five fathoms of clear, bath-warm water before hitting the floor. Because the stone below is newly quarried pale travertine, the floor behaves like an anvil for the sun, hammering the glare back upward. The chamber is entirely flooded, intensely bright, and small—scarcely fourteen paces across from one squared wall to the other.

Every surface is human work. There is no riverbed gravel, no mud bank, no natural fault. The walls are sheer vertical planes of massive ashlar blocks, stacked dry or bedded in thin, hard ribbons of lime mortar that haven't yet had the decades needed to leach or dissolve. The edges of the stones are clean, distinct, and sharply right-angled; along their faces, the parallel grooves of hand-swung picks remain crisp, completely unsoftened by current. It is an enclosed cistern or a sunken plunge-well, built deep beneath an open terrace, but filled to its brim so that the water laps only an inch below the ceiling arches.

The volume is filled with sound. A broad stone flume, wedged into the upper masonry, empties a heavy, uninterrupted torrent into the pool. It drops from a height of several feet into the standing water, churning up an enormous column of white froth and air bells that drives down toward the bottom before ballooning out into the turquoise stillness. The noise is immense, confined by the stone: a hollow, continuous roar that sets the water vibrating against the skin like a low drumbeat, echoing off the ceiling’s flat slabs and bouncing endlessly between the wet walls.

The water is remarkably warm, carrying the mineral heat of an underground spring or a terrace baked for days by uninterrupted sun. This heat keeps the circulation violent. Where the cool-air surface meets the warm body of the pool, thin sheets of convection shimmer, bending the clean geometric lines of the stone joints into brief, undulating waves. A constant surge pushes against the perimeter, spilling evenly into narrow overflow slots chiseled along the upper tier of stone, draining away unseen into stone conduits behind the wall.

Because it has only recently been filled, the tank has no mud, no sediment, and no fish. There are no snails crawling the vertical courses, no drifting crustaceans, no larvae hanging from the surface tension. Life is represented only by the early colonizers of bright water: where the midday beam stays focused longest against the eastern wall, a velvet sheen of bright emerald algae has begun to knit itself into the fine tooth-marks of the stonecutters. A few loose strands of pondweed, snapped off from some feeder pond upstream, drift near the bottom like green threads, trapped in the lazy back-eddy behind the falling torrent, pristine and rootless.

Floating submerged in the center, the space feels both monumental and tiny. The world consists entirely of six planes of white stone, emerald-tinted water, and the roaring pillar of bubbles in the corner. When the surface calms between surges, the sky visible through the roof-well is a pure, flat square of cobalt, rimmed by the pale square lip of the coping stones. Down here, suspended in the bright heat, the water does not feel like an ocean or an intrusion; it feels like the sole intended content of a great stone box, built meticulously by hand to hold nothing else.
