The basin is no larger than a four-track rail yard, sunk thirty feet below the surrounding grade between retaining walls that have long ceased to be simply stone. 

Here, masonry and cambium have solved each other. The red sandstone ashlar is held in the slow, vise-like webbing of black-barked heartwood—trees that do not grow upward toward the daylight, but sideways, horizontally planar, behaving as buttresses, joists, and lintels. Where a timber roof once covered this cut, there is now an overarching canopy of dense, braided lignum that filters the sky into clean, steady gray light, neither dim nor glaring. It has the tall, calm interior air of a cool stone nave, though overhead the ribs are alive and weeping a steady, cold dew.

The scale is modest—an enclosed precinct, maybe two hundred yards from the mouth of the bricked tunnel to the dead-end slip where three locomotive-scale gantries sit immobile. Yet within these boundaries, there is a distinct and deliberate roominess. The ground is not an impassable thicket; it is wide, level, and bare in the way a dry-dock is bare. The floor consists of pale, calcified root-plates smoothed by centuries of mineral-laden runoff, interrupted by broad expanses of coarse, river-washed slag. You can walk forty paces in any direction without touching an obstacle, your boots striking dull, solid wood or the gritty crunch of iron clinker.

Water exists here not as pools or torrents, but as an intermittent condensation regime. The air is permanently cool—around forty-five degrees—and whenever the external barometric pressure shifts, the entire yard breathes. The iron members—surviving crane arms, hollow structural columns, the fat flywheels of stationary steam siphons—drop below the dew point, and their surfaces break into a fine sweat. Droplets gather along the edges of oxidized bronze fittings, bead on the green-black bark of the bracing-trees, and fall with single, clean *taps* into stone gutters carved alongside the rails. 

These gutters are dry for an hour, then trickle with a pencil-thick stream of ice-cold runoff, then dry again as the yard exhales.

Despite the stillness, the place is not dead; it is running. Its activity is mechanical, biological, and continuous, carried out at the pace of a hydraulic accumulator. Through the center of the yard runs a system of double-action rocker beams, cast from heavy slag-iron, each fifty feet long. They do not rest on stone footings, but are cradled directly within the bifurcated trunks of ancient, dwarf-formed oaks whose wood has grown dense enough to withstand forty tons of bearing weight without splitting. 

Every several minutes, driven by a deep subsurface hydrostatic head that fills subterranean bladders of living root-tissue, one of the massive beams tilts. The movement is nearly soundless: the faint groan of compressed sapwood, the wet slide of an oil-greased iron pin, and the long, trailing sigh of displaced air from an exhaust pipe wrapped in dried lichen. A brass valve snaps open with a sharp, small *chink*, venting a ring of cool, odorless white vapor, and the beam settles onto its lower stroke for another nine minutes. 

The machines and the plants have reached an erosion equilibrium. There is almost no loose rust; the iron has developed the deep, purple-black patina of weapons kept in dry oil, protected from deep corrosion by the bitter tannins exuded by the trees that grip them. Where copper pipes cross the floor, the wood has parted around them cleanly, sealing them in living grommets that prevent vibration. The original purpose of the complex—likely a municipal water lift or a sorting depot for low-temperature industrial distillates—has been worn down by time into a self-perpetuating metabolism. The pumps lift water to keep the heartwood saturated; the wood swells to trip the counterweights; the weights drive the pumps.

Fauna is nearly absent, as the yard offers little to eat. Once an hour, perhaps, a flat-bodied river beetle, slate-gray and as broad as a saucer, will drag itself out of the slit between a steel rail and its wooden sleeper, crawl deliberately across the open slag to a dripping valve, press its head against the cold condensation, and remain there motionless for hours. Nothing hunts it. Nothing nests in the overhead ribs; the wood is too hard to bore into, the clearance too swept by the steady draft that pulls from the tunnel mouth toward the open sky above the slip.

Walking here, you are aware of the ceiling—the intricate, repetitive joinery of black wood meeting gray iron—and the wide, cool emptiness beneath your feet. There is no decay, no smell of rot; the air carries only the clean, mineral bite of wet steel, the faint peppery edge of oak bark, and the faint, recurring draft of cold steam venting into the silence. A rocker beam shifts. A drip strikes zinc. Then the floor returns to its wide, level waiting.
