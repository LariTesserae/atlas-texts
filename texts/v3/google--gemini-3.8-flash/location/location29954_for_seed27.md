There is no moisture here to soften the edges of things. If you were to bring an apple into this altitude, the air would not rot it; it would pull the cellular water through the skin in a frantic, hissing vapor until only a paper-thin husk of dry sugars remained, rattling against the floor. 

Yet everything beneath the sky is alive. The entire terrain is plant matter—not soil, not stone, but an ocean-deep substrate of dead-hard, oil-tempered lignum that has spent epochs calcifying into something denser than basalt. It forms a high, windswept plateau suspended leagues above whatever planetary mantle lies below. The ground is a tectonic tangle of roots the width of highways, worn entirely flat across geological time by the scouring of fine, diamond-hard particulate winds. There are no hills, only the ancient, smoothed knuckles of world-spanning boughs, planed down into broad, polished terraces of petrified ebony and iron-veined pith.

The cold here is dry and pristine, hovering just below freezing, but without water there is neither frost nor ice. The chill exists only as a stiffening agent, keeping the great vegetal arches rigid against the vacuum-clear sky.

Above these flattened boles, the landscape is surprisingly empty, dominated by negative space. Great distances stretch between the features: a kilometer of bare, gray-black grain, polished to a dull waxen sheen, before the eye catches the next spire. The light is a thin, perpetual dusk, the sun a pale, copper coin struggling through an upper atmosphere that has been dyed dusky violet by floating spore-dust and volatile hydrocarbons. 

Where the vegetation rises into the air, it does not grow as green leaves, but as dry, resonant instruments. Massive hollow pillars—resembling petrified bamboo or fluted structural bone, hundreds of meters in diameter—stand spaced across the terraces like the columns of a ruined forum. They do not drink water; they circulate a dense, non-aqueous coolant—a clear, heavy siloxane that tastes of nothing and leaves no dampness behind. 

Humanity, or whatever inherited its desire for order, has taken these dead and living pipes and inhabited them. High-tier technology has been grafted into the dry cambium with terrifying subtlety. Cities are slung between the ironwood flutes, not resting on them, but anchored by gossamer fields of repulsive gravity that cause tiers of habitation to hover inches off the wood, casting no weight upon the ancient fibers. These settlements are clusters of unreflective, dark-gold polyhedra and suspended transit threads that hum with femtoscale computational pulses. From a distance, they look like organized fungal blights clinging to a cedar fence, but up close they are impossibly clean, silent geometries where matter is manipulated at the atomic grain. Monorails of compressed light flicker between the trunks, vanishing into boreholes carved into the heartwood millions of years ago.

Despite the sparse placement of the cities, the air is violently loud. 

The fluted boles are open to the sky at their upper rims, miles above, and they catch the thin, fast wind of the high altitudes, funneling it down through their internal chambers. The plateau acts as an enormous, tectonic pipe organ. It does not play a tune; it generates a sustained, bass-heavy acoustic pressure—a deep, wood-grain roar that vibrates through the soles of the feet and rattles the jaw. Mingled with this planetary drone is the frantic, high-register clicking of the fauna.

The creatures here are dry-chemists. Without water to act as a solvent, their biology relies on sulfurous lipids and electrical charges stored in laminated scales. They scuttle across the ebony floor in endless varieties: segmented mantis-like forms three meters tall, whose carapaces are lacquered with the same dry resin that coats the trees; tiny, mirror-sided arachnids that string conductive webs of raw carbon across the gullies to harvest electrostatic currents from the wind; and broad, flat scavengers that slide across the polished wood on cushions of repelling charge, grazing on the dry, powdery flakes sheared from the bark. Overhead, things shaped like thin kites of cured leather circle on the thermals, hunting the mantids with blindingly fast, needle-pointed lances that discharge kilovolts upon impact.

Movement is constant, dry, and sharp. There is no rot here, no decay as we understand it, only mechanical wear and electromagnetic combustion. When a branch finally shears off from the weight of eons, it does not fall to create soil; it shatters into dry, needle-sharp splinters of raw carbon fiber that skitter across the plateau like dry reeds before being swallowed by the industrial intake vents of the hovering cities.

The cities drink the dry dust. They refine the lignin into structural diamond and breathe out an atmosphere that smells exclusively of ground pepper, cold cedar, and the clean, stinging trace of ozone. Stand long enough on the smoothed rim of a root-canyon, watching the dim coppery light fade into total starlight, and the sound of the wind through the deadwood will convince you that the ground itself is thinking—slowly, loudly, and without a single drop of rain.
