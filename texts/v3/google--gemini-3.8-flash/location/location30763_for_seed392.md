The floor has no bottom, but it has seams. 

Across a space the width of an inland sea, the ground is an uninterrupted expanse of hand-pressed terracotta tiles and thick, squared timbers of pale heartwood, notched together with dry wooden pegs. There is no bedrock beneath it, nor gravel, nor silt. If a tile were pried up, there would only be another tile, wet and fitted to its neighbor with a pale, limey paste that smells faintly of crushed shells and river mud. But nothing is loose enough to pry. Every edge is razor-clean; the tool marks—the rhythmic scallops of an adze, the broad thumb-swipes that smoothed the clay before firing—are as sharp as if the hands that pressed them had drawn back only seconds ago. Yet the air holds no dust from the cutting, and the sawdust has already been washed away, or never existed.

It is violently, blindingly bright. The light does not fall from a sun; there is no disc overhead, only a uniform, white-gold sky that fills every angle of vision equally. Because the light comes from everywhere at once, there are no shadows. A broad, fat-leaved plant does not shade the tiles beneath it; the underside of its leaves glows with the same flat, white luminescence as the wet clay below. Without shadows to give relief, distances become strange—a low parapet of fired brick four miles off seems to press against the eyelash, while a basin three feet away looks vast and vacant.

Heat presses down like a wet cloth laid over the mouth. It is the steady, unmoving heat of a bathhouse just after the fires have been stoked, hovering at an exact, unvarying fever-pitch. 

Water covers almost everything, but it does not flow. It rests in a skin three millimeters deep over the tiles, held in place by low, thumb-molded ridges of mortar that divide the world into millions of shallow, rectangular pans. In these pans, the water is perfectly still. It does not ripple, even when an animal steps through it. The surface breaks around an ankle, parts cleanly without a splash, and the meniscus closes behind the foot with the instantaneous, glass-like snap of warm oil. There is no current, no drainage, and no evaporation. Droplets that have beaded on the vertical faces of the bricks hang there indefinitely, swollen to the very brink of falling, never losing their volume to the dry air and never dropping.

Out of the joints—out of the hairline seams where timber meets baked earth—the plants have forced their way. They are monstrously lush, but they are sparse, standing isolated across the great tiled shelf like solitary pillars. 

There are ferns whose frills are thick as boiled leather, their stems bound to the terracotta by clusters of pale, fibrous runners that weave through the joinery like hemp rope. There are squat, tuberous things with leaves the size of skiffs, patterned in livid scarlets and pale greens, utterly rigid in the still air. None of them are wilting, none are yellowing, and none bear dead leaves at their base. Growth here seems to have reached a terminal fullness: every leaf is distended with hot water, saturated to its absolute limit, sweating microscopic beads of moisture that catch the omnidirectional glare and turn the plants into solid shapes of white, blinding crystal. 

Between these solitary green eruptions lie vast, empty squares of wet brick. A quarter of a mile of empty, shining tile; then three identical, wax-skinned shrubs rising from a pegged joint; then another quarter-mile of pale, adze-hewn wood submerged in clear, hot water.

The creatures that inhabit this expanse are everywhere, though they do not herd or gather. They are scattered at even intervals, like trees in an orchard. 

Large, soft-bodied grazers—beasts shaped like low-slung tapirs, with hides of pebbled, hairless rind the color of river clay—wade through the shallow pans. Their hooves are wide, wooden pads, grown directly from the bone, which strike the baked mud with dull, muffled thuds. They feed without urgency, stripping the thick, wet rind from the stems of the solitary ferns. They do not blink. Their eyes are flat, yellow lenses that take in the shadowless world without flinching. 

Above them, hovering at chest-height over the water, are swarms of beetles as broad as saucers. Their carapaces are glazed like primitive pottery—rough-edged, slightly uneven, bearing the faint, burnt-orange swirl of woodsmoke from some forgotten kiln. They do not flap their wings; they hang stationary in the thick, humid air, suspended by some quiet failure of weight, rotating slowly on their axes once every few minutes to present a different side of their lacquered shells to the sky.

The silence is enormous, almost physical. It is not the silence of an empty desert, but the muffled quiet of a room hung with heavy wet wool. The tapir-things move without breath sounds; their nostrils do not flare or wheeze. When a beetle shifts in the air, there is no hum of wings, only a faint, oily displace of heat. The only sound is the rhythmic, minute *tick* of water drops that do not fall, a microscopic tension held across miles of wet stone, and the dull, wooden *clop* of a distant beast shifting its weight from one square yard of joinery to the next.

Further out, near the vanishing point where the horizontal white plains meet the vertical white sky, the geometry begins to tilt without rising. 

A stretch of timber floorboards, eighty yards across, lies at what should be a thirty-degree incline, yet the skin of water upon it remains perfectly level with its own surface, neither running down the slope nor spilling over the edge. It clings to the wood as if the idea of downward had been decided by the carpenter who laid the planks, rather than the world beneath them. Beyond that tilted plane, the brickwork forms low, roofless chambers—walls four feet high, enclosing nothing, with doorways that open onto identical expanses of wet red clay. 

The masonry here is slightly different: the bricks are larger, the color of raw pork, bonded with thick, gray slashes of mortar that still show the deep impressions of human fingers. There are no tools lying about, no discarded mallets or scrapers of copper or stone, but the presence of the making is absolute. The world has not been abandoned; it has simply been left in the state of having just been finished. 

The heat does not build. The glare does not fade. Two miles away, a great, pale-skinned beast lifts its head from a trough of still water, its jaws dripping clear drops that rejoin the film below without making a sound. It stands motionless for an hour, or three days, its hide steaming faintly against the flat, white ceiling of the world, pinned inside an afternoon that has no way to end.
