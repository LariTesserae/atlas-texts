At this altitude, the air has the thin, iron-bitten chill of a cellar opened after an age, yet the peaks themselves have long forgotten their sharpness. They are not the violent needles of young ranges, but the melted, heavy shoulders of an ancient crust—domes and scoops of pale, felspathic granite, ground down by two billion years of wind and ice until they resemble worn teeth or the humped backs of sleeping cattle, sitting far above the rest of the world.

There is no rain to speak of, yet nothing here is dry. A perpetual, low-slung ceiling of slate-grey cloud brushes the rock, moving in an unbroken, silent trot from west to east. It leaves its fleece on everything. The moisture is gathered out of the wind: it beads on the oily needles of low, creeping junipers, coats the shale in a greasy film, and trickles down vertical grooves worn into the stone by millennia of slow, cold dripping.

The vegetation is dense, stunted, and swollen with this caught breath. It fills every gully and shelf, competing for the dim, tea-colored daylight that leaks through the cloud deck. Giant, pale liverworts, thick as boot-leather, carpet the rock faces. Dwarf willows with trunks no thicker than a thumb twist horizontally across the shelves, their bark bearded with black, hair-like lichens that weep continuously into stone troughs. Beneath the brush, the soil is a deep, spongy mattress of peat that never freezes solid, kept just above the frost-point by the slow, subterranean breathing of the plateau’s deeper strata.

Wedged into the scoop of one massive, worn amphitheater lies the settlement. It does not look planned so much as accumulated, like drift material caught in a bend of a weir.

The architecture is heavy, industrial, and distinctly tiered, pressed tight against the curving granite walls to stay out of the prevailing slipstream. Buildings of dark river-cobble and tarred timber are stacked five and six layers high, connected by wet wooden ramps, iron ladders, and web-works of suspended cables that disappear into the mist above. The density is claustrophobic; balconies overhang narrow passages paved with damp zinc sheets that rattle underfoot. Between the houses, huge mechanical drums—atmospheric condensers built of riveted sheet-iron—revolve at the speed of a clock’s minute hand, driven by counterweights and the cold draft funneling through the clefts. They are coated in coarse coconut-husk fibers that comb the moisture from the fog, squeezing it out between grooved rollers with a rhythmic, wet *ch-clack* that echoes across the hollow.

The technology here is heavy, greasy, and soot-bound. It is built to endure the pervasive rot of the damp without ceasing. Steam pipes, heavily lagged with bitumen-soaked hemp, crawl over the rooftops like swollen veins, hissing white plumes into the cold gloom where they instantly vanish into the grey overhead. Small, stationary engines putter in sunken cellars, pumping gathered brine and mineral runoff into battery-houses where lead-plated vats hum with a quiet, dull vibration. 

Light is scarce and precious. The sun is only ever a luminous bruise in the mist, appearing for an hour at midday before the cloud thickens again. The settlement works by the glow of dim carbon-filament lamps encased in thick, wire-caged glass, their amber halos illuminating the damp mist for only a few paces. 

Despite the sheer mass of human construction and the hum of the pumps, it is a strangely quiet place. The altitude and the vast pelts of moss swallow sharp noises. When an iron winch unspools half a mile away, it sounds like someone drawing a finger across a wet pane of glass. A dropped spanner produces only a brief, leaden *thud* against the peat. 

Life is active, but its movement is mechanical and hydrological rather than animal. Fauna is nearly absent; there are no sheep on the slopes, no crows in the gables. Occasionally, an alpine swift—pale-bellied and wide-winged—cuts silently through an alley between two tenement roofs, gone before its shape can be fixed, or a blind, soft-shelled crustacean is dredged from the deep condensation cisterns, its translucent legs twitching against the iron grate. 

Instead, the dynamics belong to the machine-weather cycle. Every three hours, a valve opens somewhere in the upper terraces, and an icy flush of distilled water races down wooden flumes through the heart of the lower district, driving bucket-wheels and washing the slate grit from the tracks. The air shifts constantly: cold downdrafts smell of high stone and clean snow, followed immediately by warm, greasy updrafts smelling of engine tallow, damp wool, and the faint, mushroom-like breath of the peat bogs below the foundations. 

It is an environment suspended between the geological end of things and an obstinate, grinding continuity. The mountain has already been worn down to its final, stubborn core, yet the people within it have built an engine that runs purely on the sky dragging itself across the rock.
