There is no soil here that was not carried in a hopper or made from the rot of dropped leaves on corrugated iron. The bedrock is not granite; it is a five-mile-deep lattice of box-girders, cross-braced stanchions, and riveted sheet-steel, rising so high into the atmosphere that the ambient air should be freezing and dead. 

Instead, it is warm. The entire mountain-scale mass breathes heat. Miles below the catwalks and terraced flumes, immense boilers and slow-churning dynamos run on some deep, forgotten cycle, radiating a steady, feverish warmth up through the shafts. The heat meets the cold sky, and the superstructure sweats. A persistent, lukewarm dampness clings to every surface, condensing on the underside of crane-arms and dripping in heavy, rhythmic beads into the foliage below.

It is violently bright. At this altitude, the sun is unobstructed by haze, hammering down with a high-glare intensity that bleaches paint down to pink primer and makes the puddles on the steel decks shine like mirrors. The light cuts through the steam that drifts from the ventilation stacks in tall, clean columns, illuminating clouds of pollen and rust-dust.

The green has colonized everything, but it has not broken it. The relationship between the iron and the growth is old enough to have settled into a thick, functional truce. Broadleaf creepers, swollen with moisture, wrap tightly around structural uprights, their tendrils finding purchase in the pitted hollows where rivets have corroded into black thumbs of iron oxide. Vast hanging gardens of tough, fibrous ferns droop over the edges of former train decks and gantry tracks, their roots forming dense, spongy mats that hold gallons of lukewarm condensation. Orchids with thick, rubbery leaves grow directly from the grease-traps of long-seized winches, nourished by the mineral-rich rinse of industrial runoff and decaying moss.

Looking outward, the scale is regional, stepped like a terraced valley carved from pig iron. Great slopes of plate-metal descend in staggered shelves the size of towns, each shelf bordered by deep gutters and safety balustrades now choked with wild ginger and trumpet vines. Below these shelves, the drop is sheer—a drop of thousands of feet where the wind howls against open cross-braces, until another complex of decks bulges outward into the sunlight, dark red and emerald green against the pale blue void.

The sounds are heavy, layered, and loud. The machine itself produces an oceanic, sub-audible thrum—a low vibration that rattles the handrails and travels up through the soles of your boots, punctuated every few minutes by the deep, hollow clank of a massive balance-pipe venting pressure somewhere in the gut. But atop that mechanical basso is the deafening chatter of life: thousands of birds with oily, dark plumage nest in the conduit networks, screaming in harsh, territorial squawks as they dart along the sunlit spans. Fat skinks, pale yellow and patterned like grease stains, sun themselves on the warm tops of junction boxes, skittering into pipe-mouths when the hawks swoop past. The drone of insects—heavy-bodied beetles that bore into the wet insulation of old wire-runs and clouds of gnats that breed in the condensation pools—rises to a dull, metallic roar in the heat of midday.

Water moves constantly, but slowly. There are no rivers, only the flumes: zinc-lined channels three yards wide, built to carry away boiler discharge or catch high-altitude rain, along which dark, clear water creeps at a walking pace. Watercress and thick green algae trail like hair in the flow, swaying over submerged rivets. In places, a flume has rusted through, and a fine, warm spray billows out over the void, catching the hard sun to make fleeting rainbows that vanish as the wind shifts.

Nothing is fast here. The machinery turns on geological clocks—a counterweight the size of a church slips downward by half an inch every week, groaning through greased sheaves; a cooling shutter fifty paces long groans as it expands against its mounts in the noon glare, then contracts with a single, ringing *tonk* as the shadows stretch. The plants grow quickly in the warm steam, but they are constantly cropped, weighed down, and swallowed by their own accumulated mulch, rotting into the gutters to form a thick, black peat that the bird-flocks dig through for grubs.

The air smells intensely of wet zinc, hot gear oil, and the sweet, slightly rotting musk of saturated compost. It is an entirely manufactured ecosystem that has forgotten its manufacturers, functioning now with the settled, unhurried confidence of an ancient marsh that just happens to be suspended two miles in the sky on stilts of blackened iron.
