The horizon does not bend down; it dissolves into an immense, flat glare. 

This is the floor of a hollow shell, or the sun-facing terrace of a world-sized manifold whose original geometry has long since surrendered to entropy. It is entirely artificial, yet it has existed for so long that the concept of "construction" feels as prehistoric as the formation of granite. Beneath the feet there is no soil, no bedrock, no mantle—only infinite compressions of pale, vitrified ceramics, dense weave-metals, and crystalline substrates worn down by eons of thermal tides into rolling, low-relief swells. The elevation varies by mere dozens of meters across hundreds of leagues, shallow steps and sunken runways that have been rubbed smooth until they resemble petrified dunes or the dry bed of a light-bleached sea.

The light is everywhere and nearly absolute, pouring down not from an isolated orb but from a vast, radiant diaphragm spanning the upper atmosphere. It is a dry, white-gold illumination, so saturated that cast shadows do not sharpen into blackness; they are simply diluted to faint lilac smudges. With this light comes a steady, enveloping warmth—the dry, motionless heat of baked porcelain or an idling transformer. There is no moisture in this air. What humidity exists has been reclaimed by microscopic intake vents flush with the pavement, leaving an atmosphere so chemically inert and dessicated that sweat would instantly sublimate into gas, leaving behind only salt crystals.

Despite the desolation, the landscape is not empty; it is overwhelmingly dense. Every square meter of the floor is a packed, multi-layered palimpsest of micro-architecture. Tiny hexagonal conduits, flush relay-nodes, braided carbon filaments, and nested busways lie tightly packed, shoulder to shoulder, sealed beneath a glassy glaze that time and ambient friction have polished to a soft, satin finish. It is impossible to place a foot without stepping upon millions of dead or idling circuits. In places where the top laminate has worn completely through, older strata are exposed: intricate, micrometer-scale honeycombs of copper-rose and dull titanium, ground flat by centuries of sweeping dust.

The activity here is relentless, though it moves at its own rhythm. Because the scale is continental, the terrace manufactures its own weather out of dry forces. Every few hours, a shimmering wave—a dry front of static potential—sweeps across the flats. It produces no rain, but rather an electrostatic wind that lifts fine, frictionless powders of ground sapphire and silica, moving them in vast, ghostlike drifts across the pavement. In the wake of these static fronts, the floor re-routes its dormant charge. Entire plains of ceramic tiles softly pulse from ivory to pale amber, releasing stored heat, their microscopic relief expanding and contracting with an almost imperceptible collective sigh.

Vegetation here does not grow from cellular division; it crystallizes along lines of field leakage. In the hairline fractures where two vast tectonic plates of the world-floor meet, sparse growths take root. They look like pale, brittle broomcorn or tufts of wire-thin fiber optics, standing knee-high and motionless in the dry heat. These stems are silica-sheathed, drawing trace elements of vaporized metals from the seams below, their translucent tips glowing faintly as they bleed static back into the radiant sky. They are stiff, chime-dry to the touch, and grow so slowly that a cluster may remain unchanged for a thousand years.

Fauna is exceedingly scarce, reduced to solitary, specialized entities that are neither entirely animal nor purely machine. Far out across the glaring swells, one might catch the motion of a *skimmer*—a low, wide mantle of segmented quartz, two meters across and thin as a leaf, gliding soundlessly half an inch above the ground on magnetic repulsion. It grazes upon the static charge of the floor, hoovering up the sapphire dust to replenish its shell, leaving behind a faint, polished wake that the next dry storm will erase. When two such creatures cross paths, there is no contest or display; they adjust their trajectories by fractions of a degree, sliding past each other in absolute, indifferent silence.

The dominant sensation of the place is this profound quiet. There is no foliage to rustle, no water to lap or crash, no thunder in the electrostatic squalls. The wind is a friction of dry molecules against ceramic plains, moving with a faint, velvet hiss that sits right at the threshold of human hearing. Deeper down, if one presses an ear to the warm, smooth laminate of the floor, there is a low-frequency drift—a cosmic, sub-audible thrum, like the sound of an entire continent dreaming under tension, running calculations whose purpose evaporated ten million years ago, but whose machinery is too perfectly balanced to ever stop.
