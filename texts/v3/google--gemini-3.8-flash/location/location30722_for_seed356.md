There is no light by which to see it, but it has the weight of an engine room spanning an ocean basin. 

The heat is the first thing that registers: a dull, steady warmth, like the inside of a closed fist or the core of a freshly turned compost heap that has achieved an absolute, immovable thermal balance. It does not fluctuate. There are no seasons, no diurnal shifts, no drafts from some distant, cooler shore. The air is saturated to the point of suspension; water does not rain down so much as it continuously gathers upon every surface, thickening until it slumps downward in heavy, silent ribbons.

This entire continental expanse is made of plant, yet nothing here resembles a forest. It is plant as sheer, relentless infrastructure. It is a single, continuous, vegetative mass that has organized itself into the logic of heavy industry without ever having been designed or built. 

Underfoot—if one could stand upon it—there is no soil, no sediment, no stone. The floor is an endless, interwoven pavement of lignified plates, tight as boiler iron and sharp-edged as freshly stamped sheet metal. Because the system is young, these plates have not been rounded by decay or silted over by dust. Their seams are crisp, hair-thin, and slightly raised, exuding a tacky, clear pitch that smells faintly of boiled linseed and vinegar. Beneath these plates, one can feel the thrum of immense hydraulic tensions: sap lines the diameter of aqueducts, running under pressures that would burst lead piping, coursing through fibrous conduits that do not pulsate, but simply strain with a constant, silent torque.

Rising from this floor are vertical columns—trunks that do not taper. They ascend hundreds of meters into the blackness, perfectly parallel, grouped in vast, orderly colonnades that mimic the manifold layouts of petroleum refineries or heat-exchange banks. Their surfaces are not rough bark, but ribbed, cold-cured cellulose that has hardened into an enamel-like rigidity. Between them run intricate cat’s-cradles of pressurized tendrils, stretched so taut they would ring like steel wire if struck, though nothing strikes them. They exist in total stillness.

There is no fauna. Not a mite, not a nematode, not a fungal spore drifting on an updraft. The ecosystem is closed, aseptic, and entirely autogenous. Lacking animals to pollinate, feed, or burrow, the vegetation has abandoned the soft vulnerabilities of petals, fruit, and open foliage. Instead, the canopy above—felt only as an oppressive ceiling that catches the rising steam—consists of interlocking, hexagonal baffles made of dense, oily cork. These baffles function as massive condensers. The warm moisture that evaporates from the lower vascular runs hits the underside of the canopy, trickles into channeled veins, and is drawn back down through hollow, vertical taproots by simple capillary pull and hydrostatic siphon, looping endlessly without losing a single drop.

The "industrial" nature of the place is not a metaphor overlaid onto trees; it is the physiological reality of the tissue itself. The plants here do not run on sunlight—in this absolute darkness, photosynthesis is impossible. Instead, the flora is galvanic and chemosynthetic. The roots plunge miles downward into deep tectonic lyes, drawing up mineral salts and heavy metals, passing them through tiered arrays of biological voltaic piles. Certain thick, horizontal runners are wrapped in spiraled sheaths of conductive, copper-rich pith, generating continuous, low-amperage electromagnetic fields that keep the vascular fluids moving without the need for mechanical hearts. 

The atmosphere carries a sharp, dry tang of ozone despite the heavy wetness—the byproduct of billions of minute electrolytic reactions occurring along the surfaces of broad, leaf-like plates that hang in the dark like cooling fins. 

The scale is staggering. If an observer were dropped into it, they could walk for weeks along these endless corridors of extruded bark and vascular conduit without encountering an end, an opening, or a change in pattern. It creates its own internal micro-meteorology: not winds, but vast, continent-wide rolls of thermal displacement that move at an imperceptible crawl through the flues formed by the column groves. 

Everything is unnervingly quiet. It does not have the rustling hush of an ordinary woodland. There is no wind to stir leaves, and the foliage itself is too rigid to flutter. The sounds that do exist are low, heavy, and sub-audible: the faint, vitreous *tink* of a silica-heavy shoot expanding by a micron against a sister limb; the smooth, pressurized *hiss* of fluid moving through microscopic sieve-tubes at high velocity; the occasional wet slap of an accumulated gallon of water falling from a condenser baffle onto the hard, resinous apron below. 

Because the landscape is geologically fresh, there is no rot. No leaf litter decomposes on the ground; old tissue is not discarded, but systematically stripped of its mineral content, desiccated, and calcified in place to serve as structural bracing for the next tier of vascular pipes. The edges of every node, every joint where a branch bifurcates into twin manifolds, are as clean and unmarred as fresh millwork. 

In the pitch-black, miles beneath the upper baffles, the system exists in a state of suspended production. It is manufacturing nothing but its own volume, maintaining its internal pressures, sweating its clear dielectric fluids, and holding a warm, motionless vigil over a world that has no use for eyes, no memory of weather, and no room for anything that breathes.
