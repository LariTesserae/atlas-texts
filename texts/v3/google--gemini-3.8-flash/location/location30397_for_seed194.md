It exists inside a subterranean gall, a hollow burl the size of an amphiteater, sealed miles beneath the surface within the heart-timber of an organism that died when the world was young. 

There is no light here—none has ever touched it. If a hand were held an inch before the eyes, there would be no outline, no gradation, only the optic nerve straining against an uncompromising, absolute pitch. Yet the space is immediately legible through the skin and the ear. It is bone-cold, dry as ash, and enormous within its own strict boundaries: a sealed cavity carved into dead cambium, suspended like an air bubble trapped in fossil resin.

The floor, the ceiling, and the swooping, bowl-shaped walls are entirely composed of seasoned grain. There is no stone, no soil, no clay. The substrate is pure, ancient lignum, cured across hundreds of thousands of years until every trace of sap has sublimated into the void, leaving behind a fibrous matrix as dense as iron and brittle as slate. Without a single drop of water to rot it, the wood has not decayed; it has calcified through desiccation. The air tastes of dry starch, antique cedar, and crushed tannins, with an astringency so intense it dries the throat on a half-breath. 

Though the chamber itself is a hidden pocket—a solitary, secret room hollowed from the deep wood—its interior volume is largely void. It is an immense, hollow shell, empty across its central expanse. But clinging to the parabolic curves of its perimeter, rising tier upon tier like the scaffolding of an abandoned drydock, is an entire city.

It is an urban lattice built without eyes, constructed entirely through grain-sense and touch. Thousands of rectilinear cells, catwalks, and cantilevers wrap the inward slope of the cavity. These dwellings were not grown; they were subtracted. The builders used wedges of dense ironwood and heavy flint adzes to cleave the growth rings into streets. The chisel marks are everywhere, stiff and chalky beneath the fingers, each bite of the blade preserved with the sharpness it had the hour it was struck, because nothing here weathers. The ramps follow the spiraling annual rings of the mother-wood; the doorways are narrow vertical slits, aligned along the radial rays where the timber naturally parts under tension.

Suspended across the void are high, gossamer-thin bridges made of braided grass and twisted bark-fiber, thick as a wrist and stiffened by age into rigid, arching bones. They span hundreds of feet of black air, connecting the upper rims of the city to the lower declivities. Beneath them, the central drop plunges into a concave floor polished smooth by the passage of vanished bare feet, worn down through millennia of friction until the timber rings look like contour lines on a map, undulating under the palm.

The place is not silent; it is deafening. 

Because the chamber is dry, hollow, and braced by thousands of tightly fitted joints of seasoned wood, it acts as an immense resonant soundbox—a blind violin body buried in the deep earth. Every tectonic shiver, every infinitesimal settling of the continent pressing on the outer shell of the root, is captured, multiplied, and cast into the void as a sharp, percussive crack or a booming, organ-like drone. When a fiber shears three miles above, the sound arrives in the pocket like a cannon shot against a dry skin. The air vibrates continuously with low-frequency groans, the timber creaking against its own mortises with slow, agonizing torpor. Whispers do not travel; they are either absorbed instantly by the porous, splintered walls or carried along a single taut grain-line to ring loudly into a room on the far side of the cavity.

There are no creatures here. Not a moth, not an insect, not a mite. The moisture content is too low for life; the protein of the timber has long since crystallized. What remains of the inhabitants are only their deliberate, simple technologies: bins carved into burl to store dry, unsprouted seeds that never saw loam; stacks of scraped-bark pallets; stone-headed mallets left precisely where a joiner set them down before the air grew too still to breathe. 

Everything here is finished. The city reached its final, static equilibrium ages ago. The wood can dry no further without crumbling, yet it cannot crumble because there is no wind to stir the dust and no water to swell the fibers. It sits in the frozen dark, an intricate, abandoned hive of dead pulp and primitive joinery, singing loudly in the black each time the earth shifts its weight.
