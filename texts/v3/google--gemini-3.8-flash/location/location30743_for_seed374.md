The crust is brand-new. It has not had time to soften, to round out at the edges, or to gather dust; it is a regional terrace of warm, grey-white travertine and volcanic lime, rising only a few fathoms above an endless shallow sea. Water bleeds from it continuously. It does not fall from clouds—the sky is a low, heavy lid of pewter fog that traps the heat—but wells up through hair-fractures in the mineral floor, pooling in flat, scalding slicks that cool to blood-warmth as they fan out. Because the stone is fresh from the deep, nothing has weathered. The fractures are razor-sharp. The steps between terraces are crisp right-angles of crystalline calcium, stark as shattered porcelain, unrounded by centuries of rain because centuries have not yet touched them.

There is no green. Not a weed, not a blade of marsh-grass, not a spore of moss. The biology here bypasses the kingdom of plants entirely. The slick film on the stones is bacterial—a grey-brown, greasy veneer that feeds directly on sulfur and dissolved iron. 

Yet the plain is smothered in a city.

It stretches past the limits of vision into the fog: a sprawling, low-slung metropolis built without metal, without timber, without kilns. It is made of the crust itself, hacked out while still soft with heavy clubs of river-slate, and of alluvial clay hauled from the sea-trenches. The dwellings are huddled so tightly that the roofs form an unbroken, undulating upper floor, linked by thousands of shallow ramps and bone-pegged ladders. Millions of circular, dome-topped cells, each plastered by hand with thick, grey lime-wash that stays perpetually damp in the steam. Narrow alleys, little more than deep fissures cut into the travertine, run like irrigation ditches between the foundations, slick with wet run-off and the refuse of an immense, teeming population.

The air does not move. It hangs thick and body-warm, smelling of curdled river silt, wet hair, and the sharp, vinegar tang of fermenting fat.

The noise is a physical weight. It is deafening, constant, and flat, lacking any cadence or lull. It is the composite roar of hundreds of thousands of voices shouting across rooftops, combined with the tireless, dry, rhythmic clatter of stone adzes chipping at the fresh terraces to carve out new cells. Beneath that human din lies the sound of the fauna. The shallows and the roof-edges are packed with animal life that thrives in the scalding, plantless brine. Rafts of greasy, flightless diving birds, thick-necked and grey-feathered, mass in the millions along the canals, screeching with a raspy, brassy rattle that never pauses. Fat, pale river-dogs—amphibious, hairless hounds bred for their grease and hides—whine and paddle through the flooded alleys, barking in frantic echoes off the wet lime. Overhead, under the grey blanket of the fog, clouds of blind, broad-winged insects hum with a pitch like a tearing sheet, vibrating the water in the puddles.

Nothing changes here. The water wells up at the same rate every hour of the day and night; the fog neither thickens into storm nor burns away into sun. There are no seasons, because there is no vegetation to track them, no falling leaves, no sprouting shoots, no dry period to crack the mud. The mud is always wet. The lime is always setting, forever half-cured, kept pliable by the steam that sighs from the cracks underfoot. 

In the alleys, people move in dense, jostling files, their skin slick with grease against the mineral spray. They carry bundles of salted meat, wicker traps full of crawling river-scorpions, and baskets of wet, grey clay scooped from the embryonic shores. They wear clothes beaten from the leathery skins of bottom-feeders and cloaks made of knotted bird-quills. They do not look up, because the sky offers nothing to look at—only the dull, pearl-colored dusk that illuminates the city from all directions at once, casting almost no shadows. 

Every surface is slick. Water condenses on the brows of the builders, drips from the eaves of the mud domes, runs in silent sheets down the unweathered steps of the terraces, and gathers in the immense cisterns where whole communities harvest the fat, milky blind-eels that breed in the deep cracks. The work of building is endless, not because the old structures fall, but because the mineral shelf keeps pushing upward out of the floor, swelling in slow, tectonic blisters that must be leveled, hollowed, and inhabited the moment they cool enough to touch.

It is a world born yesterday, fully populated, without a past to erode or a future to wait for: a vast, warm, deafening shelf of raw rock and wet lime, humming with an eternity of meat and water.
