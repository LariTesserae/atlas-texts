The altitude does not cool the air; it only thins it, leaving the heat sharp and suffocating, like steam trapped under glass. 

Across an expanse of miles, suspended leagues above whatever solid planet might rest below, there is only a system of monstrous, woven architecture. It is entirely alive, and yet it is unmistakably an engineering project. Nothing here grows by the casual drift of seed and soil. Vast, hollow buttresses—some the width of city avenues—arch out across chasms of pale blue air, connecting colossal polygonal pylons that thrust ten thousand feet up into the lower cloud deck. There is no earth, no stone, no brick; the substrate is compressed lignum, braided rush-flesh, and green, fibrous timber that has been guided, strained, and tensioned into vaults, stairs, cantilevered decks, and sheer vertical facades. 

Yet this city-scale framework is sparse. It does not choke the sky like a jungle. Between one monumental pier and the next lies a mile of raw void, washed by howling thermal drafts. The scale is gigantic, athletic, and empty. A single arching truss of wet, pale bamboo-flesh will spring from a faceted tower, reach three thousand feet across the emptiness toward another sheer red-pith wall, and carry nothing along its back except a roaring, open flume of hot water.

Water is the system’s blood and its ruin. It rises from the core of the pylons under immense, vegetative pressure—a hydraulic pumping so violent that the inner walls of the wooden colonnades shudder to the touch. Boiling or near-boiling, rich in dissolved silicates and vegetal gums, it sheets down the faces of the towers in glass-smooth films. It weeps from millions of deliberate pores in the fluted columns, dripping into the abyss in curtains that are immediately torn to ribbons by the high-altitude winds. Clouds form directly off the architecture: white cauldrons of condensation puff out from the balconies, roll across the empty spans, and strike the adjacent towers with sudden, localized squalls.

Because the system is young, everything is sharp. There is no rot here yet, and almost no soil. The wood is unweathered: lime greens, violent chlorotic yellows, and the bloody raw amber of newly split heartwood. The breaks are clean, splintered fractures rather than decayed slumps. Everywhere, the architecture is in a state of rapid, violent formation. Great beams of cellular grain, eight feet thick, creep out into empty space overnight, their growth tips translucent, wet, and spear-pointed, whistling as they suck moisture from the saturated air. 

Then they fail.

The turbulence is ceaseless. Without fauna—without a single beetle, worm, or bird to nest in the eaves or cushion the silence—the acoustic space is dominated entirely by mechanical stress and boiling fluid. Stems as dense as iron groan under torque, producing subterranean groans that vibrate through the ribcage. When a cantilever extends too far into the wind shear between the towers, it does not droop; the pressurized sap inside reaches a critical cavitation point, and the beam snaps with the dry, deafening crack of a field artillery battery. Thousands of tons of wet green splinters shower into the mist below, leaving behind a jagged, bleeding stump that immediately begins to sprout compensatory guide-struts, furiously rebuilding itself into the void.

To stand on one of the broad, raked terraces is to be blinded by wind-blown spray that smells of crushed ferns and boiled iron. The flooring beneath is woven from fibrous reeds as thick as ship’s hawsers, lashed into tight, springy parquets that yield slightly with every step. There are stairways that lead nowhere, spiraling around the perimeter of hexagonal ventilation shafts; there are vast, sloping aqueducts whose water runs clear and scalding beneath canopies of living thatch; there are enormous, roofless courtyards whose perimeters are defined by fluted pilasters of pale, ribbed wood, all open to the sky and entirely deserted.

The light is high, bright, and flat, scattered evenly by the mist, glinting off miles of wet timber. Below, between the bases of the pillars, there is only a floor of roiling, sulfurous fog; above, the architecture simply continues until its spires become fine green hairs dissolved against the white sky. 

It is a world being grown with frantic, structural intent—a cathedral or a machine of regional proportion, burning itself out by the very vigor of its design, perpetually tearing itself apart under the weight of its own rushing blood, and relentlessly throwing fresh, wet timber back into the gale.
