It is no larger than the bed of a heavy wagon, perhaps nine paces long and four across, sunk beneath an enormous depth of dry masonry, yet it is flooded with an intolerable, dry radiance that leaves nowhere to cast an edge. 

The light drops from five narrow vertical shafts cut through the dead weight of the overhead mass—slits no wider than a hand, polished inside until the chalk-pipe walls act as burning-lenses, delivering the full white fury of the noon sky straight into this cellar without bringing its heat. The air down here remains cellared, chilled to the temperature of well-bottom stones in late autumn, dry enough to parch the tongue in a minute’s breathing.

Everything in this space has been made. There is no bed-rock exposed, no floor of tumbled gravel, no earth. The ground is a floor of interlocking limestone dice, each roughly the size of a knuckle, fitted together without mortar so tightly that a hair cannot be pressed between them, though centuries of a steady, whistling draught have gently domed their centers and smoothed their seams. 

From this floor rise low, densely crowded pilasters of unglazed earthenware and stacked slate. They are not pillars that support a ceiling; they are baffled partitions, standing cheek-by-jowl, spaced only an inch or two apart, dividing the air into thousands of thin vertical slices. The space is completely saturated by them. To enter it would be impossible; one could only look into it or wedge a hand into its outer slots. It is an architecture built for no body to inhabit, packed to its limits with crafted density—slates ground flat, ceramic fins stamped with tiny, repetitive indentations to increase their surface area, and bundles of split reed lashed together with dried gut that have cured over generations into the hardness of bone.

Between these closely ranked partitions, where the cold draft shears continuously, a peculiar sort of growth has taken hold. It is not green. It has the pale, grey-white texture of dried parchment or horny fish-scale—crustose lichens and brittle, branching lichenoids that form stiff, paper-thin ruffs along the edges of the ceramic fins. They have no sap. If snapped, they break like dry clay, releasing a faint smell of cellar-mold and powdered chalk. They do not seem to need moisture; they drink only the chill and the constant mineral dust carried down the shafts, their rigid fronds locked into the prevailing blast so permanently that they have grown into the exact shape of the turbulence.

And the sound is immense.

It is not the sound of machinery, for there are no wheels, no pendulums, no ironwork anywhere in the vault. It is the raw, furious acoustics of the wind caught in the flutes above and compressed into these narrow fins below. The shafts act as organ pipes blown by the sky; down here, the air does not blow as a breeze—the pressure is too balanced, the exit slits too uniformly choked—but it rings. It vibrates the teeth. It is a continuous, deafening, multi-toned chord, louder than a waterfall, flattened into a single unchanging howl that never drops an octave and never rises. The ceramic fins resonate in sympathy, singing with a dry, ceramic hum that can be felt in the heels through the floor-tiles.

Because the pressure from above never relents and the openings never alter their geometry, nothing moves. The reed bundles do not sway; the lichen fronds do not tremble. They are braced by the very force of the air that feeds their sound. The glare remains absolute, washing the buff-colored slate and chalk-white lichen into a flat, depthless plane where distance is impossible to judge. 

It is an engine of pure stone and drying wind, fully finished, long settled into an unyielding equilibrium. It has worn away its rough edges long ago, ground its dust to powder, and now exists in an eternal, roaring noon, perfectly cold, perfectly full, and entirely alone with its own noise.
