At six thousand meters, the sky does not scatter light; it drops it like lead. The sun is an unblinking furnace of raw white glare, yet the air retains none of it. It remains knife-cold, thin enough to make the lungs ache with dryness, though the basin floor itself sweats.

This is a hanging notch, a fractured amphitheater scarcely three hundred paces across, pinned between sheer towers of splintered, dark schist. The rock has no rounded edges. Its faces are geometrically violent—steep, clean cleavages formed by recent geological thrusts, the fractures still unsoftened by centuries of frost. Downward, the world falls away into an oceanic drop of empty blue-violet air; upward, three needle-peaks scrape the troposphere. The scale is intimate, enclosed like a cathedral’s apse, yet the yawning drop just beyond its threshold creates a terrifying emptiness. The air inside this pocket feels strangely hollow, an echoing vacuum between steep walls.

Against all logic of elevation, the floor of the notch is choked with a strange, condensed lushness. Black and rusted-orange alpine cushions, three feet deep and densely packed as peat, coat the floor. These are not mosses, but dense colonies of miniature vascular shrubs whose leaves are covered in microscopically fine, silver-gray down that traps the melting hoarfrost. Every morning, a sea of cloud rises from the lowlands, washes across the ledge, and is instantly torn apart by the thin wind, leaving behind a slick, shimmering film of water. By midday, under the ferocious brightness of the overhead sun, the rock faces warm just enough to weep fine ribbons of water into small, black-bottomed pools before it freezes again in the shadows. The moisture is local, brief, and constantly cycling between vapor, ice, and trickle.

Despite the near-total silence—broken only by the hiss of wind against bare granite edges and the soft, dry *snip* of rock expanding in the light—the pocket seethes with movement. 

Fauna thrives in the hyper-oxygenated micro-layer hovering over the moss mats. High-altitude silk-runners—spindle-legged creatures the size of field mice, clad in thick coats of greasy, down-like wool—dart between the crevices with uncanny swiftness. They have pale, lidless eyes shielded by crystalline nictitating membranes to endure the blinding ultraviolet light. Above them, broad-winged alpine moths, soot-black to absorb the solar furnace, drift in erratic, flutterless glides, riding the sudden updrafts like scraps of charred paper. There are predators, too: small, slate-scaled vipers that remain dormant until the high noon hits their basking stones, at which point their blood thaws and they strike with a frantic, stuttering speed, hunting the runners before the shadows return and turn them back to stone.

Man has been here, though rarely, and always quietly. 

On the southern lip of the shelf, where the stone juts out over the long drop, stands a windbreak of stacked slate, held together without mortar. It is knee-high, primitive, built to shield a crouching hunter from the prevailing northern gale. Within the crevice of the wall sits a small cache: a handful of notched bone pins, lengths of plaited animal sinew still supple from grease, and an unworked blade of knapped green quartz, its edges as sharp as the frost that rims it. These are not the leavings of antiquity; the marrow on the split sheep-femur beside the stone is barely dried. The toolmaker was here within the week, waiting for something that flies up from the depths of the cloud deck below.

Everything here happens quickly and without a sound. A moth is plucked out of the air by a runner leaping two feet into the thin air; the runner lands silently on the damp velvet of the cushion-plant; a shadow crosses the white stone as a great, broad-spanned scavenger swings past the notch without flapping, inspecting the basin with a single, yellow eye, then is pulled away by the wind into the great, blinding blue void.
