At this altitude, the air should be freezing, but it is not. The heat here does not come down from the sun—though the sun sits stark and white in a thin, pale purple sky—it pushes upward through the stone itself. 

The terrain is a forest of raw geological needles, basalt horsts and jagged granite needles thrust twelve thousand meters into the upper sky by some recent, violent tectonic buckling. There has been no time for wind or rain to round them. The edges of the stone are knifelike, fresh as shattered flint, still carrying the glassy slickness of the subterranean heat that squeezed them out. They rise in dense, near-touching clusters: thousands of black and dull-iron spires, some only twenty yards thick but rising for vertical miles, separated by sheer fissures so deep the bottoms cannot be seen. 

Suspended within, between, and across these spires is an industrial city built of cold-rolled steel, corrugated lead, and unpainted iron. 

It does not sprawl; it nests. The built mass fills the chasms between the stone pillars like slag poured into a mold. Enormous modular platforms, held by braided suspension cables the thickness of redwoods, hang directly over the abyss, anchored by heavy counterweights of raw quarried pig iron. Tier after tier of catwalks, pipeline bundles, and smelting galleries bridge spire to spire, webbing the entire mountain-complex into a single, high-density apparatus of production. It looks less like a city for habitation and more like an enormous valve manifold fitted over the planet’s open wounds.

The atmosphere is heavy with the smell of scorched stone, hot transformer oil, and flash-boiled brine. Because the elevation is extreme, the ambient pressure is low, meaning water boils at a lukewarm temperature; yet moisture persists everywhere in an intermittent, trickling state. Condensed steam from deep exhaust vents collects against the cold undersides of the steel cantilevers, then drips into the suffocating heat of the open platforms, sizzling faintly on the black iron stairs. Puddles form in the troughs of corrugated zinc roofs, oily and clear, only to evaporate, re-condense on the next tier above, and drop back down in a slow, closed cycle of mechanical humidity.

It is remarkably quiet. Sound travels poorly in the thin, boiling air. The heavy industrial machinery operates on scales of patience that baffle the ear: massive hydraulic pistons, three stories tall and caked in graphite paste, cycle with the speed of continental drift, taking hours to complete a single compression stroke. When a valve vents miles below, it does not roar; it arrives as a low, dry thrum that passes through the soles of one's boots long before it reaches the ears. The only steady noise is the faint, windless clicking of hot iron cooling in the drafts and the rhythmic, hollow *plink* of condensation falling into dry gutters.

There is nothing green. No lichen clings to the raw basalt, for the stone is too hot and too newly born from the interior; no spore has drifted high enough to take purchase. The only non-mineral presences are scarce and unnatural: lithic scavengers that resemble jointed iron tongs, moving across the cable networks with the agonizing slowness of rust. They have no eyes and seem to feed entirely on the zinc galvanization of the pipes or the dried crusts of mineral salts left where the steam vents breathe. Once in a great while, a solitary gray bird, wingless and flat-bodied, will let itself fall from a crane housing, catching the boiling thermal updrafts with wide, leathery flaps of belly-skin, gliding from one needle to the next without making a sound.

The layout of the urban works has the dense, claustrophobic complexity of a submarine’s engine room, but magnified to the scale of a mountain range. Gangways wind around the perimeter of the spires, clinging to the raw rock with massive expansion bolts that groan as the stone expands in the noon heat. Living quarters, if they can be called that, are pressurized lead drums tucked into the narrowest clefts where the rock faces almost meet, their round portholes glowing with a dull, yellow domestic light. Steam cranes with counterbalanced jibs hang dormant over empty space, their hooks swinging in arcs that take minutes to finish.

From an open gantry at the city’s outer edge, the sheer scale of the extrusion becomes visible. The spires do not end with the city; they continue out toward the horizon, a sea of razor-sharp black teeth rising above a low, flat blanket of sulfur-yellow clouds that marks the lower world. The light here is sharp, clear, and undiffused by dust, casting ink-black shadows across the fresh fractures of the peaks. 

Nothing rots here. Nothing decays. The world is being assembled rather than worn away; it is embryonic, unpolished, and intensely hot, governed by the slow expansion of metals and the patient upward crawl of the earth’s newest crust through a scaffold of soot-stained iron.
