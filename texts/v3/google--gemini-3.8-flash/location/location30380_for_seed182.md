The heat is the first thing that establishes the boundary of the place: not the dry baking of an open desert, but a heavy, sweltering, stagnant warmth like the interior of an engine housing that has sat in wet shade for a century. It smells of hot iron, boiled well-water, river silt, and the pungent, vegetable grease of crushed stems. 

This is the Lower Sump of a terraced borough, sunken sixty feet beneath the surface paving of the upper district. It is not large—perhaps the footprint of an old parish church or a rail yard turn-table—but it has been filled to its margins with an intensity that leaves no void unaddressed. The walls are not ancient; the limestone ashlar and dark vitrified brick still possess their sharp, squared arrises. Chisel marks from the excavation are crisp and unrounded by centuries of grit, yet every flat plane has been requisitioned.

Between the masonry retaining walls and the sheer limestone faces, an entire municipal sub-quarter has been wedged in. Multi-tiered iron walkways, painted in red-lead that has dulled to the color of dried liver, zigzag across brick tenements that are stacked three and four rooms deep. Cast-iron soil pipes, steam conduits lagged in decaying tarred hemp, and bundles of gutta-percha telegraph lines cross the narrow chasm in such numbers that looking upward reveals only a cross-hatched ceiling of black metal and dripping lead.

Through this lattice, the vegetation has climbed, not by creeping along the margins, but by seizing every warm, vibrating surface. The plants here are massive, fleshy, and extraordinarily dense, adapted to perpetual dimness and the intense thermal exhaust of the works below. Enormous aroids with leaves broad as carriage hoods have forced their way between the catwalk risers; their foliage is not a sunlit green, but an oily, blackish-bronze that drinks the scant light. Waxy, tuberous vines, thick as a stoker’s forearm, wind directly around the high-pressure steam mains, using the scalding iron as a trellis. Where the lagging has frayed, the bark of these creepers has grown calloused, forming corky, blistered collars that drink the condensation weeping from the joints.

Light arrives only as a secondary rumor. Far above, through the iron grates that carry the traffic of the city above, daylight enters as a gray, filtered haze, instantly smothered by the foliage and the steam. The only steady illumination comes from low-voltage filament bulbs housed in thick, ribbed-glass maritime cages, glowing with the dull orange of an ember about to go out, and the periodic dull cherry-red blush of exposed pipe manifolds. In this twilight, colors flatten into shades of asphalt, wet coal, liverwort, and zinc.

Water is not present as a standing pool or a torrent, but as a condition of the surfaces. The air is always at the tipping point of saturation. Beaded moisture gathers on cold rivet heads, runs down the bevel of the brickwork, and falls in deliberate, heavy drops onto the broad leaves below. Every few minutes, a single *plink* or *thud* sounds through the vault as a suspended drop releases from a girder into the heart of a fern. Otherwise, it is extraordinarily quiet. The machinery that hums somewhere in the rock behind the walls is low-frequency and continuous—a muffled, thoracic vibration that can be felt through the soles of one's boots rather than heard. The great leaves swallow echo. Conversations held ten paces away would be reduced to a soft, woolly rustle.

There are almost no living things with legs. The air is too hot, too still, and the oxygen too thick with sulfurous steam for common vermin. One might watch a wet rail for an hour and see only a solitary, translucent slug, long as a finger, tracing a trail through the iron scale, or a pale blind cricket motionless upon a cold valve wheel. The life here is structural and vegetal; it does not scurry.

Everything changes on a timeline that defies the eye. The dynamic here is glacial, dictated by the slow calcification of mineral scale inside the pipes and the steady, hydraulic swelling of the vines as they pry brick from lime mortar. A valve may take four months to seize entirely; a single leaf may spend three weeks uncurling from its fibrous sheath, rasping imperceptibly against the wet rust of an iron beam as it forces its way toward the warmth of a condenser. 

Human presence is legible everywhere—in the numbered brass tags affixed to shutoff cocks, the coal-scuttles set beside narrow tenement doors, the laundry hung between railings that never truly dries, only cures in the hot, oily draft—yet the inhabitants are currently elsewhere, perhaps withdrawn into the deeper, cooler brick recesses behind the rock-face. What remains in the open air is a dense, mechanical terrarium: a cathedral of wet iron, sharp-edged brick, and swollen, darkling leaves, breathing quietly in the dark.
