The horizon is an open terrace of cold-rolled steel, sharp as a sheared coupon, stepping down in mile-wide shelves into an atmosphere so bright and thin it hurts to look through. 

There is no rock beneath this; there is no soil. Every contour is a manufactured product—stamped zinc-coated floor plates, interlocking I-beams whose milling burrs are still bright and hooked, immense bulkheads of planished iron bolted together with hex-heads as broad as millstones. Because the assembly is recent, the edges have not yet softened. There has been no grit or scouring wind to dull the corners of the flanges; if you dragged a knuckle along the lip of an expansion trench, it would open your skin cleanly.

Yet the space behaves erratically, as if the engineering was executed to precision specifications that simply failed to agree with Euclidean space. A gangway made of diamond-tread plate runs straight toward a bank of dormant condensers, but as it continues, the grade tilts upward while remaining level to the stride; walking it, you find yourself looking down at the very deck you departed, which is now somehow suspended overhead like a ceiling of greased tin, identical in every detail down to the pool of clear water reflecting the non-existent sky. In certain bays, three shadows fall from a single stanchion, each pointing toward a different missing sun, each casting a cool, palpable draft across the deck.

The water here is a quiet, clinging thing. It does not gather into rivers because there is no gradient that stays true long enough to carry a current. Instead, it condenses directly out of the pale, glare-choked air onto the cold ironwork, sweating through seams where the lead caulking has curdled. It sits in shallow, skin-thin sheets across square acres of plate metal, shivering only when a counterweight shifts five levels below. Sometimes it rises: droplets form on the undersides of girders, detach, and fall upward into the blinding white zenith, disappearing like sparks.

It is cold—not the killing frost of a summit, but the damp, persistent chill of an empty machine hall before the boilers are lit. The light is total, unshadowed, and dryly chemical, washing down from everywhere at once, turning the galvanized surfaces into blinding sheets of silver. 

In the seams and expansion joints, life has found purchase, though it is thin and colorless. A brittle, hair-like wire-grass, slate-gray and stiff with zinc salts, sprouts from the tallow used to pack the bearing races. In the corners where structural uprights meet at impossible sixty-degree squares, a pale liverwort spreads in geometric mats, strictly following the lines of the mill scale, refusing to cross the weld lines. It has no smell, only a faint, wet odor of clean machine grease and chilled iron.

The animals, however, are everywhere. They are silent, plentiful, and completely indifferent to the failure of the horizon.

Large, flat-bodied creatures—smooth as dressed tallow, the color of wet lead—roost by the hundreds along the webbings of the great box-girders. They have no eyes; their snouts end in broad, sensitive pads that rest flat against the steel, drinking the microscopic vibrations of the framing. They move only when the structural load shifts, shuffling on dozens of blunt, horn-tipped claws that leave no scratches on the fresh paint. Above them, nesting in the lattice towers that stretch out into the glare, are flocks of heavy, owl-like birds with plumage like frayed hemp. They do not call. When they launch themselves, they don’t fall into the air but seem to slide along invisible planes of shear, drifting sideways across the valley without flapping, their wings locked stiffly at ninety-degree angles.

Down in the drainage wells—where the condensation drips into dark, vertical conduits that lead nowhere—schools of pale, soft-shelled crustaceans cluster around the intake grates. They graze on the microscopic bloom of graphite and lime that leaches from the uncured concrete collars below. Their shells clack faintly against the ironwork: a light, dry rattle, like dried beans dropped onto a tray.

It is this clatter, along with the faint, singular *ping* of contracting metal, that constitutes the entire voice of the region. The industrial machinery—though vast, though complete with flywheels forty feet across and hydraulic cylinders as thick as silos—is not running. It is waiting, or perhaps it has finished. Once every hour or two, a valve deep within the floor will unseat with a sound like a wet tongue pulling from teeth, release a long, cold breath of air smelling of compressed oil, and reseat. The sound travels across the miles of open steelwork without echoing, absorbed instantly by the white emptiness above.

Beyond the immediate basin of shelves, the terrain repeats. You can look across twenty miles of what should be distance, but the perspective does not foreshorten correctly; the distant cooling stacks appear the same size as the near ones, perfectly sharp, their ladder-rungs distinct, crowded together as if the world were an elevation drawing that has been folded into a fan. Between them, through gaps where the floor plates have simply not been laid, there is only the white glare, and beneath that, far down through thousands of feet of identical, unpainted framework, the faint, gray glimmer of another deck, damp and quiet, waiting for its weight to be measured.
