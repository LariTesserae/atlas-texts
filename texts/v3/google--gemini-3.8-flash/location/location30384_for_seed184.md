The water here is not an intruder; it is the medium through which all weight and history are held. It is dense, mineral-bitter, and tepid, lingering at the exact temperature of resting blood. It fills a collapsed vaulted borough—perhaps three tiers of municipal ironwork, tenement balconies, and steam-distribution infrastructure, all crushed together within a sunken stone basin no wider than a cathedral close. 

There is virtually no open space. If you were to extend an arm, your knuckles would graze either the fluted flange of a cast-iron feed pipe, the spongy velvet of black-leafed water-ivy, or the deadened lead sheath of a high-voltage trunk line. The precinct is choked to its margins. Tenements with cast-iron balconies have subsided into the engine trenches; boilers the size of houses are wedged between brick tenements, their rivets furred with copper-green filamentous growth that trails upward in the weak convective currents.

Light barely enters from above; what illuminates the district is an internal, sleepy glow. Occasional indicator lamps—heavy glass bulls-eyes sealed in brass—still burn beneath their thick lenses, fed by residual galvanic currents buried somewhere in the flooded foundations. They cast circular, amber-tinted beams into the soup, illuminating millions of suspended particulates: flakes of rust, spores of velvet-mold, and greasy beads of paraffin that rise at the rate of an inch an hour. Farther down, where the water is warmest, the housings of dormant steam pumps radiate a dull, infrared pressure that you feel on your skin rather than see.

The water does not circulate with any violence. Its movement is languid, governed purely by heat gradients. Near the great heat-exchangers, which still seep warm saline from deep geological jackets, the liquid rises in viscous, syrup-like ribbons, warping the view of the machinery beyond. Where it cools along the high cyclopean masonry walls that hem the precinct in, it slips downward again, dragging fronds of ribbon-fern and dense, oily weeds down with it in a slow, circular breath that takes an entire day to complete.

Vegetation has claimed the mechanics without dismantling them. It is not oceanic flora, but something adapted strictly to this pressurized, warm dark. Great curtains of bladder-kelp, black-green and slick with grease, anchor themselves to the spokes of massive flywheels. Broad, pelt-like mosses grow thick over the dials and pressure gauges, their roots finding purchase in the sulfurous lead putty that once sealed the glass faces. In the lee of an overturned locomotive chassis, a grove of segmented, bamboo-like horsetails stands six fathoms tall, their joints swollen with air pockets that gently tug their iron foundations toward the roof. 

The silence is vast, deadened by the mass of the liquid and the padding of waterlogged turf. The industrial roar that once defined this iron warren has been compressed into a phantom register: the low, sub-audible hum of sixty-cycle current leaking through salt; the infrequent, metallic *clink* of a copper rivet shearing under centuries of rust-expansion; the faint *thrup* of an oily gas bubble liberating itself from a silted manifold to drift upward toward the drowned vaults.

Life with intent is rare here. Once in a long while, something long and pale—a blind river eel grown massive and slow on boiler slime, its body translucent enough to show its spine—threads its way between the cables. It does not dart. It drifts through the drowned doorways of the old trade offices, its fins fanning the settled red rust, displacing nothing, undisturbed by the copper-tasting quiet. 

The place has reached a heavy, immovable balance. It does not feel ruined so much as cured, preserved under miles of pressure and broth, slowly digesting its own ironwork over centuries while its warm gardens grow thicker, tighter, and more deliberate in the dark.
