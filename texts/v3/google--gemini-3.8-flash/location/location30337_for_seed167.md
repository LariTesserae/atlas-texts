The water here has the weight of a warm, salt-thick oil. It holds heat without releasing it, trapped in a broad subterranean depression where the sea floor bows downward into a basin three miles across. There is no sun; light ended hundreds of meters above, and down here even bioluminescence is rare—a spent currency that the ecology long ago discarded in favor of scent, pressure, and electrical hum.

In the complete darkness, the landscape reveals itself as a city. 

It is entirely biological, constructed without a single tool or hinge, yet it possesses the dense, deliberate geometry of an ancient, sprawling capital. The material is biogenic stone—layers of aggregated calcium carbonate, cemented silt, and the hardened, chitinous excretions of trillions of tubicolous organisms that lived and died over tens of thousands of years. The buildings rise from the basin floor like worn ziggurats and hollow fluted minarets, some standing twenty meters tall, their surfaces rounded and softened by the gentle, perpetual scour of the bottom current. 

These are not the jagged, accidental branches of a reef. They are planned by instinct, organized through the chemical stigmergy of blind, colonial engineers. Narrow alleys—channels no wider than a shoulder—run between immense residential towers, laid out in curving, efficient grids that funnel the thermal upwellings from the seabed. Every surface is an accretion of micro-chambers, ventilation pores, and shared plumbing. Warm water, heavy with dissolved iron, sulfur, and carbonate, seeps steadily from fissures in the foundations, rising through the interior conduits of the towers like air through a furnace chimney, warming the surrounding liquid to the temperature of fresh blood.

The vegetation is nearly nonexistent. What little plant life exists takes the form of tough, pale encrustations of red algae adapted to chemical seepage, clinging like thin lichens to the high ledges where water moves fastest. In place of kelp or weed, the landscape is populated entirely by animals.

The density is staggering. Every cubic meter of the warm, blind soup holds life. The walls of the stone structures are alive: millions of small, blind vermetid gastropods and serpulid worms line the masonry, their feathery feeding nets extended silently into the dark currents. When something large passes, a hydraulic ripple runs through them, and thousands of tiny doors click shut in unison—a quiet, dry *tuck-tuck-tuck* that carries through the water like knuckles against slate.

Beneath the towers, the ground is soft. It consists of a deep, settled carpet of shell gravel, fecal pellets, and the shed carapaces of small arthropods, smoothed out by centuries into undulating roads and plazas. There are arterial routes—wide boulevards carved out by the collective transit of the city's mobile inhabitants. Blind, pale cephalopods, their skins thick and ridged like raw suede, glide through the thoroughfares with steady siphonal pulses, navigating purely by the taste of the chemical gradients laid down by the towers. 

The architecture is in dynamic equilibrium. It is an eroded, ancient landscape, rounded down at the edges by steady currents, but it is continually repaired from within. Wherever an archway collapses under its own bulk, dense clusters of builder-worms swarm the fracture, sealing the wound with fresh lime-putty within seasons. 

Movement here is constant, yet curiously muffled. The soundscape is composed of water against stone—a deep, low-frequency pressure wave that throbs against the ear rather than ringing inside it. There are periodic dull thuds as gas pockets bubble up through the silt and burst, the scratching of crab limbs over porous lime, and the soft, pneumatic sigh of water circulating through internal aqueducts. 

There are tiers of life occupying every elevation of the structures. In the deep foundations, where the water is hottest and thick with iron sulfide, mats of pale, thread-like bacteria form curtains that sway in the draft, grazed by slow-moving, armored isopods the size of loaves. In the mid-levels, inside the hollow courtyards of the towers, shoals of blind, translucent fish drift in tight toroidal formations, their lateral lines attuned to the geometry of the walls, never once grazing the stone. Higher still, on the rounded pinnacles that jut into the cold boundary-layer where the warm plume meets the true deep sea, predatory polyps with tentacles like thick rope hang waiting, motionless, their bodies stretched out into the current like gargoyles over a drowning city.

It is a world without horizon, without sky, without a single straight edge made by a ruler, yet it has an urban order that feels deeply familiar. Thousands of lives brush against one another in the dark, linked by the architecture they share, all of them sustained by the warm, thick blood of the Earth pushing through the floors of their stone hives.
