The floor of the rift is not soil, but a floor of fresh, fractured glass. 

It dropped into the earth only recently in geological time—a collapsed tectonic trough five miles wide, flanked on either side by sheer scarps of obsidian and black basalt whose edges have had no time to dull. The stones are still razor-bright. To move across them is to walk on heaps of shattered blades, yet the rock is already disappearing beneath a violently accelerated ecology that feeds on geothermal blood and sheer friction.

It is nearly entirely dark. The sky above is choked off by a dense, low-hanging roof of thermal smog, soot, and the vast, overlapping ribs of high-altitude foliage that bridge the chasm far above, catching whatever ambient sunlight might exist and swallowing it whole. Down here, light is reduced to dull, fugitive glints: the oily sheen of condensation on wet stone, the momentary spark of struck quartz when something heavy scrambles over a scree slope, and the soft, bruised violet of deep-tissue bioluminescence that pulses only when an organism is crushed or dying. 

Despite the darkness, it is not cold. A heavy, greenhouse heat clings to the trough, thick and wet, hovering around the temperature of an animal’s mouth. Water does not sit in pools; it hangs in the air, coats every vertical surface in a slick, lukewarm skin, and trickles down the fault lines in intermittent, erratic pulses. Everything smells of hot iron, wet leather, and fermenting pulp.

The noise is the first thing that overpowers the senses: it is not a background hum, but a physical assault. The acoustic reverberation within the trench magnifies the churn of millions of bodies into a deafening, continuous roar. It is the sound of an avalanche that refuses to stop—a churning drone composed of stridulating insect limbs, the clicking of blind jaws against basalt, the wet, rhythmic slapping of muscular tails on mudstone, and the harsh, tearing shriek of fibrous stalks being dismantled by millions of mouthparts simultaneously. The sound presses against the eardrum like a flat palm.

Nothing in this place stays still. The dynamism is frantic, resembling an overturned anthill scaled to the size of a province. 

Out of the fractured stone grows a vegetation of deranged vigor. Without true sunlight to guide symmetry, the flora grows by touch and pressure, throwing out thick, corded vines the width of a man’s torso that twist around each other like mooring cables. There are fleshy, rubbery ferns whose fronds are stiff as sheet metal, and pale, liver-colored stalks that shoot upward twenty feet in a matter of days, only to be dragged down, stripped, and trampled by the fauna beneath. The plants do not wait for seasons; they pump sap violently, snapping their own seed-pods with the force of small detonations, showering the darkness with sticky, pea-sized grains.

Through this hyperactive undergrowth moves a fauna so dense that species lines seem to blur into a single, writhing tide. The trench floor belongs to pale, muscular things: multi-jointed arthropods the size of hounds, heavy-ribbed amphibians with skin like wet canvas, and long, sinuous predators that lack eyes entirely, navigating by the intense heat radiating from their prey. They exist in an unsustainable frenzy of consumption and reproduction. A dead creature does not rot; it disappears under a carpet of flat-bodied scavengers in minutes, stripped to clean, steaming bone before its nerves have fully stopped firing. 

Yet among this feral pandemonium, there are distinct signs of purpose—a rough, emerging intelligence that has learned to exploit the terrain. 

Tied off between the sharp obsidian pillars are massive, crude cordages made from braided creeper-vines, some as thick as ship’s hawsers. These are not buildings; they are hunting lattices. High up in the dark, perched on shelves of razor-sharp basalt, sit the makers: pale, knuckle-walking bipeds with leathery, hairless hides and faces dominated by cavernous nasal cavities and broad, sensory whiskers. 

Their technology is urgent, heavy, and purely functional. They wield large cleavers knapped from the fresh obsidian, their edges bound to heavy wooden splints with gut and sinew. They do not farm; they engineer bottlenecks in the dark. They drop deadfalls—slabs of basalt rigged with trip-cords of woven hair—into the migration troughs below, crushing swarms of the armored amphibians beneath hundred-pound stones, then scramble down the slick basalt to butcher the kill before the smaller scavengers can devour it. Here and there, wedged firmly into the rock crevices, are simple bone wedges, driven deep with river-stones to guide the fissures and force the chaotic traffic of the floor into narrow killing chutes.

The air never clears. The perpetual dampness condenses against the high cold edges of the rift walls and falls back down as a warm, sporadic drizzle that sizzles where it touches thermal vents. Every few minutes, a section of the freshly exposed rock gives way under the weight of the climbing vines and the scrabbling claws of hundreds of animals, sending tons of sharp scree sliding into the valley, instantly burying dozens of creatures and tearing open fresh scars in the stone. 

The scar is populated before the dust can settle. New shoots immediately probe the fresh rubble, blind mouths lick the newly exposed moisture from the basalt, and the roar continues unabated—a deafening, blind, swarming machine made entirely of meat, green sap, and young stone.
