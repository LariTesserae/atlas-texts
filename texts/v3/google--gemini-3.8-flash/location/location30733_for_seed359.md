The space is an enclosed court, approximately sixty paces across, entirely assembled from dry-joined timber, unbaked slip-clay, and dense windings of pale hemp cord. It has the footprint of an abbey’s drying-yard or a high-walled granary, but its vertical bounds are unsettled: looking upward, the squared wooden rafters repeat into a warm, hazel-colored ceiling that seems only thirty feet overhead until a bird flies into it, whereupon the rafters become an inverted floor upon which small benches and grain-chests rest without falling, their joinery completely fresh, pegged with white pine that has not yet had time to oxidize.

The air is bone-dry and warm, carrying the temperature of clean, sun-warmed linen or flour just ground between stones. There is no moisture anywhere—not as humidity, not in the tissues of the living things, not in the substrate. Where moisture would usually pool or seep, there is only a superfine, weightless silt, fragrant like powdered cedar and dry starch, drifting in soft drifts against the baseboards. 

Despite the total lack of water, the courtyard is choked with a high, lush understory. The vegetation does not drink; it weaves itself into existence from the surrounding craftwork. Huge, broad-bladed ferns rise out of the seams between floorboards, their frills composed of razor-thin shavings of spruce, curling tightly at the tips like fresh plane-shavings, yet genuinely alive, respirating an odorless, tepid draft. Tangles of sweet-smelling, pith-filled rushes push upward in dense thickets, their stems made of dry, hollow reed that splits if stepped on with a sound like snapping toothpicks, spilling no sap, only a cool, dry air that smells faintly of toasted oats. Along the balustrades, creeping ivies with leaves like pressed blotting paper advance by unspooling their own dry fiber into the grain of the wood, sewing themselves into the architecture with meticulous, hand-stitched running loops.

The construction is entirely non-mechanical, showing the marks of flint blades, adzes, and bone awls. Every beam has been leveled by eye; every wicker partition has been interlaced by calloused fingers that left their faint whorls pressed into the unbaked clay chinking. Because erosion has had no time to work—the courtyard feels as if it were completed earlier this morning—the wood smells pungent and raw, the edges of the split shingles are knife-sharp, and shavings still litter the recesses under the steps.

Yet the logic of the fabrication does not remain inside its joints. The human hands that tied the cords appear to have accidentally bound spatial rules that should not hold.

In the center of the yard, where four low timber walkways meet, there is a corner that possesses five right angles. To walk around it is to make four turns and find oneself standing on the vertical face of the opposite wall, looking down at the path one just left, though one’s weight presses naturally against the timber now underfoot. Light enters evenly from everywhere and nowhere—a steady, warm amber daylight that casts shadows in three distinct directions at once, none of them dark, all of them slightly stiffer than the air, like sheets of dry parchment leaning against the fence posts.

The fauna is numerous and quiet. Moths as large as roof tiles cling to the reed-thickets, their wings thick and soft as carded wool, patterned with intricate, block-printed geometric seals that look stamped by hand. They do not drink; they periodically press their dry, feathery palps into the chalk dust to groom themselves, producing a faint, papery rustle. Among the spruce-shaving ferns run small, four-legged creatures the size of stoats, whose bodies are formed of articulated osier wicker covered in short, velvet-dry mole-furs. They have no internal wetness—if one breaks a leg against a strut, it simply whittles the wood back into place with its chisel-shaped incisors, binding the splinter with its own shed hair. 

High up, roosting on pegs driven into the ceiling-beams, sit dozens of blunt-beaked martins made of stitched calfskin and down. They turn their heads in sudden, ninety-degree clicks, watching the floor. Occasionally one drops, gliding without a sound through the warm air, its wings making the dry hiss of a knife through silk, to catch a flightless seed that is drifting upward toward the floor above. 

The dynamics of the place are exceedingly slow. Growth does not swell or pump; it is braided, molecule by dry molecule, by the patient tension of the cords. The floor planks slowly push against one another, rising into shallow, perfectly formed A-frame gables over the course of days, only for the small wicker animals to gnaw the crests flat again, scattering white wood-meal across the carpets of shaving-grass. 

Sound carries poorly. The sheer density of fiber, pith, unspun fleece, and dry timber swallows vibration within ten paces. The loudest presence is the dry *snip* of a fern-leaf expanding its curl by another quarter-inch, or the faint, hollow rattle of an unbaked clay bead rolling down an incline that, if watched long enough, proves to be tilting neither up nor down, but simply into the next hour.
