Miles above any known ground, inside a swelling node no larger than the hold of a skiff, it is ninety-eight degrees and the mortar has never dried.

This is the interior of a knurled joint in an impossible spire—a vertical mass made entirely by human hands, yet standing where the atmosphere ought to be blue-black and frozen. Instead, trapped within this fist of woven wicker and unfired river-silt, the air is thick as soup, boiling with the heat of a kiln that has forgotten how to cool. There is no natural stone here; there is no bedrock. Every millimeter of surface is an artifact, assembled with bone needles, split cane, wet raffia, and heavy grey mud hauled up or hallucinated into being.

Because it is newly born—or rather, because it is trapped in the precise second of its being made—nothing has weathered. The marks of thumbs in the clay walls are sharp enough to cut; the split ends of the hazel laths still bleed white, sticky sap that smells of raw almonds and scalded milk. No water has had time to run channels; no wind has rounded a single corner. The edges of the hand-beaten clay blocks are razor-crisp, yet they are soaking wet, weeping a steady, slick sweat that pools in the palms of wicker pockets and drips through floorings that have no business supporting weight.

The scale is intensely private. You cannot stand straight; you cannot stretch your arms without your knuckles driving into a dense matting of green reed or sliding into a cool, unctuous paste of crushed shell and silt. It is a crawlspace between heavens, packed wall-to-wall, ceiling-to-belly, with an excruciating compression of things. Ladders made of twisted gut pass through apertures scarcely wider than a ribcage, only to emerge into identical, suffocating pockets where the orientation of "down" shifts twenty degrees to the left, anchored solely by the weight of wet clay slung in hanging baskets.

It is violently loud. Outside the skin of wattled bark and raw cow-hide, the upper troposphere screams—a flat, shearing gale of high-altitude wind that batters the joint like an anvil. But inside, the noise is wetter, thicker: the groaning of ten thousand hemp lashings under tension, the ceaseless, greasy suction of green timber slipping against green timber, and the deafening hiss of steam escaping tiny blowholes where the baking exterior meets the sopping interior. The structure breathes like an overdriven lung, shuddering with every thermal pulse that shoots up the spine of the tower.

Weirdness bleeds through the seams where the craft fails to meet Euclidean sense. A weaver’s knot, tied with coarse yellow twine, loops through itself six times and somehow anchors a timber that exists four inches behind your own shoulder. If you push your finger through a soft patch of unbaked wall, you do not feel the freezing gale of the high sky; you feel the greasy scalp of someone working an identical trowel on the other side of an infinitely thin skin, working to close the hole you are making before the mud can slide. Up is not away from the earth; up is simply where the clay is still warm from the palm that smoothed it.

Lushness here is monstrous and impatient. Without soil, plants do not wait for dirt; they root directly into the lashings. Pale, hairy vines—sprouting from seeds carried up in the belly of some long-dead builder—erupt from the knots in a frenzy, their leaves unfurling translucent and wet against the low, sweating vaults. Liverworts, bright as fresh bile, coat the damp rungs of the crawl-ways within hours of their tying. A species of fleshy, leafless orchid with the texture of boiled tripe forces its way between the hand-laid tiles, drinking the perpetual condensation that slides down the walls. It is a jungle with no forest floor, an orchard made entirely of cordage and unhardened silt, growing so fast that you can hear the green bark split—a constant, brittle clicking under the roar of the wind.

Light enters only where the weave has not yet received its plaster: thin, knife-like needles of bruised purple twilight that slash through the steam. These beams do not illuminate; they show only the spinning drift of reed-chaff, the floating grey lint of beaten bark, and the blinding sheen of moisture covering every square inch of the hand-smoothed world.

Animals are an error here, rare and altered. Occasionally, an earless, white-plated gecko—short-toothed and broad-fingered—struggles through a cleft in the wattle, hunting the blind water-mites that breed in the slurry. These creatures have never seen horizontal ground; their ribs are flattened to wedge into the split-cane floors, their bellies perpetually coated in wet slip. They move with a desperate, jerky twitch, vibrating in sympathy with the singing cords of the tower.

Nothing settles. The mud slides down an inch; a hand from some unseen crawl-hole above jams three green willow rods into the tear to check the slip, binding them with wet gut that twangs like an iron wire as the altitude tries to twist it. It is an architecture of emergency, an infinite attic hung at the edge of space, perpetually being built out of river rubbish by creatures who have only stone knives and wet palms, all of it steaming, screaming, and drowning in its own birth-water.
