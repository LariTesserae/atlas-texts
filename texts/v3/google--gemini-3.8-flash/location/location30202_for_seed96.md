The entire mountain flank has been paved over with hand-kneaded earthenware, slate shingles, and pale mortar, leaving not an inch of loose earth or exposed rock beneath. 

It sits in the thin, searing air of a high upland, where the sun is fierce and direct, but the air never warms past a cool, cellar-like draft. Because of the altitude and the proximity of drifting cold-fronts, moisture does not simply rain down; it condenses out of the blue sky directly onto the masonry. Every plane, lip, and riser of this enormous, multi-tiered installation is slicked with a perpetual, millimeter-thick coat of water that reflects the sky like a shattered mirror. The glare is blinding—an ocean of specular white points blazing across terracotta pinks, river-gray slates, and chalky white seams.

The construction is impossibly dense. It is not a city—there are no doors, no windows, no rooms meant for shelter. It is a continuous, unbroken landscape of water-traps, shallow pans, flumes, and stepped weirs, packed so tightly together that one could walk for leagues without ever touching a surface that was not shaped by human fingers. Tens of thousands of small, rectangular holding basins, each roughly the span of a man’s chest, are stacked and abutted against one another, stair-stepping down the grade. There are no gaps between them; the rim of one cistern forms the outer lip of the next. 

Yet the technological vocabulary is remarkably archaic. This is not the work of hydraulic pumps or iron sluices. Every wall is formed of coarse, unglazed tile, sun-baked clay bricks, or thin slabs of slate wedged together with thick bands of lime mortar. The marks of primitive tools are everywhere: the cross-hatched scraping of wooden combs on the clay before it was fired, the deep indentations of thumbs along the joins, the irregular, blunt chipping of stone adzes on the slate edges. 

Remarkably, none of it has begun to weather away. The edges are knife-sharp. The lime has not softened into rounded powder; the mortar fins extruded between pressed bricks are still crisp, standing upright like dry salt crusts. The chips of flint used to shim the drains are fresh, their fractures glassy and clean. It is as if this colossal intervention across the plateau was finished only weeks ago, or as if some strange condition of the high air preserves its pristine geometry from the dulling hammer of time.

Water moves through it slowly, almost silently. The grade is gentle enough that the water never plunges or churns into white froth. Instead, it weeps. It gathers on the highest stepped platforms, swells until it overcomes the surface tension along a thumb-grooved spillway, and slides down a sheer tile face to the basin below. The sound of this movement across miles is not a roar, but an immense, faint whispering—a trillion soft, liquid clicks, like the ticking of cool stone under an endless drizzle, interspersed with the occasional hollow *gluck* of an air bubble trapped in a narrow ceramic tube.

There is no life here. The tiles are too clean, the lime mortar perhaps too alkaline and raw, for moss or lichen to have taken hold. Even in the dead corners where water stagnates into three-inch-deep panes of liquid glass, no algae clouds the bottom. One can look straight down through the water to count the grit of crushed river-shell mixed into the earthenware lining. There are no flies skimming the surface, no mountain birds dropping down to drink, no seed carried on the upland wind that has managed to wedge a taproot into the mortar. It is entirely mineral: baked mud, quarried stone, quicklime, and snow-cold water.

The air smells cleanly of damp chalk, cold ceramic tile, and the faint, electrical tang of thin altitude. As the sun wheels overhead, the shadows change not softly, but with the stark, hard lines cast by millions of tiny, razor-edged masonry steps. From a ridge overlooking the whole valley, it looks like an impossibly vast machine carved from river-mud and split slate, abandoned immediately after its completion, lying silent under the bright glare while the mountain’s cold sweat passes through its millions of shallow basins, cell by cell, never rushing, moving slowly downward toward some unseen reservoir far below the line of the sky.
