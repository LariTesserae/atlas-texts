The air at nine thousand meters should be an empty, freezing venom, but here it is as thick as oil and smells of wet bark and boiled sugar. 

The range does not consist of mountains in any conventional geological sense, though they are made of gneiss and gray gabbro, worn down into soft, parabolic horns by rains that have fallen without season for ten thousand years. They are spires—thousands of them, each no thicker than a cathedral nave at the base, rising through the cloud-deck into a sweltering, over-pressurized thermal ceiling. The atmosphere does not thin as one looks out across the gulfs; instead, it grows heavier, golden-brown with vapor, suspended in a permanent midday heat that pools between the needles like broth in a split marrow bone.

Below, the world is hidden beneath an opaque, curdled white floor that extends toward every horizon, flat as an iron table. Above it, these needles pierce the vapor and carry the jungle with them into the sky.

The heat is not solar. It bleeds directly from the stone. Thin, mirror-bright seams of an unmachined, greasy black alloy run vertically through the granite—no wider than a thumb, unweathered, flush with the rock face. Where these veins pass, the stone sweats hot, alkaline water that bubbles out of micro-fractures, running upward as often as downward, pulled by slight local reversals in barometric potential. The rock does not freeze; it seethes. A constant, low-frequency hum vibrates through the soles of the feet—not a mechanical rattle, but the heavy, dull friction of massive thermodynamic fields anchoring a deep pocket of tropospheric pressure against the void. It feels like standing inside the lung of a sleeping mammal.

Because the rock is hot and the sky is moist, the vegetation is ferocious, though its growth is slow and dense. There are no delicate alpine mosses here. The cliffs are wrapped in thick coats of liverwort the color of cooked greens, through which thrust the roots of colossal, squat-trunked *ferro-figs*. These trees grow horizontally out into the air, their boughs counterweighted by swollen, water-filled galls that hang like bladders over the mile-deep drops. Their leaves are broad as shields, covered in a greasy, gray felt that collects the boiling mist and channels it back into the fissures. Everything drips. The sound of water is not the roar of a river, but the collective, deafening applause of trillions of large, warm droplets smacking against leather foliage, mingled with the wet *clack* of falling seedpods.

The scale is vast, yet it offers no emptiness. Looking out between two spires, the eye finds a third, a fourth, a twentieth, receding into the humid haze until they become purple silhouettes, each capped with a mop of dripping emerald, each trailing miles of fibrous root-lines that sway in the thermal updrafts like the legs of drifting jellyfish. 

Life here has abandoned the ground entirely, which lies lost and dark miles below the floor of cloud. In the canopy of the spires, life is thick and heavy. Enormous pale gastropods, their shells reinforced with concentric rings of dull, native bismuth, creep along the wet faces of the basalt, grazing on mats of sulfurous lichen. In the air between the needles, flocks of broad-winged, ribless things—neither birds nor bats, but flat, parchment-skinned gliders with three sets of forward-facing eyes—circle endlessly. They do not flap; they ride the hot columns of air that spout from the summits, descending only to wedge themselves into the damp hollows of the iron seams, where they drink the condensed mineral water directly from the rock.

Signs of handiwork exist, though they are worn so smooth by the continuous condensation that they resemble natural formations of fat or grease. High on the shoulder of the needle called *The Thumb*, there is a terrace carved not by chisels, but by something that softened the granite into porridge before withdrawing. A single, seamless ring of vitreous blue material, twelve paces wide, hangs cantilevered over an eight-thousand-foot drop, held in place by nothing at all; rain passes through its center without wetting the interior air, which remains dry and cool, a tiny pocket of forgotten winter preserved in the middle of the furnace. Inside it, small, six-legged things with carapaces like polished walnuts have built a brittle city of mud and regurgitated pulp, entirely indifferent to the impossible physics that keeps their home from falling.

There is very little wind. The air does not rush; it expands and contracts with a ponderous, tidal regularity that takes days to complete a single breath. In the morning, the heat is greasy and yellow, holding the water vapor close to the rock faces, so that the spires appear to be smoking. By afternoon, the moisture expands into vast, motionless thunderheads that do not discharge lightning, but simply let fall a hot, drenching sheet of water that smells of salt and old leaves. 

To look down is to look into a white abyss that never clears; to look up is to see the sky turn from yellow to a deep, bruised violet, where the hot, heavy envelope of this artificial basin meets the true, cold edge of the upper atmosphere, shimmering like gasoline on a puddle. Between the two, the spires endure: ancient, boiling, and buried in green.
