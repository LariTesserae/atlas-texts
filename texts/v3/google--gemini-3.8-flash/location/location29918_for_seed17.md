The light penetrates straight through, falling hundreds of fathoms without scattering. It is not the murky, silt-choked twilight of coastal shelves, but a hard, pale daylight that filters through miles of liquid as clear as cut ice. The water is bitterly cold—hovering fractions of a degree above freezing, perfectly still in its mass, yet vibrating with an all-consuming, physical sound. 

It is an elevated ocean, a drowned plateau lifted kilometers into the planetary crust where the water is held in a vast, continental basin whose rims lie far beyond any horizon. Below, the floor is not a soft mud bottom, but a fractured highland of dark, newly broken stone. These are young mountains, shorn off by tectonic uplift and immediately submerged: colossal fins of basalt, razor-sharp hornfels, and stepped terraces of pale diorite that drop off into vertiginous, blue-black chasms. There is no sand. There is no silt. Every edge is as fresh as a quarry fracture, clean and jagged, untouched by the slow rounding of biological decay or coastal wave action. 

No vegetation clings to the stone. There are no kelp forests, no films of microalgae, no crusts of lichen or moss. The rock faces are naked, washed perpetually by the bitter water, showing the raw crystalline grain of the planet's cooling interior. 

And yet, it is not silent. The sound is absolute, physical, and paralyzing. It does not rise and fall like surf; it is an invariant, monolithic roar that saturates the medium completely, vibrating in the marrow of anything within it. Part of it is the sheer hydraulic tonnage of an ocean perched at altitude, the infinite cavitation of deep currents shearing against the jagged stone peaks. But the lower frequencies are mechanical.

Miles apart, anchored into the sharp basalt ridges, stand isolated industrial titans. They are not cities or outposts, but solitary, gargantuan machines: deep-water siphon heads, four-hundred-foot titanium truss towers, and rotary impeller housings bolted directly into the bedrock with studs the thickness of redwood trunks. Built from cold-rolled steel and bronze that have dulled to a non-corrosive matte gray, they are relics of an industrial mobilization whose purpose has dissolved into deep time, yet their mechanisms have never halted. Within their housings, monstrous, un-lubricated iron shafts continue to rotate at locked, unwavering speeds, driven by the planetary siphon. Their grinding, low-frequency hum travels unhindered through the high-density water, combining with the rush of displaced brine into a deafening, continuous chord. 

The space between these relics is immense. One can look through five miles of clear, cold blue and see nothing at all—only the empty volume of the brine, punctuated by a single vertical cable, taut as a suspension bridge wire, vanishing upward toward the daylight surface, or the sudden, violent silhouette of a black stone needle rising three thousand feet from an abyss and ending in a point as sharp as a broken tooth. The emptiness is vast, clean, and terrifyingly vacant.

Despite the total absence of flora, the water is alive. The fauna does not dwell on the bottom; it belongs to the open, freezing volume. Vast, synchronized shoals of pale, armor-plated ribbonfish—each five or six yards long, blind, their skin a dull, mirror-like aluminum—drift through the empty spaces in formations that span miles, turning simultaneously without visible cues. They feed not on plants, but on the rich, sub-micron mineral flocs continuously cast off by the grinding machinery and the sheared rocks. 

Higher in the water column, cruising through the pale sunlight, move pale, cartilaginous giants: barrel-bodied, white-eyed leviathans with mouths like horizontal vents, their hides scored by the sharp rocks they occasionally brush against. They are utterly indifferent to the machines and the noise; their bodies have evolved around the deafening acoustic field, using the constant roar as a fixed sonic grid to navigate the continent-sized plateau. 

Nothing changes here. There are no seasons beneath this depth, no storms that can disturb a liquid blanket thousands of feet thick. The light waxes and wanes with a distant, pale sun overhead, but the cold remains fixed at its exact point of density. The massive, submerged bronze turbines turn their steady, unyielding revolutions. The water screams against the fresh edges of the torn rock, roaring into the clear, sunlit void, locked in an equilibrium that has existed for ages and shows no sign of ever breaking.
