There is no rock to stand on, though the ground feels firmer than a swamp. What lies beneath the feet for hundreds of leagues is an ancient, compacted mattress of lignified moss, petrified heartwood, and interlaced taproots that have grown over themselves for so long that the lower strata have turned into a dense, fibrous shale. The elevation is immense—this is a continental uplift, an enormous plateau hoisted thousands of meters into the sky—yet it does not freeze. Instead, it captures the warm, planetary thermal currents that rise from unseen lowlands, wrapping the tableland in a continuous, lukewarm bath of vapor.

The air has the temperature of a sleeping animal’s breath. It is thick with humidity, not violent like a storm, but persistent, an endless exhalation. Water exists here primarily as a film. It glazes every vertical surface, gathers into beads on the hair of the ferns, and hangs in the atmosphere as a quiet, pearlescent fug that reduces visibility to three hundred yards in any direction. The sun never shows its disk; it is merely a brighter patch of milk-colored heat that shifts from east to west behind a ceiling of motionless cloud.

Because the plateau was planed flat by glaciers or wind before the plants took over the mantle, there are no peaks, no ravines, no dramatic cuts. The topography consists entirely of slow, rolling mounds of vegetative tissue. The land undulates like a drowned meadow magnified to the scale of an empire. Every few miles, the sponge-earth gives way to shallow, terraced basins where water rests an inch deep over black, tannic peat. These basins do not drain into rivers; they simply sweat downward through the porous fiber, filtered by miles of living sieves, or evaporate back into the low fog.

The vegetation does not grow in competitive bursts. It has achieved an absolute, unhurried monopoly. There are trees, but they do not resemble the solitary timber of lower worlds; they are collective organisms, broad-topped and swollen with water, their barks like wet cork or elephant skin, seamless and spongy to the touch. Their branches touch and graft into one another, forming continuous elevated walkways thirty feet above the lower mire. Below this canopy, in the jade-colored twilight, hang curtains of pale, beard-like lichen and shelf fungi as wide as ox-carts, rubbery and cool, which secrete clear, odorless drops of sap into the moss below.

Sound cannot travel far through such mass. It dies within dozens of steps, smothered by the universal upholstery of liverworts and damp wood. What remains is a soft, wet friction: the steady, rhythmic *plink* of condensed mist dropping from high lobes; the low, hydraulic creak of colossal roots shifting beneath the surface as they swell with water; the occasional, distant slumping of an ancient peat-shelf giving way into a lower hollow with a sound like a heavy sack of grain dropped into mud.

Yet it is not devoid of will. Here and there, rising from the swells of living moss, are things shaped by hands that had no metal.

They are small, quiet architectures. A low ring of smoothed, river-washed cobbles that must have been carried from some forgotten borderland eons ago, now half-submerged in the creeping flesh of an enormous polypore. Hollows carved directly into the buttress-roots of the great cork-trees—chambers just large enough for a body to lie curled inside, the tool marks still visible as faint, shell-shaped gouges made with flint or bone, their edges long rounded by the slow creep of bark trying to heal the wound. Sometimes one comes upon a line of wooden pegs driven into the floor, black with age, marking a threshold that leads from one stretch of silent fern-waste into another that looks identical. There are no wheels, no joints of iron, no glass. Whatever culture exists—or existed—here moved like the water: entering the wood, resting briefly in its pores, and leaving behind only what the fibers could swallow without indigestion.

Life here moves at the speed of settling silt. The fauna are soft, heavy, and pale. Great terrestrial slugs, their skins patterned like the underside of birch bark, browse the fungal shelves, leaving behind wide, glassy trails that temporarily repel the mist. In the hollows beneath the root-archipelagos live broad-bodied creatures resembling wingless bats or blind badgers, covered in a waterproof, oily fleece. They do not run; they climb with a slow, four-handed grip through the maze of root-knees, their blunt snouts searching for the small, white crayfish that inhabit the flooded chambers deep inside the peat. Occasionally, high in the canopy where the vapor thins slightly into gray luminance, a flat, translucent bell drifts by—an aerial medusa or spore-sac, buoyant on pockets of warm biological gas, trailing thread-fine stingers that harvest the floating pollen.

There are times when the dim light turns from pale zinc to an amber tone, indicating that somewhere far above the cloud-deck, an afternoon is deepening. The air does not cool. If anything, the mist grows thicker, tasting faintly of cedar heartwood, warm loam, and sweet, fermenting acids. 

Walking here, if one can call it walking, is a rhythm of gentle sinking and soft recovery. The surface gives four inches under a footstep, exhaling a quiet hiss of displaced water, then slowly expands back into shape behind the heel. To travel across it is to realize that this shelf has forgotten the earth from which it grew; it has become its own floor, its own sky, and its own ocean, suspended between the two in a warm, permanent patience.
