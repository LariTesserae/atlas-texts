The fracture in the plateau is no wider than an amphitheater, perhaps four hundred paces from its northern lip to the southern drop, but it is deep enough to catch the cold like a cistern. The rock here has not had time to round itself. Its edges are clean, glass-sharp shales and split black ironstone that broke under some sudden, recent unseating of the bedrock. There are no talus slopes of gravel yet—only sheer, fractured sheets and stepped ledges that still smell of crushed flint and subterranean salt.

And into this tight wound, an entire town has been pressed.

It is built without mortar or square iron nails, yet it possesses the dense, layered calculation of an old port quarter. Dwellings of dry-stacked shale and roughly dressed basalt blocks lean hard against one another, sharing walls, sharing foundations, their heavy timber lintels notched with flint axes and bound at the corners with thick wraps of rawhide that have swollen, turned gray, and set hard in the wet. The roofs of the lower tier serve as the alleys and walkways for the tier above; where a roof gives way, a bridge of wet split birch poles spans the gap to the next door-frame. There is no open ground. If you dropped a bone needle from the upper lip of the chasm, it would bounce four or five times off oiled hide awnings, sodden reed mats, and projecting log beams before finding a dark pool at the floor.

The water does not fall from the sky so much as it leaks continuously from the rock itself. The severed strata in the canyon walls are pressurized; cold, clear seams of moisture seep from between the razor-edges of the stone, running down the masonry in hair-thin, quivering sheets that coat the walls in a constant gloss. Everything is slick to the touch, chilled just above the point of frost. When you place a palm against a door-post, a skin of water gathers beneath your fingers within seconds. Below, in the crawlways between the lowest huts, water moves along hand-chiseled troughs—shallow gutters that murmur softly as they carry the drainage toward an unseen sump beneath the central stack. 

The light at midday is flat and silvery, bouncing off the wet slate and the pale stone faces until the shadows are filled with a strange, shadowless gray. It does not penetrate deep into the lower levels, where tallow dips burn in stone hollows, smelling of sheep fat and cold lard, their flames small and stubborn in the damp draft.

Vegetation has had no time to establish, and the cold water does not invite it. There are no bushes, no reeds, no ferns. What little green exists is a film of dark liverwort clinging to the mortarless chinks between stones, and a greasy, nickel-colored lichen that crusts the birch timbers where they meet the rock. The wood does not rot quickly here—the water is too cold, too devoid of soil organisms—it merely cures into a spongy, petrified gray, soft enough to carve with a fingernail on the surface, iron-hard within.

Because the space is so choked with habitation, sound behaves strangely. It is mostly quiet, but never empty. A dropped mallet produces a dead, wet clatter that dies instantly against the hide roofs; speech does not carry across three thresholds, dampened by the constant, low-frequency hiss of seeping walls and the breath-fog of people moving in close quarters. You hear the creak of structural poles adjusting under the weight of waterlogged sod, the soft, rhythmic scrape of bone adzes trimming wet shingles, and the occasional dull plop of a water-swollen beetle falling from an awning into a cistern.

Fauna is small and rare, strictly adapted to the dark crevices where the water pools. In the deep stone bins carved out beneath the hearthstones, where the cold run-off collects before spilling deeper into the shale, pale, ribbon-like loaches with no eyes swim in slow circles, their whisker-barbels grazing the silt. Sometimes a gray-necked nightjar or a diver bird will drop into the cut from the upper world, perch for an hour on a cantilevered chimney-vent of stacked slate, its breast feathers wet from the draft, before beating its wings against the narrow air and climbing back into the wind above.

The place is not static. The ground breathes. Twice a day, the volume of water seeping from the fracture swells—perhaps driven by some distant, slow pulse in the subterranean table—and the inhabitants move quickly, silently, adjusting weir-stones with wooden levers, banking up river-clay around their sills, checking the tension of the hemp and sinew stays that anchor the higher tiers to the native rock. It is an urban density maintained by pure hand-craft and immediate vigilance: a city without iron or wheels, clinging to a newly cracked stone shelf, drowning very slowly, and continuously lifting itself by inches out of its own cold sweat.
