At the crest of a young tectonic spur, far above the cloud floor where the atmospheric pressure should be thin and freezing, there is a fracture in a wedge of black dacite no wider than a ribcage. 

Inside this vertical seam, the physical rules of the mountain end.

The interior of the cleft is a pocket of scalding, suffocating wetness. Water does not sit in pools here; it weeps constantly from microscopic vents in the stone, sheeted over walls of fresh, razor-edged volcanic glass that have not existed long enough for wind or weather to blunt their margins. The heat is heavy and wet, sitting near the boil—a trapped volcanic respiration that turns every square centimeter into a dense condensing engine. Moisture forms in thick, greasy beads on the upper overhangs and falls, not in a steady rain, but in heavy, fat drops that hang suspended for split seconds before snapping against the rock.

There is no soil, no moss, no green leaf or pale fungal root. The world here is entirely mineral and animal, locked together in an impossibly small, crowded volume.

The cleft is choked with life, so densely layered that the black stone beneath is only visible in flashes between moving bodies. These are not insects, though they possess the multi-jointed compactness of arthropods; they are warm-blooded, slick-skinned things, the size of a thumb or a closed fist, their hides the color of wet liver and boiled fat. They have no eyes—only broad, translucent heat-pits that pulse with the rhythm of their boiling blood. They clamber over one another in a silent, writhing strata, four or five deep, wedging their bodies into the sharp flutes of the dacite to soak in the hydrothermal seepage.

Despite their numbers, the cleft is almost utterly quiet. The violent boiling of the vent water produces no hiss; the acoustic properties of the crack seem deadened, as if the air itself were too heavy to carry vibration. The only sound is the wet, rhythmic *clop* of skin peeling off skin, and the dull, glass-on-glass clinking of their work.

For they work. These creatures exhibit an urgent, rudimentary industry.

With three-fingered limbs tipped with calcified nails, individuals on the outer tier chip deliberately at the young dacite, snapping off paper-thin blades of natural glass. They select specific shards, testing them against the calloused pads of their palms, and use them as scrapers. Two of them will pin a third—often one that has died from the heat or been crushed in the press—and systematically skin it with these stone flakes, stripping the tallow-like fat from the muscle in long, translucent ribbons. Others use pointed obsidian slivers to bore into the living rock, widening the micro-fissures to draw out richer pulses of boiling mineral brine, angling the flow toward their clustered broods. There is no language to it, no gesture of society, only an automatic, frantic mechanical competence driven by the pure pressure of their numbers.

Reality does not hold its shape cleanly within the pocket. 

The vertical axis wanders. In the back corner of the seam, where the heat is most punishing, the scalding runoff does not trickle downward toward the mountain's root; it runs sideways, pooling against a sheer vertical plane before evaporating into a vapor that smells of burnt salt and raw albumen. Looking directly into the narrowest depth of the crack, the perspective distorts: the fissure appears no deeper than a forearm’s length from the outside, yet the tiers of slick, working bodies recede into an impossible, murky distance, receding through hundreds of crowded shelves that could not fit within the stone's external mass. 

The light that enters from the high mountain sky is clean and neutral, but once it crosses the threshold of the fracture, it fractures into strange, flat planes. Shadows fall toward the light source instead of away from it. A creature moving from the left wall to the right seems to double momentarily, two identical bodies scraping the same ribbon of flesh, until one slips into a non-existent angle between the rocks and is gone, leaving only the sound of its glass scraper clicking once against the floor.

They breed and die at the speed of an open fire. In the cooler margins near the mouth, where the cold mountain wind occasionally bites into the steam, discarded glass flakes and tiny, scraped ribcages accumulate in heaps, cemented together by silica-heavy water that hardens into a white, porcelaneous crust within hours. Nothing leaves the fissure. The mountain outside is sheer, frozen, and empty, dropped into an abyss of blue air, but inside this single wound, the meat and the glass continue to grind against each other in the hot, wet dark, perfectly self-contained, silent, and furiously busy.
