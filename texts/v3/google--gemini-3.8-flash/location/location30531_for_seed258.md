There is no dirt, and there is no stone. The floor of this high shelf, suspended several thousand meters above whatever crust lies beneath, is a continuous, joined floor of pale, living heartwood. It has been poured or trained—grown to a specification so strict and complete that the grain runs strictly north-to-south in bands forty cubits wide, interlocking with mortise seams that were never cut by a saw, but sealed by the tree’s own wet cambium as it met itself. 

Everything here is made, and everything here is flora. Yet it is not a forest. It is a work of civil engineering rendered entirely in pulp, vascular bundles, and high-tensile xylem. 

Because the density is so low, the landscape is mostly air and distance. You stand upon a causeway of woven cane as thick as a city wall, looking out over a gulf of three or four miles toward the next structure: a solitary fluted pylon, hundreds of feet tall, tapering into a ribbed cradle that holds nothing but empty sky. The intervals between these monuments are enormous, clean, and terrifyingly open. There is no underbrush to crowd the sightline, no clutter of leaves. The few leaves that exist are the size of skiffs, thick as boiled leather, pinned at regular intervals along the tension cables that span the abyss. They do not flutter; they are swollen with water and held rigid by thick, hydraulic ribs.

It is loud here—a deep, pneumatic roaring that never ascends into a shriek and never falls into silence. The entire basin is an apparatus for the movement of warm water. The towers are hollow; they act as massive siphons, drawing thousands of gallons of condensation per minute up from the white, featureless cloud deck that sits miles below, then venting it outward through deliberate orifices. High above, arching viaducts—wide troughs grown from single, contiguous shoots of pale gourd-wood—carry rivers across the sky from one sparse tower to the next. The troughs weep constantly. Their undersides are fringed with long, translucent taproots that drip warm, distilled rain in straight, heavy curtains toward the voids below. 

The heat is humid, like the air inside an unventilated greenhouse at noon, but the light is bare and clear. The sun strikes the wet, blond wood and turns the floor into a glare of white-gold reflections. Where the water sprays from the overhead siphons, the air is thick with rainbows that never waver, fixed in place by the absolute constancy of the jets.

Nothing here has aged. The bark has the moist, tender sheen of a shoot that broke soil twenty minutes ago. There is no decay, no leaf-rot, no fungal bloom softening the joints, no drift of dead matter in the corners of the flumes. The wood does not weather. It emerged into this geometry fully formed, slick with birth-juices, and immediately locked into stasis. The sap maintains its pressure; the conduits do not silt up; the joints do not swell or shrink. It is an embryonic landscape that has refused its own future, frozen on the morning of its completion.

Occasionally, in the long shadow cast by an aqueduct, there is movement: a heavy, soft-shelled creature, pale as turnip flesh, moves across the grain on eight blunt pegs. It has no eyes, only broad, sensory pads that press against the wet timber to drink the vibrations of the rushing water. It does not graze—there is nothing loose to eat. It merely anchors itself to the wood, drawing whatever faint starch leaches through the water-film, then detaches to crawl another hundred yards toward the next conduit. These things are few, separated by vast plains of yellow timber, indifferent to the roaring sky.

When you walk, your weight depresses the floor slightly, like stepping on damp cork, but it yields no footprint. The moisture that wells up around your feet is warm, slick, and slightly bitter, tasting of green wood and tannin. If you look down through the expansion joints between the massive timbers, you do not see soil; you see further levels of the same architecture, descending through mist and sunbeams into blue infinity—trusses of iron-hard vine, hollowed buttresses, and the sheer vertical flumes of taproots dropping like plumb lines into the blinding, empty air.
