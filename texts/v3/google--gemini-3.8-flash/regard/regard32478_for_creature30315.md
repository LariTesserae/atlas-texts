A wise power does not mistake a monument for a prison, nor does it mistake an eternal duty for peace. 

It would come without a tear in the sky, for to shatter the white-gold dome would be the crude vanity of a lesser god, spilling cold gravel and storm-winds across an expanse that had never learned how to shiver. It would not bring rot, nor would it blow out the fever-pitch heat like a candle, knowing that to chill this timber suddenly would cause every dry-pegged joint to warp, splitting the floor from horizon to horizon until the inland sea folded into the bottomless nothing below.

Instead, the power would arrive as weight. 

It would descend at the center of one of the roofless chambers—not through the glare, but by simply being there, sitting upon the four-foot wall of raw-pork brick as though it had been seated there since the adze was lifted. 

It would take a shape that the floor could understand. Not an angel of light—there was already enough light here to blind an empire—but a creature of cool, unglazed stone, dark as wet slate, with broad knees and shoulders that did not sweat. Its presence would not break the three millimeters of water; it would rest upon the wall, its feet dangling inches above the meniscus, casting the first thing this world had ever known: 

A shadow.

The shadow would fall across the wet terracotta like a bolt of dark wool thrown over a mirror. 

It would not be cold, but it would be *less*. It would be a narrow corridor of violet and gray, twelve feet long, lying across the shallow pan. Where the shadow touched the water, the blinding glare would simply cease to shout. The red clay beneath would show its true color—not the bleached, sun-struck pink of the scalded plain, but a deep, autumnal ochre, rich with iron and the memory of river beds.

The saucer-beetles suspended sixteen inches above the four corners of the chamber would not flee. But their violet muscles, which had pulsed once every twelve seconds since the beginning of time, would stutter. 

*Chut.* 

A pause of fourteen seconds. Then sixteen. 

One of them would tilt slightly on its invisible tether, its glazed carapace dipping into the edge of the slate-colored shadow. As the dark crossed its lacquered rim, the tiny inverted basin of burnt umber would lose its buoyant dispute with the floor. It would sink. It would not crash; it would drift down through the thick air like a loosened leaf, its rim touching the surface of the water with an infinitesimally soft *snick*. 

The water would not swallow it. The beetle would float there, its hollow belly at rest on the terracotta, its pulse slowing to a long, dry click once every hour. For the first time, it would no longer have to work to keep the sky at bay.

The smooth-shouldered one in the pen would turn. 

It would not turn with fear, for it possessed no organs for alarm, but with that slow, hydraulic swing of sixty stone of tallow-colored brawn. Its broad, blunt face would point toward the shadow. The gristly, five-lobed pads of its hands would remain resting in the gray furrow it had just drawn—that endless, rhythmic stroke keeping the mortar from the terror of setting.

The wise power would not command it to cease. It would know that to pull the creature’s hands from the seam would be an amputation of its purpose; it would die of an unbearable emptiness before the lime could even whiten on its fingers.

Instead, the power would reach down from the wall. 

Its hand—rough, dry, smelling of nothing more violent than crushed flint and rain that had fallen ten thousand miles away—would lay itself over the gray-white back of the smooth-shouldered thing’s hand. 

It would not push. It would simply take half the weight.

For three hundred days, they would sit together in the shadow of the low wall, pressing the thumb along the line. *Schlock.* The sound would soften. The gray paste displaced by their joint movement would no longer be smeared onto the creature’s thigh to be reabsorbed; the slate-dark visitor would catch the surplus clay in its own palm, rolling it between its fingers into small, dense marbles, perfectly round, perfectly smooth.

And as they worked, the power would speak—not with a voice, for the dense, wet silence was too heavy for words, but with an intention pressed directly into the grit of the mortar:

*It is done enough.* 
*The sill is true.* 
*The house will not fall down if the mason washes his hands.*

At the end of the year, the smooth-shouldered one would draw its broad thumb to the end of the brick. It would reach the peg-joint where the pork-colored masonry met the pale timber floorboards. 

There, for the first time, it would not lift its arm to begin the seam anew. 

Its gristly fingers would twitch, confused by the sudden stillness of its own bloodless meat. The tallow-colored flesh across its shoulders would loosen, sagging down over the low parapet not with exhaustion, but with the sudden, luxurious luxury of gravity accepted. It would lean its blunt, faceless head against the slate-cool knee of the visitor. 

The gray mortar beneath their fingers would skin over. 

It would not dry with a crack. It would cure with a quiet, mineral sigh—a faint release of heat that rose into the shadow like a curl of steam from tea. The bond would knit, dense and permanent, locking the bricks into a finality they had been denied for eons. 

And then, the wise power would reach out with a single, dry fingernail and touch the drop of water that hung on the vertical face of the pork-brick—the drop that had hung swollen to the brink of falling since the world was first smoothed.

The power would touch its swollen underbelly.

The tension would break.

The drop would fall.

It would drop four inches through the shadow and strike the shallow pan beneath with a clear, sharp, ringing *ping*—a sound so thin, so clean, that it would travel through the floorboards across an inland sea, vibrating through the dry wooden pegs, through the adze-scalloped timbers, down into the bottomless tiers of wet tiles.

Down in the dark, the great canal-long wedge—the elbow of the world—would hear it.

It would not startle. But its slow, blind drift between the third and fourth tiers would ease to a halt. It would wedge its immense mass firmly into the lime-milk seams, not to shift, not to turn over in a feverish half-sleep, but to anchor. It would settle into the stone wharf like an oak pile driven home, feeling the finality of the floor above it set into bone. 

Out on the open plains of shining terracotta, the tapir-beasts would hear the drop. 

One of them would pause. Its wooden hooves, planted square upon a pegged joint, would feel the wood beneath them cease to pull. The capillary draw would stop; the timber was no longer parched, no longer demanding to be ballasted against the void. 

The beast would lift its long, jawless snout. It would not discharge its lukewarm brine onto the sill. Instead, its horn-plated lips would part. 

From its mouth, a breath would go out—a long, misty plume of air that smelled of boiled turnip and sweet heartwood. 

And for the first time, the meniscus of the three-millimeter sea would wrinkle. 

A ripple, no higher than a hair’s width, would leave the beast’s hooves. It would travel across the shallow rectangular pan, hit the low mortar ridge, and lap—softly, with the sound of a tongue touching the roof of a mouth—against the brick.

The benevolent power would not dismantle the world. It would not banish the heat, nor would it strip the plants of their thick, boiled-leather fronds. It would leave the floor bottomless; it would leave the joinery tight.

It would simply stay sitting on the four-foot wall, casting its twelve-foot shadow, letting the shadow stretch—by the width of an adze-stroke every century—toward the eastern horizon. 

It would give them an evening. 

Not a night—night would be too dark, too much like the void beneath the tiles. But an evening: long, amber, cooling by half a degree every hundred years, until the water began to lap against the bricks in rhythm, and the creatures could lie down upon their sides in the wet clay, close their flat yellow eyes, and learn what it means to wait for a morning.
