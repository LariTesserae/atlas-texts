The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have been an atrocity. A single lumen of the noon that bleached the scree outside would have scorched their pearl-blind retinas like hot needles; it would have boiled the shadow-mats of clubmoss, withered the scarlet thread of the welters’ gills, and driven the thousands of loaf-things into a frantic, suffocating crush against the back wall, trampling their young into the grease.

Nor did it arrive as warmth. To raise the cleft from five degrees to fifteen would have set the molds running wild. The delicate, slow machinery of the necrophores—whose acid mouthparts were calibrated to the sluggish decay of near-freezing silt—would have been overwhelmed by aggressive green rots. The tallow in the pelts of the large ones would have thinned, gone rancid, and sloughed from their bellies, exposing their pale, unpigmented hides to fungal boils.

A foolish benevolence would have wanted to open the roof. It would have looked upon the hunched, forehead-heavy creatures sitting in their own ancient tallow and seen prisoners in need of an sky; it would have wept over their lack of words, their lost astronomy, their fingers that knew only the rhythm of *clack* and the soft rinds of beetles. It would have brought them fire, and in doing so, turned their ninety paces of saturated paradise into a grease-fed chimney.

Wisdom, however, has a heavy hand that knows how to remain shut.

The power entered not from the sky, for the sky had nothing to offer this place, but through the stone itself. It came up through the deep roots of the massif, travelling along the slip-fault as an infinitesimal easing of pressure—the way a sleeping sleeper shifts a shoulder to let the blood return to an arm. 

It observed the clockwork. It watched the female lift the river cobble, strike the beetle, and feed the fat to the throat-clinging child. It watched the loaf-things breathe their turnip-scented breath into the hollow of her groin. It watched the blind welter yawn its thirty gallons of mud, and the saucer-wide isopods drift through the moss like pale thoughts through a slow mind.

It saw that there was no tragedy here. The tragedy had happened fifty thousand years ago, outside, on the plains, where the species had burned its bridges, hunted its gods, screamed itself hoarse with syntax, and eventually ground its bones to chalk under the tread of empires. What was left here in the dark was not a ruin; it was a finished sentence. It was the soft, grease-slick period at the end of the long, hysterical paragraph of the world above.

They did not want to stand up. Standing up was for things that had to run from teeth. They did not want to speak. Speaking was for things that lived too far apart to feel the drumming of a heel through wet peat.

Therefore, the power made three adjustments, none of which produced a sound, none of which disturbed the four-degree chill.

First, it attended to the keystone. Eighty feet above, where the walls leaned together like the teeth of a worn zipper, the plug of sod and gravel had been slowly thinning. In three hundred years—a finger-snap in the cleft’s time—a spring thaw on the alpine ridge would have punched through, sending twelve thousand tons of mud and glacial melt roaring down the throat of the rift, scouring the travertine benches bare, drowning the loaf-things, and washing the great welters out onto the sunlit scree below to bake and burst.

The power reached into the mortar of that plug. It did not turn it to granite; granite would have blocked the weep. Instead, it encouraged the ancient, fibrous roots of the high alpine sedges to knit tighter, petrifying the detritus just enough to hold the weight of a glacier, while opening the microscopic pores so that the water would continue to weep down the fluted gutters at the exact speed of sixty drops a minute, forever.

Second, it looked upon the mountain's future. It saw that down in the green valleys, men with iron drills and yellow machines would one day smell the limestone, seeking stone for cement to build their brief, square hives. It laid a quiet, repelling knot in the geological strata miles below—a fault of bitter, pyritic shale that would turn any surveyor's core-drill aside, making the massif appear hollow, brittle, and worthless to those who measure the earth in tons.

The third thing it did was smaller still, and perhaps the only thing that could be called an act of tenderness.

It entered the stone bench at the very rear of the cleft—the sixty-foot strip buffed to gray butter by five hundred centuries of sleeping shoulders.

The creatures lived long lives, but they were not free of the damp. Even with four inches of tallow-blubber, the cold of the limestone crept into the spongy bone of their spines during the decades they sat leaning against it, rocking slightly from side to side to ease the stiffening of their joints. It was not a agony, only a dull, persistent stone-ache that accompanied them into old age until they slid down into the runs for the necrophores to clean.

The power did not warm the stone. But it altered the lattice of the calcite beneath that greasy smear. It made the limestone *drink* the ache.

When the eldest mother, her belly fur sparse and graying, backed her shoulders into the familiar groove that evening, she settled her rump into the moss-sponge, drew two loaf-things into the crook of her knees, and let her weight fall against the rock.

She waited for the familiar, cold pull in her lower ribs—the slight, heavy knot that had lived between her shoulder blades since her third child was weaned.

It was not there.

The rock was still four degrees. The tallow on her pelt did not soften. The water still wept from the runnels with a liquid *glop*. But the stone behind her was no longer merely indifferent; it was receptive. It held the shape of her spine as though it had been carved for her marrow alone. It drew the stiffness out of her gristle and took it deep into the dark mountain, where there was plenty of room for cold things to be cold without hurting anyone.

The old female let her rubbery nostrils flare. Her leaf-shaped nose-leaf twitched, sampling the dark. The smell was the same: river bottom, peat-rot, beetle fat, and the sweet turnip breath of the loaf-thing snuffling at her thigh. There was no light. There was no voice.

She lifted her left heel and drummed twice against the silt—a low, seismic thud that traveled thirty yards through the sod.

*Safe,* the strike said through the bellies of the others. *The silt is deep. The water is running.*

A dozen yards away, a smaller one answered with a shift of its flank. The hollow *clack* of an anvil-stone sounded, once, as a fresh water beetle was cracked in the dark.

The power drew its attention back into the stone, folded itself into the slip-fault, and became as motionless as the rest of the mountain—leaving the little clockwork ark to tick on, greasy, cold, and whole, until the sun should finally tire of the sky and leave them the rest of the universe to sleep in.
