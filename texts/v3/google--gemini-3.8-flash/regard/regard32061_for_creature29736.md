An unwise power would have brought the sun.

It would have parted the ceiling of milk-white vapor with a sweep of its hand, believing that light is always a mercy. It would have dried the sponge, burned away the jade twilight, and turned the lukewarm air into a brisk, blue autumn. It would have cured the white lung-rot with a breath, straightened the spines of the makers, given them thumbs that could grip an axe, and shown them how to extract the copper sleeping miles beneath the shale. And in doing so, it would have killed them all within twenty turns of the sky—scorch-blistering their fat-cushioned feet on parched wood, suffocating the tea-colored breathers in hard cakes of caked mud, and deafening the sleepers whose ears were tuned only to the damp subterranean creak of swelling roots.

A wise power does not cure a world of itself.

When the presence arrived, it came not as a descending shape or a trumpet of air, for any displacement of vapor would have caused a draft, and a draft would have alarmed the aerial medusae, tumbling them from their warm thermal pockets. 

It came as an attention. A vast, patient density, filtering downward through the cloud-deck like heat soaking into deep water, until it was diffuse enough to occupy the miles of wet fiber without displacing a single drop of mist.

It lingered first in the grafted branches, watching two fleece-beasts meet upon an aerial bridge. It observed them press their greasy, tubular bellies together, rolling over each other like wet sacks of flour until they swapped places, neither having shown anger, neither having hurried. It felt the twenty-two pulses a minute of the makers resting in their root-cells. It smelled the sweet, sour fermentation of the peat-shale, the ancient digested forests turning slowly into stone miles down, and understood: *here, nothing has ever been hurried, and therefore nothing has ever been broken.*

It examined the makers’ lives and saw no squalor in them. To live seventy years in the warmth of a sleeping beast’s breath; to wake and sleep to the hydraulic music of trees; to know oneself so thoroughly a part of the floor that one’s very passing is merely a seasoning of salt for the next shelf of fungus—this was not a fall from grace. It was a grace so complete that it had never required a name.

Yet the power saw, too, the peg.

It saw the maker curled on its left side in the amber-lined cell, pressing the mastoid bone behind its ear against the peg of petrified pine, straining through the silence to hear if another heart still beat ten miles away across the mist. 

It saw how they loved the stones.

It saw an old one—her mane grayed with wood-dust, her lungs rattling with the slow, white weeping of fungus spores—dragging her three-pointed frame through the sponge toward a ring of cobbles that had been placed three thousand years before. The maker’s knuckles were bleeding where the spade-shaped nails had split against an exposed knee of ironwood. Her breath whistled like an open reed. She was crawling toward the only thing on the continental shelf that did not yield: the gray, river-washed granite, smooth and cold, that promised that somewhere in the universe, a thing could exist that did not turn to mud.

The power saw that the ring was sinking. 

The polypores were slow, but they were inexorable. The rubbery, shelf-like flesh of a giant fungus had already swallowed four of the seven stones, rolling its pale yellow lip over their mineral crowns. In another generation, the granite would be buried four feet down in the fibrous shale, lost to the hands of those who needed to remember what firmness was.

And so, the power acted. Its intervention was so quiet, so small, that had a maker been watching with human eyes, it would have looked like nothing at all.

First, it attended to the borders. 

Around the edge of the great uplift, where the plateau dropped in miles of vertical basalt into the lower world where other kinds of creatures ran, conquered, and burned, the power knotted the thermals. It reinforced the great updrafts of warm planetary air, weaving them into a permanent, vertical gale that tore any seed from the lowlands to shreds and tossed any ascending machine or winged beast back into the low valleys. It made the plateau invisible from below: an unbroken ceiling of featureless vapor that appeared to any curious eye as an empty atmospheric dead-zone. It gave the tableland its forever.

Then, it descended into the peat beneath the stone-ring.

It did not blast the polypore or wither the moss. Instead, it set its will beneath the granite cobbles, miles deep into the lignified shale where the ancient roots had turned to stone. Very slowly, over the space of forty daylight shifts, it pushed upward with the gentle hydraulic lift of a seedling breaking soil. 

The seven stones did not leap from the ground. They simply refused to drown. The fungus, finding the mineral strangely unyielding and cold with a deep, subterranean coolness, retreated half an inch, its yellow lip curling back. The stones remained upon the swell: bare, wet with condensation, washed clean of acid, perfectly balanced on the crest of the peat-cushion so that no matter how many centuries of leaf-litter fell, the gray granite would always stay precisely at the surface of the green velvet.

Lastly, the power came to the nursery-hollow where a child lay, her chin resting on the listening-floor.

Her mother was far off, three miles into the west, waiting beside a water-terrace for the blind crayfish to surface. The child was lonely with that vast, quiet loneliness that belongs only to creatures whose world has no sky. She had tapped twice with a piece of petrified wood—*tock, tock*—asking the timber if the world was still there. 

The wood had carried it, but her mother had not heard; she was too deep in the hunt, her chin pressed against the root-bark, her ears filled with the slow, distant slump of an ancient shelf settling into the shale.

The power did not speak in a voice. A voice would have split her skull like a wedge.

Instead, the power leaned against the continental taproot, twenty miles down, where the oldest heartwood met the bedrock of the world. It touched the grain of the petrified pine with a single, infinitely gentle pulse of intention.

Ten miles away, the child’s molars buzzed.

It was not her mother’s tap. It was deeper, slower, resonated at a frequency that did not come from any living branch, but from the shelf beneath the shelf—the ancient, buried spine of the continent itself.

*Tock.*

The child lifted her chin. The air was lukewarm. The mist hung at the entrance of her cell, pearlescent and still, smelling of cedar and sweet, fermenting rain. 

She set her jaw back against the floor, waiting.

Three beats of her slow heart passed. Then, from through the grain of the wood, through the fat of her jaw, through the marrow of her small skull, came the return: a soft, iron-hard resonance that hummed with the mineral permanence of granite, yet was as warm as the breath of an otter.

*You are on the wood,* the tap told her, without a single word. *The wood is on the stone. The stone is holding. Sleep.*

The child drew her greasy, bark-braided mane across her face to filter the damp air. Her breath slowed to the rhythm of the dripping canopy. Outside, an aerial medusa drifted through the jade fog, trailing its silver strings, and down in the black silt, the breathers opened their toothless mouths to the warm rain.

The power remained, not above them, but beneath their feet: a silent, unmoving floor that would never let them fall.
