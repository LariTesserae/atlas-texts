The Power that turned its attention upon the plateau of Oun did not possess eyes, but it possessed an immense, unhurried comprehension. It had watched stars curdle into iron; it had watched civilizations of spun light dissolve into entropy; it knew the mathematics of bone. 

A foolish power, seeing Oun, would have wept. 

A foolish power, moved by that shallow pity which is only the vanity of the witness, would have reached out with broad, luminous hands to "heal" the plateau. It would have ripped away the white roof of sulfur-haze to let in the blue sky; it would have cast down sweet rains; it would have scattered seeds of barley, grass, and cedar across the terraces.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered every living thing in Oun within six hours.

The unshielded sun would have cooked the turnip-skinned children alive, blistering their sebum-coated flesh until it peeled from their barrel ribs. The fresh rain would have choked the sled-oxen, whose lungs required the thick, alkaline broth of the plumes. The roots of the sprouting clover would have found no earth, only dead basalt and iron slag, rotting immediately into a black, septic slime that would foul the cisterns. The mast-gulls, blind to any sky not made of steam, would have wheeled in terror into the freezing upper ether and died of burst hearts. 

Wisdom does not shatter what it finds. Wisdom sits down in the grease.

The Power understood that Oun was not a fallen world awaiting redemption; it was an extreme, magnificent organism that had achieved a terrifying, calcified perfection. Life here had made a bargain with the absolute: it had surrendered chlorophyll, surrendered color, surrendered the songbird and the dry leaf, and in exchange it had built an empire of fat and iron that could endure the cooling of the stars. 

The people of Oun were not captives waiting to be rescued from their bodies. Their thick ribs were masterpieces of pressure-adaptation. Their horn-padded fingers were miracles of utility. To erase their struggle would be to declare their millions of dead—the men crushed into hydraulic lime, the women whose tallow greased the winches—to have been meaningless errors in a ledger.

And yet, the Power was benevolent. It felt the bone-deep ache that vibrated through the bedrock. It knew the terror of the fifteen-year-old diver wedging his shoulders feet-first into a dark siphon thirty yards down, tasting old fat through a gull’s-crop bladder, his heart hammering against the iron walls until the suction seized him. It knew the raw misery of the scraper-men whose tongues were permanently ulcerated from the alkaline curd of the mush. It knew the long, heavy exhaustion of the sled-bulls, pushing flank-to-flank for ten hours in the slick slag until their knees cracked.

Therefore, the Power did what only the truly wise and loving can do: it made no spectacle. It wrought no wonders that could be seen from an aqueduct. It altered not a single law of the plateau, but instead slipped into the seams of the world like oil between bronze teeth.

***

The first thing it touched was the **mush**.

It did not change the color; it remained the gray, glistening paste scraped from the underside of the flumes, smelling of iron-scale and dead crabs. But deep within the colonial bacteria that metabolized the sulfur-wash, the Power tilted three molecular bonds. A subtle shift in the enzyme chain—nothing more.

That evening, around the twelve-foot communal cauldrons in the dry-dock wards, the lightermen filled their pewter pots as they had for six hundred years. They blew the steam from the surface and lifted the heavy spoons to their mouths. 

The mush still tasted of brine and wet iron. It still filled the belly like hot lead. But as the gray paste slid over the tongue, it no longer burned. The corrosive bite, the sharp lye-sting that wore the enamel from their teeth and left their gums bleeding, had vanished. It left the mouth not with an alkaline crust, but with a clean, cool sensation, like water drawn from the very bottom of a deep well before the first smelter was lit. 

When the men finished eating, they found they did not need to reach for their bronze spatulas to scrape their tongues. They sat on the basalt steps, their spoons dangling from thick fingers, looking at one another through the steam with dull, slow surprise. They did not have the words to praise it; they merely grunted, wiped their chins with greasy hide cuffs, and discovered that their throats did not ache.

***

Next, the Power entered the **siphons**.

It went down into the wet, lightless labyrinths beneath the stamping vats, where the warm grease of four centuries sat stagnant in the elbows of the conduits. 

It did not clear the pipes. To clear them would make the divers obsolete, and a caste without work in Oun was a caste that was boiled down early. Instead, the Power altered the *tension* of the grease. 

Three days after the change, a boy named Kael—whose skin was mottled with the slate-gray scales of the third-vat divers—greased his ears and hips with ox-butter, took his gull-crop bladder between his teeth, and slid feet-first into Conduit Seven to dislodge a bloated pipe-eel that had choked the main draft of the Saint-Malo locks.

He expected the choking panic. He expected the blind, black suffocation, the grease packing into his nostrils, the tearing suction that pulled at the flesh of his calves like the mouths of dead men. 

He found the pipe. He kicked his heels into the blockage. But as he worked with his short iron spade, the dark was not hateful. 

The grease seemed to hold him gently, parting around his limbs with a soft, yielding warmth that supported his ribs rather than crushing them. The suction of the siphon, usually a mindless, tearing brute, seemed to pulse with a regular, breathing rhythm that gave him purchase. When his spade bit through the eel's gristle and the great plug gave way, the returning rush of forty tons of water did not smash him against the bronze grates. It lifted him. It cradled his scaled back and floated him smoothly up through the manhole into the light of the sluice-gang, coughing and gasping, his spade still in his horn-padded hand.

The old master-lighterman who hauled him out by his hair looked down at the boy, then down at the siphon.

"The pipe was soft," the boy muttered, shivering in his ox-butter, unable to explain why his chest did not feel broken.

"The head-pressure was low," the master grunted, not believing it, but marking the valve-log with an unbroken tally. 

***

Then, the Power touched the **sleep of the Dredgers**.

It could not give them pillows of down or linen sheets; such things would rot into wet mold in forty-eight hours beneath the weeping masonry. The people continued to sleep upon their slabs of polished slate, their heads resting upon lead ingots and carved lumps of tallow-wax.

Yet, as they lay down in their rectangular slots cut into the dead-weight basalt of the arches, the stone seemed to lose its dead, stubborn chill. The slate did not become soft, but it ceased to take more from their bones than it gave. The lead blocks under their necks seemed to balance their heavy skulls at precisely the angle that allowed the gray fluid of their brains to clear itself of the nickel-dust fumes.

And into their sleep—a sleep that had always been an empty, black exhaustion punctuated by the dread of the trip-hammer—the Power introduced the first dreams Oun had ever known.

They were not dreams of meadows or skies they could not comprehend. The Power was too kind for such cruelty. 

A lighterman would close his eyes to the hum of the bronze gears, and he would dream of *balance*. He would dream of standing upon the deck of an iron scow that moved without hauling-cables across a water so calm, so frictionless, that it seemed to be made of liquid glass. He would feel the weight lift from his knees. He would feel his father’s hand, thick and horn-fingered, resting upon his shoulder—not driving him to the winch, but merely resting there, warm through the hide-smock, while the barge drifted forward into a milky, silver peace that had no work at the end of it.

In the morning, the men woke with the same turnip-colored skin, the same calloused ribs. But their eyes, which had been flat and pewter-colored for thirty dynasties, carried a tiny, interior spark. They looked at the great four-story waterwheels not as engines of punishment, but as old companions in the mist.

***

To the **Candelers**, sitting in the drained basins among the skeletons of the forgotten ships, the Power gave no voice, for they had chosen silence. 

It did not blow out their small blue wicks. But it blessed their tallow. 

As the hermits boiled the yellow grease skimmed from the reservoirs, the blue flames that rose from the earthenware cups ceased to burn with merely a passive light. When a Candeler sat before his wick, watching the buckler-crabs climb the basalt steps, the flame would occasionally emit a single, microscopic spark that smelled—faintly, so faintly that a dog could not have named it—of something utterly alien to the plateau.

It was the smell of dry, sun-warmed stone. 

Not stone washed in sulfuric sweat. Not stone greased with the run-off of foundries. But the smell of high, barren granite that had known three thousand years of clean wind.

The Candelers did not know what the smell was. They had no word for *dry*. But when that tiny scent touched their nostrils, an ancient, forgotten circuit in the base of their skulls would close. Their shoulders would drop from their ears. They would sit by their small fires and smile into their laps, their yellow horn-pads resting gently on their knees, feeling that the dead beast whose skull they inhabited was not angry, was not weeping in agony, but was simply resting, as they were.

***

And finally, the Power entered **The Stroke**.

The Great Basin pumps could not be stopped. To halt the pumps was to drown the lower wards and dry the upper flumes, collapsing the lungs of the city. 

The trip-hammer struck its quarter-hour measure; the phosphor-bronze gears turned in their bone-oil; the massive pistons drew back against their vacuum seals with their forty-second groan: *hiss-shuck... hiss-shuck.*

The Power did not quiet the machine. It *tuned* it.

It adjusted the clearance of the valve-seats by the thickness of a grease-molecule. It softened the strike of the iron counterweights against the basalt stops. It smoothed the tooth-mesh of the four-story wheels.

The noise remained deafening to an outsider, but within the city, the acoustics shifted. The sound was no longer an assault that battered the eardrums into deafness and shook the fillings from the jaw. It became an underpinning. It became a low, resonant drone that sat beneath the water-wash like the purr of a vast, sleeping animal. 

The mast-gulls along the cornices stopped throbbing their crimson throat-pouches in panic at every shift of the sluices. The waves of red meat subsided into a soft, steady pink. The six-winged birds settled their heavy white chins into their breast-down, closed their horn-lids, and for the first time in five hundred years, their leathery wings did not twitch in their sleep.

***

The Power remained on the plateau for a long time, watching.

It built no temples. It carved no commandments into the wet basalt. It left behind no prophets with fevered eyes and blistered tongues. It understood that a god who demands to be noticed is merely a tyrant looking for an audience, and Oun had enough masters in its iron and its stone.

When the Power finally withdrew its attention, turning toward other clusters of light in the cold dark, Oun remained Oun.

It was still a place of fat and lime, of blind river-worms and grease-boilers. Men still died young, their ribs still went into the kilns, and their children still wore the belly-skins of scavenged gulls. The sky was still an unbroken ceiling of white greenhouse haze that cast no distinct shadows.

Yet the lighterman on the Great Aqueduct of Saint-Malo, leaning over the greasy balustrade at the change of the watch, felt that the work had somehow ceased to be a weight that was breaking him. He wiped the nickel-dust from his mouth, looked out over the leagues of identical, water-smoothed courtyards, and felt the warm, fat air fill his great lungs without resistance.

Below him, the wheel turned once every seven minutes. The piston completed its stroke. 

And in the silence between the groan of the bronze and the fall of the water, the stone did not feel like a tomb. It felt like a home.
