A clumsy benevolence would have wept. 

It would have seen the total absence of green, the thirst that does not even know it is thirsty, the blind things scraping their own limbs into lintels, and it would have let fall a tear of divine pity. And that single drop—bloating, hot, and heavy with surface tension—would have struck a third-story cornice like a shell of boiling vitriol. The water would have hissed into the flint-glass foam, bursting the capillary walls of forty in-dwellers, turning their brittle parchment into a foul, steaming paste, shorting out the spinning hair-whorls of the striders, and dissolving the silent avenues into a collapsing slurry of lime.

Wisdom, however, knows that to impose one world’s grace upon another is merely an ambient form of murder.

The power that arrived at the lip of the siltite basin possessed neither wings to cast shadows nor breath to disturb the dust. It had no temperature to bleed into the cellar-cold air. It arrived merely as an *attention*—an immense, patient weight of noticing that settled over the square like an additional layer of the sky’s dry haze.

It observed the knife-keen edges of the cliff. It saw the wire-cut freshness of the stone. And because its wisdom encompassed deep time, it saw what the basin could not: that beyond this four-block trench of silence, the outer world was wet, erratic, and vast.

Somewhere, leagues away, a front of warm, low-pressure air was already gathering over a shallow sea. Within three seasons, or thirty, the prevailing winds would tilt. A gray shelf of nimbus would wander across these plateaus. The first rain would come—not with malice, but with the thoughtless, heavy laughter of the damp earth—and when the downpour hit this sunken town, the streets would boil and dissolve down to the keystones in an hour, leaving only a pit of white sludge.

The power considered this. 

It did not consider "uplifting" them. It did not dream of granting eyes to the paling-striders, for eyes require lenses of saline jelly, which would freeze or turn to crust in seconds, and what was there to see that the stone’s tension did not already report with greater precision? It did not breathe consciousness into the keystones; to make an urchin forty feet wide, trapped under millions of tons of tectonic slate, aware of its own grinding confinement would not be mercy, but the invention of a new and exquisite hell.

The town was already whole. It was a closed equation of dust, charge, and gravity. Its beauty was not in what it lacked, but in the absolute, razor-edged thrift of what it was.

Therefore, the benevolent power did what only the wisest actors can bear to do: it touched almost nothing. It changed no laws. It spoke no word that might disturb the sound-eating floors.

Instead, it went down into the margin between the basin and the sky.

High above the sheer walls, where the stagnant haze met the moving currents of the upper atmosphere, the power laid a skin—not of glass, and not of force, but of sheer, continuous *deflection*. It tilted the local curvature of the air by the width of a dust grain. 

Under this mantle, no moisture could ever descend. Any stray cloud drifting overhead would find the air above the square perpetually convex, sliding harmlessly around the rim, shedding its rain upon the barren siltite plains miles away, leaving the sunken basin in its eternal, cellar-cool drought. The knife-cut cliffs would never weather. The wire-sliced walls would remain forever yesterday afternoon.

Then, the power reached down—infinitely thin, cooler than the stone—and did one small, intimate thing.

It sought out the oldest keystone, deep in its cold cistern beneath the southern arcades. 

The great urchin was grinding down a sunken pillar of dental molding. Its calcite plates were locked in immense, unfeeling strain, slowly fatiguing, as all matter must over eons, shedding microscopic flecks of exhausted crystal into the void.

The benevolent power did not stop the grinding. It did not lighten the weight of the city above. 

It simply aligned itself with the fibrous mineral hairs inside the spinning vaults of the paling-striders, and through them, down the long aqueducts, into the radial sockets of the deep floor. It introduced into the basin’s closed electrostatic loop a single, frictionless harmonic: a charge that does not decay. 

It was not a thought. It was a current. 

In the human world, it might have translated as a sustained, sub-audible hum—the clean, perfect fifth of an iron bell that never fades. Here, where sound could not travel, it manifested as an infinitesimal ease in the stone. 

The spinning whorls within the striders found their suspension subtly reinforced, their joints moving with an absolute zero of resistance. The in-dwellers, drawing the airborne silt into their parchment leaves, found the dust settling into their pleats with a fractionally cleaner geometry, extruding facades that were five percent more porous, ten percent more flinty, perfectly resilient against the shearing of their own arches.

The keystone did not thank the power. It did not know the power was there. It merely felt the descending column slide into its jaws with a smoothness like oiled basalt, and ground it into flour with an effortless, eternal cadence.

Having done this—having secured the roof against tears, and eased the friction of their dry eternity—the benevolent power withdrew. 

It did not linger to be worshiped. It did not leave a monument.

In the basin below, nothing seemed to have altered. 

At the northern rim, a new architrave completed its crawl, buckled along its crystalline stress lines, and snapped free. A pale strider stepped down from the dental molding, its antennae testing the dry, silent draft. It paused, turned its blind, chalky face toward the empty sky, found neither moisture nor menace, and walked quietly down the narrow street, searching for a wall that needed to be held.
