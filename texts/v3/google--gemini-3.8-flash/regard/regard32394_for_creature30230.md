A foolish god would have pitied them.

A foolish god, looking into that reeking, vertical wound, would have seen only the horror: the living meat skinned by its own brood, the blind faces drowned in scalding fat, the spade-skulls wedged like broken shims into the tearing fabric of space, and the spent hulls pushed out into the freezing sky to ring like bricks against the scree. Such a power would have reached down with a broad and luminous hand to heal them.

It would have parted the black dacite to give them room. And in doing so, it would have dropped the internal pressure, quenched the hydrothermal vents, and let the thin, killing frost of the upper atmosphere rush in. Within six minutes, the frantic pulse would have slackened; within ten, forty thousand thumb-sized engines would have frozen into grease.

Or it would have softened their hides, so the volcanic glass would not bite. And they would have starved, unable to harvest the tallow from their dead.

Or it would have caught the falling ones at the lip of the threshold and laid them gently back upon the shelves. And the cleft, choked by its own unstoppable fecundity, would have backed up upon itself, the brood-sludge smothering the micro-fissures, the collective respiration poisoning the air, until the entire seam drowned in its own stagnant rot.

Wisdom does not shatter an engine because it smells of burning oil.

The power that arrived at the crest was neither human nor small. It possessed the patience of sedimentary beds and the sharp, clear gaze of light passing through cold vacuum. It hovered at the lip of the crack, no larger than the gap itself, feeling the greasy, tallow-thick plume of heat brush against its consciousness. It saw the mathematics of the place. It understood instantly that this was not a pit of torment; it was a hearth. A solitary, improbable furnace, burning at the absolute roof of a frozen world, held together by an ecology so tight and violent that a single misplaced grain of sand would bring down the whole impossible architecture.

It saw that they did not suffer as men suffer, because they did not wish to be elsewhere. They were the fire. The fire does not lament the wood it consumes; it simply burns, fierce and narrow, until the fuel is done.

Yet benevolence is not mere detachment. Benevolence is the desire that what *is*, should flourish in its true nature, and that nothing bearing the spark of exertion should be wholly unloved.

So the power did not alter the mountain. It did not blunt the glass. It did not quench the boiling brine. 

Instead, it made itself thin.

It slipped into the cleft not as a force, but as an environment—infinitesimal, odorless, weightless. It went first to the place where the shared breath hung thickest, where the air was an emulsion of atomized fat and boiling mineral spray. It dissolved itself into that steam.

It did not change the chemistry of the mist. The sulfur still bit; the heat still hovered near the boil. But into that heavy vapor, drawn through thousands of ventral slits and pulsed over thousands of boiling hearts, it introduced a single, subtle resonance. 

Before the power’s arrival, the collective hum in the rock had been an itch—a tense, frantic vibration born of pure pressure. Now, as the slick-skinned workers dragged their gills through the mist, their heat-pits did not merely flare from bruised violet to raw pink; they steadied. A vast, quiet coherence entered their shared circulation. They did not become philosophers; they did not drop their obsidian scrapers to gaze into the void. They continued to strip the fat, to bore the glass, to crowd and clamber. But the frantic blindness of their motion softened into something like rhythm. The terror that sits at the base of pure mechanics receded, replaced by the deep, physical contentment of a creature that fits precisely inside its skin, inside its work, inside its moment.

Then, the power moved deeper.

It traveled past the breeding tiers, past the gelatinous dams where the yolk-nodules quivered in the brine, down into the impossible shelves where space warped and slipped out of true. 

It came to the anchor-things.

Here were the creatures whose bodies were living gaskets, their spade-skulls pressed into angles that had no right to exist, their flesh drying into amber to keep a fold of creation from snapping closed. They lived in cold, dry dust and the smell of ancient filings, wedged forever between surfaces that ground against their bones.

The power did not pull them from their niches. To remove them would be to let the geometry collapse, sealing the cleft forever.

Instead, the power took up the load.

It slipped its own immutable substance into the non-existent angle, just beneath their skulls. It did not push them away; it simply shouldered ninety-nine parts of the strain, leaving only enough pressure for their muscles to know they were still anchored, still needed, still home. 

For the first time since the tectonic spur had buckled, the deep-dwellers felt the mountain cease to tear at them. The stone behind them was no longer a yawning, indifferent shear, but something immovable, steadfast, and warm. They remained locked in the seams, their tallow setting into amber, but they dried now not in agony, but in a profound, unmoving peace—monuments embedded in an arm that would never tire.

And finally, the power turned its attention to the waste-edge.

This was the hardest mercy. Wisdom knew the chimney must remain open; the spent ones must be nudged into the void, or the seam would suffocate. 

The power did not stop the crowding. It did not warm the bitter alpine draft that waited two centimeters outside the lip. 

Instead, it stationed a sliver of itself on the wind, just beyond the frost-line.

When a spent worker was nudged over the rim by the press of the new brood—its nails broken, its good hand having flayed the frost-bitten skin from its other arm—it did not tumble into a sudden, horrifying cold. 

As the mountain’s wind hit the dying creature, before its hot moisture could flash into frost, the power caught its brief, thumb-sized awareness in a field of absolute stillness. In that fraction of a second before the freeze took it, the creature felt no bite of sub-zero air, no sickening plunge into the blue abyss. It felt only that its work was finished, that its fat had fed the hearth, that its glass had served its turn. 

Then the frost came, instantaneous and clean. The tallow crystallized; the meat petrified into dark, dense mineral.

Far below, where the scree slope swept down into the quiet snowfields, the frozen stones fell. 

*Clink. Clink. Clink.*

They rolled down the talus, hollow and light, ringing like fired porcelain in the vast mountain silence. 

The power did not sweep them away. But where they came to rest in the high snow, beneath the cold stars and the thin, empty air, the stones did not look like refuse. Arranged by the subtle breath of the wind that had guided their descent, they lay scattered across the white crust in long, spiral paths—a wide, enduring geometry that caught the morning sun and held it, faint and bright, reflecting the sky back to the sky.

Inside the dark wound above, the furnace roared on. 

Glass scraped on bone. Brine washed over the eggs. The mist cycled through ten thousand small, wet throats. Nothing was saved, in the way a man counts saving; nothing was spared the fire. But the mountain had ceased to be an accident of crushed meat, and had become a temple: narrow, scalding, and complete, tended from within, supported from behind, and honored at its quiet, frozen end.
