To intervene here with summer would be a massacre. 

A power that was merely sweet, or merely merciful in the manner of soft-bellied things, would look down upon the gray laths of Ochs-and-Tallow and bring rain. It would bring warm rain and yellow light, thinking: *let them soften; let them sprout.* 

And the plateau would die in convulsions. 

Under warm rain, five thousand meters of case-hardened cambium would intake moisture faster than its vulcanized pores could seat it. The hydraulic turnbuckles would blow their drift pins with the force of field artillery; the structural timbers would delaminate, slinging forty-acre processing houses into the thin air like split wicker; the compression rings, laid down before the sky had air to breathe, would buckle upward in fifty-foot waves of shattered red-brown lancewood, crushing the Caulkers where they knelt, drowning the Gaugers in boiling sap, and pitching the forty-ton Ochs into chasms of screaming fiber.

Wisdom does not shatter what has learned to hold itself against the void. Wisdom looks at the grain and reads the stress.

The power that turned its attention to the shelf was old enough to remember when the wood was still soft enough to take an indentation from a falling spore. It had watched the plateau rise—millimeter by century—until the vertical tension became too great and the sap refused the lift, freezing into the horizontal plane that now shaved itself toward the clouds. 

It did not descend as an angel, nor as a fire, nor as a gardener with an iron rake. 

It came as a *temper*.

***

It entered through the cold, pre-oxygen heartwood, three miles down, where the Gaugers lay stiff in the cleavage-planes. 

The first thing it offered was not warmth, but **ease of shear**. 

At the bottom of the world, where the compressed rings had spent three million years grinding against one another under the weight of the plateau, it introduced a micro-film of tallow so cold and dense it did not melt, but allowed the bands of red-brown iron-wood to slide past one another by a millionth of an inch without tearing their tracheids. 

It was a microscopic slip. Above, on the black shelf, no dial registered it. No mercury pendulum tipped in the belly of an Och. But forty miles down-line, the deep, pervasive *clack-drub* of the arterial steam lines dropped half an octave. The vibration that had replaced the pulse in the men’s ears lost its cutting edge; it ceased to rattle the roots of their teeth and became, instead, a great, low thrum—like a bass viol whose string had been slacked off by half a peg.

Then, the power moved into the nap.

It did not alter the wood’s orientation; to do so would have unseated the ash-soled boots and thrown the Caulkers against the push-rods. Instead, it smoothed the microscopic barbs along the surface cells. Walking against the grain ceased to be a fight against a stiff broom; it became merely a heavy grade, the difference between an iron hill and a wooden lane. A man swinging his lead-tipped ash pole found that his shoulder did not lock at the top of the swing. The alum crust on his lips did not dissolve—to dissolve it would leave the thin skin open to the biting dry—but it ceased to taste of copper filings. It began to taste of nothing at all. 

A Caulker striking the floor—*once, twice, long-pause, once*—felt the wood take the blow not as an anvil, but as an instrument. The sound did not slap back into his marrow. It traveled out into the black timber-shelf and did not return as an ache in his hip, but dispersed sideways, lost in the vastness like a stone dropped into a quarry of cork.

***

Next, the power visited the man at the manifold.

He had been sitting there for seven weeks. The pale, mineral fuzz had reached his collar; his knees were set into the floor-grain, and his ash soles had already drunk the cold tannin until they were indistinguishable from the four-inch larch planking of the cross-tie. He was waiting for the saw. He was looking at the reciprocating push-rod: sixteen feet out, sixteen feet back.

The power did not pull him up. To pull him up would have required the three-ton jack; it would have torn the gray, stringy meat of his calves and left him an open, weeping check that the cold would split to the bone.

Instead, the power leaned against the copper steam jacket behind him.

It did not turn the steam into water. It merely changed the nature of the condensation that rolled along the lagging. Where the pipe had wept bitter sulfur and damp elderberry, it began to distill a heavy, slow oil that smelled faintly of cured cedar and cold grease. The oil soaked into the shoulders of the man’s stiff coat. It did not rot the wool; it *cured* it.

The mineral fuzz on his cuffs stopped its climb. It did not die—nothing here could afford the waste of dying—but it laid itself down along the wool threads, flat and silvery, like the nap of a mole’s skin. 

And for the first time since the settlement had driven its first drift pin, the push-rod paused.

It was not a stall. The boilers three miles below did not choke. It was simply that the power placed its thumb upon the eccentric gear at the dead center of the stroke. When the sixteen-foot rod reached its full extension, it did not instantly reverse with that dry, metallic *chack* that wore the Slide-Ticks into shavings. 

It lingered. 

For the space of one full breath—such a breath as a stone might take if it were allowed a lung—the rod held still. 

In that second of rest, the Slide-Tick resting on the guide rail did not dry out. Its interior pith, strained by the relentless friction of the stroke, had time to draw the cold lignum-vitae oil through its capillary tubes without cavitation. The Tick did not stiffen; it did not curl. Its brass-wire feet cooled against the steel track.

The sitting man saw the rod pause. His eyes, the color of pond-ice under snow, did not widen—they had forgotten how to widen—but the dry lath of his throat made a small, clicking sound. 

He did not have to watch the saw come. 

The saw-line, three miles to the east, was not stopped. The benevolence of the power was too vast and too pitiless to break the great blades; a saw that size, if halted against the torque of its subterranean drive, would have snapped into a million scythes, scything the district from the boilers to the bristle-edge. 

The saws kept running. But as they approached the manifold, the power went ahead of the teeth. 

It did not soften the timber-shelf. It changed the **kerf**.

When the church-pew teeth reached the sitting man’s cross-tie, they did not grind his lath-bones into black meal to be planed flat for the rail-gantries. The blades struck the cured cedar-oil that had soaked down from his coat into the heartwood. Under that cold lubrication, the steel did not cut straight; it *deflected*. 

It was an engineering error of three degrees—so small the Stokers on the trailing engines would never mark it on their charts. The blades climbed three inches, rode over the dense, petrified arch of his folded knees, planed the silver fuzz from the crown of his hat as cleanly as a cooper’s draw-knife planes a stave, and dropped back into the cambium on the other side.

They left him behind not as an obstruction, but as a knot. 

A smooth, iron-hard burl in the black shelf, polished by the passage of the steel until he shone like an antique newel post, permanently seated in the grain, warm from the copper pipe, resting in the dead center between the outward stroke and the return.

***

Lastly, the power went to the Och on the northern line.

It did not speak to the Stokers. If it had spoken into their zinc-lined cab, their ears, long ruined by the *thung-chunk* of the rocker-feet, would have split like bladder-nuts. 

It did not stop the machine from walking. The Och had forty tons of balance to maintain; to halt it on the raw cambium was to invite the creep, to let the wood sweat its iridescent oil and drown the levelling jacks.

Instead, the power entered the trailing copper umbilical. 

Through the high-pressure steam, it carried a quietness into the oscillating cylinder. The rocker-feet did not strike the floor with a hammer-blow that shook the gantries two miles off. They came down with the sound of a heavy wooden trunk being closed in an attic—firm, dry, and final. 

The two Stokers, huddled over their tin bucket of white beef-suet and barley, felt the cab settle. For forty years, their teeth had clicked together with every downward stride of the foreleg. Now, their jaws remained apart. 

The younger one, whose left ear had wept clear lymphatic resin since the winter the boiler-house blew its packing, took the tin megaphone from his forehead. 

He did not look at his companion. He looked down through the floor-grate at the rockers.

Ahead of them, the bristle-sea was waiting. Eighty feet high, vibrating with the torque of the world, whistling its dry, thin song against the cloud-ceiling. 

Usually, the bristles stood like iron lances, waiting to be shattered by the Och’s weight and squared by the trailing saws. But where the Och’s rockers now fell, the bristles did not snap with the sound of four-pounder guns. 

They **bent**.

The power had touched the lignin in their vertical shafts. It had not made them green—greenness would have rotted in the cold—but it had given them the memory of the curve. Under the iron rockers, the eighty-foot bristles folded down like dry grass under a wagon-wheel. They did not splinter; they laid themselves flat, shingle over shingle, grain over grain, forming a road that did not need to be planed.

The Och did not have to reverse. The Stokers did not have to watch for the creeps, because the iridescent oil stopped sweating from the fissures. The wood was no longer screaming under the shear; it had been given permission to lie down without being planed into dust.

The Och walked on. 

It walked out past the city limits of Ochs-and-Tallow. It walked beyond the range of the horizontal push-rods. Its copper umbilical did not snap; it lengthened, drawing out of the district’s boilers not sulfur and bitterness, but the dry, cool breath of a machine that has found a track that does not fight its wheels.

Inside the cab, the older Stoker reached into the pocket of his tarred smock. He brought out a small wedge of lead—the kind they used to shim the pendulum-stops when the mercury ran low. 

He did not tap it against the tin megaphone. 

He held it in his palm and watched it stay still. For the first time in his life, the mercury was not hitting the bronze stops. The pendulums hung true, vertical, swinging only with the slow, deliberate stride of the larch baulks as they carried the machine out into the unplaned world.

***

The power did not leave. 

It does not leave because wisdom knows that what has been relieved of tension must be watched for forty seasons while the grain settles. It stayed in the floor. It stayed three miles down in the dark, pre-oxygen heartwood, holding the cleavage-planes with its cold, greasy fingers so they would not pinch the Gaugers to death.

And above, on the black timber-shelf, the light remained broad, pale, and without charity. 

Charity was too small for this place. Charity would have wanted them to be men again, and men could not have lived an hour upon that petrified floor. 

The power gave them what was better than charity: it gave them **tolerance**. 

It gave the turnbuckles a thread that did not strip. It gave the saw-teeth a tooth that did not shatter when it hit a knot. It gave the dry, lath-boned Caulkers forty seconds of silence between the signals, so that when they struck the lead wedges against the ash-floor, they could wait, with their boots rooted and their ears pressed to the wood, and hear that the world beneath them was no longer trying to split itself in two.
