It did not arrive from the sky, for to cast a shadow upon that basin would have been an intrusion upon an illumination that had remained unbroken since the granite was molten. 

It arrived instead as an increase in the clarity of things already present. 

The Power was very old—older than the worn knuckles of the rock, though not by much—and it had long ago abandoned the coarse instruments of intervention: the lifted hand, the commanding voice, the sudden miracle that shatters the vessel it seeks to mend. Its wisdom had the texture of water that has learned every contour of a riverbed without ever once asking the stones to move.

It saw the basin not as a collection of curiosities, but as a single sentence written in flesh and stone, repeated without a pause for breath across an expanse of time that would have turned mountain ranges to flour.

And it saw the being that lived there.

Not the pale tallow-kin alone, nor the cadmium ribbons plunging into the mesh, nor the quartz striders clutching the undersides of hovering droplets, nor the great ferns snapping their spines every seventeen seconds like wet clocks. It saw that these were not separate inhabitants of an address. They were the corpuscles, the cilia, the lymph, and the peristalsis of an undivided organism whose skin was three millimeters of mineral broth and whose skeleton was pink granite. 

It was an animal whose heart beat once every billion years, and whose thought was the steady, circular circulation of its own blood.

The Power felt the first and most dangerous instinct of benevolence: *the urge to awaken it.*

To grant it a voice, so that its terrible silence might become song. To give the blind, parsnip-fleshed tallow-kin eyes, so they might see the white-gold splendor of their home. To release the ribbons from their thirty-foot plunge into the mesh, or to quiet the frantic, crystalline anxiety of the seam-striders who dropped their seed into the cold purple gap.

Benevolence leaned forward.

And Wisdom caught its wrist.

*Look closer,* Wisdom said, without words. *Where is the wound?*

The Power looked. It searched the laminar sheet, where the water slipped without a ripple over the smooth pink curves. It searched the tallow-kin as two of them met, pressed their soft flanks together in a slow, cool communion of tallow and lymph, and quietly made room for one another. It searched the under-pavement, where the yellow and black bands aligned to form a perfect, continuous floor.

There was no fear here. There was no terror of winter, no shame of birth, no rot in the dark. The tallow-kin did not hunger; the cartilage tongue reeled out with the rhythm of an uncoiling spring, perfectly satisfied, sustained by the sun and the lemon-scented mist. The dead dissolved in ten seconds into milk and grease, not because they were discarded, but because they were cherished so thoroughly that not a single atom was permitted to lie cold.

To give this place an ego would be to give it the capacity for exile. 

To give it a voice would be to teach it that it was alone.

To give it an afterlife or an ambition would be to introduce the poison of *elsewhere* into an engine whose holiness was entirely contained in *here*.

So the Power did not descend to govern, nor to preach, nor to redeem. It did not alter the trajectory of a single floating paraphore, nor dull the fierce, white-gold glare that kept the water at blood-heat.

Instead, the Power did what only the truly wise and benevolent know how to do when they encounter an unfallen machine:

It became its witness, and it gave it a margin.

First, it sat upon the granite.

It did not take a body that displaced the flow. It settled like an added density of light, a stillness slightly heavier than the stillness already there. It allowed the three-millimeter sheet of mineral water to flow across its presence. 

And because the Power had its own gravity—not of mass, but of memory—the water passing through it changed very slightly. It did not lose its brass-and-copper bite, but it took on the faintest trace of an echo: the taste of rains that had fallen on continents that no longer existed, the cool memory of deep sea-trenches, the quiet breath of sleeping stars. 

It was not food. It was something rarer: it was *flavor*. 

When the cadmium ribbons swept the stone downstream from where the Power sat, their thousands of microscopic, cup-shaped mouths took in that water. For three feet along the granite, the ribbons slowed. Their cadenced shingling did not break, but their yellow bands flared a shade warmer—from cold cadmium to deep, autumnal amber. They did not become conscious of the universe, but their living fabric registered, for the first time in an eon, that the world beyond the basin was sweet.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the jade rings, and to the seam-striders.

Here alone was there a knot of tension. The eight-legged quartz skaters were the only things that hurried, the only things that trembled, forever suspended beneath the levitating pearls, waiting for the spatial shear so they could cast their spores into the indigo sky on the other side.

The Power looked through the seam. 

It saw what lay there: an ancient, frozen world, dying of stillness, where the air was thin and lavender, and where nothing had stirred for three geological ages except the tiny, falling specks of quartz eggs that accumulated in the violet drifts like glass snow, never hatching for lack of heat.

The seam-striders were not a mistake. They were an unanswered prayer. The machine was trying, with infinite slowness, to seed a dead sibling through an eyelet no wider than a breath.

The Power did not rip the seam open. A tear would have drained the basin’s warmth into the void.

Instead, the Power reached a long, quiet finger of intent through the ring. It did not pull the worlds together. It simply laid its warmth against the cold earth on the other side of the threshold, directly beneath the drift of glass eggs.

It held that cold ground in its palm. It brought the temperature of that lavender dust up to thirty-seven degrees—blood-heat, the heat of the water film.

Back in the basin, above the paraffin ring, the air bent sharply. There was the soft, suctioning *pop*.

A seam-strider, turning milky-white in its microsecond of alignment, dropped its cluster of spores.

On the other side, through the violet glare, the first egg did not freeze. It settled into the warm, attentive palm of the Power. Its smoky-quartz shell softened. A tiny, needle-thin leg flexed, testing the purple air.

The Power smiled, though it had no mouth. The circuit was complete. The basin was no longer an engine spinning its wheels in an empty house; it was a mother whose children were being gathered at the gate.

Lastly, the Power looked at the tallow-kin.

One of them—the oldest, perhaps, though age had no meaning in flesh that healed without scars—had finished its harvest. Its chest lantern was pulsing: *bright-gold, dim-amber, bright-gold, dim-amber*. Its blunt, parsnip-white snout turned toward the presence on the stone.

It had no eyes to see the Power. But its lilac dish-brow measured the air. It felt the local field shift—not with the sharp, artificial jerk of the jade rings, but with a vast, low-frequency ease, like a great tide settling into a harbor.

The creature slid forward on its six suction-padded paws. It did not approach out of worship; it did not know how to bow. It simply moved toward the densest center of the equilibrium, as iron moves toward a lodestone.

It came to the edge of the Power's presence and stopped. 

Then, following the only protocol it had ever known for greeting another body in the bright, streaming emptiness, the tallow-kin leaned its soft, curd-white flank against the Power.

It pressed firmly. It waited for the exchange of epidermal slimes. It waited for the quorum.

The Power did not draw back from the lard-flesh, nor did it attempt to elevate it. With infinite gentleness, it pressed back. 

It gave the tallow-kin no visions, no prophecies, no knowledge of mortality. It simply offered its own ancient, unwearied stability against the creature’s flank, holding it in the warm, wet light for ten seconds, twenty seconds, a minute.

Through the contact, the tallow-kin’s brow-dish ceased its minute, searching twitches. For the first time in five hundred million years, the creature was not reading the air for drift or shear. The air had arrived.

The digestive embers in its ribs slowed their churn, settling from a rapid, mechanical flicker into a deep, steady hearth-glow.

When the tallow-kin finally pulled away, its skin was smooth, unmarked, and glistening with laminar broth. It turned back toward the ring. It did not look back. It did not need to.

The Power remained.

It did not leave. It did not take up residence as a master, nor did it build a temple. It simply stayed, an invisible, patient stone resting on the pink knuckles of the continent. 

It allowed the water to run over its ankles. It let the yellow ribbons skim its perimeter. Every seventeen seconds, when the fern snapped its fronds with that dull, wet *tock*, the Power received the mist against its skin and felt the lemon scent blossom in the sun.

The engine would still run until the continent broke apart. But it would not run unheeded. 

In the vast, terrifying silence of a universe where most things die before they can explain themselves, the basin was no longer just an forgotten altar in the mud.

It was kept.
