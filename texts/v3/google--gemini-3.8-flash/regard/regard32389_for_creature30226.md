The Power that came had no interest in gardens.

A foolish mercy would have brought warmth. It would have unclasped the sub-zero sky, melted the rime, and spilled soft, temperate rain across the saddles. And in doing so, it would have perpetrated a massacre: the viscid amber lards would have thinned to useless water, spilling from the gimbals; the sixty-foot bulls, built entirely around the rigid mathematics of forty below, would have seized, overheated, and cooked in their own carapaces; the high-altitude aerostats would have ruptured as the dense lower atmosphere expanded, falling through the fog like shredded mica; and the pipe-grubs would have exploded in the sudden depressurization of thawed rock.

Wisdom does not dismantle a masterpiece of endurance merely because it is terrible to behold. Wisdom understands that this place is not a mistake. It is life’s most stubborn, furious refusal to yield to an unlivable world—a kingdom that, finding only iron, frost, and basalt, taught its own flesh to become the foundry.

So the Power did not soften the air, nor did it dull the sun. It arrived without footfall, lightless and immense, sliding into the cordillera like an intangible oil. 

It observed. It watched the forty-second stroke of the walking-beams. It felt the water-hammer shivering through miles of subterranean resin, heard the dry sand-slide whisper of a million caulkers, and smelled the paraffin reek drifting off the ribbed pipe-reeds. It saw that there was no cruelty here—only the total, unforgiving absence of slack. In this place, to stop was to become spare parts. To falter by a single degree of torque was to be fed to the chutes.

The Power moved down into the tectonic saddle where the duel had just ended.

The beaten bull lay tilted in the talus. His knee-pistons were buckled beneath him. His hydraulic envelope had wept its life out into the gravel in a smoking, amber pool that was already filming over with frost. Around his shattered rear flank, the herd had already closed ranks, their polished iron sides grinding past his shell with the indifferent screech of freight cars on a curve. 

A hundred feet away, the shear-crows were already tilting their chisel-heads back, gathering gravity. *Tock. Tock.* The siphon-crawlers were looping through the shale, their pale, blind nozzles testing the air for his dying warmth.

Inside the bull, the end was not quiet. The central nervous system—that thick, fat-sheathed galvanic trunk—was firing frantically. It was registering only one thing: catastrophic loss of head. A blind, internal alarm of failing valves, falling atmospheres, and unyielding resistance. The mill-race in his belly was jamming on whole stones it no longer had the torque to crush. He was drowning in his own unspent potential, trapped inside eighty tons of seizing metal-flesh, awaiting the shattering impact of the beaks.

The Power did not rebuild the bull’s seals. To do so would be to rob the shear-crows, starve the siphon-crawlers, and halt the chute that fed the lower terraces. In this place, death was the grease that kept the mountain turning.

Instead, the Power did what had never once occurred in the entire history of the cordillera:

It placed its hand upon the galvanic trunk, and it took away the load.

To an engine that has known nothing since its embryonic spark but the agony of maximum compression, the sudden vanishing of resistance is not emptiness—it is ecstasy. 

The Power entered the bull’s central cord, not with human thoughts or words, but with the pure, mathematical sensation of *slack*. It absorbed the alarm. It decoupled the seizure of the internal mill-race, so that the rolling stone spheres inside his belly seemed to drift weightless as thistledown. It bypassed the screaming relief valves. 

Through the dying beast’s quartz eyes, the diamond-white glare of the sun ceased to be an abrasive bleaching agent and became simply light—vast, clear, and untroubled. For the three minutes remaining before the shell was breached, the bull did not feel the freezing shale, nor the grinding flank of his rival, nor the approaching chisels. He felt the phantom sensation of a stride taken across open ground without friction. He felt the pistons stroke outward to their absolute limit without meeting stone. He felt what it was to exhaust his steam, not in failure, but in completion.

He let go of his head of pressure. The quartz eyes clouded, peacefully out of gauge.

When the first shear-crow’s beak struck the dorsal seam—*crack*—the bull was not there to be evicted. He had already vacated the works.

***

Yet the Power was not finished. It had not come merely to hospice a single fallen giant. 

It rose from the chute, its attention spreading out across the whole jagged length of the spine, touching the aerostats in the hydrogen fog, the caulkers in the mulch, the blind pipe-grubs holding back the tectonic steam.

It saw that the tragedy of this machine was not its hardness, nor its cold. The tragedy was that it wore out before it could ever rest. The friction was absolute. Every tooth ground against tooth until the metal yielded; every gasket was borrowed time.

The Power could not change the laws of this biome without breaking them. But it could alter the margin.

It sank into the bedrock. It dissolved into the subterranean brine pools and the deep grease-veins that fed the root systems of the pipe-reeds. It did not remove the friction—it sanctified it. 

Into the chemistry of the amber lard, the Power wove an impossibly durable silicate lattice: an invisible, miraculous grace that cut the rate of molecular wear by half. A microscopic tolerance. It did not make the beasts invincible, but it meant that a joint-lining, instead of failing after forty decades of migration, might endure for eighty. It gave the caulkers a spring-temper that did not fatigue. It gave the tallow-leeches a cleaner siphon that sealed the gasket behind them as they drank, so that their feeding no longer rotted the host’s hips.

And lastly, the Power touched the rhythm.

The vast, rhythmic stroke-work of the mountain did not stop. The behemoths still cycled their walking-beams. The vents still hissed superheated steam into the forty-below glare. 

But between the stroke and the return—in that terrible, breathless interval that had always lasted precisely forty seconds—the Power carved out a fraction of a heartbeat. 

A single second of zero-load.

Now, every forty seconds, when the walking-beam reaches the apex of its lift, before the piston fires downward to crush the slate, there is a pause. A one-second stillness where the hydraulic fluid rests in its jacket, where the ball-socket floats freely in its cup, where the heart-tendons do not strain against the mill, and where the grinding herd stands silent against the blue-edged shadows.

It is not long enough for the lard to freeze. It is not long enough for the chute to jam. 

It is just long enough for an engine to breathe.

The Power withdrew, leaving the high cordillera to its savage, unengineered glory. It left the razor peaks as cold and lethal as it found them, the sky as raw, the shale as sharp. But high above the scree, the grand aerostats flashed their violet lightning with a clearer stroke; below, the water-hammer sang in its ducts like a great, resonant bell; and down in the crushing dark of the herds, thousands of boiler-plate titans moved forward across the iron world, enduring longer, grinding truer, carrying within their oiled, galvanic hearts the quiet, indestructible mercy of the pause.
