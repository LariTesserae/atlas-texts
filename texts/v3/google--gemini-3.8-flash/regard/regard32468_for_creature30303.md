The power that arrived did not descend like a thunderbolt, nor did it part the halite sky. To tear open the ceiling would have been to drop an ocean of dead weight onto their heads; to blot out the noon would have plunged a people who have no lids into the screaming madness of an absolute void. 

A clumsy god would have brought warmth. It would have kindled a fire on the flags, ignorant that heat would boil the thirty-six-degree grease of the lake, curdle the delicate sulfur-metabolizing microbes, rot the cold lard-wings in the air, and suffocate lungs designed to draw oxygen only from cellar-damp stone. 

A sentimental god would have spoken. It would have filled the acoustic sludge of the chasm with choirs or commands, forgetting that these people possess no ears for speech, no larynx to return a cry, and that any sound louder than the dull *clack* of a bone mallet would shatter the brittle vestigial bones in their skulls and leave them writhing on the slate.

A foolish god would have stopped the work. It would have commanded the builders to lay down their wedges and rest, and in forty days the aragonite fur would have needle-welded every flag, the rills would have choked, the black anaerobic sludge would have pooled over the terraces, and the city would have become the glassy, flooded tomb of lime it had fought for ten thousand years to stave off.

Wisdom does not shatter what it loves. Wisdom sits down upon the floor.

The power entered as an immense, patient attention—an awareness that seeped through the fracture-cracked selenite like groundwater, settling into the acoustic viscosity of the deep until it occupied every pore of the tonalite, every grain of the lime mortar, and the marrow of every thick-necked builder hauling on the sinew ropes.

It watched. It studied the mechanics of their grace. 

It saw that they were not fallen kings or cursed beasts. They were an organ. They were the slow, steady ventricle of an archaic Earth, keeping a corner of the lithosphere porous by the sheer, unreckoned majesty of their stubbornness. They had no pride, no grief, and no cruelty. When they stripped the skin from a fallen brother, they did not dishonor him; they folded him back into the sieve, using his bones to lever the stones that would keep his children dry. There was an awful, immaculate perfection in them.

Yet the power saw, too, the place where the machine ground down to bone.

It saw the margin. The margin was a razor’s edge. 

Forty days. If a work-gang fell behind by three weeks, the aragonite conquered the terrace. If a runner slipped and crushed a wrist, he could not be spared to heal; he went into the sluice. And in the pens, when they huddled hip-to-hip in their tight, twelve-bodied hexagons to take their four-minute micro-sleeps, their hands never went still. Their fingers twitched against the stone, scraping, knotting, searching for calcification in their dreams, terrified with an animal terror that if they relaxed for one complete breath, the chasm would cement itself around them forever.

They lived without a neutral gear. They were an engine that could never idle without tearing itself to scrap.

So the benevolent power made no declaration. It performed no miracles that could be seen by eyes of amber. It merely laid its will against the laws of the deep, touching only three things, so quietly that the rhythm of the sledges never faltered.

First, it touched the sweat of the stone.

It did not halt the mineral flow, for the moisture was the city's lifeblood. But it altered the geometry of the precipitate. Deep within the cold limestone pores, where the calcium and bicarbonate ions collided in the dark, the power introduced a microscopic flaw into the lattice of the crystal. 

The mineral that sweated out upon the flags was no longer aragonite—needle-sharp, interlocking, and hard as tempered iron. It became a softer polymorphism: a crumbly, chalk-brittle vaterite that formed in loose, floury rimes rather than interlocking fangs. 

When the next work-gang threw their weight against a thirty-day wall, expecting the brutal, joint-shivering shock of their horn wedges biting into solidified stone, the wedges did not bounce. They sank smoothly, with a dull, clean *shuck*. The crust parted like packed sea-salt. 

The gang did not pause to wonder. They had no concept of wonder. But as they cleared the seam in two blows instead of twelve, the frequency of their galloping foot-taps against the bedrock dropped by a fraction of a beat. Their shoulders, knotted into cords of iron lard, found a half-inch of slack.

Second, the power entered the tonalite.

It took into itself the shock of the work. For millennia, the blunt impact of bone mallets against bedrock had rebounded upward through the thick pads of their feet, fracturing their shins over decades, grinding their hip-sockets to gravel, and bursting their chests by their fortieth year. 

The power became an acoustic sink within the stone. When the mallets struck to signal the haul, the tonalite still carried the frequency—the syncopated pulse that triggered the synchronized contraction of sixty pairs of thighs—but the punishing, bone-deadening recoil was swallowed. The earth ceased to punish the feet that struck it. The bedrock, which had always been an unyielding anvil, became slightly, imperceptibly elastic—yielding to the weight of their sledges, carrying the runners forward with a fraction less friction, as if the shale were greased by something finer than lard.

And third, the power touched the pens.

It waited until a work-gang had finished an eighty-stride stretch, until the elder sledgers had stacked their sledges and crawled into the chest-high corral. It watched them wedge their bodies together, hip-to-hip, backs out, heads bowed between their knees under the flat, unblinking glare of the noon sky.

As the outer ring fell into the shallow trance of exhaustion, the power did not alter the cellar chill of the air. It did not create a roof to trigger their panic of the falling mountain. 

Instead, it made the flags beneath their calloused feet hold a strange, subtle reservoir of presence. Not heat—heat would have sickened them—but an absence of drain. The stones ceased to suck the vital grease from their marrow. For the first time since the first of their kind had chipped a slate flag in the deep, the stone did not feel like an enemy waiting to lock their joints.

The elder in the center of the cluster let out a long, wheezing breath through his spiracles. A puff of vapor hung in the dead air.

His hands, bound in dry gut, lay across his knees. 

For a thousand heartbeats, the fingers twitched—knotting an imaginary cord, feeling for the aragonite fur that was always creeping, always welding, always coming to choke the rills.

The stone beneath him hummed. It was not a sound; it was a density, a seismic assurance running up through his pelvic bones, saying what no tongue in the chasm had ever possessed the architecture to say:

*The rill is open.*
*The floor is breathing.*
*It will wait.*

The elder’s thick, pale fingers gave one last, small shudder.

Then, they went flat. The gut cord slipped from his palm and lay still upon the shale. His thumbs uncurled. His heavy head sank two inches deeper into the hollow of his collarbones.

Around him, eleven other bodies felt the release pass through their touching flanks like a soft, slackening wave. One by one, the twitching stopped. The hands lay open, palms upturned to the halite light, soft and pale as river stones resting in the shallows.

They slept. Not for four frantic minutes, but for twenty. For thirty. 

Above them, the four-winged lards wheeled in their frantic, mute collisions, and down the wide boulevards the blind crabs poured like spilled water over the low walls. The city remained what it had always been: a vast, shadowless machine of stone and sinew, grinding away in the cold cellar of the world.

The work did not end. The city would still be carried three hundred paces to the east, and three hundred paces back. 

But when the elder woke, his amber eyes blinked once against the glare—not because he had grown lids, but because the darkness inside the sleep had been deep enough to leave an after-image. He picked up his bone mallet. He placed his bare foot upon the tonalite. 

The stone answered him, solid and light, ready for the chisel. And he set his shoulder to the rope not as a creature fleeing an avalanche, but as a master turning the wheel of a house that would never, ever fall.
