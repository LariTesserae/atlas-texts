The power that found the trench did not come with wings or a sudden washing of sweet wind. To descend upon such a place with clean light and absolute stillness would have been an act of slaughter. 

A foolish benevolence would have smoothed the cliffs, and in doing so, stripped the platform-dwellers of the narrow clefts that held their copper wedges. A clumsy god would have silenced the seismic shudder, and thereby starved the herd, whose entire blind cycle depended upon the sky falling in slabs of fresh slate to crack the carapaces and feed the churn below. If the sulfur were swept away in an instant, the people—whose hairless, scarred skin had grown thick to turn the acid steam, whose milky horizontal eyelids could not bear the unsoftened fire of a blue sky—would have curled upon their bone platforms and blistered to death in the sudden, freezing clarity of the upper air.

Wisdom knows that life does not ask to be rescued into an alien paradise; it asks for a margin within its own terror. It asks for a grain of purchase against the shear.

So the power did not speak, for the larynx had long since died here, and words could not pierce the *choc-choc-choc* of sixty million ivory foot-pads striking the bedrock. Instead, it became weight, mineral, and time. It entered the rift through the only avenues the rift understood: tension, chemistry, and vibration.

***

The first thing it touched was the deep rock, six miles beneath the crawling floor, where the basalt was still soft as tallow and tearing itself apart in spasms. 

The power did not weld the rift shut. It laid its vast, patient intention against the tectonic edges like a hand steadying a chisel. It did not stop the tremors, but it smoothed their stutter. Under its pressure, the violent, unpredictable shearing of the basalt began to cool into geometry. Over the course of twelve cycles of the red twilight, the rock learned the secret of columns. 

Where the cliffs had dropped in jagged, blind guillotines that pulverized whole camps in their sleep, the stone began to cleave along regular hexagonal joints. It gave warning. The basalt began to sing before it parted—a low, bell-like drone that hummed through the copper pegs and the gut-rigging twelve minutes before the slab peeled away, giving the mothers time to haul the sleeping-shells higher, giving the tallow-men time to read the seam before the weight fell. 

And when the rock fell, it no longer fell in wide, flattening sheets that scoured the defiles into lifeless basins. It fell in stepped blocks, piling at the base of the walls in crude, terraced aprons. For the first time since the earth split, there was ground near the margins that did not belong entirely to the herd. There were high, broken steps of stone where a man could stand without greaves, where the white swine could not climb, and where a dropped tool was not instantly swallowed by forty thousand tons of blind, forward-grinding hunger.

***

Next, the power went into the water.

It did not drain the sulfur steam, for the steam kept the rift warm and fed the iron-slime. But deep within the narrow fissures, where the subterranean condensation bled through razor-edged slate, the power seeded a new vein: a porous, pale green zeolite, soft as soapstone, that ran in long, horizontal ribbons through the basalt just forty feet above the Hangs.

As the acid water seeped through these green veins, the rock itself drank the vitriol. It took the dissolved copper, the bitter alum, and the iron-scum out of the moisture, leaving it behind in the dark as harmless crusts of malachite. 

When the water emerged on the cliff faces, it was no longer a stinging grease that rotted human gums and dropped the teeth of thirty-year-olds. It gathered in tiny, cold runnels that dripped from the underbellies of the hexagonal stone steps—clear, sweet-tasting, smelling of nothing more violent than wet gravel. 

A child, waking in an inverted dorsal plate, could reach her hand through the rib-timbers and catch the drop on her knuckle. She did not have to wait for the flyer-skins to sweat in the upper draft; she did not have to drink the tin-tasting water of the cool-hoods. The cliff had learned how to weep without poisoning its children.

***

The third work was the smallest, and therefore the most dangerous.

Benevolence looked at the flyers—the skin-winged, four-fingered things roosting in their leathery thousands on the upper ledges, dropping like stones to snatch the babes from the bone-baskets. 

It did not exterminate them; without their wing-vellum, the humans could make no steam-curtains, no water-bags, no waterproof cloaks to turn the grease. But the power touched the thin marrow inside their hollow, glass-like finger-bones. 

It introduced an ache. 

When the flyers banked through the heat-shimmers and caught the salt-smell of human sweat, the scent no longer triggered the lunging strike of an unthinking belly. It triggered a sharp, dry itch in their hollow joints—a faint, nervous revulsion that made their three-toed feet curl inward and their wings miss a beat. They did not cease to hunt the small, whip-tailed lard-things that darted between the carapaces, but they began to give the swaying rib-platforms a wide, circling berth. They still roosted above, their castanet-clicks echoing through the glare, but they watched the camps not as wolves watch sheep, but as crows watch men: with a wary, irritable distance.

***

Down on the floor, in the cream-colored churn, the butcher’s life did not become easy. The power was too wise to make the sweet-root fall into men’s laps; a life stripped of its fierce discipline down in the defiles would have unraveled in a season. 

The tallow-man still dropped from the overhanging ladders. He still danced the crush, leaping from white back to white back while the yellow foot-pads clamped the biofilm and the air shook with the deafening *clack-whir* of the plates. He still severed the anterior muscle-stalks with his copper-headed adze while the gray, vinegar-stinking whey pooled around his ankles.

But now, when he drove his hook into the fibrous knuckle of the eight-foot skimmer-stilt, the great stilt-beast did not immediately try to scrape him off against the shale. 

The skimmers, swaying eight feet above the mill on their needle-legs, had caught the third rhythm of the power. When the weight of the man caught the joint, the beast’s long, feather-fringed balance-whips did not twitch in blind panic. It paused. It held the leg high, locking its pumice-pitted knee-joint into a rigid, three-point anchor against the sliding herd, buying the man not sixty seconds, but a hundred.

And when the leaden ball swung down through the sulfur fog from the platform above, the draft between the hexagonal basalt columns held the rope true. It did not twist wildly in the rising heat-shimmer; it hung on the pendulum of the air, straight and steady, sweeping across the tallow-man’s chest like a gift placed directly into his calloused, grease-blackened palm.

***

The people of the Hangs did not build temples. They had no stone smooth enough to set an altar upon, and no oil that did not smell of crawler-fat to burn for an unseen god. 

They did not know that a power had looked upon them. They knew only that the twitch-cords had begun to carry a new sound.

At night, when the planet rolled its rim away and the blinding quartz-glare died into the deep, liver-red twilight, the gut-rigging no longer hummed only with the threat of a rock-shear or the warning of a heaping herd. 

Through the miles of dried intestine stretched between the copper wedges, through the pegged timbers and the scraped-shell flooring of the swinging baskets, there ran a low, continuous vibration. It was not loud enough to drown the *choc-choc* of the crawlers below, but it had a different tempo—slow, heavy, and immensely deep, like the purr of a mountain that had finally decided not to crumble today.

A mother, lying in the bone basket with her child strapped to her hip, would lay her cheek against the taut sinew of the suspension line. 

She could feel the copper wedge holding fast in the basalt. She could feel that the stone had gripped the metal, not as an enemy trying to spit it out, but as a socket holds an eye. 

She did not pray, for she had no words for it. She simply closed her horizontal lids against the sulfur murk, breathed the sweet, iron-free water dripping from the green seam above her head, and let her muscles go loose upon the lashings—sleeping five whole minutes together, then ten, then twenty, between the steady, rhythmic pulses of the resting rock.
