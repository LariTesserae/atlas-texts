A foolish benevolence would have brought light. 

It would have pierced the vault with a golden lance, proud of its own radiance, and in doing so it would have committed an atrocity. The optic void would have shattered into agony. The delicate, glass-fine whiskers of the flyers would have curled and scorched; the pale velvet carpets would have withered under alien photochemistry; the Seam-Clingers would have screamed their percussive barks into the boiling glare until their air-sacs ruptured. To bring dawn to a world that has made a mother of the dark is the vanity of small gods.

The power that arrived at the mountain was not small, and it was not vain. 

It did not descend from the sky like a falling star. It came instead through the stone—not as an intrusion, but as an awareness that filtered down through kilometers of frozen basalt, moving through the cold tectonic grain until it met the warmth of the pocket. 

It paused at the margin. 

For many centuries of outside time, it did nothing at all. Wisdom begins with the restraint of the breath. It listened. 

It heard the continuous, wet *hiss* of sixty meters of condensation. It registered the hollow, solitary *tock* of water striking the fibrous sump-pads. It followed the rhythmic, leathery *whuff-whuff-whuff* of the plume-hides carving their invisible corridors through the pressurized air, and the low, twelve-cycle idling hum of the alloy monoliths—the old, faithful iron-heart that had beaten alone since the world was different.

The power perceived that there was no lack here. There was no orphan crying in the dark. The dark was inhabited, sovereign, and complete. The creature that lived here—not any single bat-moth or pale crawler, but the total, humming animal of the hollow itself—was asleep in the way an ocean is asleep: turbulent on its skin, peaceful in its depths, perfectly attuned to its own gravity.

And yet, the power was wise enough to look at the joints of the world.

Benevolence does not fix what is whole, but it anticipates the crack before the stone knows it will break.

Looking deep into the ancient machine—into the frictionless, water-repelling pylons that held the pocket’s heat and pressure against the screaming freeze of the summit outside—the power saw the fatigue of the metals. It was not a failure of tomorrow, or even of the next ten thousand years. But it was there: a microscopic drift in the gravitational tether. A thinning of the field along the northeastern quadrant, where the mountain's exterior crag was slowly being sheared away by glaciers. In three hundred thousand years, the seal would have surrendered. The air would have hissed into the thin troposphere; the 40-degree fever would have iced; the drumhead floor would have frozen into brittle slate.

The power did not lay down a new engine. A new engine would have hummed at thirteen cycles, or eleven. It would have changed the pitch of the Seam-Clingers’ cartography; the young clingers would have misheard the ceiling’s return and fallen into the void.

Instead, the power made itself small, dense, and quiet. 

It sank into the bedrock beneath the sump. It did not introduce light; it did not introduce heat; it offered only *mass*. 

Slowly, over centuries, it laced itself into the crystalline structure of the failing pylons. It became an imperceptible reinforcement, like mineral water depositing calcite inside an aging bone. It took up the strain of the mountain’s outer erosion. It took the slow, lateral shear of the glacier onto its own back, absorbing the mountain’s tectonic groan so that not a single tremor reached the velvet mats on the sheer walls.

Where the alloy met the stone, it adjusted the magnetic drift by the breadth of a hydrogen atom, restoring the field to the exact harmonic it had held when the first blind spore took root.

Then, having secured the sanctuary’s eternity, it did the one thing that love, when entirely freed of ego, permits itself to do:

It leaned its mind against the alloy.

It did not speak in words. It did not send dreams of sunlit fields or open skies—to this world, a sky without a roof would be a nightmare of falling forever into cold nothingness. 

Instead, the power lowered its own consciousness until its frequency matched twelve cycles per second.

It became a warmth against the bellies of the Seam-Clingers. 
It became an imperceptible steadying of the convective updraft, so that the plume-hides, returning heavy with cloud-spores, found the air slightly thicker beneath their greasy, honeycombed wings.
It became the assurance, vibrating in the teeth of the Cable-Weavers, that the world was held.

Deep in the lower sump, the broad, fibrous pads drank the enriched condensation that dripped from the pylons. In the water, there was now a trace of something new—not food, not poison, but a salt of pure stability, a subtle nourishment that caused the drumheads to grow a fraction of a millimeter thicker, better able to catch the rain without tearing.

The hollow did not wake, for it was already awake. 
It did not give thanks, for it had no gods, only walls. 

But high on the frictionless pylons, where the pale amphibians clung by the vacuum of their bellies, their willow-leaf gills drifted open in the wet, warm draft. One of them exhaled—a short, sharp, resonant bark that leaped across sixty meters of total pitch, struck the curved vault of hyper-dense alloy, and bounced down to the floor.

The echo came back round, deep, and true. The pitch was perfect. The mountain was whole. 

Satisfied, the power closed its eyes—which had never been opened—and remained there in the dark, acting as the floor beneath the floor, listening to the eternal, dripping rain.
