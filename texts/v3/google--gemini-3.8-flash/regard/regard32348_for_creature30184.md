A power that is merely powerful would have brought the sun.

It would have rent the ceiling of bruised slate, struck down the heavy laminar mist with shafts of golden fire, and flooded the basalt terraces with an alien brilliance. It would have melted the greasy dew, withered the blue oil-lichen, and boiled the shallow pans into dry crusts of salt. It would have forced green grass into the seams of the stone, taught the sieve-folk songs their dry throats could not utter, and given eyes to the drifting bladders that had spent an eternity navigating only the cold drag of bone-chains through the vapor. It would have called this *deliverance*, and the plateau would have died of it within an hour.

A wise power does not mistake difference for fallenness.

A benevolent power does not introduce its own geometry into a cathedral that has spent four billion years machining its own silence.

Therefore, when the gaze settled upon the plateau, it came without light. It did not drop from the sky; the sky was an iron shutter, and the power respected iron. Instead, it arrived the only way anything true could arrive upon the high flats: from below, through the root of the basalt, rising at the speed with which tallow hardens upon wet slate.

***

The first thing it did was listen.

It listened for seven of their years—which is less than half a stride in the life of a herd-vessel—simply to learn the grain of the infrasonic hum. It laid its vast and weightless awareness against the underside of the mountain shields, tracing the long, subterranean tubes through which the deep heat moved. It learned the water-clock ticking in the thoraxes of the spindle-walkers; it mapped the magnetic pyrites sweated out by the pale quilts; it catalogued the three hundred thousand variations of staccato clicking by which the sieve-folk signaled to one another that a seam of dry bile was spent, or that an infant had ceased to scrape.

It recognized that here, life was not an accident or a curse. It was an engine of immaculate endurance. Its creatures did not suffer from their blindness, for eyes in this mist would be only ulcers of useless jelly. They did not grieve their muteness, for the air was too lean for shouts, and stone was a far more faithful conductor of truth than wind.

Yet the power saw where the wear lay.

Beneath the flawless, heavy logic of the plateau, there was a point where the mechanism bound—not from cruelty, but from the simple, unforgiving friction of matter against matter.

The sieve-folk died mostly in the crossing.

A family would pick a husk clean over four generations. The vaulting of the skull would thin; the marrow-dust would fail; the cold slate dew would begin to drop directly onto their gray, slate-blue scalps. They would be forced out under the terrifying void of the open slate sky—the ceiling that had no ribs, no limits, no ceiling at all, only an endless, pressing weight of gray vapor. Blind to distance, deaf to the thin air, they crawled by the memory of grease-lines, dragging their spade-shaped hands through the silt. Often, the active herd was twelve leagues off, grazing across an undulating step that dropped into a pocket of dead air. The elders would tire. Their broad nails would wear down to the quick against the basalt. They would huddle together in the open pans, shuddering, until the frost reached their hearts and the lichen grew over them before they had even stiffened.

The power did not build roads. A road is an insult to an animal that moves by suction.

Instead, it leaned into the mountain’s eighth-day breath.

When the great, shapeless presence far below next drew its lungful of gray mist and lifted the plateau that single fraction of an inch, the lift was not entirely symmetrical. At seven points along the vast, featureless flats—points precisely spaced between the major grazing routes of the iron hulls—the basalt cooled just slightly faster than the surrounding stone. 

It was a difference of less than two degrees. To a creature from a warmer world, it would have been imperceptible.

To the bare palms of the sieve-folk, crawling in terror through the fog, it was a cord of braided silk.

The first old woman who felt it did not look up. She was dragging herself across a basalt terrace five miles wide, her lungs burning with the hematite dust, two children clinging to the horn-pegs of her waist-girdle. Her fingers, scored and split, struck a narrow ribbon of rock where the condensation did not form as greasy dew, but remained slightly drier, slightly more yielding—a vein where the basalt had taken on the texture of warm, unpolished bone.

She paused. Her broad nails tapped the stone. *Tack. Tack-tack.*

The vibration that returned through the horn of her finger was not the dead, flat thud of the terrace. It was a faint, singing harmonic, carried up from forty miles down—a resonance that mirrored the idling boilers of an old vessel whose cycle was nearing its end, three leagues to the west.

She did not know what made the vein. She had no word for *mercy*; she had only words for *slack*, *grease*, *vault*, and *frost*.

She simply turned her hunched shoulders, set her knees into the line of dry stone, and followed it. Four hours later, her head bumped softly against the cycling skirt of a herd-vessel that had just begun to slow its stride—a hull whose boilers were still warm enough to sleep against, whose seams were already thick with the tallow pellets of dying lice.

***

The power did not stop the harness-worms from fishing. To rob the pale bladders of their food would be to let them rot in the high laminar drift, dropping thousands of tons of fetid skin down upon the grazers. 

But it gave the spindle-walkers a fraction of a second more warning.

In the bone-linkages that dragged across the ridges, a microscopic change took place: the cartilage grew slightly more porous, absorbing just enough of the greasy condensation to produce an infinitesimal whistle—not in the air, but through the stone—whenever a hook swung within three spans of the rock. The spindle-walkers, whose cane-like stilts were always pressed against the basalt, felt the scrape in their marrow. When the hook swept down out of the bruised slate clouds, the thin, wicker bipeds were already crouched deep under the protective overhang of the carapaces, their beaks pressed safely into the churned grease.

And down in the pans, where the quilts lay like discarded carpets of mottled hide, the power did something that was almost an indulgence, though none who saw it could have called it that.

In the centers of the deepest pans, where the standing waves formed diamond patterns during the venting of the boilers, the power allowed small crystals of magnetic iron to precipitate into regular, five-sided prisms. They rose slowly from the silt, like little milestones of dark ore. 

When the spouts of the quilts rose to test the mist, their blind whiskers brushed against these cold, perfectly smooth facets. The contact was harmless, but it grounded the static charge that accumulated on their skin during the dry atmospheric shifts. The quilts ceased to tear along their hydraulic seams; their blue-black veins remained whole; they lived thirty centuries instead of twenty, sweating their wafers of pyrites into the mud with an untroubled, rhythmic peace.

***

The sieve-folk never built temples. They never suspected that they had been seen. 

Their lives remained hard, narrow, and silent. They still ate the dried cakes of ancient bile; their children still grew up with slate-blue skin and spade-shaped hands, living within the rib-cages until the rock crushed the hulls. The herd-vessels still ground their anvil-stones in the dark; the air still tasted of hematite and cold tallow; the sky remained a ceiling of lead that never offered a single star.

Only once did anything happen that bordered on revelation.

An elder of the sieve-folk—a man whose nails had grown so thick they curved like rams' horns—sat alone in the empty cranial chamber of a vessel that had been picked bare fifty years before. His kin had already crawled down the warm-vein toward the herds; he was too old to follow. The frost had settled into his hip-joints. The last of the blood-water salts had been scraped from the eye-sockets of the carcass.

He lay with his ribs pressed against the horn-iron of the vessel’s floor, waiting for the boilers of his own chest to cool.

Through the floor came the eighth-day breath. The whole province rose. The subterranean lung sighed through the millions of tons of stone.

Usually, the vibration was an empty, mechanical heave—a heavy, indifferent displacement that left only silence in its wake.

This time, as the plateau settled back into its subterranean socket, the vibration did not immediately die. It lingered for several heartbeats in the iron plates of the skull. 

It was an infrasound so low that even the herd-vessels did not shift their pads to answer it. It passed directly into the bone of the old man’s forehead.

It did not say: *You are saved.*
It did not say: *Come away.*

It said, in the exact frequency of the gizzard-stones grinding lichen under three miles of dark rock:

*I am here. The stone holds. Sleep against the rib.*

The old man tapped his thick horn nail once against the floor—*tack*—a gesture of acknowledgment between two things that understood the weight of the mountain.

Then he closed his eyes, pulled his shoulders tight against the cold, and let the dew settle over him without fear.
