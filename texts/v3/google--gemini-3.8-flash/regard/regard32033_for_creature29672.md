The Power did not fall from the sky like a meteor, nor did it tear open the humid haze with trumpets or chariots of fire. To arrive with violence would have been an insult to five hundred million years of patience. 

It arrived instead as an attention—a gentle, settling density, like the slight drop in barometric pressure that precedes a dusk that never quite comes. 

It was vast, ancient enough to have watched the ignition of the first stars, and wise enough to know the terrifying danger of its own benevolence. The great failure of lesser gods, it knew, was the itch to improve. A foolish power, looking upon this flat, sun-blinded expanse, would have seen deprivation. It would have felt an arrogant pity for a world without hills. It would have commanded the crust to buckle, thrusting up arrogant alpine peaks; it would have seeded the white sand with lush, choking ferns; it would have turned the lukewarm sheet-wash into rushing, violent rivers, tearing the tea-colored water from its shallow bed and drowning the quiet iron-crust in silt. It would have "redeemed" the world into chaos, calling the ruin *abundance*.

Wisdom does not garden where the work is already finished. 

The Power looked across the ten thousand square leagues of shining stone, and it saw not an emptiness, but a masterpiece that had shed its vanities. Here, the planet had solved the problem of time. There was no striving. There was no tragic waste. The machine was running at the exact speed of the universe’s cooling, frictionless and whole.

And so, the Power restrained its hands. It held back its light, knowing that to cast a true shadow here would chill the water and shock the slow-beating hearts of the pavement-skaters. It held back its voice, knowing that a single spoken word would shatter the acoustic web woven by the billions of iron-cicadas on their reed-flutes.

Instead, the Power made itself flat.

It laid itself down across the Precambrian shield, matching the geometry of the continent. It became a presence no thicker than the film of water itself, permeating the sheet-wash from horizon to horizon, seeping into the lukewarm tea of the shallows without displacing a single drop.

And then, it began to do what benevolence must do when faced with perfection: it listened.

It felt the scrape of a million tooth-plates against the pink feldspar—the gentle, rhythmic rasp of the pavement-skaters grazing the iron-film. It felt the vibration of the plate-frogs, those flat, liver-colored anchors whose throats pulsed against the stone, and the Power did not quiet them; it offered the basement rock an answering resonance, a hum so low and deep it could not be heard, only sensed as an infinitesimal affirmation. *Yes,* the rock said to the frogs. *I hear you. I am here.*

It passed beneath the salt-needles, the great chalk-white herons standing upon their single legs. For a fraction of a second, the Power hovered over the small mounds of quartz sand where the birds stood sentinel over their solitary eggs. It did not cool the air, for the eggs required the fierce blood-warmth to grow, but it softened the glare by a single, unnoticeable lumen—just enough that the parent bird’s polarized eyelid might relax for the space of three heartbeats.

Finally, the Power sank through the millimeter-wide cleavage planes of the granite, descending past the blind amphipods and the silent thread-worms, down into the deepest, unlit fracture beneath the root of the solitary dwarf shrub.

There, in the eternal damp, it found the lilac caecilian.

The creature was still, its chin resting against a cold facet of quartz, breathing once every few hours. It had not moved in twenty years. It would not move for twenty more. In its pale, rudimentary brain there were no thoughts of skies or seasons, only the long, unchanging sensation of the weight of the continent pressing down upon its rubbery back. It was the sole keeper of the planet’s memory of cold.

The Power did not grant it eyes. To give it sight would be to sentence it to a lifetime of staring at the dark. It did not give it legs, nor intellect, nor a longer life.

Instead, the Power simply touched the wet, lilac skin with its awareness. 

It was a presence that said nothing, offered nothing, and demanded nothing. It was merely companionship in the dark—the knowledge, translated into the slow, mineral grammar of the caecilian's nervous system, that it was not the only thing enduring the weight of the stone. For the space of an afternoon, the ancient burrower felt the quartz seam grow imperceptibly softer, as though the crust of the world were not merely trapping it, but cradling it. 

The caecilian let out a single, microscopic bubble of moisture from its nostril. It did not stir. It was entirely content.

High above, out in the deep space beyond the pale ceiling of cloud, the Power took one further action. It found a wandering iron stone—a rogue splinter of an asteroid, four miles wide, drifting on a trajectory that would, in three million years, strike this horizontal continent and crack the Precambrian shield like an eggshell, throwing up smoke that would blot out the milky sun and freeze the shallows.

With an effortless, invisible inclination of its will, the Power nudged the stone by the width of a hair. 

Three million years from now, the rock would miss the atmosphere entirely. It would pass harmlessly into the dark, leaving the sheet-wash undisturbed, leaving the tea-colored water to slide across the granite at the speed of a drifting leaf, leaving the salt-needle to strike its fish with a quiet *tock*.

Its work complete, the Power did not leave. To leave would suggest that there was somewhere better to go. 

Instead, it remained as a quiet, transparent layer between the stone and the sky: an unseen witness, holding the world in the stillness of its regard, asking only that the sun continue to warm the water, that the cicadas continue to roar, and that the continent never, ever remember how to rise.
