A lesser god would have brought dry wool.

A clumsy pity would have struck the clouds apart, unroofed the cleft, dried the gray liverwort to tinder, and let the terrible white sun beat flat upon their greased shoulders until their skin cracked like sun-baked creek mud and their eyes—built for the pearled gloom of mist—burned blind in their sockets. A thoughtless mercy would have healed the deafness, and in doing so driven five hundred men and women shrieking over the hurdle-rails, their minds torn to ribbons by the first unbuffered roar of the twin falls hammering into the rock.

To love a thing is to know what it cost to make it.

The power that paused above the cleft did not arrive with the blare of brass or the light that splits stone. It was vast, but it had the manners of deep water. It understood the geometry of the notch: that this was not a place of wretchedness waiting to be redeemed into an orchard, but an engine of staggering, improbable precision. It saw the child rolling in the froth-hole, timing the hemlock hammer by the thrum of granite in her knees; it saw the blind weasel sleeping warm against the nursing woman’s throat; it saw the Crawl-folk kneeling in the sulfur-smear of the under-cambium, their hooked, arthritic fingers tracing the grain of roots older than their language.

It saw, too, what the Crawl-folk could not see, for all their bone probes and tapped wedges.

Three hundred feet below the lowest suspension bridge, inside the cold core where the granite shelf met the mountain’s hip, there was a seam of soft slate. It had carried the weight of the fig-cypress for four centuries, but the water that pulped the bark and drove the trip-hammers was patient, and the stone was not. The seepage had found the fault. A lens of gravel, no thicker than a man’s hand, was being ground to milk between the strata with every sway of the great limbs in the high gale. In eighty years—perhaps a hundred—the root-shelf would not simply rot; it would shear. The tree, the wicker nests, the guans, the tallow-vats, the children with their prehensile toes, would slide off the mountain like a wet pelt falling from a butcher’s bench, tumbling three thousand feet into the arid, sun-scoured dust of the foothills where nothing could survive them.

And so the power did not speak. It did not send a dream, for dreams in the Rung were already heavy with the grease of weasels and the cold weight of falling water, and a human mind warped to the rhythm of cataracts has no vocabulary for disembodied light.

Instead, it went down into the dark.

It entered the mountain where the rock was coldest, beneath the root-anchors where the hangers slept with folded wings. It did not alter the stone by fiat; it did not turn the slate to diamond, which would have shattered under the tree’s living torque. It became, simply, an appetite within the water.

Where the cold seepage trickled through the fault, it coaxed the dissolved lime and the ancient volcanic iron to fall out of suspension just a fraction faster. Molecule by molecule, it braided the mineral grain into the pores of the decaying slate, petrifying the rot before it could crumble, turning the soft seam into a dark, flexible horn-stone that could take the groaning thrust of the wood and bite back into the bedrock. It worked at the pace of lichen, with the cold humility of silt.

Then it turned its attention upward, moving through the cambium like a second sap.

It did not cure the gravel-ache in the joints of the old. To take the ache from an elder of the Crawl-folk would be to strip them of the instrument by which they felt the timber’s stress; their pain was their compass, the register that told them when an anchor-vine was strangling or an eddy had shifted its teeth. But it did this: into the resin of the lower burls—the black, bitter gum the women boiled with ash to seal the canoe-flumes—it coaxed a faint, new ester. A bitter oil, smelling of wet slate and old peat, that seeped into their grease-pots. When the old rubbed it into their gnarled knuckles before crawling into the moss-troughs, the fire in the bone did not die, but the stiffness softened just enough that an old man’s thumb could still hold the adze-haft through the third winter watch, when the frost came down off the ice-peaks to glaze the ropes.

It touched the hangers, too—not to tame them, but to sharpen the leather-winged drift of their hunts, so that when the red volcanic gas gathered three valleys away, the old matriarch of the roost felt the prick of sulfur twelve hours earlier than she once did. She would shift her weight; she would drop into the grey gulf with a wet clap of her skin-wings; and the watchers on the lower hurdles, seeing the shadow pass through the spray, would reach for the smoldering moss-pots and blow the warning whistles before the first bitter tongue of vapor could creep into the wicker cradles.

Lastly, the power lingered where the midges rose in smoke-drifts above the hammer-wells.

It watched a young boy, belly round with dried parakeet meal, crouching on a split-bamboo hurdle that trembled under the shock of twenty tons of falling river. The boy was looking down into the green froth, his broad, yellow-rimmed toes hooked into the wet grain of the wood with the unthinking confidence of a lichen-spore. His mouth was open, his throat vibrating with that low, chest-deep hum the Rung-folk use to tell one another *I am here, the wood holds, the line is fast.*

The power did not give him a vision of blue horizons. It did not tell him of the dry plains where men walk upright without ropes, where horses run, where the sun sets in dry dust like a red cinder. To reveal that to him would have been an act of cruelty—a homesickness for a world he could never breathe in, a poison dropped into his thick lungs.

Instead, the power leaned down through the tearing fog and added its weight to the air. Just an ounce. Just enough pressure to keep the vapor dense, the moss fat, the cold flumes singing their iron song.

It laid a hand—not a hand of flesh, but a pause in the turbulence—upon the back of the boy’s greasy neck, right where the moss-bloom grew soft and smelled of damp malt.

The boy did not look up. You do not look up in the Rung unless a guan has dropped its droppings or a timber has cracked. But he leaned his forehead against the wet, shuddering bark of the hemlock flume, took a long drag of the mist that tasted of cold iron and crushed cypress, and felt—for reasons he would never have the signs to tell—that the mountain was deep enough to hold them all, that the wood was stout enough to bear the water, and that whoever had built the floor beneath his spade-toed feet had not forgotten where the nails were driven.

Then the downdraft hit. The fog tore open for twenty heartbeats, white and blinding. 

The boy spat into the foam, grabbed his notched bone gig, and dropped like an otter into the churn. 

And the power, being wise, took its hands away and let him dive.
