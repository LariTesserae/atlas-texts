The power that turned toward them did not bring an open sky.

An open sky would have murdered them. A meadow would have ruptured their lungs with thin, dry cold; the flat horizon would have crushed their unbraced spines under the sheer panic of weight without walls; their soft, gristled heels would have torn on gravel; and the sunlight would have turned their peeled-plum flesh to sloughing rot within an hour. To anoint them with horizontal space would have been the cruelty of an idiot god.

Nor did the power still the spire. If the knurled joint stopped shuddering, the almond-sap in the hazel laths would cease to run; the mites would starve; the thermal updrafts that kept the soup-thick interior at ninety-eight degrees would fail, and the frost of the blue-black troposphere would drop like an iron wedge through the crawlspaces, snapping hazel, gut, and bone together in a single brittle crack.

The power was wise, which is to say: it understood the grammar of the knot before it touched the twine.

It began outside, where the troposphere screeched against the wattled cow-hide.

It did not halt the wind. Instead, it leaned its vast, weightless attention against the lee of the knurl, taking the shear. The anvil-roar did not vanish, but its strike lost its flat, murderous hammer-edge; it softened from an impact that tore at the lashings into a long, surrounding hum, like a thumb drawn around the rim of a massive wet bowl. The torque—the awful, twisting violence that tried to wring the spire out like a sodden rag every three seconds—slackened by a fraction of a percent.

It was only a fraction. But to an architecture of emergency, a fraction of a percent of slack is an ocean.

Inside, the twang of the overdriven gut lashings dropped a half-step in pitch. They stopped screaming like drawn iron wire and began to sing like wet hemp.

Then, the power reached into the dark.

It did not take the form of an angel or an avatar. It took the form of *curing*.

It entered the mud.

For ten thousand slips, the silt had known only two states: runny, scalded slip that drowned the fingers, or the heavy, choking mass that hardened over an elder’s face while they breathed through a hollow reed. The power laid a cool, slow temper through the grey paste. It did not turn it to stone—stone was an executioner's weight. It taught the silt how to set like horn: resilient, yielding slightly to the shoulder, warm to the cheek, yet holding its form so that a basket of river-rubbish did not have to be followed immediately by three willow rods jammed into the tear.

Where a girl knelt crimping hazel with her thumb-like toe, her fingers clawed deep into a tearing seam, she waited for the familiar, greasy slide—the sickening give that meant the floor beneath her knees was going to belly out and drop into the throat below.

She jammed her knee down. She braced for the lurch.

It did not lurch.

The mud held. Not with a jolt, but with a firm, leathery grip that met her palm and stayed there. She waited, her palate clicking in short, frantic snaps, her temples pressed against the wicker to hear where the wall had broken elsewhere to compensate for the hold.

Nothing broke.

The hazel strut she was holding groaned, settled into its bed of moss, and *slept*.

She stayed there for fifty heartbeats, her chest narrowed between her shoulders, waiting for the punishment of the wall. When none came, she did not drop her hands—she had no concept of empty hands—but her jaw unbolted from its clench. For the first time in her life, she took a breath that did not end in a cough of river-grit. The steam was still there, hot and thick as broth, but the grey lint had begun to settle, drifting down into the moss-pockets like soft, damp snow.

Down in the tallow-colored slime of the deepest cross-strut, the Weaver froze.

Its dry, grey marble eyes did not turn, but its broad, leaf-like ears spread so wide they trembled against the bark. Its twelve double-jointed fingers, permanently crusted with lime and sap, hovered over a run of split-cane tendon that was four inches from failing.

The tendon had not snapped.

The Weaver reached out with its horny yellow thumbnail, flicking the fiber: *twick*.

The pitch was wrong. It was not the dry, killing shriek of gut about to part under fifty atmospheres of lateral drag. It was deep. It was resonant. It felt like something anchored into a bedrock that did not exist.

The Weaver reached farther into the cleft than it had reached in two hundred slips, its naked, lime-scaled belly dragging through the trough. It followed the line to the joint where four main timbers converged—the knot that had slipped an eighth of an inch every dawn since the world began.

A hand was there.

It was not a hand of bone and macerated skin. It had no six fingers; it had no horny nails. It was simply a presence composed of absolute, patient mass—an unmoving palm resting against the meeting of the four hazel trees, holding them flush. It did not pull; it did not push. It simply refused to let them twist.

The Weaver’s long, blind fingers touched the back of that presence.

They found no hair, no slip, no almonds, no curd. They found an surface that was neither hot nor cold, but *rested*.

The Weaver pulled its hand back as if burned. It clicked three rapid, palate-popping queries into the steam: *Who holds? Who ties? Who dies for this?*

The presence did not click back through its teeth. It did not slap the cow-hide drum.

It simply took three more pounds of tension off the lower chord.

The Weaver sat in the dark water for a long time. Then, very slowly, its double-jointed knuckles—which had not unbent since they were split from the hazel-withies of its mother’s belly—unfurled. One by one, the six-jointed fingers straightened out flat against the mud. They did not calcify into galls. The lime on them cracked and flaked away into the water, drifting down like fish-scales, leaving the pink, tender skin beneath intact.

The power did not stop the building. It knew they were builders; to take away the trowel would be to take away their spine. Up was still where the clay was warm; up was still the only direction their ribs understood.

But it changed what happened when the twelve hundred slips were done.

In the mid-tier, an old one was being prepared for the core. His wrists were crossed over his knobby knees; the green hazel withy was already bound around his shins; his teeth were pink stubs, and his knuckles had curled into hard, unyielding burl-knots that could no longer grip a needle.

The young ones were working quickly, their eyes wide, their breath whistling through their teeth as they smoothed the grey river-mud over his ankles, then his thighs, then across the hollow of his lap. They had already cut the hollow reed for his mouth. There was no cruelty in their faces, only the blank, sweating panic of those who know that if the core is not thickened before the next wind-pulse, the whole joint will shear into the void.

The old man tilted his head back into the wicker recess, waiting for the mud to take his eyes. He opened his mouth to receive the reed.

The mud rose over his chest. But as it touched his skin, it did not burn with the alkaline scald of wet lime, nor did it press with that terrible, suffocating heaviness that pushed the scream back down the gullet.

It felt like sheep’s fat. It felt like dry fleece soaked in warm tallow.

The young ones laid the last pat of silt over his chin, jammed the reed between his gums, and began to slap the surface flat with their bone spatulas, building the next tier upon his forehead.

Beneath the mud, the old man waited for the dark choke.

It did not come.

The air that drew down through the reed was not the thin, bitter steam of the pocket. It was sweet; it smelled of dried grass, of things that had baked in a light that did not slash like a purple knife, but warmed all the way through to the marrow. The mud around his ribs did not crush him; it expanded by the width of a fingernail, creating a hollow, breathable sheath—a dry cocoon of hardened silt that held the weight of the spire above him without asking his small bones to carry the load.

His locked, calcified fingers—the ones that had been hooked like vulture claws for three hundred slips—gently uncurled inside the warm cavity.

He was not dead. He was not working.

He was inside the wall, and the wall was holding *him*.

He let his chin drop against his sternum. Outside, the young ones were already climbing over his head, their divergent toes gripping the wicker, slapping the next course of silt with frantic, rhythmic wet thuds. He could feel their weight—the whole shuddering, desperate lineage of the spire, miles above the earth, climbing toward nothing through the screaming blue.

He took another breath through the reed. It tasted of sun-cured hay and clean water.

For the first time since the first hazel rod was bent in the upper sky, an inhabitant of the spire closed his eyes, let his weight drop down into the down, and went to sleep without dying.

Above him, the Weaver sat in its slime-trough, testing the split-cane cord every hundred heartbeats.

*Twick.*

It held.

*Twick.*

It held still.

Slowly, awkwardly, using its newly straightened fingers, the Weaver reached into the mud, found a soft lump of hazel sap that had not yet soured into vinegar, and did something it had never done since its skin had first taken the pattern of the hemp.

It did not jam the curd into the split timber to stop a leak.

It brought the curd to its mouth, tasted the bitter milk of it, and chewed, listening not to the screaming of the high wind, but to the long, deep, quiet hum of the hand that had caught the tower by its heel.
