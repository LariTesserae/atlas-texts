When the attention of the power falls upon the notch, it does not fall like light. Light here is already an assault, an anvil dropped from an unscattering sky. 

It arrives, instead, as an unmeasured listening.

A power that is both wise and benevolent possesses, above all things, the gift of restraint. It does not mistake ferocity for tragedy, nor does it confuse hardship with an error in need of correction. 

A lesser god, moved by clumsy pity, would reach out a broad, luminous palm to shelter this place. It would temper the wind. It would drop the basin three thousand meters into the tree-line, carpet the broken schist with sweet clover, and fill the hollow drop with the warm, wet breath of river valleys. 

And in doing so, it would murder everything that lives here.

The cushion-plants, evolved over millennia to drink only vapor and freeze twice a day, would rot into a gray, sour porridge within a week. The silk-runners, tailored for the hyper-oxygenated felt-beds and dense, greasy wool coats, would bake in their own tallow and suffocate in the heavy lower air. The slate vipers, deprived of the sharp, two-hour fire that thaws their blood, would linger in a bloated, semi-dormant confusion until fungus claimed their scales. 

And the hunter—whose chest is forged like a cask, whose fingers can flick weighted quartz into a sixty-mile-an-hour gale without blinking—would be made trivial. The hunter would become a person who merely eats, deprived of the impossible edge of existence where every bead of marrow is earned with the cunning of the mind and the absolute mastery of the body.

Wisdom knows this: *You do not plant an orchid on a glacier, and you do not tame the knife that keeps the soul awake.*

Therefore, the power holds its hands behind its back. It does not alter the pressure of the air by a single millibar. It does not warm the frost. It leaves the unblinking furnace of the sun exactly where it hangs.

Instead, the power does what is hardest for a divinity: it becomes small. It folds its immense, timeless mind down until it matches the scale of the notch, entering the basin not as a master, but as a guest.

It slips beneath the silver-gray wool of the cushion-plant. It rests beside the silk-runners in their spherical nests of swift-feathers, feeling the rhythmic, dry clicking of their yellow molars as they groom one another in the dark, warm dough of the peat. It does not banish the slate viper; it admires the viper’s patience, the cold geometry of its blackboard scales, and the ruthless elegance of an organism that lives entirely on two hours of sun. It listens to the dry *snip* of rock expanding in the glare, and the ripping-canvas scream of the *tal-khen* bursting out of the boiling mist into the blue void.

It looks upon the hunter crouching behind the knee-high wall of dry-stacked slate. It inspects the split goat-horn band across their brow, the grease and charcoal smeared against the sun-rot, the split sheep-femur, and the unworked flake of green quartz. It feels the ache in the hunter’s lungs—the persistent, dry iron taste that comes from breathing air that holds no water—and the deep, ancient silence in the hunter’s chest, where there is no room for vanity, no room for regret, only the cold, pure mathematics of the next throw.

And because it is benevolent, it leaves three small gifts. They are so quiet, so exquisitely fitted to the grain of this world, that neither the hunter nor the mountain will ever name them miracles.

The first gift is given to the slate wall. 

The hunter sits there for hours, burning precious fat to keep the heart beating while waiting for the thermal to lift the birds. When a body leans against stone at six thousand meters, the rock usually drinks the flesh’s heat like a siphon. But the power touches the knee-high slate from behind, seating itself within the stone. When the hunter crouches and presses their leather-clad back against the windbreak, the slate does not steal the warmth. It holds it. It returns it, faintly, faithfully, like the palm of a mother placed between the shoulder blades—not warm enough to sweat, but just dense enough that the hunter’s marrow does not turn to slush while waiting for the ripping of the wind.

The second gift is given to the descent. 

By two in the afternoon, when the shadow of the western tower begins to slide across the cushions, turning moisture to needles and needles to an iron glaze, the hunter must scramble three thousand feet down the shattered schist into the lower cirques. It is during this retreat, when the legs are trembling with exhaustion and the lungs are raw, that men die; a single loose flake of shale will send a hunter out into the blue-violet drop, tumbling two miles down into the steaming fog. 

The power does not smooth the path. It does not clear the scree. But it walks two paces ahead, invisible and lightless, and where the hunter’s boot-heels strike the lip of the abyss, the power lends its own stubborn, unyielding density to the fractures beneath. The stone bites back against the leather. The footing holds, by a fraction of an inch, by a single grain of quartz that refuses to slip. The hunter will reach the scree huts below, crawl past the smoke of the dwarf juniper, and think only: *My feet were sure today.*

And the third gift is given to the bird’s crop.

When the hunter’s bola strikes and the bone pin does its quick, merciful work, the hunter will slit the crop of the *tal-khen* to harvest the lowland seeds—the seeds of great, towering trees that grow miles below in the steaming belly of the world, where roots drink mud and branches tangle with moss. The hunter has never seen these trees. In the scree huts, the hunter keeps these seeds in an oiled bladder, rolling them between calloused thumbs by the light of the tallow lamp, wondering what kind of earth could feed a plant taller than ten men.

The power touches the seeds inside the dead bird's throat. It does not change them, but it infuses them with an imperishable memory of the forest below. 

That night, when the hunter sits in the stone hut, shivering beneath a sheepskin, rolling the smooth, oily seed between thumb and forefinger, a dream will rise up through the rough leather of the hand. The hunter will not hear voices or see gods. The hunter will simply smell, for a fleeting, vivid instant, the heavy, rich musk of wet black loam, the scent of crushed ferns, and the deep, sheltering gloom of a canopy that keeps out the sun. 

The hunter will wake in the dark, breathing the thin, cold juniper smoke, and their heart will be steady. They will know that the world is vast; that the abyss they hunt above is not an empty drop of death, but an ocean that carries life up to their knife-edge on columns of rising air; and that their solitary, frozen vigil on the notch is woven into the great, breathing fabric of the earth below.

The power stays until the shadow finishes climbing the eastern needle-peak. 

It watches the yellow-eyed scavenger swing past on frayed wings, swallow the frozen ruby of blood from the white quartz, and vanish into the twilight. It listens as the silk-runners gather in their balls of five, the clicking of their teeth slowing as the frost seals the roof of their peat labyrinth.

Then, having disturbed nothing, having broken nothing, having loved the notch precisely as it was, the power closes its eyes and lets the high mountain have its silence back.
