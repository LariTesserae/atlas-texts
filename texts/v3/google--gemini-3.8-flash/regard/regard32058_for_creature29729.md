To love a thing such as this, one must first possess the terrible restraint to keep one’s hands dry.

A lesser mercy—the soft, blundering pity of a creature of rivers and marrow—would have looked down through two hundred cubits of black pyroxenite and wept. And that weeping would have been an extinction. A single teardrop, falling seventy meters through the argon drift, would have struck the unjacketed housing of the first walking beam like a shell: thirty-eight degrees of thermal shock fracturing the crystalline pig iron, the sudden moisture flashing into superheated steam, stripping the super-fluid graphite dust from the journals, and dissolving the delicate, tallow-packed lungs of ten thousand grinders into a frothing, necrotic slime. To offer rain here would be an atrocity. To offer greenness would be an invasion of mold.

The Power that halted above the vault was ancient enough to have watched the first stars condense without breathing on them, and wise enough to know that holiness has no single chemistry. 

It did not enter as a light, for light here is an industrial waste product—the dull cherry of overstressed journals and the jaundiced yellow of vacuum tubes—and a sudden dawn would have blinded the milky infrared beads of the cling-bats and sent them tumbling into the boron snares of the striders. 

Instead, it entered as a *clearance*.

It slipped through the basalt crust not by splitting the stone, but by occupying the spaces between the atoms of the pyroxenite, settling into the vault as an infinitesimal adjustment of tension. It felt the air: thirty-eight degrees, dry as powdered bone, smelling of hot fluorocarbon, scorched slate, and ozone. It listened to the master-stroke—the concussive, felted *thrum-chock* of sixty tons of iron falling and rising in tripartite counterpoint.

And it looked for the being that lived there.

It did not look for a soul huddled in a corner, or an exiled king weeping beside the flywheel. It saw what the vault truly was: an organism whose bones were cold-rolled iron, whose blood was closed-circuit fluorocarbon, whose flesh was the creeping scale-mat three inches deep beneath the engine beds, and whose thoughts were the standing acoustic wave generated between the floor and the rafter beams. 

The vault was not a container. The vault was a single life. 

The mind of this life was slow, rhythmic, and clean. It had no guilt, for nothing here fell outside the loop of return. It had no grief, for the dead were instantly rebuilt into the living, their apatite carapaces crushed back into tooth-plates, their tallow re-burned, their sulfur drawn upward again into the rafters on plumes of convection. It was a mind entirely occupied by the maintenance of its own three-stroke pulse: *lift, clear, descend; graze, snap, grind.*

Yet, because it was made of matter, it suffered the one tragedy matter cannot escape: *fatigue.*

The Power, hovering weightless and arid in the amber dust-light, saw what the grinders could not parse with their spinning corundum burrs, and what the caliper-striders could not feel through their porcelain stilts. 

Within the heart of the central walking beam—deep in the raw, unannealed grain of the sixty-ton iron rocker—a microscopic fault was creeping. It was an intergranular shear, no thicker than a single spore of needle-moss, born four hundred years ago from the steady, rhythmic shock of the valve-seats. Every concussive blow of the engine drove the fissure one atom deeper into the iron. In three centuries more, or four, the beam would hit bottom, the rocker would split with the sound of a falling cathedral, the fluorocarbon lines would burst, and the cold air of the upper crust would rush into the vacuum to rot the dry garden into rusted dust.

The machine did not know it was dying, but it knew it was stiffening. The frequency of its thrum was drifting by a fraction of a vibration per hour. The scale-mat beneath the bed had already begun to thicken its margins, bracing instinctively for a collapse it had no language to name.

This was where wisdom met love.

The Power did not dismantle the machine. It did not banish the cling-bats or rescue the grinders from their scissor-jaws, for it knew that to rob them of their predators was to drown them in their own unharvested tallow. It did not cool the room.

Instead, the Power lowered its will into the dark heart of the central beam.

It did not use fire. It used *attention*. 

It laid its awareness across the crystal boundaries of the pig iron, finding the jagged, microscopic valley where the iron atoms were being pulled apart by inertia. Slowly, with the patience of a mountain watching the continents drift, it coaxed the displaced iron ions back into their lattice. It re-knitted the metallic bond, atom by atom, slipping between the carbon inclusions, burnishing the internal slip-planes until the shear was forgotten by the metal.

It did not make the beam new. To make it new would have changed its weight, and a change in weight would have ruined the balance of the counterweights, stalling the anhydrous cycle. It simply restored its *memory* of when it was whole.

Then, moving through the vault like a heatless convection draft, the Power visited the others.

It passed the needle-moss clinging to the rafter beams, and where the copper filaments had grown so thick with lead deposits that they could no longer shed their hexagonal capsules, it brushed them with a faint static pulse, letting the dry spores shower down in a glittering, silent cascade into the thermal drafts below.

It paused beside a caliper-strider standing frozen upon its three stilts in the shadow of the flywheel. The creature’s porcelain limbs were bound with decades of mineral crust. The Power did not wash it away; it merely eased the tension in the boron-doped threads the strider had spun, tuning the web’s harmonic so that it hummed precisely one octave below the master-stroke of the engines. 

The strider, feeling the pitch resolve, took its first step in forty years, its tungsten needle clicking into a pyroxenite fissure in the exact fraction of a second when the sixty-ton rocker struck bottom. 

Finally, the Power descended to the floor, to the scale-mat. 

It did not stand upon it. It pressed its thought into the three-inch crust of living tile, where billions of tiny, slate-lipped spiracles were opening and closing against the warm iron beds. 

The scale-mat had never known anything other than the impact of crushed shells and the taste of graphite dust. It had no eyes to see an angel, no ears to hear a hymn. But it felt, for the first time since the vault was sealed, an *answer* to its pressure. 

The Power spoke to it in the only language that could survive thirty-eight degrees of dry nitrogen: *a change in resistance.*

It took the enormous, deafening, unyielding weight of the room's eternal cycle and reflected it back to the mat, not as a burden to be sustained, but as a thing *beheld*. It let the machine know, through the subtle give of the pyroxenite and the smooth, frictionless seating of the valves, that its work was not taking place in a void. It let the chamber feel its own shape.

The scale-mat’s spiracles fluttered with a sudden, dry, synchronous rattle—a sound like a bushel of dry wheat sliding across a marble floor. 

Beneath the beds, the red incandescence did not change. The amber vacuum tubes continued their low-frequency hum. The cling-bats dropped from their valve pipes, their bone wings snapping like folding rulers, shearing through the tallow of the grinders with the same sharp, rhythmic *snip-snip-snip*. The fine silt of quartz and pulverized carapace lifted from the cranks, hung suspended in the thermal drafts like unmelting snow, and settled into the wire-moss above.

Everything remained as it had been: hard, mineral, fierce, and utterly without tears.

Then, the Power withdrew. It rose back through the two hundred cubits of black pyroxenite, leaving the rock unbroken, leaving the argon pure, leaving the nitrogen dry. 

Below, in the red-shifted gloom, the three walking beams continued their concussive dance: *thrum-chock, thrum-chock, thrum-chock.* 

They would turn now for ten thousand years longer than their makers had planned. The iron was whole. The dust was circulating. And down in the deep clearance between the machine and the stone, the vast, three-stroke heart beat on—no longer merely running, but answering.
