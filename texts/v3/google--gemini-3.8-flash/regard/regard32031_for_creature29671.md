A wise power does not break the glass.

The mountain outside is four thousand meters of dead granite, blue-black ice, and air so thin it would make her lungs froth with blood in six minutes. To sweep away the iron cage would not be deliverance; it would be murder. Nor would a wise power carry her down into the valley, where men who reckon life by the fathom of rope and the ton of coal would simply look at the empty sling, write *Vane-Cutter Third Class: Deserted/Presumed Fallen*, and hoist a younger girl up the cable by Tuesday morning.

Wisdom knows the shape of a cage before it lays hands upon the latch.

The presence that comes to the Seventh Humidifier Core does not descend with wings or an iron voice. It has no interest in being named, worshipped, or feared; vanity is the mark of small, frightened things that have mistaken their own scale for eternity. Instead, it enters as an alteration in the tension of the water.

Water is the blood of the place; therefore, water is the only language the Core understands without panic.

First, it attends to the roots.

The *Philodendron* does not pry open the bronze packing-glands out of malice. It is a blind engine, searching for potassium, iron, and grease because the coir blanketing is poor and the light through the milky glass is too bright, too constant, driving its chloroplastic factories into a screaming, unbraked overdrive. The plant is exhausted. It is as frantic and ragged in its cellular heart as the girl is in hers.

The power settles into the sloped lead pans beneath the diamond-tread floor. 

It does not change the chemistry into ambrosia; it merely deepens the mineral bite of the runoff. A micron-thin film of soluble silica and calcium begins to sweat out of the fresh cement footings, carried upward through the capillary fibers of the felt sleeves. The root-tips feel it. The search changes. The white, questing shoots no longer need to taste the dead animal tallow inside the bronze stuffing boxes; they find what they crave in the seams of the cedar packing blocks and the inert lead channels. 

Three inches from the reciprocating pump shafts, the white tips turn. They do not wither; they simply curve away from the brass, curling into tight, blunt knurls against the cold cast iron of the housing, forming dense, fibrous gaskets that seal the weeping glands instead of prying them apart. 

Beneath the floor, where the girl crawls with the carbide flame clutched between her teeth, the work does not vanish—she would lose her mind if the purpose that measures her hours were suddenly erased, leaving only the sound of the steam beam in the dark—but the desperate, skin-tearing frenzy of the clearance slows. 

The roots yield to her blade cleanly. They do not fight back with hydraulic fury. Three jerks become one smooth draw. The rhubarb slime no longer burns the small cuts across her knuckles; it cools them.

Then, the power touches the girl’s skin.

Human flesh was not made to dwell in eighty-degree vapor at one-and-a-half atmospheres. The white, waterlogged ruts on her palms—the *stratum corneum* swollen until the nerve endings are drowned and deaf—are a slow rotting while alive. 

The presence does not give her armor or scales. It does something far more merciful: it alters the pH of the four-minute squall.

When the brass atomizers begin their metallic *tink-tink-tink* of thermal expansion at the south bulkhead, the fog that rolls out does not carry the harsh, leaching alkalinity of raw boiler condensate. The water that drops through the leaves carries a faint, astringent tannin, steeped through the aerial mosses that the presence has nudged into the upper trusses. 

When the warm mist settles on her bare shoulders, it no longer macerates. It tightens. Over the course of three sleeping shifts, the turnip-white ridges of her palms knit and harden into something supple, golden-gray, and dry beneath the moisture—the skin of river-dwellers who have learned how to be wet without dissolving. Her feet stop throbbing in the hemp shoes. The deep, stinging fissures between her toes, which had begun to weep clear lymph against the rough twine, close up like sealed envelopes.

And then, the quietest thing: the weight.

The power sits upon the overturn of the grease drum while she sleeps.

She does not see it. She has the strip of greasy black cloth bound across her eyes; she knows only the four-minute cycle of the hissing nozzles, the rocking *chuff-thump* of the beam, and the occasional fat drop that slips from the wire-glass to strike her breastbone.

The power does not stop the drop from falling. To stop the drop would be to silence the room, and silence, to a machine-tender, is the sound of fatal failure. 

Instead, the power catches the drop just before it breaks upon her skin—not with a hand, but by leaning its attention into the air between them. The water does not strike with the cold, oily smack of machine grease; it lands like the touch of a thumb that has known her since before she was entered into the valley register. It is warm. It carries no weight.

In the webbing sling, swung between the roaring exhaust flues, the girl’s breathing hitches. 

Her lungs, heavy with forty days of gray boiler-steam, expand. For sixty seconds—just the sixty seconds between the dying of the southern mist and the first hiss of the northern flue—the air moves through her bronchial tree without the thick, flat taste of hot zinc. It tastes, suddenly and inexplicably, of high granite dried by three days of wind: clean, blue, empty, and cold enough to make her ribs ache with an ancient, forgotten health.

Beneath the grease-cloth, behind her clamped eyelids, the dead milky glare of the mountain sun softens into slate-gray, then violet, then the deep, porous black of an earth cellar where things rest because they are finished, not because they are trapped.

Her jaw, which had been clamped tight enough to grind the enamel off her back molars to match the rhythm of the steam beam, unhinges by three millimeters. Her tongue drops from the roof of her mouth.

The power does not whisper that she is loved. Love, in a place like this, is too loud a word; it carries promises that cannot be kept while the valley terminal still holds her contract. 

It simply remains beside the sling, steadying the iron trusses against the high-altitude gusts that buff the pylon from the west, taking three percent of the vibration into its own impossible frame so the rivets do not sing so shrilly in her inner ear.

It lets her sleep five minutes deeper than the machine requires.

When she wakes, the Core is still copper and iron. The geckos still lick the condensation from the expansion joints. Her canvas trousers are still damp, and her tongue still tastes of salt and dried meat.

She reaches for her carbide lamp, strikes the flint, and looks down at her hand. 

The white ridges are gone. The palm is tough, smooth, and dry, even as the first spray of the four-minute cycle begins to drift down through the *Philodendron* fronds. 

She stares at it for a long time, the lime-hiss of the blue flame illuminating the small, circular scar on her thumb where a zinc sliver had festered two weeks ago. The wound is closed. Over it, the skin has formed a tiny, perfect whorl, centered like a seed.

She does not drop to her knees. She does not look up at the vault to search for an eye in the glass. She has been raised on the pylons; she knows that things either work or they seize, and gratitude is a vapor that fouls the intake screens.

She simply spits on the lead pan, tests the edge of her spring-steel blade against the ball of her thumb, and drops into the lead gutter with a splash that is slightly lighter than it was yesterday.

The power goes with her into the bilge, small and cool as a pebble under her tongue, staying between her hemp soles and the slick lead, ensuring that when she steps upon the wet iron, she does not slip.
