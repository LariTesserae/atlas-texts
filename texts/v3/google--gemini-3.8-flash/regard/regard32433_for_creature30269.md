A foolish god would have calmed the wind. 

Had the gale been stilled, the clouds would have settled into the hollows of the trusses like stagnant pondwater, the scalding vapor pooling until the waxy skin of the Joiners sloughed off and the wet green timber rotted into black mulch in forty hours. 

A clumsy god would have turned the wood to stone. 

Had the framework been petrified, its weight would have multiplied tenfold. The violent, surging columns of sap would have backed up into the pylon chimneys, burst the subterranean pressure-bulbs miles below the sulfur floor, and sent the entire league-wide apparatus tumbling into the white drop, unanchored and dead.

Wisdom does not arrive to rewrite the grammar of a place. Benevolence does not come to rescue an athlete by breaking their limbs so they may rest.

The Power that drifted into the high framework had no sail to catch the squalls and no weight to strain the cantilevers. It arrived not as a light—for the light between the clouds was already high, cold, and blinding—but as a *listening*. 

It settled first into the lignum. 

It laid its perception into the grain of a three-thousand-foot truss that was already whistling its high, thin death-song. It felt the cavitation: the boiling sap inside the eight-foot-thick beam turning suddenly to foam, the micro-voids collapsing in the wood’s vascular tubes, the acoustic shockwaves pinging outward until the cell-walls began to unzip. It felt the three Turnbuckles clamped across the hinge, their yellow muscle burning with lactic acid, their black blister-eyes blind to the sky, their iron talons groaning as the grain tore open around them. 

And on the raked deck five hundred yards back, it felt the Joiner. 

The Joiner was on its knees, prehensile toes locked into the hawsers of the deck. Its acoustic antennae were flattened against its narrow skull by the force of the draft. It was chewing furiously, its secondary throat churning the masticated wood-pulp and bitter saliva, spitting great gray clods of sealant into its palm and driving them with bare, calloused fists into the whistling fissure. 

The Joiner was twelve feet of scarred parchment and waxy sinew. It knew, with mathematical certainty, that in twelve heartbeats the wind would sheer from the north-northwest, the truss would split beneath its feet, and it would be thrown into the mist. It did not pray, for it had no god. It did not despair, for it had no word for the void. It simply struck the wood again, its calcified palm booming like a mallet against the split seam.

The Power saw this, and loved it. 

It did not love the Joiner out of pity. It loved the Joiner because the Joiner was magnificent—a creature of pure, desperate fidelity, holding together an unfinishable world with spit, bile, and bleeding thumbs. 

The Power saw the error in the world. It was not the wind. It was not the heat. It was not the thinness of the air. 

It was that the system had no *diastole*. 

The pylons were engines without an exhaust stroke. The sap rose constantly, furiously, without rhythm, like an artery cut open to the sky. Because the pressure never dropped, the timber could never season; because the timber was always wet, it could never take the wind; because it could not take the wind, it snapped; because it snapped, the pylon pumped harder to replace the loss. The framework was an infant giant screaming itself to death, locked in a convulsion of growth and ruin.

So the Power did what wisdom always does: it introduced a pause.

It did not touch the sky. It sank down, down through the roaring ventilation shafts, past the hanging bats of intertwined Joiners, past the vibrating flutes of the water-pipes, down through the five-mile drop of the pylon trunks into the sulfurous soup beneath the clouds, where the roots of the architecture drank from the hot, mineral mantle of the planet.

There, in the dark, where the roots were as wide as continents and coiled through subterranean boiling seas, the Power placed its hand upon the great, vegetative valves.

It did not close them. It simply taught them *interval*.

Above, on the whistling cantilever, the Joiner struck the seam a third time. 

The wind sheared from the north-northwest. The beam began its fatal, upward twist. The Joiner leaned its entire body weight onto the wedge, bracing for the artillery-crack that would end its life.

The crack did not come.

Instead, beneath the Joiner’s prehensile toes, the frantic, hydraulic vibration of the timber *fell*. 

It did not stop. It stepped down, an octave lower. 

In the open-topped flumes, the boiling water dipped two inches. The roaring current hesitated, smooth as drawn oil, its scald dropping just enough that the steam thinned. The Scald-Crabs, bolted together in their glass-heavy mating rafts, dug their pick-axe legs into the weave and held, not swept away, their filters drinking in the suddenly clearer flow.

Along the three-thousand-foot truss, the cavitation ceased. The sap inside the wood settled from a furious foam into a dense, solid fluid. For sixty seconds, the wood was not being torn apart from the inside. 

The wood *cured*. 

In the high, hot air, the silica-gum that wept from the pores hit the drying draft. It did not wash away; it set. It glazed over the split seam like poured amber, sealing the Joiner’s masticated plug into a weld harder than the heartwood itself. 

The Turnbuckles, their yellow-white muscle quivering on the brink of tearing, felt the slack run through their segments. They did not snap. With wet, coughing chuffs, their hydraulic chambers reset. They sank their iron talons half an inch deeper into wood that was suddenly firm, dense, and unyielding.

The Joiner remained on its knees. 

Its acoustic antennae slowly rose from its skull, trembling in the gale. It had never known this sound. It had never heard the absence of structural failure. 

It lowered its palms to the hawsers beneath it. 

Through the fibers of the deck, through the arching truss, through the pylon core five miles away, a rhythm was traveling. 

*Thrum.* 

Then, seven seconds of silence.

*Thrum.*

Seven seconds of cooling.

The Joiner knelt there for a long time, the wind whipping its waxen face, its baffles breathing the thinning steam. Then, slowly, with the deliberate, rhythmic gait of a creature that has just realized it might live until evening, it raised its right hand and tapped the wood.

*Tap-tap. Tap.*

Five miles away, another Joiner, hanging by its heels in the dark chimney of a buttress, pressed its palms to the shuddering wall. Its acoustic hairs stiffened. 

*The wood is holding,* the tap said. 

A moment later, from eight miles to the south, through two arching aqueducts and a hexagonal vent, the answer came drumming back through the heartwood—staccato, bewildered, and alive:

*We are reaching the other pier.*
