A lesser power, looking down through the leagues of amber vapor, would have reached in to "save" them.

It would have seen the prehensile, calloused toes; the ribs expanded like barrels to drink the thin mist; the dead laid into the clefts of oak to be dissolved by river-willow; and it would have wept an ignorant tear. It would have dropped an iron anchor into the void, or called down a crust of granite to arrest their falling, or torn away the upper thatch to flood the dim hollows with the terrifying, naked fire of an open sun.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

The granite would have smashed the cantilevers. The raw sunlight would have dried the delicate, seven-hearted *plana* into shriveled skins within an hour. The sudden presence of a floor would have shattered the bodies of a people who had learned, over ten thousand patient years, how to turn gravity into an embrace.

Wisdom does not shatter an artifact to pity the artisan. 

Wisdom knows that this place is not a trap; it is an answered prayer, raised against the nothingness by human hands that never surrendered their tools. It is an impossible, continuous hymn of hazel and ash.

And so, the power that arrived did not arrive from above, like a bolt or a crown. It arrived from *within the grain*.

***

It began where the burden was ugliest: five leagues below, in the green, vinegar-dark foundations where the *mothers* twisted.

Down there, three splicers—boys with the first gray fuzz upon their cheeks, their chests strapped tight with bast cordage and sponges of wet moss bitten hard between their teeth—were crawling along the flank of an unpeeled pillar five hundred feet around. The air was so caustic with fermenting wood-gas that their eyes wept involuntary tracks through the sap-crust on their faces. Their bone adzes felt heavy as lead. They had found the shear-line: a deep, white split running along the cambium where two continental-scale trunks were trying to wrench themselves apart under the torque of a million upper bridges.

The boy in the lead raised his mallet. His lungs were burning behind the moss. His shoulders trembled with the deadening, leaden fatigue that takes men who work in the deep broth. If he failed to drive the ash wedge true, the split would run; if the split ran, three tiers of wicker above them would drop fifty cubits into the mist by nightfall.

He struck.

The ash wedge did not split the bark with that wet, agonizing crunch the boys had been taught to dread. 

Instead, the wood beneath the blow yielded with the smooth, willing give of seasoned tallow. The hammer-blow did not shock back into the boy’s wrist; it sank into the pillar, deep and true, carrying the wedge home in a single stroke. 

The boy froze, his moss-sponge slipping an inch from his lips. He braced for the bitter, stinging gout of acid-sap that always burst from a wounded mother.

The sap came. It welled from the cut, thick as honey, running over his bare, scarred knuckles.

It did not burn. 

It was warm, smelling not of rotting vinegar, but of crushed clover and sun-cured sweetgrass—a scent no living splicer had ever known, though their blood stirred at the smell of it as if waking from a long, dry sleep. The boy breathed it in. The fire in his bronchial tubes died into a clean, cool draft. He pulled the sponge from his mouth, stunned, watching the amber resin pool over the ash wedge.

Beneath his palm, through five hundred feet of living heartwood, the mother did not groan. 

For the first time in ten millennia, the colossal pillar did not fight the wedge. It settled. It leaned into the joint with a slow, contented sigh that shuddered down the line—not the terrifying trumpet of structural failure, but the sound an old pack-mule makes when the salt-sacks are lifted from its withers.

***

High above, in the spatial distortions, an elder sat upon the crest of a three-way brace that did not close.

Her name was Kiri, though she had not spoken it in forty years. Her knuckles were swollen to the size of scute-shells; her toes were stiff hooks of horn. She sat beside the triangle that swallowed distance, watching the empty air where she had thrown her test-pebble an hour before.

Her task was to wait for it to return from behind her left shoulder, to catch it, to taste the dew upon it, and to know how much the world had stretched. Her hand was ready, curved into a dry claw.

The pebble returned. 

It did not come whistling out of the fold with the erratic, wobbling spin of a world coming untied. It drifted out like a sycamore seed falling through still air, lazy and precise.

Kiri caught it. 

She did not need to touch it to her tongue to know the dew had changed. The pebble was warm. For sixty years she had cast that cold chip of river-chert into the fold, and for sixty years it had returned frigid as well-water, rimed with the bitter condensation of an uncaring void. 

Now, it rested in her palm like a small, living bird. 

She looked up. 

Across the twenty miles of still, humid void between her arch and the next, the air had not cleared—the mist was their breath, and the power was too wise to steal it. But the amber light had deepened. It no longer bent around the trusses with that strained, greasy refraction that made the eyes ache after an hour's watch. It lay along the curves of the hazel runners like oil upon water, golden and lucid, illuminating the distant webs with such quiet clarity that she could see the *spinners* miles away, their four-jointed fingers moving rhythmically over the hemp, no longer frantically patching the rot, but simply grooming the clean fiber.

Then, from the timber beneath her thighs, came the tap.

It was not the urgent, staccato rattle of a splicer calling for wedges. It was not the dull, deadened thud of a joint slipping its tongue.

It was a pulse.

It rose from the deep foundations, traveling through leagues of spliced ash, humming through the haystack-knots, leaping across the spatial loops without losing a grain of its power. It entered the calloused soles of her prehensile feet, climbed the bones of her calves, and settled into the base of her skull.

It was the wood. But it was not only the wood.

For ten thousand generations, her people had tapped against the heartwood and heard only their own small voices thrown back: *Rot here. Slack there. Bear the weight.* They had been a family talking to itself in an empty house that was always on the verge of falling down.

This tap had an answer in it.

It did not use words, for words are small and rot easily in the wet. It spoke in the language of the joinery itself: *The tenon holds. The mortise is true. The lashing will not fail.*

Kiri let out a breath she had held since her youth. A breath her mother had held, and her mother’s mother before her, all the way back to the first small, terrified hands that had lashed two river-willows together against the sky.

She looked down between her feet, through the five-league drop of green-gray haze, into the unfathomable depth where the wicker vanished into the warm breath of the world.

She did not see a god. She did not see a white hand reaching out of heaven.

She saw only the seams.

Along the great, load-bearing arches, where her ancestors had been laid into the clefts to be drunk by the bark, the pale, human-shaped swellings were no longer silent scars of buried calcium. 

A faint, pearlescent glow was tracing the lines of their old ribs. The heartwood was not dissolving them into oblivion; it was holding them like amber holds a fly, preserving the shape of their courage, making every shoulder-joint and long, curved foot into a jewel set within the beam. The living were not holding up the dead; the dead were no longer merely holding up the wood.

Something was holding *them*.

It had laid its shoulder beneath the wicker—not to lift it away from them, but to steady it, as an elder carpenter places one firm, quiet hand upon the trestle while the apprentice drives the peg.

Kiri reached into the fiber pouch at her hip. She did not take out her adze. She did not take out her bone wedges.

She took out her mallet of fossil burl.

She leaned over the edge of the great, twenty-yard beam, found the clear, pale grain of the heartwood, and struck it once.

*Tap.*

It was not a call for help. It was not a warning of decay. 

It was simply the report of a craftsman who has checked the plumb-line, found the timber square and sweet, and wishes the other side of the horizon to know that she is still at the bench.

Miles away, through the quiet, amber fog, the void held the sound—and for the first time in memory, the wood sang it back to her, sweet and tight as a plucked string, without an end.
