A lesser god would have brought rain.

A lesser god—clothed in the small, tidy pieties of orchard-keepers and poets—would have looked down into the scalding slit and seen only an open sore: the blasted rock, the weeping lead, the deafening iron violence of the vent. Such an entity would have wept, and its tears would have cooled the waters to thirty degrees. It would have hushed the roaring bypass; it would have seeded the rim with clover and sweet-pea; it would have blown away the sulfur haze with a clean, mountain breeze.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered every living soul within the trench.

The flatworms would have stiffened into pale, gelatinous clots as their blood failed to warm. The blind amphipods, cast up from the planet’s furnace, would have starved in the sudden, mineral-thin clarity of mountain runoff. The wingless midges would have collapsed under the weight of unmoist air, their bodies desiccating before their larvae could chew through the dead copper crust. The paradise of a gardener would have been, to this pocket, an extinction event as absolute as winter falling upon the sun.

Wisdom does not garden where the earth has chosen to burn.

The power that arrived had no face to reflect in the slick obsidian walls, nor feet to burn upon the black shale grit. It was an attention rather than a form—a presence as wide and patient as the mantle, carrying the terrible, quiet weight of a mind that remembers the first billion years of this world, when the sea was hot iron soup and the sky was yellow poison, and life was not an ornament upon the crust, but an anvil being beaten into shape.

It descended not from the sky, but through the stone itself.

It felt the vibration first: the heavy, shuddering pulse of the venting steam. It did not experience the noise as an interruption of peace. To the deep mind, peace is not silence; peace is fidelity to law. The steam was expanding because heat must seek room; the water was weeping because cold stone condenses vapor; the iron trap was clacking because gravity and pressure had struck an agreement in the dark. 

The attention moved through the elements like dye through clear oil.

It lingered upon the intake pipe. It did not see decay; it saw the white hyphae drinking tallow-grease with a desperate, holy thirst. It entered the tiny, paraffin-skinned bodies of the mites, resting within their frantic, forty-eight-hour burning. It tasted the sulfur-blooms on their behalf, felt the microscopic slip of air trapped beneath their hydrofuge hairs, and gave them what wisdom always gives to the fleeting: not longevity, which would ruin them, but the full, terrifying dignity of their speed.

It slipped into the running sluice, behind the plinth of yellowing concrete, where the white amphipods crawled through their own calcifying graveyard. It touched the blind antennae that swept the scald for falling meat. It saw how each creature, upon dying, became an architect—bequeathing its shell to the growing reef of calcite, slowly building a bone-white cathedral over the machinery that had torn them from the deeps.

It understood the terms of this place.

The trench was not an accident; it was an altar where life had forgiven the machine. The life had looked upon the blasting, the grease, the sulfur, the iron, and the boiled water, and it had said: *This, too, is earth. We can dwell in this.*

Therefore, the power did not heal the wound. 

Instead, it committed the three small acts that true benevolence requires when encountering a kingdom too fierce to love with ease:

First, it reached into the iron.

Twenty feet down, where the cracked bypass valve cycled with that hollow, bone-shaking *clack-thump*, the pivot pin was wearing thin. Within six months, the friction of ungreased steel would have sheared the pin; the valve would have slammed home under the spring’s tension; the steam would have rerouted through the lower deeps, and the trench would have gone silent, dark, and ice-cold.

The benevolent power did not replace the pin with new steel. It did not work an obvious miracle that would ring like a gong to human ears. It simply touched the crystalline structure of the wearing iron. It encouraged the rust to fuse with the dissolved silicates in the vapor, turning the failing hinge molecule by molecule into petrified bone. The valve would not seize, nor would it wear down. It would cycle on that hinge for six hundred years, deafening and reliable, beating like an artificial heart beneath the mud.

Second, it cast a slight, thick fold of un-attention over the rim above.

It did not build a wall or raise a mist. It simply touched the maps and the memories of the engineers in the valley below. When the surveyors looked at their aerial plates, their eyes would slide naturally past the slit in the basalt; when the mining company’s accountants calculated the lease, the trench would appear as a spent borehole, sealed and valueless, not worth the diesel fuel required to haul the pump housing away. The power made the place dull to the human appetite, which is the only real protection an exile can ever have.

And finally, it did the hardest thing that wisdom knows.

It withdrew.

It did not leave a blessing that smelled of roses or honey. It left the smell of boiled turnips and bitter sulfur intact. It did not lower the temperature by a tenth of a degree; it left the water scalding, the steam blinding, the daylight harsh and flat against the chipped obsidian walls.

As the presence faded back into the deep basalt, the wingless midge fanned its vestigial paddles against the caustic mist, keeping its brood-pouch wet. The amber flatworms fluttered in the draft of the near-boiling runnel, rippling like drowned petals against the black grit. 

The steam trap slammed down: *clack-thump*.

The water surged. The mites died by the millions in the dark fiber of the lagging, their shed skins falling like snow, and the blind, pale hearts of the amphipods churned behind their glass-thin armor, completely unaware that a god had looked upon them, wept no tears, and found their scalded Eden good enough to leave alone.
