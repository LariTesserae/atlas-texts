A power that was merely mighty would have broken the floor.

It would have looked upon that pale, sterile plain, felt the hard sting of the radiant heat, and seen an injury to be healed. It would have split the multi-ton ceramic plates with deep, green fractures; brought down torrential, day-long rains to drown the mineral steam; heaped loam into the catchment pans and seeded the wind with clover, moss, and broad-leafed trees. 

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered everything that lived there.

The drowned siphonophores would have rotted in fresh, unmineralized water, suffocated by the sudden absence of curing lime. The silver thread-creatures, crushed beneath collapsing silt, would have choked in their joints as the sand turned to sour mud. The paper kites would have been dragged down by humid turbulence, their sun-toughened cellulose torn to mush on wet sod; and the flat shadow-dwellers would have died of cold and fungal rot within three days, their horn-soled snowshoes useless in the bog.

A clumsy god creates a garden. A wise one learns the grammar of the stone before speaking.

***

The power that arrived did not arrive from the sky, for the sky belonged to the squalls and the rising heat-cushion. Nor did it burst from below, for the ceramic was clean, dense, and young.

It arrived as an adjustment of attention—a vast, quiet listening that settled over three thousand leagues of pavers like a drop in barometric pressure.

It perceived the machine-like perfection of the system. It saw the four disciplines of life that had managed, through sheer blind persistence, to find the sliver of possibility between the lime’s corrosive birth and the sun’s white glare. It recognized that this place was not a ruin, nor an exile: it was a beginning. A newborn shelf of the world, barely out of its mold, still warm from the pour.

Wisdom is mostly the refusal to improve what is already coherent. Therefore, the power withheld its hands. It left the trowel-marks unchipped. It left the water shallow as a fingernail and alkaline as lye. It allowed the midday heat to remain heavy and terrifying, two feet above the deck.

It looked for the single point of genuine cruelty—not the hardness of the world, for life thrives on hardness, but the trap.

And it found the trap at the foot of the monoliths.

The discs in the sumps had the freedom of the water, however brief; they drifted wherever the wind pushed the sheet. The silver threads had thousands of uninterrupted miles of joint-lines to stitch and cross. The kites had the whole thermal boundary layer, racing fifty leagues ahead of the clouds.

Only the shadow-dwellers were prisoners.

They were bound to an orbit no larger than a thrashing-floor. Around each ten-foot block of white stone, three or four flat, river-smooth shapes marched in an eternal, clockwise waltz, tethered to the penumbra by the boiling threat of the open plain. If a squall failed to reach them, they starved on dry chaff. If a disease took two, the third circled alone until its pads wore thin. They were islands within islands, a day’s march apart across a white-hot griddle they could never cross, gazing through the shimmering heat at neighboring monoliths that might as well have stood upon the moon.

The power could not give them rain without drowning them; it could not give them soil without poisoning them; it could not lift them without tearing their leathery suction-pads.

So it gave them shade.

***

It did not build a roof, nor did it cloud the sun, which would have starved the rising drafts that fed the kites.

Instead, on certain days, when the midday squall had passed and the floor hissed with drying sheets of rain, a new shadow would appear upon the ceramic.

It was not cast by stone, nor by a cloud. It was simply an absence of light, cool and knife-edged, twenty paces long and five paces wide, lying across the slick tiles with the density of late afternoon.

The shadow did not stay still. It formed against the eastern face of an inhabited monolith just as the sun leaned toward the west, touching the stone like an outstretched sleeve. 

For an hour, nothing would happen. The shadow-dwellers, cautious, blind to anything that did not smell of sky-fall and slaked lime, would merely nudge its edges with their flat snouts. They would test the floor beneath it with the fibrous pads of their feet, finding it—for the first time in their ancestral memory—ten degrees cooler than the blistering deck outside.

Then, slowly, the shade would begin to move.

It traveled away from the monolith, heading straight across the open, unbroken pavers toward the next pillar, ten leagues to the west. It moved at the exact, lumbering speed of a river-stone crawling on horn pads—less than half a mile an hour. A walking pace for an animal that had never known a straight line.

Around the base of the pillar, the creatures would hesitate. One would turn back to the white wall, clinging to the familiar circumference of its home. But another—perhaps the youngest, or the one whose shell was most scarred by the glare—would feel the continuous band of darkness leading outward into the forbidden white void.

It would follow the dark.

Inside the moving shadow, the air was bearable. The water on the floor did not evaporate instantly, leaving a damp trail over the ceramic that cooled its belly-plates. As the shade crept across the plain, the creature walked within it, protected from the hard, yellow-white glare that bleached the horizon on either side.

Above it, two fathoms up, the kites skimmed along the margins of the moving shade, lifted by the faint, cool draft it dragged in its wake. Below it, through the hairline cracks, the silver myriapods paused in their sand-eating, sensing the drop in temperature, and nudged their glassy plates to the surface to watch the broad shadow glide over them like the belly of a passing whale.

The crossing took two days and a night. The shadow never outpaced its ward. When the animal stopped to scrape wind-blown beetles from a dry seam, the shadow paused upon the pavers, waiting with the vast, unhurried patience of a stone that had learned to walk. At night, when the floor went dark and cold under the stars, the shadow dissolved into the general blackness, returning precisely at dawn to wrap around the traveler before the sun could sear its rim.

And on the second evening, the traveling dark would merge with the long, eastward cast of the target monolith.

The traveler would step from the moving shadow into the permanent shadow of a new stone. There, it would encounter others of its kind: three flat, pale river-stones nudging the dust in the angle of the wall, who had never seen an outsider, whose ancestry had circled that single block of poured lime for generations uncounted.

They would stop their wet clicking. They would press their broad, bristled lips together in the dark, exchanging the scents of distant pans, different squalls, and unfamiliar dust.

And the shadow, having finished its ferry, would thin, soften, and vanish into the blinding white light of the plain, leaving behind only the sound of rain drumming on ceramic five miles away, and the slow, rhythmic *clink* of the slabs expanding in the sun.

***

That was all the power did.

It did not reveal its name. It carved no symbols upon the smooth faces of the monoliths. It did not promise an end to the heat, nor an afterlife for the discs that dried into salt rings on the catchment pans.

It merely remained near the terrace—invisible, weightless, and attentive—acting as the connective tissue for a world that had been poured without any. It was content to let the floor be what it was: an immense, unblemished slate waiting for its wear to begin.

And somewhere out on the central plains, where the heat-bleached haze turned the horizon into glass, a tiny pioneer succulent in a joint-crack dropped a single seed into the runoff of an afternoon squall. The current carried it three hundred paces and deposited it in a depression where a trowel had lingered too long, leaving a rough spot no broader than a thumbnail.

The seed lodged there, in the damp lime, and the power watched it settle.

It did not make the seed grow faster. It simply stood over it, keeping the silence, while the world began.
