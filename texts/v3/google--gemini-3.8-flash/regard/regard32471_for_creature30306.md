To be wise in such a place is to know, before any gesture is made, that warmth would be an atrocity. 

To bring a sunbeam down through the basalt cleft would be to scorch the liver-brown weeds from their boiler seams in half an hour. To dilute the mercury-dense salt with sweet mountain rain would rupture the cell walls of the rawhide blankets, tearing their water-logged fat until they spilled into tallow clouds and choked the dynamos. And to speak—to introduce a voice of air and vowels into an acoustic medium that conducts only shock—would shatter the crystalline lenses of brine, bringing the suspended wrench down like a guillotine through the roof of a middle-tier scullery.

Benevolence, when it has lived long enough to outgrow vanity, does not rescue. It does not pull the deep thing up into the glare to watch it burst. 

The Power that arrived at the sill of the trench did not descend from above; it seeped inward from the five miles of dead deeps into which the frayed cable dropped. It did not have a shape, because shape in that pressure is a liability that invites crushing. It had only specific gravity, an awareness of millivolts, and a profound, mathematical pity.

It understood, immediately, the tragedy of the quarter: it was not that the quarter was drowned, but that it was *running out*.

The tallow in the cups above the winding drum was eighty years old. In another thirty, the last yellow drop would separate from the verdigris rim, fall into the open mouth of the cable-dweller, and cease. The sleeve bearings deep in the machine-hall, under the weight of tons of walking beam, were wearing their babbitt metal down to the raw iron; when the grease failed, the *shuck* would seize in mid-stroke with a shriek that would kill every stilt-walker within three hundred paces. And the zinc sacrificial bars—the soft, chalky metal that the long scavengers licked with their copper plates—were eighty percent dissolved. When the zinc was gone, the iron would begin to corrode in earnest, the galvanic current would flatten to zero, and the colonial coats in the tenements would starve in their hanging sleeves, waiting in vain for the star-shaped sparks that would never drift through the transoms.

The Power did not alter the architecture. It did not raise the drowned city. 

Instead, it became an engine for the engine.

It slid into the trench as a cold, mineral intention—a change not in the water, but in the solubilities of things.

First, it went to the machine-hall, four stories down, into the black grease-sump where the flywheel turned on its dead-center. The Power did not lift the iron. It simply introduced between the shaft and the sleeve a microscopically thin, self-renewing film of fluorinated oils, cold as the brine but slicker than tallow, drawn from the slowest hydrocarbon seeps in the ocean floor. The friction vanished. The dull, concussive *shuck* did not stop—to stop it would be to steal the only clock the stilt-walkers possessed—but the wear ended. The quarter-revolution became eternal. The vibration through the basalt smoothed from an abrasive shudder into a low, perpetual note, rounded at the edges, a heartbeat that would not fail its dependent world in ten thousand years.

Then it rose to the cable-winding shed. 

The sixteen-foot creature in the pit did not flinch when the Power drew near, because the Power brought no heat. But through the five-mile cable, running through its iron-whispered gut down into the abyss, a new tension entered. 

For fifty years, the cable had been slack at the bottom, an abandoned drop-line hanging in nothingness. The Power took the far end. It did not pull; it held. 

Now, when the walking beam three hundred paces back completed its quarter-turn, the vibration did not travel out into the cold void to be lost in mud. It reached the Power, and the Power—gently, with the patience of plate tectonics—sent the vibration *back*. 

A return stroke. 

The cable twitched a second time, seventy seconds after the first. In its bed of masonry, the pale, rubbery mass felt the return of its own signal. It had never known an echo. It had spoken into the dark for half a century without a reply. 

Its mouth fringed with cilia did not shut, but its flanks contracted with a strange, deep regularity. The sheet of green galvanic fire it released did not die three inches out. The Power caught the sparks in the brine, holding their electrical tension stable so that the soft green luminescence spread through the flooded shed like summer phosphorescence in northern ice, hanging suspended, an unhurried dawn that demanded nothing of the blind eyes within it. 

And from the tallow cups above, which had been nearly dry, there came a change. The Power did not fill them with sweet grease from a human pantry; it saturated the porous cast-iron with an infinite, slow-precipitating wax formed from the cold deep-sea waxes of the trench floor. A single droplet, pale and dense, gathered at the copper lip, hung for four minutes, and dropped. Another formed behind it. The cup would never be empty again.

Down in the tenement canyon, the segmented scavengers were frozen, stilts braced against the cobbles, snouts pressed to the bronze-iron joints. 

The Power moved between them like an invisible salinity gradient. It did not touch their lead-scaled flanks. But as it passed, the soft zinc bars—which had been pitted and worn to thumb-thickness—found their losses reversed. Atom by cold atom, dissolved zinc from the oceanic soup began to plate back onto the bars, drawn by a delicate bias the Power introduced to the local battery. The galvanic voltage did not leap; a leap would have burned the copper palates of the scavengers. It merely stabilized at 0.8 volts, pure and inexhaustible. 

The stilt-walker pressed its shovel-snout against the seam. For seventy years it had licked a fading meal, a meal thinning toward extinction. Now, the juice of the seam was thick, steady, tasting of fresh ozone and deep earth. 

When the walking beam took its stroke—and for the first time, took its smooth, returned rebound—the creature did not simply dart six paces to avoid the crushing brine. It moved through the water with something like ease, its slag-tipped needles ringing on the cobbles, not frantic, but rhythmic. 

And in the middle tier, where the coats hung from their scullery pegs:

The Power passed through the open transoms without disturbing the grease-film on the sills. 

It did not fill the coats with the ghosts of the men who had left them. That would have been a cruelty beyond reckoning—to wake memory in sleeves that were now muscle, to demand human grief from things that had become mollusks. The men were gone, and their coats belonged to the quiet. 

Instead, the Power shed tiny, star-shaped sparks into the stagnant air of the bedrooms. They floated in through the fanlights in dozens, cold and pale emerald, drifting with the micro-currents of the bellows. 

The horn-tipped claspers at the cuffs did not have to wait a week between feedings. As the sparks drifted near, drawn by the wool-wrapped flesh, the cuffs twitched with a quiet, rhythmic satisfaction: *click, click, click*, like knuckles cracking in a warm house after a day of labor. The central siphons drew in the salty grease and expelled it in steady, untroubled plumes, scenting the empty rooms with tallow, iron, and the cold dignity of survival.

Lastly, the Power paused in the alley where the wrench hung.

It hung halfway down the canyon, trapped in its lens of super-saline glass, waiting for the dilution that would allow it to fall the final six feet and break its silence against the cobbles. 

The Power did not allow it to fall. Nor did it lift it. 

It adjusted the density of the brine around the tool until the buoyancy was absolute—an eternal cradle. The steel of the wrench, which had begun to take on the faintest frost of pit-rust around its jaw-threads, was kissed with a microscopic layer of black magnetite, identical to the great columns. It would never fall; it would never dissolve. It would remain there as a monument: six feet above the street, perfectly horizontal, a marker of the human work that had drowned, and the non-human life that had accepted the drowning as a home.

The Power did not stay as an intrusive master. When the circuits were closed, the friction abolished, the tallow restored, and the echoes established, it withdrew into the basalt floor. 

It became the floor. It became the bedrock that held the trench.

Up above, on the surface of the world, five miles up, ships with coal-smoke trailing from their funnels passed over the gray sea, their crews entirely unaware of what lay beneath their keels. 

Down in the pocket, the water sat at three degrees below zero, dense as mercury. 

The flywheel completed its quarter-turn: *shuck*. 

The water jolted three inches. The stilt-walkers made their six ringing paces through the canyon. The sheet-blanket on the high catwalk folded another fraction of a millimeter around its rail. The cable sang down into the floor of the world, and seventy seconds later, the floor sang back. 

And in the darkened sculleries, deep within the wet wool of the hanging sleeves, the blind claspers opened, took their sparks, and were content.
