It would not bring light. 

To bring light into the cleft would be an act of barbarism. Ambient light would pierce the curdled halos clinging to the liverwort, bake the suet-density of the water into thin, boiling broth, and sear the flat, beveled faces of the turners, who have never known the insult of a photon. Light would be an eviction.

Nor would it widen the fracture. A clumsy benevolence—the sort that belongs to sun-warmed gardens and open meadows—would see the choking crush, the razor-hard thorns, the sideways drag of the slate, and take pity. It would wedge a great, warm crowbar into the seam and pry the slabs apart, spilling the pocket out into the wide, cold mud of the flooded quarry floor. 

And in doing so, it would murder everything. 

The hydraulic tension would collapse. The keystone would drop like a severed tooth. The recursive throat would snap shut, crushed by the sudden, flat physics of the exterior world, and the turners—spilled into water too thin to walk upon, water that does not push back against their enamel needles—would tumble aimlessly into the silt, kicking their legs in a vacuum, their herringbone work shattered, their truing forever broken.

Wisdom knows what mercy is at this depth. Wisdom understands that to the quarry-turners, space is not a field to wander in; it is an uneven, jagged insult that must be ironed flat, measured, and sealed. Their paradise is not an open sky. Their paradise is the *fossil*. It is the final, airtight stillness where nothing shifts, nothing grows, and the geometry of the rock is at last forgiven.

Therefore, the power would contract itself. 

It would divest itself of radiance, of heat, of breath. It would compress its vastness until it was thinner than the blade of a mason’s knife, sliding between the two slabs of slate without displacing a single molecule of the heavy, mint-scented fluid. It would enter the cleft not as a sovereign, but as a medium.

First, it would look outward, to the world above the fracture. 

It would see what the blind turners cannot: the thirty fathoms of drowned quarry water; the rusting derricks slumped in the upper shallows; the winter frost that, forty years from now, might bite deep enough into the limestone shelf above to drop an eighty-ton boulder directly onto this seam, pinching the fracture shut before the Litigant can reach its seat. 

Against this, the power would act as an unyielding, invisible mantle. It would take the shock-waves of distant blasting, the seismic groans of the quarry floor, the shifting gravel, and ground them out through its own quiet will. It would hold the two slabs of slate immutably rigid, keeping the gap precisely the width of an open book, sheltering the cleft from the clumsy accidents of the upper earth.

Then, it would turn inward, toward the little commonwealth of needles and lard.

It would observe the terrible math of the race. 

Because there *is* an error in the cleft. The turners do not know it, but the power, in its wisdom, would calculate it in a fraction of an instant: the liverwort is growing by four-tenths of a micron too fast per winter. Its glass-hard, white root-needles are multiplying along the portal’s margins at a rate that the turners’ three-pair bone limbs cannot match. In eighty winters, when the lead tip of the Litigant finally arrives at the three-inch arch, the opening will not be clear. It will be barred by three cross-laid fibers of silicified moss. 

When the tapered needle of lead enters, it will not seat flush into the herringbone floor. It will strike those three microscopic bars. The hydrostatic pressure behind it will not equalize; it will twist. The keystone, held only by the friction of the dense water, will be kicked out of square. The arch will collapse. The Litigant will fall sideways into the brittle lawn, its amber grease leaking into the silt, unseated, unpinned, an eternity of work ruined by three strands of plant-fiber.

The turners would not weep—they have no tear-ducts, no voices. They would simply begin again, clearing the rubble in the dark, sawing with their ruby snouts at roots that would outlive them forever, trapped in a purgatory of unfinished truing.

This is where the benevolence would touch the stone.

It would not burn the roots. It would not sweep the moss away. To do so would rob the turners of their sacred toil; it would invalidate every tap of their enamel knees, every drop of chalky saliva they have kneaded into the clay tiles. 

Instead, the power would reach into the cell-walls of the liverwort. 

It would make no sound. It would simply adjust the mineral uptake of the plants, ever so slightly. It would temper the silica in the white root-needles, rendering them not soft, but marginally less brittle—just enough that when a turner addresses a root with its beveled snout, the fiber will give way after twenty-four days of sawing instead of twenty-eight. 

A four-day gift. A microscopic dispensation of grace, hidden inside the hardness of the wood. 

The turners will never know they were helped. They will feel only the familiar, bitter grit of the plant snapping under their work; they will lay their tiny clay tiles in the cleared space; they will tap the floor three times with their bone-white legs, listening for the echo, and find that the line has held. The portal will stay open. The math will balance.

And then, the power would do the hardest thing wisdom must ever do:

It would step back into the stone, and it would wait.

It would sit in the heavy, suet-thick dark beside the pale, greasy coils of the measure-keepers, feeling them sag thirty winters to complete a single turn. It would smell the wintergreen and bruised mint exuding from the flanks of the Litigant as it creeps forward, micron by micron, over the span of two centuries. 

It would watch the tiny herringbone floor creep out toward the arch, tile by tooth-sized tile, perfectly level, true to within a thousandth of a millimeter.

And when the day finally comes—when the great lead needle reaches the portal, slides into the unmortared river slate, and seats with a tiny, hydraulic click into the clay floor—the power will not pull them into the light. 

It will be there, filling the microscopic gaps between the lead and the stone, acting as the final, frictionless seal. It will allow the sideways gravity to collapse into zero. It will allow the space to close its lips. 

And as the water turns to stone, and the thicket turns to jet, and the turners fold their six needle-limbs beneath their solder-scale plates to sleep inside the unyielding, unmarred, perfectly rectified geometry of their finished world, the benevolent power will stay with them, caught inside the fossil, holding the silence forever, so that they will never have to be crooked again.
