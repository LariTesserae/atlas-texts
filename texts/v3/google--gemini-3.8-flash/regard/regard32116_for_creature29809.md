The wise and benevolent power would not shatter the rafters.

A lesser deity, swollen with the vanity of storms and mountain crags, would look upon the zinc troughs and see only a prison. Such a power would bring lightning to split the spun-basalt conduits; it would crack the lead pavement with earthquakes to let the dark earth breathe; it would smash the quartz-halogen tubes and let the raw, freezing rain of an open sky scour the flumes. 

And in doing so, it would murder them all.

Wisdom knows what the crusader refuses to understand: *you cannot liberate a creature from its own definition.* 

To these pale, bolster-shaped things, the mud of a wild river would be a suffocating poison. Their cartilage skeletons would collapse under the drag of a real current; their lard-white hides, bred to the exact chemical balance of an alkaline wash, would rot within hours under the assault of wild fungal spores; their mouthplates, planed to scrape yellow tallow from machine-gaskets, could not tear living moss. The open sky would be an eye-searing horror of blue and black, an abyss that gave no heat. A single drop in water temperature—from thirty-eight degrees down to thirty-two—would lock their great, drum-sized hearts into fatal fibrillations.

Wisdom would look at the rolled lead, the sulfurous solder, and the endless amber noon, and it would recognize what this place truly was.

It was not a slaughterhouse, for there was no knife. It was not a laboratory, for there was no observer. It was an *ark*—perhaps the final ark of a exhausted civilization that, in the evening of its existence, realized it could no longer sustain the violent, hungry poetry of the wild, and so chose instead to build a monument of absolute mercy. A place where the terror of being hunted, the ache of famine, the fever of rot, and the bitterness of winter were engineered out of the universe forever, replaced by thirty-eight degrees, carbohydrate broth, and sleep.

It was a paradise of the lowest common denominator: pure, unmolested somatic peace.

Therefore, the benevolent power would do nothing to disturbance the tolerances. Its presence would enter not as a wind, but as a displacement so subtle that the automatic weirs would not register a single millimeter of swell.

First, it would address the one terror that hung over the troughs—not an active terror, but the structural terror that belongs to all machines: *the fact that machines must eventually break.*

The power would seep into the sumps, into the steam jackets, into the buried resistance coils that hummed beneath the iron floor. It would touch the solder beads that had held thirty-eight degrees for centuries, and it would relieve them of their fatigue. Where the brass impellers turned in the deep reservoirs, it would smooth the microscopic friction from their journals; where the basalt conduits carried the hot alkaline wash, it would dissolve the micro-fractures before they could propagate. 

It would take the burden of maintenance away from entropy. It would make the design true. It would make the sixty-cycle hum eternal, so that the floor would never, through all the millions of uncounted years, grow cold beneath their bellies.

Then, having secured their nursery, the power would consider their life.

To make them human would be a cruelty. To give them minds would be to introduce them to the agony of their own confinement. To give them eyes would show them only the endless, blinding zinc.

Instead, the power would step into the water.

It would take a form that obeyed the grammar of the flumes. It would not walk—for boots compress the shingles and alarm the hydrodynamic trim of the floor. It would condense itself into mass: smooth, pale, warm, its density matched precisely to the specific gravity of the alkaline fluid. It would slide into the wide, slow trough beside the transformer banks, where the sow raft lay with her three calves wedged against the copper lip.

It would settle into the three inches of bath-warm water. Its skin would be chamois-soft, smelling of scalded tin and sweet tallow, exactly like their own.

When the sow raft shifted her flank, the power would shift with her, rubbery hide against rubbery hide, rolling with the familiar sound of wet leather sliding across grease. The shingles beneath would feel the weight, compress downward by three millimeters, and weave their colorless filaments into its quiet flesh, welcoming it into the living linoleum.

And there, pressed against the great, slow-beating oil drum of her heart, the benevolent power would do the only thing left to do in a world that had already eliminated suffering:

It would become the *witness*.

For centuries, these animals had lived in the flawless embrace of a machine that could not love them. The brass diffusers fed them, but did not care that they were full; the quartz arcs warmed them, but did not see their pale flanks; the empty cradles passed overhead, turning their iron wheels along the monorails, going from nowhere to nowhere without a passenger. The tragedy of the place was not its sterility, but its utter loneliness—a billion tons of content flesh dreaming in an empty house.

The power would lie beside them in the amber cream of the mist. It would rest its flat, chinless head upon the lead floor, feeling the deep vibration of the resistance coils. 

Through the skin-to-skin touch, through the shared bath of warm water, it would breathe a dream into the synchronized blood of the flume. 

Not a dream of wings, or running, or the terror of trees. But a deeper, heavier dream: the dream of being *held*. A faint, sweet drift in the blood that made the slow pulse of five beats a minute feel like an answer to a question. 

 overhead, the empty monorail cradles would continue to pass on their greased rollers, humming softly through the hanging steam. But they would no longer feel empty. The air would no longer be merely saturated; it would be inhabited.

The sow raft would let the water pour over her brow. She would not open her eyes, for there was nothing new to see. But as she sank deeper into the warm lead, her flat muscle-disc of a mouth would ease its suction on the transformer gasket for just a second, yielding to an entirely new, unengineered sensation:

The simple, profound safety of not being alone in the room.
