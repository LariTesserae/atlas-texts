A foolish power would have smoothed the rock.

It would have sent a warm, dry wind through the split, driving the mist back into the clouds. It would have choked the roaring cataracts with boulders, laid down three feet of sweet, crumbly black loam across the ledges, and planted clover. It would have reached down with golden fingers to unpick the mutton-tallow from the ears of the children, murmuring promises of birdsong and the rustle of dry reed-grass in the lowlands.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

To warm this cleft would be to rot their larch-wood frames within a season, blooming the grey fungi into a fury that grease could not choke. To dry the air would split their sodden, broad-heeled feet into bleeding rifts, unseat their center of gravity, and starve the pale salamanders whose migrations keep the galleries from collapsing. To bring them down into the plains would be exile: their cask-flared ribs would ache under the thick, soupy pressure of the low valleys; their dead ear-canals would throb in the humid heat; their silent, physical tongue—built for the narrow ledges and the edge of the void—would look like madness to people whose mouths are full of wind and vowel.

A wise power does not break a vessel to inspect the water inside it. 

The watcher that came to the elliptical rift had no face, nor did it carry light. Light here was already too sharp; it needed no more mirrors. It came instead as a heaviness—a deep, slow consciousness that settled into the dark schist like an extra bar of iron in the earth’s lattice, sliding itself into the very stone that had not yet learned to crumble.

It understood what the people had built: not a tragedy, but an equilibrium. A terrible, razor-thin masterpiece of animal persistence. 

Therefore, it did not speak. No voice could pass through the singing quartz, and words are cheap things made of expelled air. Instead, it went to work upon the physics of their endurance.

First, it attended to the pegs.

Where the fire-hardened oak pins entered the hairline cracks of the unweathered stone, the moisture carried a microscopic traffic of minerals. The watcher leaned upon that migration with the patient gravity of centuries. It did not magically strengthen the wood; it simply nudged the silica and dissolved iron in the water to weep into the pores of the oak, petrifying the pins grain by grain. Under its quiet attention, the timber at the anchor-points stopped rotting. It turned into dark, wet flint-wood—supple enough to take the shudder of the wind, but dense enough that it would never shear again under the weight of the communal halls.

Second, it lowered its awareness into the center of the void, into the blue-black throat where no eye could reach.

The people believed that below the mist lay only emptiness—a bottomless percussion that pulverized whatever was thrown into it. They dropped their dead without a prayer because they thought the rift was an earless mouth.

The watcher made itself the floor.

Two thousand paces down, far beneath the lowest net where the spider-limbed boys snatched the falling thrushes, the watcher shaped the cold up-draft. It did not stop the fall; falling was the rite they had chosen. But where the dropped bodies tumbled through the blinding spray, turning like leaves in the draft, the turbulence ceased to shred them against the wet quartz. The air down in the dark grew dense, cushioned with moisture, thick as cold oil. 

When an old grease-gatherer or a child whose waist-rope had frayed rolled off the larch timbers into the mist, they were no longer broken against the teeth of the mountain. They were caught by the up-swelling breath of the throat, carried outward into the dead center of the chasm, and lowered—gently, through miles of singing water—into a deep, calm cistern of pure snowmelt that the watcher held cupped in the rift’s root. There, in the freezing, absolute dark, away from the sun’s scouring light and the rot of the air, the watcher laid them into beds of fine quartz silt. They did not bloat. They did not vanish into nothing. They rested, preserved like marble, an unbroken company resting in the foundations of the city they had lashed to the sky.

And finally, the watcher touched the foreheads.

It could not undo the deafness; the deafness was their armor, the calcified wall that kept the cathedral-roar from tearing their minds into twine. But it knew the loneliness of that wet, insectile life—how two people must wedge themselves against a rock-bolt, shoulder to shoulder, and drive bone against bone simply to say that a child had drawn breath.

The watcher took up residence in the rock-bolts.

It did not alter the abrasive hum of their speech. But whenever two inhabitants leaned together, pressing their frontal plates against the iron-slick quartz, the stone no longer returned merely the cold, dead vibration of falling water. 

Beneath the wet percussion, it added a counter-note.

It was faint—so subtle that an inhabitant could never have named it, could only have felt it as a sudden, inexplicable slackening in the muscles of the jaw. When forehead met forehead, the skull felt not just the tremor of the other’s voice, but a vast, resonant stillness underneath it. It was the feeling of being braced. It was the sensation that the mountain was not merely waiting to finish a fracture, but was, for a moment, holding its breath so that they might speak.

They did not build altars. They had no stone to spare, and they would not have known what to place upon one if they had. 

They simply went on shuffling. Their wide, white feet slid over the algal sheen of the larch; their hands, stiff with grease and pitch, drove the wedges and turned the creaking spoked-drums; their children swung through the safety nets like water-spiders, snatching dead birds from the spray.

Yet, on the highest galleries, when the morning light sliced through the crags like a clean blade, a grease-gatherer would sometimes pause. He would slide his flat foot along the pale larch, testing the wood. He would find it firm—mysteriously firm, though the mist had beaten against it for thirty winters.

He would look out across the two hundred paces of free, roaring air toward the opposite cliff, where the other half of his people moved like grey lice along the stone shelves. He would raise his hand, tap two fingers against the hard tendon of his wrist, and press his knuckles to his hip.

*The rope is slack,* the gesture said. *The timber holds.*

And down in the blue-black gradient of the mist, through three miles of solid, singing quartz, the silence held them back.
