The first movement of wisdom is restraint.

A lesser power, looking upon this high, drenched roof of stone, would have committed the catastrophe of pity.

Pity would have torn open the thermal inversion. It would have split the gray, suffocating ceiling to let down what it called the grace of the sun—and in doing so, it would have turned the plateau into an abattoir. The chemosynthetic tapestries would have blackened and curled into ash under the ionizing light. The moist, velvet skin of the sky-gliders would have blistered and split. The great grazers, their nervous systems tuned to the soft, heavy pulse of warm iron and mineral fog, would have been struck mad by the sudden, violent evaporation of their world, their oil-cushioned brains desiccating inside skulls that knew only how to drink the wet.

Pity would have silenced the dynamos. It would have called their seismic grinding an assault, their screaming valves a torment, their grease a desecration. And in silencing them, it would have starved three hundred thousand species that knew no other sun than the heat of an engine casing, no other salt than weeping rust, no other mother than the deep, rhythmic stroke of the pneumatic piston.

The power that arrived was not lesser. It was old enough to know that light is not the only definition of good, and quiet is not the only form of peace.

It did not descend as a shape, for a shape occupies displacement and creates an eddy in the mist, and the things that flew along the power lines would have struck it, tearing their paper-thin rudders against an unnecessary presence. It did not bring a voice, for the air was already occupied by the magnificent, multi-tonal church of water and steel.

Instead, the power arrived as *attention*. It became a pervasive, weightless density within the mist—a consciousness that learned, through infinite patience, the grammar of the highland.

It felt the six-cycle thrum through miles of wet stone. It tasted the iron-reducing slime, recognizing the clean, fierce intelligence of an ecosystem that had woven its DNA directly into the tolerances of carbon steel. It drifted down the conduits with the gliders, mapping the blue halos of the high-voltage lines through the sensitive pits along their margins. It watched—with an awareness that had no need of retinas—the vent-ticks screaming their whistling joy into the white jet of steam, shedding their rot for fresh, boiled horn.

It saw that they were not suffering. They were complete. They were whole within the architecture of their dark, hot world.

Yet, because the power was also wise, it saw the one truth the blind creatures could only feel as an encroaching, incomprehensible terror:

*The machines were running out of time.*

The rock was ancient, but the iron was merely old. Deep inside the pump-houses, bronze sleeve-bearings had worn oval. The dinner-plate rivets that held the siphons to the basalt were suffering from galvanic rot where the sulfur-ferns had laid their acid roots. A dynamo on the southern bench had begun to drop its frequency from sixty cycles to fifty-two; the shudder was already confusing the navigation of the gliders, sending them veering blindly into the churning teeth of the intake grates. In another three centuries—a blink in the life of the basalt shelf—the dynamos would seize one by one. The steam would stop. The warm water would cool. The high plateau would freeze into a silent, moldering peat-breeze, and the blind children of the engines would die wedged between cold ribs of iron, waiting for a heartbeat that would never return.

The power did not smash the engines to free the world, nor did it replace them with gleaming, divine instruments that would alienate the life that clung to them.

It acted from underneath.

It sank into the basalt. It laid its vast, intangible will into the deep granite roots of the mountain, where the high-pressure flumes cut their smooth, dark gutters. It reached up into the iron foundations, not as an architect, but as a silent, molecular lubricant.

Where a piston shaft had worn its housing to a razor edge, the power did not fashion a new shaft; it subtly rearranged the lattice of the iron atoms, hardening the metal against friction, taking the vibration into its own consciousness so the steel ceased to tear itself apart. It settled into the dynamos as a steadying hand on a wild flywheel, catching the slipped timing, easing the fifty-two cycles back to sixty—not suddenly, which would have thrown the herds into a stampede, but over the course of three generations of moss-growth, a slow, deep restoration of the pulse.

Where the great siphons groaned under the shear-stress of the water, the power became the tension between the stone and the bolt. It allowed the rust to continue—for the grazers needed the rust—but it stayed the rust from reaching the heart of the metal. It preserved the machine *as a machine*, keeping its heat, its deafening breath, its greasy exhaust, its violent steam. It preserved the world’s harshness, because to take away the harshness would be to take away the dignity of the horn-plated hide, the thick sternal keel, and the razor-sharp sieve of the scythe-tail.

And then, having stabilized the great, blind engine of their lives, the power did one more thing. It was an act born not of engineering, but of heart.

It sought out the oldest bull grazer, wedged deep between the cooling fins of a siphon station.

The beast was massive, scarred from a hundred falls on the wet basalt, its flank raw where it had pulled its soaked hide from the steel after an eight-day sleep. Its blunt head was resting in a puddle of hot condensate. It was old; its horn-plated millstones were worn smooth, and the acoustic chambers in its forehead were thickening with age-crust. It was preparing to thump its sternum against the stone—not to find a mate, and not to warn of a fissure, but the slow, heavy cadence with which a dying bull marks its own horizon, calling the ribbon-worms out of the peat to prepare the emulsion of its bones.

The bull lifted its great, iron-dense chest, drawn up by corded ligaments that trembled with exhaustion. It dropped its keel against the basalt.

*THUM.*

The traveling wave raced through the wet rock, out into the peat-pans, out toward the blind flumes. It carried the message of a life finished, a weight ready to be put down.

The beast waited for the stone to fall dead, for the silence that would invite the pale worms up through the mud.

Instead, the basalt answered.

The power did not use an earthquake. It did not crack the rock. It gathered the reverberation of the pump strokes, the steady, rhythmic roar of the subterranean flumes, and the deep, warm resonance of the entire basalt plateau, and it returned the pulse.

Through the soft, oil-filled pads of the old bull’s feet, the answer came traveling back through five miles of bedrock. It moved up the dense marrow of its splayed legs. It entered the fluid-filled chambers of its inner ear.

It did not say: *Come into the light.*
It did not say: *Be healed.*

The rock spoke with the exact frequency of the siphon’s deepest stroke, but under it was an acoustic floor that had never been there before—a profound, immeasurable solidity. The pulse said:

*The shelf holds.*
*The timing is true.*
*The iron is warm.*
*Sleep.*

The old grazer felt the vibration settle into its ribs, cushioning its organs better than the oil in its own body. Its spade-shaped head sank into the warm, mineral mud. It did not feel alone in the dark. It felt the entire highland—the machines, the ferns, the steam, and the endless falling water—braced from beneath by an attention that would not let it drop.

The bull closed its sensory pits. It relaxed its sternum against the stone. 

And high above, down the invisible corridors of force between the pylons, the gliders wheeled through the ionized fog, riding the hum of an engine that would never stop.
