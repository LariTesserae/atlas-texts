A lesser god, or a young one, would have torn the clouds open.

The instinct of crude pity is always to smash the ceiling: to flood the brown-black gloom with the hard, blue glare of the upper troposphere, to strike down the stagnant warmth with pure high-altitude wind, to show these huddled, barrel-chested creatures the terrifying expanse of the stars and demand they rejoice in their smallness beneath them.

A wise power does not do this, because wisdom understands the physics of mercy.

If the cloud ceiling were torn away, the thermal inversion would collapse within twenty minutes. The air at twenty thousand feet is a razor; the warm, oxygen-heavy soup trapped in the masonry saddles would rush upward into the void, replaced by a freezing down-draft that would freeze the wet fern fronds into brittle green glass before the hour was out. The blind softshell turtles, trapped in the cooling slurry of their sunken vaults, would slow into stone and never wake. The civets’ cataract eyes would burn and rupture under ultraviolet fury. And the people—whose barrel ribs were forged to pump stagnant, low-pressure soup, whose skin had known only the lukewarm spit of the drift and the grease of bats—would choke on the thin, sharp frost, shivering themselves to death on flagstones they could no longer cling to with bare, prehensile toes.

To cure a world of its dark by destroying the conditions of its life is not benevolence. It is merely the vanity of the light.

The power that arrived sat not in the sky—for there was no sky to speak of—but entered the brickwork. It settled into the lime joints, the wet gravel cores of the retaining walls, the deep, sweating footings where the fired clay bit into the ancient metamorphic shale of the ridge.

It spent a long age simply listening. 

It felt the rhythm of the place: the *clack-tonk* of the macaque dropping his weight upon the rocking capstone; the low, iron hum of millions of frogs in the silt-wells; the dry, tearing-canvas rush of bat-wings through the upper viaducts; the high, dental clicks of the people crouched upon their six-inch ledges, trading news of fruit and grubs through the dense acoustic roar. It tasted the saltpetre the serows licked from the weeping mortar. It smelled the hot, sweet ammonia of the guano dunes, and the cool, rich loam of the silt-traps where the dead lay wrapped in beaten fig-cloth, feeding the black-green ferns.

It found no evil here. There was cruelty, of course—the krait striking the blind skink in the wet moss, the macaque snapping the neck of a bat, the slow starvation of an old hunter whose toes could no longer find purchase on the slime-slick ashlar—but these were the cruelties of hunger and gravity, clean and free of malice. 

The people did not feel damned. They did not grieve for the sun, because one cannot mourn an abstraction for which there is no word. They loved the warmth of the brick against their bellies when the drift blew harder than usual. They loved the sweet, wine-sour tang of persimmons fermented in slate hollows. They loved the dry, powdery security of the bat-lofts, where generations had curled together in the soft, ammoniac dunes, their deep chests rising and falling in unison against the subterranean rumble of the mountain.

Yet the architecture was finite. 

The power, calculating the slow mathematics of the terrace walls, saw the fracture lines that the people could not see. The roots of the strangler figs were patient, but they were wedges. Three centuries hence, the great southern rampart—the three-story wall of leaning brick holding back seventy thousand tons of compost and terrace-water—would shear at its base. When it fell, the drainage would fail. The cisterns would burst their lips; the stagnant water would scour out the lower galleries; the bat colonies, choked by mud and sudden cold, would abandon the viaducts; and the people would starve on isolated towers, clicking into an empty, falling gloom.

So the benevolent power set to work, not as a conqueror, but as mortar.

It did not alter the clouds. It left the brown-black gloom intact, the lukewarm drift falling its twenty minutes every cycle, the air thick, wet, and smelling of old lime and crushed leaves. 

Instead, it moved through the deep foundations. 

Where the lime had leeched away and left the horizontal joints hollow, the power caused the microscopic calcite crystals to regrow—not into artificial stone, but into living, self-healing seams that gave slightly with the shifting of the mountain and tightened under load. It taught the fig roots to turn. It did not kill them—the figs were food and cloth—but where a taproot drove straight toward the structural heart of a bearing arch, the root met a gentle bitterness in the brick, a mineral distaste that made it bend, circle around the vault, and bind the exterior masonry in a basketwork of living wood rather than split it apart.

It touched the silt-traps. In the deep sediment, where the dead were laid under halves of brick, it quickened the work of the blind crabs and the thread-worms, turning the bodies of the ancestors into rich, binding humus more rapidly, so that the calcium of their bones entered the fern roots without putrefaction, rising up into the fiddleheads as a quiet, enduring strength that held the terrace edges from crumbling.

And then, very softly, it touched the lofts.

It did not give the people fire. Fire in those unventilated crawlspaces would have been mass suicide, a suffocating smoke that would have driven the bats from the rafters and blackened their lungs. 

Instead, it gave them dreams that did not frighten them.

For generations, their sleep had been black and immediate, broken only by the vibration of the brick under their flanks. Now, as they lay curled in the dry guano dust, their barrel chests pillowed on one another’s shoulders, the power let them feel the shape of the world they lived on.

They did not dream of sky—sky was nonsense, a void that had no meaning to a creature of planes and ledges. But they dreamed of the *underside*. 

They felt, in their sleep, the immense mountain beneath the brick: the cold, ancient granite that bore the weight of the terraces without groaning. They felt the vastness of the masonry grid not as a cage, but as an embrace—a colossal hand of fired earth holding them above an emptiness they would never have to see.

And it touched their tongues.

It did not restore vowels; vowels would still be shattered by the acoustics of the arches. But into their sharp clicks, their dental chirps, and their palatal slaps, it introduced new intervals—subtle variations in pitch and resonance that allowed them to say things they had never had the shapes for.

One evening, three terraces up, a young mother sat upon a narrow lintel sixty feet above the black roar of the fern-chasm. She held a child whose skin was pale as lime-wash, its broad toes gripping her forearm with reflexive ease. 

She did not look up, for there was nothing above but the sweating slate of the viaduct ceiling, dark and low. But as she clicked to her kin along the wall—sharp, dry sounds that cut clean through the booming iron drone of the frogs—she found a new sequence of taps against the roof of her mouth. 

She did not know where it came from. It was a rhythm that resonated through the teeth, vibrating in the small bones behind her ears: *click-t-klick... tsk... tonk*.

It did not mean *fruit*. It did not mean *the bat is fat*. It did not mean *the brick is loose*.

Across the artificial ravine, fifty paces away in the brown dark where she could see only the pale smudge of another body pressed flat to a buttress, an old man heard it. The sound carried along the damp face of the wall, clear and precise. 

He paused, his fingers buried in the mossy mortar, his blind, cataract-milky eyes fixed on the empty gloom. 

He raised his chin, clicked his tongue twice behind his front teeth, and sent the rhythm back to her: *t-klick... tsk... tonk*.

It meant: *The wall holds.*

It meant: *We are here, and the stone is warm.*

Below them, deep in the dark gut of the foundations, the mountain rested in its mantle of brick, wet and breathing, safe from the sun for another ten thousand years.
