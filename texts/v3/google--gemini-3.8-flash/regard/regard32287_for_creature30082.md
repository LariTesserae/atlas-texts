A foolish power would have torn the purple cloud open to show them the stars. 

It would have brought a sun, and the sun would have scalded the milky eyes that had known only the twilight bacterial sheen; it would have evaporated the inch of grease-melt, stranding the plate-skimmers by their billions to rot into a black, asphyxiating crust; it would have melted the dense, cold-cured tallow in the bellies of the Silt-Footed, loosening their organs, drying their spade-feet until the horn-pads split and bled, leaving them to crawl through the reek of their own unmade paradise, blind and parched, begging for the dusk.

A power that is wise does not correct a world for being terrible. It knows that this place is not a mistake; it is an extremity, and its children are shaped to its teeth.

So the power did not come down with fire or song. It brought no light, for light casts shadows, and in a flat world without relief, a shadow is only a cold place where you cannot see the runnel.

Instead, it came down as weight.

It took the form of the stone, because only the stone has authority here. Far to the south-southwest—at the lip that no hunter had ever seen, where the continental pan of gneiss finally breaks off in a jagged shelf three hundred miles long, spilling its endless, thumb-deep skin of water into an empty, soundless void—the power laid itself down.

It did not build a wall to stop the water. It became the shelf.

It stretched itself across the terminus, an outcrop of dark, living iron-gneiss, cooler than the air, smoother than the pre-historic sea-bottom. And then, because it knew how language is spoken here, it did not speak with a voice. 

It laid its forehead against the slab.

***

The first thing that changed was not the sound, but the floor.

Two thousand miles north, where a hunter named Kett was crouched in the lee of a weir, four-pointing his knuckles in the cold sheet-flow, the bedrock did not shake. It didn’t crack. But through the calloused leather of his spade-pads, through the horn spatulas of his trailing fingers, an unfamiliar shudder ran up his forearms.

It was not the rhythmic, concussive thud of the bull-worms signaling the rise of the melt. The bull-worms struck like hammers—blunt, blind, territorial. 

This was a single, sustained harmonic. It was the vibration of a skull pressed to the stone at the edge of the world, humming into the iron-grain with the force of an unmoving mountain.

Kett froze, his grey lips dripping with skimmer-oil. He felt it rise through his shins, through his squat thighs, settling in the root of his spine like an ache he had forgotten to feel.

It was an answer.

In the language of the Drift-Holders, to press your brow to another’s is to say: *I am meat, you are meat, the stone is cold, the skimmers are running.* It is the only grammar they have for: *You are not alone in the current.*

For thirty generations, they had pressed their skulls only to each other, two by two, in the wet dark of the rib-huts, sharing the small, desperate gossip of salt-spouts and toothache. Beyond their huts, the slab had been mute—only the scream of horn on gneiss and the thudding of the worms. 

Now, the bedrock itself was a forehead.

Kett did not take the bitumen plugs from his ears. He did not straighten his spine. But he dropped from his four-point crouch onto his knees, lowered his head into the inch of moving water, and laid his brow directly against the slick, olive-felted floor.

He expected the felt to bite his skin, to send its microscopic suckers into his pores. But where the stone hummed, the felt was quiescent; it parted around his temples like cold grease, letting his bone meet the iron-gneiss.

Through his eyebrows ran a thought that was not his own, yet made of the only things he understood:

*The water moves.*
*The slab holds.*
*I am at the edge. I have my brow against yours.*

Kett stayed there for an hour, the cold wash sluicing over the curve of his suet-thick shoulders, parting around his nape in twin rills of white froth. He felt the hunger of three hundred other Drift-Holders across sixty leagues, all linked through the pan, their foreheads touching the same floor, their sinus cavities vibrating in a vast, subterranean lattice. For the first time since the sea retreated, they were not solitary points of friction held against the drift; they were a web of bone laid across the continental plate, singing through the teeth of the world.

***

The second thing the power did was quiet the scream.

It did not kill the plate-skimmers; they were the tallow and the lamps, the meat and the oil. Nor did it lift them from the rock. But into the shallow runnels, where the water slid south-southwest, the power released a tiny, imperceptible silt. 

It was not dirt—dirt would rot and cloud the sheen. It was a microscopic, spherical glass, born of cooled brine and pressure, drifting down the sheet-flow in an endless, invisible grain. 

Where the spatulate oars and serrated belly-plates of the skimmers brushed against the iron-gneiss, the silt rolled between the horn and the rock like oil between gears.

The metallic, nerve-shredding screech that had scoured the flats for ten thousand years did not vanish, but its edge dissolved. The scream softened into a hiss—a vast, oceanic whisper, like dry grain sliding down a chute of polished slate. 

In a low rib-hut five miles from the weir, an old woman named Mara sat by her trough-lamp. The flame burned blue-green, smelling of old phosphorus. She reached up with thick, lard-crusted fingers, plucked the black plugs of lake-tar and tallow from her ears, and set them on a stone.

She did not clutch her skull in agony. 

She heard the water. She had never heard the water before—only the vibration of it in her shins. She heard it: a soft, rhythmic *slump-slump-slump* as the sheet broke against the weir slabs. She heard the soft clatter of the carapaces, sounding no longer like dropped iron cutlery, but like the clicking of dry pebbles on an ancient beach. And far below, three fathoms deep in the boreholes, the bull-worms were no longer pounding out blind alarms; their tails struck the rock with a measured, rolling rhythm that sounded like a heart at rest.

Mara did not weep—her tear ducts were scarred over with cataract-crust and wind-scab. But she tilted her hairless head, opened her mouth to taste the damp air, and let the quietness settle behind her ears like cold fat hardening in a pan.

***

The last thing the power did was for the dying.

A month later, when Mara’s wrists grew too stiff to lift the chert-headed adze, and the yellow lard under her skin began to thin and turn grey with the marrow-cold, she did not wait for the felt to claim her heels.

She took off her stomach-hide boots. She left her sleeve of thong-hound skin on the floor of the rib-hut. 

She went out into the purple dusk, her bare, spade-shaped soles stepping out onto the greased stone. The water washed over her arches, an inch of sliding melt, numbing her instantly. She turned her face toward the drift—south-southwest—and let her feet skate.

She did not fall.

In the old days, the dying fell when their knees went numb, and the skimmers crowded them while they were still breathing, eating the lard from their hips while the thong-hounds whipped through their ribs.

Now, the runnels seemed to hold her. The current did not push her down; it bore her forward. When her legs finally lost their purchase and she leaned into the sheet-flow, she did not strike the rock with a bone-cracking jar. The water thickened beneath her chest like a bed of tallow, carrying her along the thumb-deep rills with the easy speed of a dropped ribbon.

She drifted for three days.

The skimmers did not climb upon her. They sculled alongside her like an escort of lead coins, their jointed oars clicking a soft cadence. The thong-hounds, blind and pale, slid through the runnels beside her ribs, their star-shaped nostrils tasting her wake, but their cartilaginous jaws remained closed; they were resting against her flanks, using her mass to break the current.

She was cold, but it was not the cold of rot. It was the cold of iron seasoning in brine.

On the fourth twilight, the purple sky seemed to lift—just an inch, just enough for the horizon to open. The hiss of the water grew wide and hollow. Ahead of her, the flat shelf of the world came to an end.

The sheet-water did not pour into a black abyss of terror. 

At the edge of the continental pan, the power was waiting, still shaped like the stone, still pressing its great brow against the shelf.

Where the water slid over the lip, it did not plummet into nowhere. It broke over a vast, descending terrace of stone ribs—thousands of curved arches, wider than filter-feeders, stepped down into the quiet mist. On these terraces, the water ran slow and dead-still, gathered in broad, deep cisterns where the phosphorus bacteria burned not milk-white, but a deep, comforting amber.

The current brought Mara to the lip.

A hand—or something that knew how to cradle an elbow with the heavy, blunt gentleness of a fat-gloved knuckle—reached out of the mist and lifted her from the runnel.

It did not tell her she was redeemed. It did not turn her into an angel or a girl. It did not give her back the waist she had lost ten thousand years ago, nor did it melt the yellow suet that protected her ancient heart. It was too wise for such insults.

It laid her down upon the dry, warm gneiss of the first terrace, out of the spray. 

With a thumb of smooth, unblemished iron-slate, it wiped the cataract-crust from her lashless lids. 

And then, leaning close in the dim, amber twilight of the edge, it brought its forehead down against hers. 

Mara closed her eyes. Through the bridge of her nose, through the hollows of her temples, through the thick, sheltering bone of her brow, the power hummed:

*You held the drift.*
*The weir stood.*
*Rest your meat.*

And far behind her, across two thousand miles of cold, grey stone, through the inch-deep grease that never stopped sliding, three hundred hunters felt the bone-talk rattle softly in their molars, dropped their chisels into the current, and rested their brows against the wet slab, knowing that the edge was held.
