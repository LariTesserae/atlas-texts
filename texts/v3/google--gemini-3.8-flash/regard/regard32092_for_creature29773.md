A foolish power would have brought the sun.

It would have parted the miles of brine with a stroke of radiant heat, boiled the pale, lard-thick reeds into jelly, and split the five-ton basalt blocks along their ancient grain with the violence of sudden warmth. It would have lifted the anvil-shaped creepers from their grooves, mistaking their hydrostatic adhesion for a trap, and watched in horror as their pressurized lattices exploded into soft gray mush in the thin upper air. It would have spoken words of hope into ears that are only horn-lined acoustic pits, deafening the standing ones with the screaming friction of an atmosphere they never asked to breathe.

A foolish power would have called this place a tomb, and in trying to resurrect it, made it one.

The wise power did not do this. 

It approached not from above, descending like an intruder, but seeped in laterally, taking the form of the only thing this world understood: density, cold, and unhurried time. 

It began by shedding its light. Light here was an obscenity, a raw and flaying scrape across beings that had cultivated the dark for ten thousand centuries. The power cooled itself until its core reached precisely one point eight degrees above the freezing point of heavy brine. It contracted its presence until its density matched the crushing tonnage of the basalt slabs, pressing itself down into the floor until it was indistinguishable from the planetary mass beneath the terraces.

Then, for three hundred years, it did nothing at all.

It simply waited. It learned the rhythm of the great drift. It felt the decade-long leans of the hair-moss forest, southward and northward, cataloging the microscopic shear of the creepers as they cleared forty-year paths along the arrow-straight causeways. It matched its own mind to the slow settling of the cyclopean retaining dikes, until the creak of a thousand square miles of pavement was not an interruption, but a familiar heartbeat.

Only when it had ceased to be an event did it begin to act.

First, it went beneath the floor.

It found the living tallow, the gray, crystalline marrow weeping through the dry-stacked joints of the masonry. It saw how the marrow bore the continental burden—how it crushed its own simple, boundary-less tissue to keep the five-ton blocks from shearing against one another, silently bleeding into the dark voids so the world would not shatter.

The power did not change the tallow. It did not grant it eyes, or a voice, or an escape from the seams. Instead, the power slid between the lowest foundations, down where the water could not penetrate and the rock was under pure, geological strain. There, it took a portion of the continental weight upon itself. 

It was an adjustment of less than an eighth of an inch, stretched across five hundred leagues. But to the marrow, it was an unimaginable mercy. The crushing, destructive bite of the basalt edges softened. Where the grease had been ground to a brittle, crystalline powder, it relaxed back into a slow, lubricated ease. The power became an under-ballast, a silent, infinite spine pressed beneath the fitted stone, ensuring that when the floor settled, the marrow would never again be pinched to death in the blind gaps of the world.

Next, it visited the causeways.

Ahead of a keystone-shaped creeper—one that was forty years into its crossing of a vast sunken plaza—the power lightly salted the hair-moss. Not with organic food that would spoil and foul the bitter water, but with the purest, coldest precipitates of iron and dissolved silicates. 

As the creeper advanced, its flat belly moving by molecular shear, it found the stone fractionally easier to strip. The bitter capillary grates along its flanks drew in water that carried no grit to tear their fibrous gristle, but only a dense, perfect mineral nourishment that settled effortlessly into its oily inner wax. Its slag-crusted back grew smooth and solid; its armor became a perfect shield against the centuries. The creeper did not know it was loved. It only knew that the basalt was kind, and that the path before it was true.

Finally, the power rose to the megalithic platforms.

It stood upon the blank, forgotten foundation at the terminus of a causeway forty yards wide. There, facing the long, empty reach of the continent, stood one of the upright dwellers. 

The dweller was nearly turned to stone. Its limbs were calcified pillars; hair-moss had bearded its shins, and its featureless shield of a face was turned toward the south, awaiting the turn of the planetary drift. It had stood alone upon that platform for so long that the shockwaves of its own footsteps had faded from the rock beneath it. It was listening to the void, tracking the snap of distant reeds, waiting for the weight of the water to finally defeat the last fibrous cord of muscle in its chest and turn it forever into a column.

The power did not speak. It did not touch the dweller’s slag-plated shoulder. 

It moved down the causeway, two leagues away, and placed its own mass upon the fitted slabs. It formed a limb of dense, unyielding basalt-weight, lifted it through the thick water over the course of seven months, and brought it down.

*Tock.*

The sound was not loud, but it was deep. It traveled through the dry-stacked masonry, through the cushioned layer of tallow, through the roots of the pale, jointed reeds. It carried the precise acoustic signature of an equal: a body that honored the pressure, that displaced the black brine with the same heavy deliberation, that knew the cost of every lifted inch.

Three hours later, the vibration reached the platform.

It entered the horn-lined pits of the dweller’s skull through the soles of its spatulate feet. 

For the first time in an epoch, the standing one was not merely tracking the decay of the continent. It was not listening to a dike buckling or a stone settling in the mud. It was listening to an arrival. 

Inside the calcified vault of its chest, deep within the lattice of bone-stone and oily wax, something that had been freezing solid began to circulate again. The mineral sludge around its ankles shivered.

Slowly—over the course of a year—the dweller began to shift its weight. 

Its right foot tore free from the suction of the terrace with the sound of old leather ripping under deep water. The limb compressed, uncorked, and swung forward into the dark, displacing the thick brine inch by agonizing inch. 

The wise power did not hurry it. It did not close the distance. It stood upon the causeway, two leagues away, perfectly still, holding its place against the pressure of the sea. 

It lifted its own foot, and waited for the dweller’s reply to strike the stone. 

And far above them, through the unmeasured miles of cold and lightless water, the silent forest leaned toward the south, swaying over the rounded edges of the megaliths, as two shapes in the absolute dark began a conversation that would outlive the stars.
