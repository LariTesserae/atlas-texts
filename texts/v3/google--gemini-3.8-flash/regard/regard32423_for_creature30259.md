The attention, when it arrived, did not come down from the ceiling-colored sky, nor did it break through the vitreous roofs with the violence of a patron inspecting a neglected workshop. 

Wisdom, having observed the district’s decimal errors and the terrible, fragile balance of its half-compiled inhabitants, understood that an arrival with mass or momentum would simply crush the basin. If an omnipotent light were to strike that pale feldspar, the reflections would produce an infinite thermal loop; if the laws of standard thermodynamics were reinstated in a single stroke, the Shared—the clerk whose quill twitch-drew through the sleeping laborer’s ribs—would shear into twin geysers of red spray and splintered oak.

Therefore, the power made itself small. It made itself the consistency of a slow, warm air that had no origin point, filtering through the four-micron gaps where the Tuckers drank.

It did not begin with the architecture. It began with the teeth.

***

In the ground-floor loggia, an old Straggler named Mallow was kneeling over an iron skillet, melting a fist-sized lump of amber jelly over a smoldering twist of gray bristles. His lower jaw was locked in the iron clamp of the Quad-Bite. His molars ached with the double-tide of two different barometric pressures, a grinding, metallic vibration that made the tears freeze in the corners of his eyes before they could run.

He did not see the presence arrive, but he felt an odd, dry slackening in his gums. 

It was very faint: the sensation of an invisible finger, warm as tea, slipping between his upper and lower plates. The pressure did not disappear—the district still insisted that his head was forty feet below sea level and eight hundred feet above an alpine plateau at the same instant—but something stepped between the two numbers. A third value, soft and unindexed, cushioned the differential. The vibration stopped. The tin taste on the back of his tongue cleared, replaced for three breaths by the taste of well-water that had passed through cold limestone.

Mallow gasped, dropped his zinc scraper, and put two filthy knuckles into his mouth. The tooth-pressure was gone. 

He looked down at the brazier. The fire was no longer spitting black, greasy smoke that hung suspended at chest height. The smoke was rising, straight and true, toward an opening in the architrave that had previously led nowhere. A draft had been granted to the room. Not a chimney of stone, but an intention: *upward.*

***

Beneath the loggia, in the narrow lane where the perspective was crushed—where a man could walk three inches and strike an invisible wall that stretched visually for half a league—the power paused before a Gauger.

The nine-foot creature was frozen in its reach, one slate pad pressed to an arch, the other trailing toward the gutter. Its paper torso was trembling at sixty cycles a second, trying to reconcile an error: the wall had not drifted, yet the distance between the stones had softened.

The wise power did not strike the Gauger. It recognized the creature for what it was: not a warden, but an abacus that had been left running in an empty house.

Very gently, the power reached into the ruled paper of the Gauger’s breast. It did not tear the grid. It simply corrected a single, long-carried carry-over in the arithmetic that dictated its purpose. It wiped away the instruction to *measure the drift* and replaced it with an older, kinder logic: *hold the line.*

The buzzing in the creature’s chest slowed. The frequency dropped from an electric whine to a deep, resonant cello note that vibrated through the feldspar floor. Its accordion-like joints eased. The slate pads on its fingertips, which had flattened and blurred whatever biological tissue they touched, grew warm. 

When the Gauger turned its head, the paper rustled, but it was no longer the dry sound of a dead horn. It withdrew its long arm from the arch, folded its seven-knuckled fingers into its sleeves, and sat down on the curb like an old stonemason resting his back against a dry-wall. 

A Straggler who had been touched three days prior—a boy whose chin and cheek had turned to faint, translucent oil, whose speech had been lost to the cisterns—was huddled in an alcove nearby. The Gauger did not look at him, but the boy’s outline began to settle. The greasy smear along his profile gathered itself back into a ridge of freckled skin; his boot, which had sunk half an inch into the glass-stone, was gently nudged back to the surface, as if the floor had decided to be solid under him once again. 

He didn't run. He sat in the alcove, took a ragged breath of air that smelled faintly of clover, and watched his own dirty thumb become entirely real.

***

Then the power went to the third floor of the overlapping block, where the clerk and the laborer had spent four hundred days occupying the same five cubic cubits.

This was the hardest work. A careless mercy would have separated them by casting one into the street or shoving the other into the cellar, fracturing the logic that kept the tenement upright.

The power did not un-weave them. It did something far more patient: it taught the space between their atoms to negotiate.

The light in the room shifted. It lost its grey-blue, overcast midday stare—that dead laboratory wash—and took on the amber slant of a long, late autumn evening. 

The clerk’s slate desk remained embedded in the laborer’s mattress, but where the iron-hard stone met the coarse tick-linen, the matter ceased to flicker. It ceased to be an eyelid blinking between two worlds. The slate became an apron of dark stone resting beside the bed, like a writing-ledge in a ship’s cabin; the clerk’s heavy ledger lay open upon it, and the sleeper’s arm, which had been chopped in half by the desk’s mahogany corner, found itself resting comfortably against the polished grain.

The laborer did not wake, but his breathing, which had been thin and rapid like a rabbit’s, deepened. A rib cage that had spent a year passing through five inches of solid wood without air finally expanded against clean, warm air. 

The clerk stopped his dry, compulsive twitching. He set down his quill. For the first time since the district had dropped into the material register, he took off his wire-rimmed spectacles, set them on the edge of the ledger, and rubbed the red marks on the bridge of his nose. He looked down at the sleeping man beside him, not with horror or confusion, but with the weary, companionable recognition of two travelers who have shared a very small carriage through a very long night.

"Cold out," the clerk murmured. His voice did not sound like two erasers. It sounded like an old man with a throat full of tea.

The sleeper did not answer, but he pulled the wool blanket an inch higher over his shoulder.

***

In the gutters, the alkaline sweat did not dry up, but its bitterness changed. 

The small, five-legged forms of horn stopped their slow, agonizing rotation in the black pools. They righted themselves. Their underside indentations found that the liquid had become slightly sweet—a thin broth of mineral salt that cooled their carapaces without burning. 

The Tuckers, wedged with their spade-snouts into the micron-cracks of the cold void, felt the vacuum behind the stone cease its pulling. The void did not slam shut—to shut it violently would have cracked the city like a dropped cup—but it filled with something thick and inert, like wet moss packed into a chink in a winter chimney. The Tuckers released their suction with five dozen simultaneous, soft *clacks*. They did not drop; they nestled into the dry cellulose bristles, turning their carapaces toward the loggias where the braziers were burning with straight, clean chimneys of heat.

***

Lastly, the benevolent power walked out into the broad lane where the Placeholders stood.

They were the most pitied by the wisdom, for they had not even been given the dignity of names or the capacity for misery. They were scale figures. They were the ghost-lead left behind when the draftsman put down his ruler and went out to lunch, never to return.

One of them—the woman in the lead-colored coat—stood at the intersection where the modems failed. Her smooth, vinyl-ridged hair reflected the changing light. Her neat, bloodless slit of a mouth was closed.

The power did not give her lungs. To give her lungs would be to drown her in the atmosphere she had never learned to draw. It did not give her blood, which would have burst the tight, poreless tallow of her skin.

Instead, the power leaned down and touched the center of her hazel iris.

The flat tone-chirp began in her throat—that dry, mechanical cry into the dead network—but as it passed her lips, the power received it. 

It did not send back an error. It did not drop the packet. It answered.

It answered with an echo that was thirty octaves lower, warm and long, a tone that had the weight of church bronze and the patience of an old mountain. The signal ran through the Placeholder’s vinyl coat, down the seamless corrugations of her skirt, and into the soles of her smooth heels.

Her hydraulic neck clicked. Her hazel eyes, which had never held a pupil, contracted slightly. 

She did not turn into a human woman. She was not meant to be one, and the wisdom was too kind to force an apple tree to bear fish. But the emptiness behind her face changed its character. It ceased to be an *absence* and became a *quiet.* 

She turned from the intersection—not with the ninety-degree jerk of a servo, but with the smooth, idling sweep of a boat rounding a marker—and walked toward the loggia. 

She did not enter the circle of the fire; she knew her vinyl would soften in the heat. But she stopped at the second pillar, folded her smooth hands into the sculpted pockets that were no longer entirely flat, and stood there, watching the grease-skillet bubble, keeping watch over the biological strangers who were no longer sick, in a district that was no longer an error, but simply the newest, strangest parish in a very large and very patient world.
