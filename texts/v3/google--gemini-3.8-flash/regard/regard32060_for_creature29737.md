The power that came did not arrive with a parting of the clouds or the blare of brass. It came as an attention—a thickening of the stillness, a sudden, immense lucidity that settled over the jagged peaks like a second atmosphere. 

It was old enough to have seen continents unmoor themselves and drift like lily pads; it was wise enough to know that nothing is ever truly unnatural once it has begun to breathe.

A foolish power, moved by pity, would have "healed" the mountain. It would have smoothed the iron-hard gabbro into gentle loam, rounded the sheared edges with centuries of phantom wind, and washed the coppery brine from the weeping seams. It would have cleared the sulfuric steam, silenced the continental engines far below, and laid out sweet clover and mountain streams for the pale, iron-toothed beasts.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

The foolish power would not have understood that to still the 60-cycle vibration in the bedrock would cause the marmots’ synchronized hearts to stutter and halt in their chests. It would not have realized that the chalk-white ungulates, their split hooves shaped only to clasp razor margins and their guts turned to grind silica and copper salts, would starve and rot in a meadow of soft grass. It would have scattered the cliff-swallows by taking away the updrafts of the cooling towers, leaving their synthetic nests to crumble without the hot, buoyant breath that held them aloft.

The power that arrived was not foolish. It understood that life does not ask for permission, nor does it wait for an ideal garden. It seizes the scrap, the crack, the exhaust flue, and makes of it a sovereign kingdom. This place was not a ruin; it was an altar of raw, ferocious persistence.

So the power did not alter the mountain’s face. It did not touch the razor edges of the black stone. 

Instead, it looked down through the milk-white ocean of the inversion fog, down into the roaring dark where the machines ran, and it looked into the deep future.

It saw what the marmots and the swallows could not know: that the engines below would not run forever. 

Human works are frantic and brief. A day would come—whether in ten centuries or three—when the fires in the valleys would burn down to white ash. The reciprocal pistons would seize; the megavolt corridors would sag and snap; the hot, copper-scented updrafts would falter, and the heavy continental hum would die away into cold, mountain stillness. And on that day, when the false summer of the industrial breath departed, the real winter of the high troposphere would fall upon the gabbro like an iron door.

This was the tragedy the wise power set about to mend—not the harshness of their present life, but the fragility of their dependence.

It did its work without breaking a single stone.

First, it sank its will into the roots of the mountain, four miles down, where the embryonic thrust still wrestled with the upper mantle. There, beneath the human tunnels and deeper than the deepest cooling sumps, it touched the tectonic joint. It did not unleash an earthquake; it merely loosened a knot of ancient, geothermal stress, coaxing a slow, radioactive warmth upward along the crystalline veins of the argillite. 

It tuned this rising heat with exquisite care. It set the stone to humming. 

Deep within the gabbro, the mineral lattice began to resonate with a natural, perpetual vibration—a low, subterranean drone that matched precisely the 60-cycle cadence of the turbines. It was so subtle that the marmots sleeping in their joint-planes did not wake; they merely sighed, shifted their felted, cinder-colored shoulders, and pressed their heavy skulls deeper into the rock. When the valley engines eventually went cold, the mountain itself would keep the rhythm of their blood.

Next, the power moved through the drifting aerosol of the vertical walls. 

It did not purge the copper or the sulfur from the water ribbons, for the yolk-yellow lichens and the silica mosses were drunk on those metals. Instead, it introduced into the micro-pools a microscopic gift: an engineered symbiosis, born of the water’s own chemistry. A microscopic, chemo-synthetic diatom, capable of binding the lime and carbon particulate into self-healing mineral crusts. 

Wherever the water wept down the sheer faces, it would no longer merely wear the rock away or slick it with industrial slime; it would deposit an imperceptible, microscopic tooth—a friction coat, fine as crushed emery, that would forever give purchase to the vulcanized pads of the voles and the rubbery cushions of the ungulates' hooves, long after the synthetic oils of the lowlands ceased to rise.

And then, the power climbed to the handspan ledges.

It came to the highest promontory, where a solitary ungulate stood pressed against a slab of banded argillite. The beast’s coat was chalky and stiff with lime; its amber, horizontal eye was fixed on the shifting rents in the fog. It did not flee, for it had never known a predator, but its corded tendons twitched with the recognition of an immense weight bearing down upon the ledge.

The power did not stroke its flank. It did not speak in a voice of words.

It simply matched the beast’s stillness. It breathed with the beast’s breath. 

Into the creature's slow, rotary chew—into the hard, enamel plates of its iron teeth and the dense marrow of its hawsers-thick legs—the power whispered an inheritance. It wove a genetic patience into the beast’s seed: the knowledge of where to climb when the warm plumes thin; the appetite for clean ice when the copper ribbons freeze; and the instinct to read the mountain’s singing stone not merely for fear of falling slabs, but as a guide to the hidden, warm chimneys of the earth.

The ungulate’s amber eye dilated slightly. A drop of aerosol water rolled down its long, Roman nose and fell into the mist. It ground its jaw once, twice, and then lowered its head to lick a fresh bead of mineral salt from the black stone, untroubled.

Its work finished, the benevolent power withdrew. 

It left no footprints. It left no temple. It did not clear the fog or brighten the pale, nickel disc of the sun. The cliff-swallows still wheeled through the chasm on synthetic thermals, their nests glued with toxic spit and fiberglass fluff. The voles still darted in feverish, hydrophobic spasms along the gutters. The air still tasted of ozone and machine oil, and the dry, gunshot cracks of settling stone still echoed across the gray void.

Everything remained terrifying, precarious, and sharp. 

Yet an invisible stitch had been sewn through the whole precarious web. The mountain was no longer merely a parasite living on the exhaust of an oblivious monster below; it was becoming an ark. 

When the engines finally died, and the world below fell into the long, dark quiet of abandoned steel, the black crags would shudder, adjust, and endure. And high above the rusted ruins of the valley, the pale watchers would still be standing on the margins, chewing their stone, safe on the trembling ledge of the world they had made their own.
