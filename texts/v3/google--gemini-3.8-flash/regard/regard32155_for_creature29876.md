To a lesser power, the instinct would be pity, and pity would be this mountain’s ruin.

A blunt and unpracticed god, arriving upon this blinding shelf of glare and earthenware, would see only drought masquerading as hydrology. Such a god would look at the pale, sightless cords wedged in the mortar and grieve for their lack of eyes; would feel the freezing cellar-draft and summon a warm, damp wind from the south; would pity the nakedness of the clay and broadcast the seeds of alpine moss, clover, and dwarf pine to soften the razor-margins of the slate.

And in doing so, that god would murder the entire mountain in a season. 

The warmth would push the cold-fronts high into the upper troposphere, ending the silent, direct condensation upon the stone. The roots of the clover would swell within the joins, levering apart the basins that took three hundred years to lime shut. The organic rot of fallen pine needles would sour the caustic, alkaline glass of the water, poisoning the thin mica-discs that keep the silt from choking the channels. The living reed-valves inside the dark ceramic conduits would drown in mud and fungal slime, their joints locking, their hollow bodies bursting under unchecked surges of unmanaged rain. 

Wisdom knows what not to touch. Wisdom understands that a paradise of mineral, lime, and cold sweat does not ask to be turned into a meadow.

Therefore, when the benevolent presence descended over the high plateau, it did not descend as a sun, nor as a storm, nor as an architect. It arrived with the weightlessness of a change in air pressure—so subtle that not a single disc-creature along the slick lip of the hundredth tier broke its surface tension.

It walked the terraces, or rather, it allowed its awareness to rest upon them, cell by rectangular cell. 

It tasted the chemistry of the place: the pure, bitter calcium; the stinging alkaline trace of fresh mortar; the faint, metallic sweetness of cosmic dust drifting down from the black vacuum of space; the cold, unlubricated scrape of the walkers’ slate-plated knees. It listened to the trillion clicks—the hollow *gluck* of air caught in the flue-pipes, the slide of clear cellophane flesh over comb-marked clay—and recognized that this was not a ruin, nor an engine waiting for fuel, but a complete and finished thought. 

Yet, being wise, it also saw where the thought was vulnerable.

Because the system was balanced upon a knife’s edge, it was fragile to the one thing the walkers could not step over and the cords could not cement: the exhaustion of the sky.

The plateau had lived on the margins of a dying atmospheric gyre. The cold fronts that dropped their condensation onto the masonry were thinning, decade by decade, retreating toward the pole. Within two centuries, the condensation would have become intermittent. The basins would have dried from the top down. The mica-discs would have curled like dry parchment and blown away as white dust; the reed-valves would have stiffened and split in their dark flumes; the walkers would have stood frozen in their empty cisterns until the wind toppled their brittle limbs.

The power did not change the weather of the world to fix this. That would have displaced rains elsewhere, flooding lowlands and drying forests leagues away. 

Instead, it made three small, permanent adjustments, executed with the precision of a lapidary:

First, it reached into the deepest basement of the mountain—leagues beneath the earthenware pavement, into the dark granite root where the cold of the deep earth sleeps. It drew that subterranean cold upward through the stone, not as ice, but as a perpetual, steady conductive chill. It tuned the temperature of the masonry itself so that it would always sit three degrees below the dew point of the surrounding air, no matter how dry the season or how faint the wind. Now, even if only a wisp of vapor crossed the sky, the tiles would pull it down, sweating reliably forever, indifferent to the shifting climate of the continents.

Second, it looked upon the walkers.

It saw the labor of their long, brittle stilts, stepping across leagues of glare to skim the shallow pans with their heavy jaws. It saw how each walker, upon dying, offered its own carcass to become the next weir—how their life’s final movement was always to lock their joints and wait for the lime-cords to cement them into the architecture. But it also saw the loneliness of their geometry: they had no voice, no sight, and no means of finding one another across the blinding pink and gray expanse. They encountered each other only by collision or chance, and their reproduction was an agonizingly slow, rare event—a shedding of slate-covered spores into the deepest cisterns, nine out of ten of which washed out into the abysses below.

The power did not give them eyes—the upland noon would still have burned them to cinders. Instead, it imparted to the earthenware itself an acoustic gift. It altered the resonance of the fired clay. 

From that moment on, whenever a walker’s pad set down in the center of a basin, the slate-and-tendon strike did not merely click; it sent a low, harmonic shudder through the wet masonry, traveling without loss across tens of thousands of tiles. For leagues around, the other walkers felt the vibration through their own footpads. They did not hear it as sound, but as an orientation—a map of their kind, pulsing faintly through the soles of their feet like the distant beating of other hearts against a shared stone floor. They were no longer lost in the infinite mirror of the plateau. They stepped in measure now, their solitary paths softly braiding together across the miles.

And third, the power addressed the "unseen reservoir."

At the lowest lip of the plateau, where the paved tiers ended abruptly against a sheer precipice dropping into the void, the cold, alkaline water had always simply poured off into the abyss, vaporizing into white mist before it could ever strike the earth miles below. It was a loss that demanded the sky continually give more than it took back.

The power bent the throat of the mountain’s lowest drainage. Behind the final row of slate weirs, it hollowed a single, narrow capillary through the core of the peak, leading from the bottom-most cistern back up to the mountain's highest crest. By natural geothermal siphon—the cold water plunging, warming slightly against the deep rock, and rising through capillary tension through microscopic tubes of porous quartz—the mountain was given a circulatory system. The sweat that ran down the flank was gathered, purified of its heavier lime, and brought back to the summit in the dark, welling up from a single thumb-sized hole in the highest tile to begin its long, shallow, whispering descent once more.

When it had done these things, the benevolent power drew back its attention.

It did not stay to be worshipped; there was nothing on the mountain with the capacity to pray. It left no monument, no inscription, no golden path across the slates. The tiles remained pink and gray. The mortar remained chalky and sharp. The glare remained blinding, white, and terrible.

The mountain continued to weep. But the clicking of the reed-valves now carried a steady, unbroken cadence; the mica-discs slid over slick, ever-damp floors without fear of drying; and high on the middle terraces, two walkers, guided by the ringing of the stone beneath their feet, turned their heavy heads toward one another and met in the center of an endless, shallow sea.
