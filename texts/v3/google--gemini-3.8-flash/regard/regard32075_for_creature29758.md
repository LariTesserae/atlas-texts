A lesser intelligence, armed with pity, would have broken the basin.

It would have sent clean mountain water rushing down the dead ditches to drown the sulfur pools, dissolving the bacterial mats and starving eighty million shore crabs in a week. It would have dropped seeds of willow and wild rice into the flats, where the caustic lime would have rotted their shoots, and it would have gathered the tallow-skinned men into white rooms to scrub their tire-rubber boots, cutting their lungs on air that lacked the heavy grease of the open-hearth vaults.

Pity is clumsy. It mistakes unfamiliar endurance for an error to be corrected.

Wisdom, looking down through the low milk-bell of the clouds, saw something else entirely: an ark. A place where life had refused the clean dignity of extinction, choosing instead to crawl through the eye of a needle made of slag and copper salt, stripping away every non-essential grace until only the bare machinery of hunger, breath, and continuation remained.

It saw that the rust had become a shell; that the guano had become mortar; that the men had become part of the drainage, as blameless and quiet as the gray gobies sliding through the ditch-silt on their elbows.

Therefore, the power did not alter the sky. It did not cool the wet, sulfurous heat, nor did it cause clover to spring from the cracks in the bitumen. To change the grammar of the basin would be to massacre the things that had learned to speak it.

Instead, its work took place in the margins—in the tiny, unbearable fractions where adaptation failed and became mere torment.

First, it attended to the bones of the place. 

The guano that petrified upon the high catenaries had been eating the iron rivets, working slowly toward a season of catastrophic failure when the high roosts would buckle and plunge ten thousand herons into the ditch-sludge. The power did not scrape the dung away. Deep inside the micro-fractures of the steel, at the interface where acid met iron, it arrested the galvanic rot. It catalyzed a silent exchange of ions: the rusted iron fused with the phosphorus of the bird-lime, forming an inert, black vivianite core that was harder than the original rolled plate. The frames ceased to weaken. They became mineralized, half-metal and half-fossil, capable of holding the weight of the pelicans for another ten thousand seasons without a single truss giving way.

Next, it went down into the pipes.

In the cavernous gloom of the central motor hall, where the man sat in the elevated green shack with his inner-tube gaiters drying on his shins, there was a three-inch line that had weeping fog-condensate for thirty years. For the past six months, a seam upstream had shifted, and the drip had acquired a faint, sweet tang of lead acetate that made the man’s gums bleed and his dreams violent with iron-colored thunder.

The power did not lay a new pipe. It simply nudged a pocket of heavy silicate sediment two hundred yards up the line, rolling it like a blind snail until it lodged precisely in the fracture. The toxic seepage choked off. The water that fell into the man’s cut-down tin can ran thin, cold, and clear again—condensed from the high clouds, carrying just enough lime to keep his teeth from loosening, but clean of the lead that had begun to stiffen his fingers.

Then it went to the man himself.

It did not appear as a light, for light in that valley belonged only to the dangerous flashes of shorting cables or the oily flare of bituminous tar. It came as a pressure against his temple, no heavier than the settling of a swift on a dry truss.

The man had an ulcer on his right ankle, where the sulfur-water had crept past the rubber of his boot during a spring high tide and eaten through the dermis to the gray periosteum of the shin. It smelled of sulfur and curdled grease, and for three months he had walked with his hip canted, knowing that when a man could no longer climb the rungs to the foreman’s shack, his companion would not wait long to slide him into the outflow canal.

The power did not perform a dazzling healing. A sudden, pink-skinned patch of baby flesh would have been an obscenity against his tallow-colored shins, and the brine would have taken it again within an hour. 

Instead, it introduced a microscopic thread of an ancient, salt-tolerant lichen into the raw crater of the sore. The organism knit into the edge of his flesh, feeding on the exudate, sealing the wound behind a dry, tough, slate-gray scab that was impervious to copper salts. The ache withdrew from his marrow. The stiffness left his groin. He touched the gray patch with a thumb that smelled of dried eel-slime, pushed his nail into it, and felt only the dull, safe resistance of leather.

He did not look up to the sky. He had not looked at the sky in twenty years; there was nothing up there but the gray wool that kept the steam down. He simply stood up, his tire soles squeaking on the iron floorboards, walked to the doorway, and looked down.

Thirty feet below, the tide was turning. 

The culverts were gulping, breathing in the black, hot marsh-creek. Millions of shore crabs parted around the concrete knuckles of the crane tracks, their millions of pale legs whispering like hemp rope dragging through coal dust. The eels knotted in the pump housings were sticking their snouts into the shallows, opening their wide, amber mouths to take the warm rain as it fell from the high gantries.

The power remained, but it did not speak. In a world made entirely of patience, speech is an intrusion. It simply rested its vast, cool attention upon the basin, acting as a ballast—a counterweight to the entropy that threatens all small, stubborn things.

It ensured that the midges would continue to rise at twilight, looking like steam from the dead cooling ponds.
It ensured that the raccoons, stepping with dry claws along the three-foot headers, would find the bat-roosts full enough to sustain them, but not so full that the disease would take the colonies.
And it ensured that when the man’s time did come—not in fever and rot, but in the natural, dry winter of his blood—his companion would still have the strength to lift him, and the eels would still have their hunger, and the crabs would do their clean, scuttling work in the quiet water until the bones were chalk-white, smooth, and indistinguishable from the limestone that had held the valley before the mills were ever raised.
