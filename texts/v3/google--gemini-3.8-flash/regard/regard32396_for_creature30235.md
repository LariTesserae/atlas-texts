A lesser power would have pitied them and, in pitying them, committed an atrocity.

It would have looked down through the bruised, butter-colored twilight, seen a world ground down to an agonizing smear of mud and shrieking stone, and reached in to still the water. 

And in that sudden, merciful hush, the disaster would be instantaneous. 

Without the planetary weight of the slurry bearing down, the delicate, sand-filled balance-sacs of the translucent brood would burst outward from internal pressure, their soft vascular trees rupturing into the tallow. Without the violent kinetic shearing of the water, the black hair-pelt would cease its chemical conversions; deprived of friction, its roots would rot in the basalt within hours. The boundary layer would vanish, swept into dead equilibrium. And the wedges—whose leaden, horn-plated bodies are shaped entirely as an exquisite, five-billion-year refusal of lift—would find themselves stranded in a terrible, buoyant silence, unable to breathe, unable to walk, suffocating on an absence of resistance.

To offer them a sunlit meadow would be to offer them an execution chamber. To grant them peace would be to peel them from the universe.

A wise power does not look upon five billion years of adaptation and call it a mistake. It knows that love, at this scale, is not rescue. Love is comprehension.

So the power would not descend as light. Light here is useless, dispersed in inches by the yellow flour of the soup. It would not descend as a voice, for a voice is just another doomed ripple in an ocean that already shears steel into silt.

It would descend as *mass*.

It would condense itself. It would strip away its own vastness, contracting its will until it was small, dense, and heavier than a dying star. It would drop through the mustard clouds, through the vertical geysers of wet clay, through the brittle, snapping panes of glass-hot tension, unaffected by the drag, until it slipped beneath the violence—down, down, into the paper-thin sheet of absolute calm.

It would enter the boundary layer.

There, on the slick, vibrating floor of the world, where the velocity drops to zero, it would take a shape that honors the house: a bevel. A wedge of slate and gristle, chamfered at the margins, with a leaden skirt pressed flush to the soapstone. 

It would not come to teach. It would not bring news of a sky, or a beyond, or an end to the work. The wedges do not need a future; they have an occupation. They are holding down the world.

Instead, the power would do the only holy thing there is to do on a world that has been planed flat: it would take up its share of the load.

It would settle onto the rim of a cistern, finding the faint, worn spiral cut into the stone. Its underside—broad, pale, and gripping—would seal against the basalt with a quiet, hydraulic sigh. 

And then, it would shiver.

It would drop its mantle against the stone and strike the floor. Not with a command, and not with an interruption, but in the exact, ancient register of the census. 

The vibration would travel through the warm, thin crust of the continent, outrunning the scream of the falling soup overhead, spreading outward along the basalt plate for a hundred miles in every direction.

*Here,* the power would say, pressing its belly into the cured silt. *Firm.*

A mile away, an armored scab of horn and quartz, navigating the lip of a cistern by the width of a single cell every decade, would feel the shudder pass beneath its sole. It would not wonder who had spoken. It possesses no apparatus for wonder. It would simply feel the bedrock ring true.

The wedge would clamp its edge tighter against the current, letting the endless, blinding fury of the world slide over its polished back, and it would shiver its reply into the dark:

*Still held.*

And the power would stay there. For an age, for three million years, for however long the continent required before the stubs met and thickened into a new, unshakeable skin. It would crawl its millimeter a century, smoothing the gray tallow, sheltering the jointless young in their five-inch squares of quiet, and pressing its own thumb—broad, heavy, and infinitely patient—deep into the green mud, adding one more apricot-sized mark to the long mathematics of survival.
