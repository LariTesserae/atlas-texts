A foolish power would have brought light. 

A clumsy benevolence would have descended with the sun in its teeth, shattering the vaulted masonry, scouring away the black water-ivy, boiling the bitter soup into steam, and demanding that the lungless sleepers take the air and praise the day. That is the charity of conquerors: to cure the fish of the river by hauling it into the meadow.

The Power that paused above this sunken stone basin had neither hands nor pride. It was old enough to know that light is often an assault, and wise enough to understand that paradise is merely the condition of being completely answered by one’s world.

It did not break the surface. It did not create a ripple on the flat black skins of the gas domes, nor did it frighten the blind river eel whose ribs fanned the rust. Instead, it became *viscous*. It translated its vast, unhurried attention into the exact specific gravity of the broth, sinking downward through the drowned municipal tiers not as an intruder, but as an added density—a gentle, pervasive saturation of care.

It tasted the water. It tasted the iron-rot, the ancient mutton-tallow, the paraffin, the seep of sulfur, and the pale, cold blood of things that had never conceived of a horizon. And having tasted it, it loved it. It saw the magnificent, desperate precision of the joinery: how the chiton’s foot matched the rivet head; how the paunchy crustacean hung from the water-ivy like a heavy fruit; how the blubbery, diving descendants of the rats tapped out their dark communal geometry with chisel teeth against the iron beams.

It saw, too, the flaw that all things built by hands carry into eternity: the district was slowly bleeding to death. 

It was dying not of violence, but of exhaustion. The galvanic currents in the foundations were thinning, century by century, toward zero. The geological jacket beneath the heat-exchangers was cooling by fractions of a degree every fifty years. One day, the 60-cycle hum would falter. The carmine plumes of the sleepers would twitch in the silence, wait, and slowly stiffen. The chitons would starve along the dead iron plates. The bitter broth would drop from the temperature of resting blood to the killing chill of stone, and the warm, dark cathedral would freeze into a silent fossil.

So the benevolent Power did what true wisdom always does in the house of another: it made itself useful and invisible.

It did not touch the sleepers. It did not wake the rat-things from their oily condensation, nor did it heal the cataracts over eyes that had earned their blindness through thousand-year vows of dark. 

Instead, it sank down into the marrow of the precinct—deep into the bedrock beneath the flooded foundations, below the crushed dynamos and the lowest engine trenches. 

There, it coiled.

It pressed its own infinite, slow vitality against the dying geological jacket. Gently, without causing a single fissure in the stone or sending a single scalding bubble into the broth, it rekindled the hearth. It set the warmth to pulse at the exact, unvarying tempo of the resting blood: a five-hour diastole, steady as an iron mountain, guaranteed for ten thousand years.

Next, it found the sheared cables, the rotting zinc-and-copper plates, the salt-eaten dynamos that leaked their last sweet juices into the silt. The Power did not rebuild them; to rebuild them would change the current, alter the pitch, frighten the gills of the salamanders who measured their lives by sixty cycles. Instead, it became the leak. It fed an imperceptible, steady potential into the lead trunks—one and a half volts, perfectly impure, tasting faintly of brass and brine. 

Along the fourth-tier boiler plates, the chitons did not look up, for they had no eyes. But their pale, circular files encountered a fresh bloom of cathode scum, richer than before, tasting faintly of deep earth and enduring iron. Their grey meat softened against the plates; they rasped, satisfied, their horn-rimmed shells sealed tight against the rivets.

In the long horizontal steam mains, four feet across, the Sleeper hung by a single finger from the valve stem. 

Its external gills—wide, carmine feathers drifting in the green algae—caught a change in the water. It was not a noise. It was not a shadow. 

The sixty-cycle hum had found its floor again. The low, sub-audible vibration, which had been faltering at the frayed edges of the sleeper’s awareness for three hundred years, settled into an absolute, immovable resonance. The bronze casing beside it warmed by a tenth of a millimeter of a degree. 

A ripple passed down the lavender-tinted pulse of the creature's dorsal vessel. Its withered tail shifted six inches into the mud, settling into the hollow it had worn over ages. Its pink, fused eyelids relaxed. 

The Power did not ask to be remembered. It did not leave a monument. It simply remained in the deep strata as a quiet ballast, holding the weight of the water and the dark, ensuring that the warm soup would keep its breath, that the tallow would never freeze, and that the world would remain small, heavy, bitter, and safe.

From above, the city was invisible: just a drowned floor of earth beneath forgotten valleys. 

Down in the black, a gas bubble liberated itself from a silted manifold: *thrup*. 

High in the brick domes, a chisel tooth struck iron: *tink-tink-tink*. 

And through the salt, through the rust, through the long, furred runs of pipe, the world hummed on, heard and held.
