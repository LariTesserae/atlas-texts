A clumsy god would have split the street above.

A clumsy god, looking down through sixty feet of iron grating and seeing people breathing broth in the dark, would have ripped the paving stones away, overturned the wagons, scattered the dray-horses, and flooded the Sump with the raw, blue violence of noon. 

And in doing so, such a god would have murdered them all.

The sudden drop in humidity would have cracked their tallow-cured skin from throat to groin within a day. The dry, thin air would have withered their broad, heavy-lobed lungs, which were grown to draw life from steam and silt. The sunlight would have charred the black-bronze foliage of the great aroids, dropping tons of rotting vegetable pulp into the courts; the sudden drying of the vines would have released the hydrostatic pressure against the retaining walls, and three hundred thousand tons of limestone ashlar would have collapsed inward, crushing the barrel-vaulted tenements into a common grave. The people would have been blinded, blinded instantly and permanently, their wide, unpigmented pupils seared by the white fire of an open sky they had never asked to see.

A wise power does not mistake adaptation for imprisonment. A benevolent power does not insult the dignity of endurance by calling it mere suffering.

Therefore, when the attention turned toward the Lower Sump—falling through the grit, through the telegraph cables, through the sweating hemp like a breath that took up no space—it did not arrive as light. It arrived as an attention so total that the humidity itself seemed to register the change, softening by half a degree of barometric tension, like a held lung slowly giving way to sleep.

The power moved first into the stone.

Not into the vitrified brick that human hands had mortared, but twelve feet deeper, into the cold, ancient body of the limestone bedrock where the tenements ended. There, in the dark seams where the black well-slurry gathered before it seeped into the sumps, the power began a quiet work of sorting. 

It did not alter the water so that it tasted of mountain snow—such water would have tasted dead and thin to mouths accustomed to iron and crushed watercress. Instead, it gently drew out the invisible needles: the caustic lead salts leached from old joint-packing, the heavy, insoluble limes that settled in the knuckles of the old, the sharp mineral silts that slowly ground the kidneys to meal. It passed the seepage through an ancient, spontaneous bed of dense zeolite and soft, fine-grained chalk, so that when the water gathered in the lead gutters at the bottom of the wall, it remained dark, heavy, and full of iron, but it no longer carried the seed of the knotty, crippling gout. 

The old woman sorting roots in the dark would not find her fingers straightened overnight. A miracle of that sort would have frightened her; she would have dropped her wicker basket, suspected a rot of the bone, and retreated into her tunnel in dread. Instead, she would only notice, over the coming winter, that the throbbing in her second knuckle did not wake her when the engines banked their fires. She would find that her grip upon the tough, tuberous rind was slightly surer, that her skin did not split at the nail-bed when the cold seep gathered in December.

Next, the power moved into the iron mains.

It did not cool the steam. Without the steam, the people would have frozen in the drafty limestone vaults, and the vines that held the earth back would have died of chill. But inside the high-pressure mains, where the steam screamed through corroded elbows at six atmospheres of force, there were places where the cast iron had thinned to the thickness of a thumbnail—places where, within three months, a wall would blow out in a sudden, shrieking white wall of scalding death, filling the chasm with the "White Lung."

The power laid its will against the crystalline structure of the metal. It did not make the pipes new; shiny brass and unpitted iron would have been foreign bodies, rejected by the living envelope of creepers. It simply healed the internal fatigue. Atom by atom, through the hot lattice of the cast iron, it closed the microscopic fissures, annealing the seams from within, weaving carbon and ferrite together until the aging mains possessed the quiet, enduring tensile strength of living bone. The pipes would continue to weep their warm sweat—the planarians would not starve, the corky bark of the creepers would not dry—but they would not burst. The sudden, butcherous terror that hung perpetually behind the people’s stoic foreheads was quietly lifted away, dissolved like a knot untied beneath dark water.

Then, the power looked at the creepers.

It understood the contract between the people and the green things: the vines held the mountain back, and the people kept the vines from choking the valves. It was a good contract. It gave the men with the scraping-chisels work that had measure and rhythm; it gave their days an axis. The power did not diminish the vines. It merely whispered into their thick, milky sap a slight, chemical modesty. It bade their terminal buds turn aside, by a fraction of an inch, from the soft lead packing of the gate valves. It caused their aerial roots to seek the porous limestone rather than the vitrified brick, strengthening their grip upon the bedrock while sparing the mortar of the stacked homes. The men would still go out with their square tins of rendered fat; they would still pare the corky collars away with their walnut-stained fingers, but the work would not be a desperate race against the crushing of their lintels. It would be what it was meant to be: an unhurried gardening of the dark.

And finally, the power touched the people themselves.

It did not give them wings. It did not give them a desire for the sky, for to give a creature a hunger that cannot be fed within the boundary of its home is not benevolence, but cruelty. 

It touched the deep, barrel-vaults of their chests. It settled into the lining of their bronchi, laying down a thin, resilient sheen—much like the tallow that protected their skin—that could turn the bite of the sulfurous coal-smoke without choking the blood of air. It laid a quiet resilience into the marrow of the children who ran the catwalks at four years old, so that their heavy-soled feet would be slightly more certain against the liver-colored paint, and their prehensile toes would find the iron rivets not with fear, but with the calm confidence of a fern rooting into an ashlar cleft.

It did not relight the maritime lamp where the carbon filament had broken with a *tink*. 

To have rekindled it would have been an intrusion, an uninvited stare. The old woman did not need the light. She had lived seventy years without relying on her retinas; to flood her workspace with an artificial glare would have been to treat her long-earned intimacy with the texture of the world as a defect to be remedied. 

Instead, the power simply rested in the dark beside her. 

It took on the temperature of fifty-eight degrees—the true, steady temperature of the cut limestone. It made itself small, dense, and quiet, matching the low-frequency hum of the subterranean engines behind the walls. It entered the lead door-frames, making the metal slightly softer, slightly more receptive to the scratching awls, so that when a father carved the name of a new child or the date of a birth into the flashing, the iron point would glide smoothly, without catching on a slag-pocket, leaving a mark as clean and permanent as a runic line cut into butter.

The old woman continued to sort the roots. Her knobby, walnut-tinted fingers moved from the basket to the shelf, from the shelf to the lap, finding the taproots by their bluntness and the creepers by their bitter scent. 

For a moment, as a particularly heavy drop fell from the central telegraph bracket sixty feet above—*thud* into the belly of an aroid leaf—she paused.

She did not look up. Her tallowy, amber face did not tilt toward the ceiling. But she felt, through the soles of her bare feet flat against the wet rock, that the vibration of the world had lost a minute, jagged tooth. The floor felt more solid. The dark felt slightly less hungry. It felt, in some unnameable way, as though the heavy stone behind her back was no longer merely containing her, but holding her up with both hands.

She took a long, steady breath of the warm, broth-thick air. It did not catch in her throat. Her lungs accepted it smoothly, like warm oil poured into an empty lantern.

She grunted—a low, smooth sound that did not leave her teeth—and reached into the basket for the next root.
