The power arrived as an absence of weight upon the upper needle, where the frost-shattered phonolite usually gave back only the cold stare of the stars.

It had no name that could be pronounced without opening the throat—and here, opening the throat was an act of ruin—so it remained nameless, resting its regard upon the lip of the rift like a great, cool hand laid over the mouth of an urn.

It was immense, and ancient enough to have seen the first lichen crawl out of the sea to die upon bare basalt. It had seen empires that smelled of cedarwood and crushed mint, where men built domes of gold that hummed in the noon heat like bronze bells. It had seen worlds where life was a riot, a fever of green marrow spilling over its own edges until the earth choked on its own rotting sweet.

And so, when its attention first drifted down through the slate-blue penumbra of the basin, its instinct—the ancient, instinctive reflex of celestial mercy—was to warm it.

It considered letting an amber shaft of true daylight pierce the lee of the needle-peak for two hours at noon. It considered planting a seed of deep fire beneath the floor-cracks that would turn the cold, iron-smelling draft into a dry, hearth-side breath, so the children might slip out of their sleeping-sacks and run naked across the flagstones without the down-rolls at their throats. It considered dissolving the milk-fog before it could tumble over the rim, banishing that six-hour smother that forced the people to their knees with their hands over their mouths.

It drew its will together to do this. A single thought would have softened the phonolite. A single breath would have coaxed the sun three degrees to the south.

Then it stopped.

Because it was wise, it did not merely look; it descended. It made itself small—no wider than the width of an adze-stroke, no heavier than the shadow of a lungwort frond—and it listened.

It heard the *tick-tick-tick* of the white marmots on the roofs: five thousand claws working in rhythmic, blind solidarity against the shale. It felt the hundred-and-four-degree furnaces of the blue-smoke dormice, sewn into the ticking of a hunter’s bed-sack, purring against his lumbar vertebrae with a fierce, quiet devotion that no tame beast in the sunlit valleys had ever achieved. It watched a young mother tap three times with a parsley-green copper knuckle upon the jamb of her dry-stack tower, and it felt the stone—clean, dense, unrotted—carry that dry click through eighty yards of bedrock to the ear of her husband, who set down his horn scraper and closed his eyes in relief.

The power saw that if it brought the sun, the white marmots would run fat-sick and blind, their triple-jointed limbs thrashing on the hot slate until their living thatch sloughed into the gutters.

It saw that if it warmed the air, the cold milk-fog would vanish, and with it the strange, unearthly water that fed the black ferns out of nothing. The ferns would brown to powder; the midges would starve; the four-eyed swifts would beat their downy breasts against bare stone and plummet into the lanes.

It saw that if it gave these people voices to sing with, their own roofs would rain down upon them in a blizzard of panicked feathers.

This was not a ruin. It was a balance so acute, so desperately and brilliantly wrought, that an extra grain of sand would crush the arches. These people did not need salvation; they had already saved themselves, inch by knuckle, lard-pot by copper pin, trading the wide sky and the loud world for an eternity of quiet, oil-rubbed grace.

To pity them would be a vulgarity. To alter them would be murder.

Yet the benevolent power did not leave. A watcher does not love only that which is easy to hold.

It looked deeper, down through the phonolite steps, past the towers, past the cellar voids where the iron draft rose, down into the three-mile throat of the mountain.

There, in the black deep, it found the flaw.

The rift was young. The mountain, which had broken open three centuries ago, was not done settling. A great wedge of blind stone—forty million tons of basalt hanging in the dark below the thermal vent—was creeping along a shear-joint with the glacial, inexorable patience of tectonic weight. In forty years, perhaps fifty, it would slip.

When it slipped, it would not crush the basin; it would seal the throat.

The rising air would die. The smelling breath of cold iron and well-water would thin to nothing. The low forties would become the low twenties, then zero, then the blue, killing frost of eight thousand feet. The grease in the horn troughs would freeze to tallow-rock; the dormice would stiffen along the sleeping ribs of the children; the living thatch of marmots would freeze solid into an icy pavement, their claws locked forever in the slate. The people would tap on the phonolite, and the phonolite would give back only the dead, flat clink of frozen glass.

This was the quiet doom that hung over the small, orderly grid. They could not smell it. They could not hear it. Even the four-eyed swifts, with their upward-turning gaze, saw only the mist.

Here, the power found its duty.

It did not split the rock with a thunderclap. It did not send an angel with a horn of flame. That would have torn the larch bridges from their rawhide lashings and deafened the swifts for ten generations.

Instead, the power lowered itself into the dark below the basin. It spread itself thin, becoming a sheath of pure, cool intention, no thicker than an oil-skin, slipped into the hairline fracture between the slipping basalt and the mountain’s root.

It took the forty-million-ton shear upon its own back.

It braced its great, silent limbs against the phonolite core. With an effort that would have scorched a lesser spirit to cinder, it arrested the slip. It did not push the mountain back—that would have shaken the basin like a sieve—it merely wedged itself into the joint, turning its own ancient substance into an unyielding cushion of cold, smooth patience.

It settled there. It made itself the bedrock.

As it did, a single shudder—a tremor so minute that a cup of goat-tallow would not have rippled—passed up through the floor-cracks of the town.

Up on the roofs, ten thousand white marmots paused their *tick-tick-tick* for three heartbeats, their noses lifting toward the dark needle.

In the lanes, an old herdsman, scraping sheep-fat from his knees with a rib-bone, halted his hand and pressed his palm flat against the flagstones. He felt something through the soles of his gut-lined boots: not an earthquake, not a fracture, but a settling. A strange, resonant density in the stone beneath him, as if the rift, which had always felt like a temporary cleft, had suddenly struck an iron root straight down to the center of the world.

The phonolite rang. Just once. A note so low and pure it was felt rather than heard—a deep, bronze hum that vibrated through the ankles of the tower-builders, through the larch timbers, through the downy breasts of the nesting swifts.

Then it ceased.

The milk-fog came pouring over the rim, right on the sixth hour, heavy and cool as sweet cream. The lizards slipped into their chinks. The children drew the felt curtains tight. The white marmots pressed their skulls down into the slate, their long ribs working in unison, warm and lard-thick against the fog.

Beneath them, deep in the dark, the benevolent power held the mountain open with its shoulders, listening with immense, unmoving delight to the sound of a woman in the third tower, who pressed her tongue against her teeth and hissed three thin, dry notes through the dark to tell her daughter that the damp was back, and that they were safe for another night.
