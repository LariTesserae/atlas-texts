The power did not arrive with an escort of wind or an opening of the cloud deck. Those are the instruments of powers that wish to be acknowledged before they have understood where they are standing.

Instead, the attention gathered slowly, the way moisture gathers on the galvanized iron behind the compressors: an accumulation of density rather than a descent. If the tender had possessed an instrument capable of measuring the weight of the air down to the third decimal of a millibar, he might have noted that the air in the basin grew four grains heavier per cubic yard between ten in the morning and noon. The water in his enamel mug settled half a millimeter deeper against the tin. The sixty-cycle hum of the three-phase transformers dropped in pitch by a fraction of a vibration—not slurred, but deepened, as if the copper windings had been gently pressed between two broad, cool thumbs.

A foolish power would have made a garden here. It would have pulled the iron up by its foundations, sweetened the weeping seams until they ran with milk-warm springwater, turned the drill-flutes into mossy troughs, and driven the grey weasels out into the hedgerows to become common and brown again. It would have pitied the snails eating pulverized cylinder grease; it would have pitied the swallows breathing lead dust; it would have pitied the tender’s yellowed skin and his quiet, lonely teeth.

A benevolent and foolish power would have murdered the basin with mercy.

The power that had come was old enough to know that mercy is not always the restoration of an antique Eden; sometimes mercy is merely the granting of duration to a thing that has labored very hard to invent its own dignity out of gravel and scrap.

It did not descend to the floor of the cut. It remained at the rim, where the cleaver-strike of the quarry met the level plateau of scrub and gorse forty feet above the gantry. It looked down into the trench through the flat, shadowless light.

It saw the counterweight rise: four minutes up, the long pause, the pneumatic release, the dull *whump* against the shale. It saw that the counterweight had done this two million times, and that the bronze sleeve in the pillow-block bearing was worn into an oval seventy thousandths of an inch out of true. In six more years, the shaft would bind. When the shaft bound, the belt would smoke and snap; the diesel would run away without its load until its cylinder head cracked; the pump would drown in the rising seepage; the toads would be washed out of their flutes by bilge-water slicked with heavy fuel; and the tender would sit on his grease firkin with his brass wire brush in his lap, listening to a silence he could not survive.

The power considered the bearing.

It did not make the bronze new. To make the bronze new would be a lie; it would introduce into the basin an impossible youth that did not belong to the rusted narrow-gauge rails or the boiled-leather hides of the toads. 

Instead, the power reached down through the cold draft of the pneumatic exhausts—not with hands, but with an intention as dense and dry as slate—and touched the microscopic crystal lattice of the bronze sleeve. It did not rebuild the metal; it simply took the fatigue out of it. It rearranged the slip-planes of the copper and tin molecules so they could bear the oval shape indefinitely, settling the wear into an iron equilibrium that would neither advance nor mend. The bearing would remain seventy thousandths out of true for five hundred years, rattling with the exact loose, familiar chatter the tender’s inner ear expected, but it would never seize.

Then the power looked at the tender.

It watched him take his pocket-knife from his trousers, open the sheepfoot blade with a slow flick of his thumb, and trim the bindweed three inches from the tooth-race of the south counterweight. It watched him wind the three-inch green cuttings into a loop and slide them under the oak sleeper where the voles lived. It watched the precision with which his white-soled boots avoided the loose gravel that might roll into the drainage channel.

The tender had no soul that required saving in the ordinary vocabulary of heaven. He was neither wicked nor righteous; he was an instrument whose clearances had been adjusted until he was completely flush with his task. His lungs had learned the shape of the ozone; his knees had learned the four-minute concussion of the diesels; his blood had taken on the thin, alkaline persistence of the water that leaked from the blasted rock.

The power saw that the tender would die in nineteen years, not from violence or calamity, but because the machinery inside his ribcage would simply arrive at the end of its stroke, like an eight-day clock that has run down its weights.

The power could have lengthened his years. It could have smoothed his breathing and cleared the yellow glaze of the sodium lamps from the whites of his eyes.

It did not. To give an immortal man to a mortal basin would be to make him an exile in his own house.

Instead, the power did three very small, very dry things:

First, it went to the rock wall behind the pump house, where the main weeping seam emerged from the vertical dark of the slate. There, deep in the limestone strata three miles north from which the water traveled, it turned aside a vein of sulfurous marcasite that would have rotted the tender’s iron pipes thirty years hence. It let the clear water remain clear—cold, heavy with lime, smelling faintly of bruised celandine, good for boiling tea and good for the thick whorls of the snails, carrying no more poison than a man’s kidneys could filter out between daybreak and dusk.

Second, it laid a permanent dullness upon the minds of the men who owned the ledger in which the basin was recorded. Two counties away, in an office with frosted glass windows and tall mahogany desks, the clerks were already filing the blue-prints of the basin into dark cedar drawers. The power placed upon those drawers a thin, grey film of unremembering. The owners would continue to pay the small monthly requisition for fuel-oil and waste-cloths; the tank-car would continue to back down the spur line once every four months to discharge its black distillate into the feeder-pipe above the rim; but no inspector would ever look down into the cleaver-strike with a surveyor’s chain, and no developer would ever calculate the cubic yardage of the cut for a municipal landfill. The basin would drop through the mesh of the world’s attention, neither abandoned nor remembered, kept alive by the administrative inertia of an enterprise too vast to notice an engine that never failed to turn.

Third, the power looked at the weasel that had paused by the tender’s boot.

The weasel was staring up with its wet quartz eyes, its paws held against its oily chest. The tender was looking down, chewing his ham and mustard crust, his temple cold from the air displaced by the swallows’ wings.

For one four-minute stroke of the balance-arm, the power let the basin become utterly transparent to itself.

It did not speak. It did not make the light turn golden. The slate remained pale grey, the grease remained black, the belt whipped through the damp air with its wet, flat *slap*. But for those four minutes, the tender felt the weight of the six-ton flywheel not as a separate engine in a timber shed, but as a heavy iron circle turning inside his own pelvis. He felt the pads of the toads’ feet pressed against the wet flutes of the bore-holes three hundred yards away, their rib-cages taking the jaw-rattling *thung-clack* of the diesels with the same rhythmic resignation that governed his own diaphragm. He smelled the coal-dust mastic of the swallows’ nests up in the high steel flange, and he knew—without words, without astonishment, with the plain, flat certainty of a carpenter sighting down the edge of an ash plank—that none of this had been built in vain, that the cut was whole, that the truce was holding, and that the work was sufficient.

When the arm rose and dropped its weight with that deep, subterranean *whump*, the water in the tender’s enamel mug rang in neat, concentric rings. 

The weasel turned and slid into the conduit like grey grease.

The tender swallowed the last dry corner of his crust, brushed two crumbs of mustard-seed from the bib of his overalls with a horn-tipped thumb, and stood up. His rubber soles took hold of the shale without a sound. He took the horn-handled oiler from his pocket, wiped its snout against the clean cotton waste, and walked toward the compressor house to see how the snails were getting on with the three-o'clock shift.

Above the rim, the power withdrew its attention, leaving the sky flat, high, and brightly lit, and the cold air stubborn in the stone.
