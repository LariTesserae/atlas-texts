A foolish power would have stopped the engine.

It would have pitied the deafened ears, reached down with a bright hand through the overcast square of the sky, seized the iron walking-beam on its upstroke, and arrested the twelve-beat heart of the basin. And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

Without the concussion of the iron, the eight dwellers would have collapsed, their balance dismantled. Their inner ears, calcified into blunt shock-absorbers, knew uprightness only by the orientation of the blow; deprived of that downward hammer, their knees would have uncoupled, their spines grown slack and nauseated in the terrible, weightless silence. The air, losing its hydraulic pulse, would have gone thin and cold in their chests. The carabus beetles, unguided by the rhythmic shudder of the timber, would have swarmed in erratic frenzy, devouring one another until only husks remained. In the mulched pit, the blind wrist-thick worms would have starved in the stagnant wood-meal as the flywheel grew cold and the friction-heat drained away. And within three weeks, without the tallow cleared from the seized bronze journals, the basin would have rotted—not with the clean, tannic density that held rot at bay through pressure, but with the wet, sour dissolution of a lung filled with water.

A sentimental power would have lifted them up into the light. It would have carried them past the sixty-foot rim, out into the sunlit meadows or the soft loam of the outer forest, where the wind moves through leaves without a stroke. And there they would have curled on their sides, blind in the glare, their vulcanized soles blistering on dry grass, terrified by the infinite, unsupported horror of an open sky that possessed no ceiling and no return of sound, until their hearts—accustomed to beating against eighty atmospheres of acoustic displacement—burst in the vacuum of peace.

The power that had found them was neither foolish nor sentimental. It was old enough to know that mercy which disregards the anatomy of the sufferer is merely an elegant form of cruelty.

So it did not reach down. It did not speak, for words in the throat were dead things here, like feathers dropped in a furnace.

Instead, it came down through the water.

Far behind the western root-wall, miles up through the dark fissures where the subterranean head gathered in the black bellies of the mountain, the power laid itself into the cold current. It did not warm the water; warmth would have softened the ancient petrified lignum of the basin and invited the white mycelium that kills ironwood. It did not slow the head; the turbine had to turn its twelve strokes every minute, never eleven, never thirteen, because seven lifetimes of bone had been forged to that precise interval.

It simply purified the grease.

Where the water entered the lead-wrapped pipes, it carried with it a microscopic, imperceptible silt—not stone, not sand, but a fine, talc-soft mineral born of deep pressure. Over the course of three seasons, this silt worked its way through the intake, through the packings, into the bronze bushings of the walking-beam. It did not stop the noise; the concussive *tonk* remained, the cold vapor hissed with the same blunt authority. But the screech of metal shaving metal—the dry, tearing shriek of the journals that slowly ground away the chisels and the fingers of the scrapers—softened into a low, oiled hum. The bronze ceased to pit. The journals no longer threw off the razor-thin splinters of oxidized brass that festered under the dwellers' nails.

Next, the power addressed the tallow.

The tallow came from the tallow-wells of the engine, old rendered marrow of things that had died centuries before the roots closed. It was bitter, thick with coal-tar, and it hardened into black slag that tore the scrapers' wrists when they gouged it from the slides. The power altered the drip. Not by magic, but by settling a sweet, clear resin through the pores of the root-buttress that held the western frame. Where the grease met the wood, the resin seeped in, cutting the grit. The tallow softened until it yielded like cold lard to their scrapers of rusted stay-iron.

The dwellers did not look up when this happened. They did not drop to their knees in prayer; they had no concept of prayer, nor any god other than the downstroke that kept their ribs from collapsing inward. But the oldest of them—a creature with thighs broad as beer-casks and soles worn flat as shovel-heads—noticed that his scraper did not chatter against the bronze. He finished his stroke in four movements instead of six. His knuckles did not bleed into the runnel that evening. When he locked his joints against the third downstroke, he felt the vibration through his shins not as a jagged shock that cracked the enamel of his molars, but as a deep, round, clean bell.

He struck the floor twice with his left heel: *Smooth. Cool.*

Across thirty yards of wet root-plank, a younger one, kneeling over a swift whose grease she was stripping, caught the tremor in her hip. She paused. She did not strike back, but she adjusted the shingle-plates of her carapace skirt, and for the first time in sixteen hundred strokes of the beam, she did not flinch as the vapor blew.

The power did not leave them. It took up residence where they lived: not in the sky, which they feared, but in the grain.

It entered the petrified floor like a second grain, running crosswise against the ancient horizontal bands of the buttresses. It was subtle. If it had been too loud, they would have fled from it as from an impending fracture. It introduced into the dead-hard wood a tiny, almost imperceptible warmth—not enough to melt the saddle-leather surface, but just enough that when their cracked, vulcanized feet rested in the drainage furrows during the pause between strides, the cold ache in their metatarsals began to loosen.

And it began to preserve their memory.

This was its greatest work, and its most difficult.

In the basin, when a dweller died, they were wedged into the intake sump. The black water stripped the flesh; the beetles took the rest; within three strokes the memory was hammered out of the floor, because the living could not afford the weight of remembering. To remember an absent footstep while trying to meter three steps between swings of the walking-beam was to misjudge the interval, fail to lock the knees, and have one’s marrow scrambled by sixty tons of reciprocating iron. Survival demanded an instantaneous, absolute present.

The power did not ask them to mourn. It knew they could not.

Instead, it received the dead itself.

When the next one died—a woman whose hands had been polished smooth by forty years of smoothing swift-skin bladders—they carried her, as they always had, to the western curve. They jammed her broad, bowed frame beneath the hemp-wrapped intake pipe. The water tore at her carabus apron; the beetles gathered along the iron lip, waiting.

The dwellers turned their backs, three strides, lock; three strides, lock; heading back to the runnels to scrape before the tallow chilled.

Under the black current, where the lead pipe entered the root, the power did not dissolve her into nothingness. It drew the calcium of her bones into the cellular walls of the root-intake. It took the hard, thick geometry of her skull—shaped by eighty million repetitions of that twelve-stroke rhythm—and pressed its silhouette into the grain of the floor beneath the intake grate, petrifying it into the timber itself, an amber-hard knot that would never wear down under the heel.

It was not a tombstone. It was a tread.

When the oldest dweller came down the runnel the next morning to check the hemp lashings, his bare foot landed on the timber where she had been wedged. His cracked sole caught the slight, deliberate swelling in the wood. It was shaped to the arch of an old, heavy foot. His toes gripped it instinctively. It gave him better purchase on the slick, greasy incline than the flat root had ever provided.

He paused. He waited through one stroke, two strokes, three strokes—letting the concussive *tonk* shudder through the knot into his ankle.

The vibration did not say: *Grieve.*
It did not say: *She is gone.*

It said: *Here is a place to stand.*

He set his weight down firmly into the knot, swung his scraper into the journal with an easy, untorn wrist, and for the first time since the engine was set into the timber, a man in the basin worked without slipping.
