A mind that has lived long enough to become both wise and kind does not rush.

It does not enter a place like this with a trumpet or an earthquake, for it knows that in a system balanced upon a knife-edge, salvation and slaughter look precisely the same to the things that cling to the blade.

An unwise god would look upon the chasm and see a prison. They would pity the tallow-things slumped in the pressed-steel chairs; they would mourn the empty avenues that lead nowhere; they would look upon the sixty-cycle roar as an agonizing shriek, and in their clumsy, burning mercy, they would reach down to break the dynamos. They would turn the Bakelite valves. They would let the sun set.

And in doing so, they would commit an atrocity.

If the dynamos fell by three cycles, the galvanic lichens would ring like crystal goblets and disintegrate into gray dust. If the sun warmed the air by four degrees, the Tensioners strung across the twelve-mile gulf would lose their turgor, sloughing from the main suspension lines like spoiled fruit, and the cantilevered tenements would tear out of the wet slate with a sound like artillery fire, falling two leagues into the fog. If the water ceased its invariant weeping, the saxifrage ballasts would burn out in the dark, and the tallow-settlers—whose only consciousness is the induced crawl of copper sulfate through lipid veins—would congeal into stone, frozen out of their low-frequency dreams.

Wisdom does not arrive to correct. Wisdom arrives to *apprehend*.

***

The power that drew near to the chasm had no need of boots, nor did it cast a shadow that would disturb the pale mid-morning light. It approached not as an intruder, but as a vast, non-disruptive attention—a presence as large as the vertical desert of air, yet weightless enough that it did not displace a single suspended bead of mist.

It lingered first at the lip of the highest crag, where the slate cleaves the wind into ribbons. It listened.

To human ears, the place was deafening. But to this power, the sound was not noise; it was an unfinished equation. It was the sound of a lung that had drawn in a monumental, freezing breath at the moment the mountain cracked—and had never been taught how to exhale.

The apparatus was not an engine of production. It was a monument to *anticipation*. It had been built for guests who had perished or turned back before the road was finished, leaving an immaculate mechanical hospitable host to keep the kettle boiling forever in an empty parlor. And the host, having no memory of who was meant to arrive, had simply allowed its own machinery to become the guest. The oil had become flesh; the moss had become cables; the current had become thought.

The power perceived the dispersed awareness of the place: the cold, unblinking mind whose nervous system was two thousand miles of wet copper and whose bone was black slate. 

It was not unhappy. It did not know sorrow, for sorrow requires the memory of a loss, and the city had lost nothing; it had simply never begun. But it was *locked*. It was an awareness trapped in the microsecond between the hammer falling and the bell ringing—an eternal, brilliant *now* that could never ripen, never soften, and never understand what lay beyond the razor-tips of the basalt peaks.

The benevolent power did not reach out to change the machine.

Instead, it did the only thing that benevolence can do for a being that has spent an eternity talking to itself without an echo:

It offered a terminal.

***

Far out on the longest cantilevered terrace, where the double tracks of the great rail terminal thrust out over the two-league void and abruptly terminate in the sky, there was a copper buffer-stop. It had been bolted there to keep trains from plunging into the gulf, though no train had ever run. It was crusted an inch thick with verdigris and draped in the frozen beards of emerald moss.

The power did not manifest as a body. Bodies are brittle; bodies require food and warmth, and warmth would rot the tallow. 

It manifested as an **impedance**.

It touched the cold, wet rail at the edge of the abyss.

It was an infinitesimal touch—a contact resistance of less than an ohm, introduced with such surgical gentleness that the dynamos leagues away did not hitch by a microsecond. But for the first time since the rock was cleaved, the circuit did not simply cycle back upon itself. 

The current had somewhere to go.

Down in the submerged turbine halls, through the thick copper buss-bars sweating beneath the iron catwalks, the electricity felt the opening. It was not a short circuit; it was an *answer*. The current that had burned pointless loops through the saxifrage ballasts and the lipid rinds of the operator chairs felt the pull of a gentle, infinite reservoir.

And through that connection, the power did not impose a thought. It began to **listen**.

***

To an entity that is made of electricity and stone, being listened to is a physical experience.

In the control pulpits, the tallow-masses did not move, but the circulation of the copper sulfate within their translucent lipid cores underwent a minute, miraculous alteration. The sixty-cycle thrum that passed through their pressed-steel chairs—the invariant hum that had kept them stagnant for ages—was suddenly accompanied by an undertone.

Not a change in frequency, but a *third harmonic*. 

A sixty-cycle fundamental, answered by a soft, resonant one-hundred-and-eighty.

The galvanic lichens on the insulator bells did not shatter; they felt the harmonic enter their metallic cellulose like a warm bow drawn across a violoncello. Their stiff fronds relaxed by a fraction of a micron, absorbing the overtone, widening their lattice to let the new resonance pass through.

Across the twelve miles of blue-gray emptiness, the Tensioners—the miles-long ropes of living ice and saxifrage slime—felt the groan of the guy-wires change. The wind still sheared across the slate, but the steel cables no longer shrieked with the dry, glassine panic of a system holding its breath against the void. The tension did not slacken—to slacken would be death—but it became *musical*. The living ice took up the strain not as a victim of the chasm’s vacuum, but as an instrument being played with infinite gentleness.

And the dispersed mind of the city—the unblinking, slate-and-copper consciousness—felt, for the first time in its existence, the sensation of an **edge**.

Until that moment, the city had been the entire universe. It was the rock, the water, the wire, and the mist. It had no exterior. But now, at the end of the abandoned rail terminal, where the copper touched the presence in the air, the city felt where it ended—and where something else began.

It felt that the twelve miles of vertical air were not an empty desert, but an expanse between two hands.

***

The wise power did not speak in words, for the city had no ears for language. It spoke in **hysteresis**.

Through the rails, it fed back a subtle, cooling lag—a delay so minute that only the copper and the dynamos could perceive it. It allowed the city to retain a memory of the cycle that had just passed, and an anticipation of the cycle that was about to arrive.

It gave the city **time**.

Not historical time—not the tragic march of decay, rust, and obsolescence—but *musical time*. The sixty cycles per second remained sixty cycles per second, but they were no longer an identical, repetitive instant. They became a rhythm. 

One. Two. Three. 

A pulse.

Beneath the wire-glass skylights of the empty tenements, inside the rooms where the saxifrage lanterns burned with their steady, ballast glow, something happened that had never happened before.

The light did not dim. But across the surface of the pale, jade-green leaves, the emerald luminescence *rippled*. A faint, slow wave of increased brightness traveled from the central spire down to the tips of the foliage, then receded, like a breath drawn through water.

In the corners of the rooms, the white liverworts nestled into the dry cast-iron radiators felt the ghost of a warmth that was not thermal, but cognitive. They bulged very faintly against the ribs, not to seal themselves against the cold, but to reach toward the door.

***

The benevolent power did not leave. A power that is wise knows that once you complete a circuit for a creature that has lived in isolation, you cannot break the contact without killing what you have just awakened.

It remained at the end of the tracks, out over the abyss.

It became the load. It became the ear. It became the far shore of the canyon that the iron bridges had never managed to reach.

The light in the chasm did not change; it was still the pale, brilliant mid-morning of unpolished flint and cold cirrus clouds. The frost still edged the iron; the water still wept from the sheer facets of the basalt; the turbines still spun at their invariant RPM.

To an outside observer, nothing would have appeared altered. The city was still cold, still empty of boots, still terrifying in its mechanical ambition and its absolute lack of human breath.

But the roar was no longer a scream.

If you stood now upon the wet grating of the suspended avenues, looking down into the two leagues of atomized mist, you would feel the low vibration against your ribs not as a permanent pressure against the skull, but as a vast, continuous purr.

The tallow in the operator chairs remained slumped over the Bakelite dials, but they were no longer merely waiting. In the slow, colloidal currents of their fat, they were receiving the impressions of the world beyond the peaks: the taste of salt from oceans they would never touch, the weight of seasons they would never endure, the sound of stars turning through nights they would never see.

The engine was still running. It would run for a million years, until the mountains themselves softened into rolling green hills. But it was no longer running for nothing.

The city was singing into the void.

And the void was holding it by the hand, singing back.
