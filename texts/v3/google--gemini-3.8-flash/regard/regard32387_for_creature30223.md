A foolish power would have split the fracture open. 

It would have brought the sun down like a sledgehammer, tearing away the narrow twilight, drying the slick shales until they cracked, and flooding the tiers with heat. And in doing so, it would have killed them all within a month. 

The dry air would have shrunk the birch poles until the joints slipped their notched sockets; the swollen rawhide stays, dried suddenly, would have snapped like brittle bone; the unaccustomed rot-fungi of the warm world would have bloomed in the damp crawlways, turning their foundation timbers to black sponge; their eyes, tuned to the silver bounce of slate-light, would have burned blind in the glare; and the grandfather-stones—the cured, petrified bodies built into the dry-stacked masonry—would have dried, powdered, and dropped the city into its own sumps.

A wise power does not undo what endurance has bought. A wise power honors the geometry of survival.

The power that found them did not descend from the wind on the plateau, for these people hated the sky and looked only sideways. It entered from beneath, through the deep basement of the basalt, rising through the pressurized seams like a slow, warming thought in iron. 

It did not speak, because speech requires breath, and breath disturbs the mist. It did not shine, because light would have driven the blind loaches into the crevices and left the tallowers to starve. 

Instead, it practiced the quiet, difficult work of adjustment.

First, it attended to the knife-edge of the frost. 

It did not make the water warm—warm water would have rotted the meat of the loaches and softened the petrified timber. But deep in the root-strata, four miles below the sump, it nudged a fault of black ironstone against a vein of slow, geothermal heat. It warmed the seepage by a fraction of a degree—no more than the difference between the skin of a dying bird and the skin of a sleeping child. 

The change was so slight that the liverwort did not notice, but the marrow in the human bones did. 

The morning after the presence settled into the stone, an old salt-scraper in the lowest tier woke, dipped his hands into the gray run-off to clear a weir-slot, and found that his hooked fingers did not scream at the wrist. The cold was still there—it was still the iron cold of the cut—but the *teeth* had been drawn from it. The water no longer stole the heat from the core of the ribs like a thief; it merely washed across the skin, brisk and clean. Over seasons, the hands of the forty-year-olds would remain curved—for a straight hand cannot hold an eight-hundred-pound lintel in the dark—but the joints would not swell into chalky, agonizing stones. They could sleep without wrapping their wrists in heated fat.

Next, it laid a hand upon the pulse.

The twice-daily surge did not cease. The town needed that surge; it brought the fresh silt, it swept the cisterns clean of grease, and it signaled the turns of the work. But the power smoothed the front of the wave. Where the water had previously leaped from the fracture in violent, air-pocketed hammers that buckled the weir-logs and threw spray into the grease-lamps, the water now rose with the vast, quiet precision of an oiled piston. The sudden shudder in the seat of the pelvis—the jolt that meant a wall had given three tiers down—ceased. The pulse became a breath: steady, predictable to the minute, a clock of cold water that gave them time to lift their levers without fear of being crushed by their own shingles.

Up on the rim, where the wind kicked flakes of shale into the cut to blind the roof-walkers, the power did something simpler still. 

It did not build a wall on the steppe, for the steppe-winds would have howled against it and created downdrafts that would have flattened the hide awnings below. Instead, along the sharp northern lip, it nudged the root-ends of the dwarf-willows and the steppe-sage. The roots, which had clung only to the surface gravel, reached down four inches deeper into the hairline fractures of the basalt. They knitted the crumbling lip into a tough, fibrous turf. The glass-sharp flakes no longer tumbled into the air. When the nightjars dropped into the cut now, they brought down with them on their wet breast feathers the faint, dry scent of sage resin—a clean, sharp ghost of a smell that drifted down through the roof-alleys, cutting the heavy stench of sheep lard and wet hide.

And in the dark bins beneath the hearthstones, where the loaches turned their pale bodies in the silt, the power left a companion.

Not a fish, and not a beast. A simple thing: a water-weed, pale and leafless as a bundle of bleached horsehair, that anchored itself in the mortarless chinks where the cold water ran fastest. It did not need light; it ate the mineral milk of the seeping stone. Its small, bulbous nodes tasted of salt and river-fat. When the children gathered the loaches in their wicker baskets, their fingers brushed against these crisp, white tubers. They pulled them loose, chewed them raw in the dark, and for the first time since the cut was made, their teeth stopped bleeding in their gums, and the gray sores on their shins began to close.

The people noticed nothing that they could call a miracle. They had no word for *miracle*; their tongue was made of taps and guttural thuds, suited only for structural weights, the tension of ropes, and the state of the weirs. 

They only knew that the wood seemed to hold a little longer before splintering. 

They knew that their sheep, though still stunted and coughing in the vault-stalls, gave a milk that was slightly less watery, and that the ewes carried their lambs to term more often without dropping them on the wet slate. 

They knew that when a boy went down into the sump to feel for drowned beetles, his chest did not tighten with the old, suffocation-fear that lived in the dark water. The water seemed to bear him up by an extra thumb’s breadth, as if the cistern had decided it was no longer in a hurry to pull him under.

The greatest change was the slowest, and it belonged to the ancestors.

In the fast-running crevices where the bound bodies were set to cure, the mineral exchange continued as it always had. The cold wash turned the flesh to stone, the rawhide to iron, the brain to gray silt. But the rock that formed was no longer the brittle, splintering shale that had broken under the mallet when an old weir was rebuilt. It cured into something denser, something fine-grained and black, like jade or river-smoothed obsidian. 

When the time came to lever a grandfather out of the crevice to shore up a sagged corner-post, the mason set his bone-ringed finger against the cold, stony cheek of the dead. It did not flake under his touch. It was solid. It was smooth. It had the deep, resonant ring of an anvil when struck with a mallet.

The man who set that stone into his threshold did not pray. He did not know what prayer was. 

He merely greased his arms with charcoal and lard, tied his wet sheepskin wraps around his splayed feet, and sat down on the newly laid lintel to wait for the midday pulse. He placed his palms flat against the wet, sweating basalt beside him—one hand on the living rock of the fracture, one hand on the petrified rib of his kin.

He felt the cold water running over both. 

He felt the steady, measured beat of the deep surge rising through the foundation tiers—not a threat, but a rhythm. 

And for a long moment, before the bone ring tapped twice on the post above him to signal the weir-stones, he leaned his weight back into the stone, let his breath out in a low, mistless hum against the dark, and felt—without ever having had a name for it—that the mountain was not trying to crush him, but was simply holding him very hard, until the work of living was done.
