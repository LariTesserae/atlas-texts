The power did not descend like a falling star, for a falling star has an edge, and an edge cuts into things that have spent half a billion years learning how to meet without parting.

It came instead as an alteration in the weight of the mist. 

To the herders, who knew the air not as an emptiness but as a syrup that varied by only fractions of a grain in its grease-content and its warmth, there was suddenly a patch of afternoon where the vapor tasted slightly less of the low valley’s river-silt and more of something that had never touched clay. It was neither colder nor hotter than the flank of a sleeping mound. It simply had an interior clarity—like water that had passed through an infinite thickness of white sand until it carried no spores, no tallow, no drift.

The power had many names in worlds where names were shouted, but here it shed them. Language was an expenditure of breath, and breath on this plain belonged to the intake slits and the moist mantles of the discs. 

First, the power considered what it might give.

A lesser benevolence, young and full of the vanity of light, might have brought seed: the tall green things of the lowlands, the ferns that uncoil like iron springs, the violent ambition of trees that drill into stone and split it wide. It might have brought fire, thinking the gray shag of the herders cold. It might have brought songs with pitches that rose above the low register of water-creep and gristle-creak. It might have offered an upward gaze.

The wise power looked upon the vast, pale pavement, miles deep in breath, and understood what that gift would be: a slaughter.

To give this world ambition would be to introduce hunger. To give it hunger would be to turn the rasping discs against the mounds, or the herders against the discs. To give them an eye for the stars would make their low, heavy necks ache with a posture the stone had never authorized. This place was not fallen. It was not waiting for redemption. It was an *arrival*. The mountains had already suffered their tumult; they had been ground to meal and washed to the edges of the world, leaving only this pink floor where nothing fell, nothing hunted, and nothing was abandoned.

The power did not want to touch the balance, for to touch a balance is to declare oneself heavier than the world.

And yet, benevolence is not an absence. Benevolence is the desire that being should find comfort in its own nature.

The power drifted down across the wet domes until it found one of the standing slabs—a pale wedge of quartz-veined gneiss, perhaps eight cubits tall, that had been tipped upright seven thousand seasons ago by twelve herders whose names were now only a particular crust of tallow three fingers above the middle joint. The slab had begun to lean south toward a swale where thirty mounds lay packed flank to flank. In two more generations of the disc-creep, the base-wedge would slip in the wet lichen, and the stone would fall, crushing sixty square paces of living pavement and snapping the cartilage ribs of the great sleepers beneath it.

The herders did not know this. They did not calculate levers across centuries; they felt only the day’s grease and the hour’s heave.

The power did not reach out with a spectral hand to right the stone. That would have left an odor of iron and thunder that would sicken the scavenger-crabs in their crawlspace. 

Instead, the power entered the quartz wedge beneath the heel of the slab.

It did not change the mineral. It simply took upon itself the vibration of the stone—that slow, cold, atomic hum that gneiss keeps from the dawn of its cooling. It settled there, becoming very dense, very still, and infinitesimally sweet. It took the pressure of the four-ton leaning slab into its own heart, absorbing the downward shear of the granite and turning it into an almost imperceptible warmth—not the warmth of blood, which rots, but the warmth of dry sand after three days of cloud.

Then it waited.

Late in the fourth watch of the mist, an old herder came dragging its broad pads through the shallow dew-slick. Its gray pelt was heavy with grease; its deep, gristled eyes were cloudy with the film that comes before the final stiffening of the knees. It smelled of river silt and old wool. It had rolled its last carcass thirty seasons back; now it simply moved along the stone circles, seeking the places where the air stayed longest in the pockets.

It came to the quartz slab. It did not see the power, because the power had made itself into the texture of the mineral. 

The herder paused. It extended one broad, shovel-shaped palm, tipped with its tough, suckered pads, and laid it against the base where the weight pressed hardest.

It expected the slick, indifferent chill of the quartz, which usually tasted of rain that had forgotten its cloud.

Instead, the stone met its skin with an unaccustomed, resting truth. The vibration did not jar the bones of its wrist; it traveled up the short, thick arm and settled directly behind the thick gristle of its brow, in that small, quiet knot where the herder stored the memory of all the slabs it had ever leaned against.

The stone felt *held*.

The herder stayed there for a very long time. The afternoon mist came in over the rim of the peneplain, silvering its shaggy back, trickling into the creases of its neck. The mounds half a league away chuffed in their sleep, three thousand vents opening at once, a soft, communal hiss like a wave breaking against grease-fat.

The herder did not pray, for it had no gods. It did not thank the stone, for the stone was simply itself. 

But it leaned its great, barrel-shaped chest against the quartz, dug its round heels into the space between two pavement-discs, and closed its hooded eyes. For the first time in its eighty years of slow labor, the tension went out of the thick ropes of muscle that held its neck to its ribs. It felt the immense, downward labor of the plateau—the weight of the living floor, the patience of the bedrock, the slow passage of the atmospheric soup—and it felt that something beneath the floor was not merely enduring the world, but *approving* of it.

The power remained.

It did not speak. It did not teach. It did not save them from their quiet deaths, nor did it keep the mist-flies from their ancient office upon the drying mounds.

It only stayed in the quartz, holding the slab three inches back from the fall, slightly warm, slightly dense, so that when the old ones came to rub their tallow against the history of their kind, they would find that the world was thick enough to lean upon, and that the stone did not tire.
