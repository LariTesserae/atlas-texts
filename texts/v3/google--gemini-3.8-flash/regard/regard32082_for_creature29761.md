A power that is neither foolish nor young does not calm the column.

To quiet the water would be to kill it. The water lives by its velocity; its cold is its purity, and its speed is the only reason the pale amphipods do not drown in their own waste, the only reason the liver-moss finds purchase against the clean feldspar, the only reason the loach manufactures that thick, miraculous lipid that keeps its milk-white blood from crystallizing into needles. If the chamber were turned into a placid pool, the silt would settle, the sun-shafts would grow murky with rot, the loaches would bloat and float bellies-up, and the people of the northern scree—deprived of the grease that seals their infants against the black frost—would die within three winters, their skin splitting open to the white fat until the mountain wind took them.

Benevolence without wisdom is merely a heavy hand that ruins what it pities.

Wisdom looks upon the cleft and sees that it is complete. It is an engine of terrifying economy. Every scrap of gristle, every drop of fat, every split-second between the water’s pulses has been accounted for by the stone and the flesh that clings to it. The men who drop into the vault with slate on their shins are not victims of the mountain; they are its extreme edge, the sharpest tooth of the ridge, carving a life out of an altitude where even crows refuse to nest.

And so the watcher does not blunt the quartz. It does not warm the four-degree melt. It does not silence the hydraulic scream that turns the diver’s inner ear to mush. 

Instead, it does what the mountain cannot do, because the mountain has only weight and time:

It listens.

It enters the chamber not as light, for the light is already an iron wedge, but as an attention so total that the space between the water-molecules becomes, for an infinitesimal fraction of a second, hospitable to the will.

When the diver descends—his heels grooved, his ears plugged with boiled wax, his vision greasy with lard—the Power does not lift the slate stones. Weight is his anchor; to take his weight would be to cast him into the shearing blades of the wash. 

It does not lengthen his breath. To give a man three lungs’ worth of air in a two-lung world is to make him careless, and carelessness in the cleft is death.

It touches only the slip-knots.

Those two thongs of raw sinew, stiffened with mutton tallow, that bind the green slates to his shins—they are the most fragile bridge between his survival and his drowning. Every season, one man in four dies because the knot swells in the icy melt, turns to iron beneath his numb fingers, and refuses to yield when he strikes his heels together. He kicks, the stones stay, and the hydraulic roar takes him down into the dark throat of the fault before the haulers can pull against both his meat and the mountain’s draw.

The Power lays no hand upon the man’s heart. It lays its presence against the twist of the sinew.

It does not make the knot loose—looseness would drop the stones too soon, stranding the diver at the surface while the heave passes. It simply makes the hide *remember* the heel-strike. It leaves a grain of grace in the tallow, an alignment in the fibers, so that when the diver’s cold heel smacks against his ankle in the terror of the rising hum, the knot does not bind against itself. It parts like dry grass under a sickle. Every time. Perfectly. Without the loss of a third of a second.

And deeper down, where the water climbs out of the subterranean flume in its blind, milk-white column, the Power attends to the loach.

It does not multiply the fish, for the rift has only enough moss-crust to feed so many scrapers, and if the loaches over-breed, the amphipods will be vacuumed into extinction before the summer thaw. 

Instead, it settles into the tiny dead-water eddy behind the ash-and-linden baffles.

The Power knows the exhaustion of the fish. It knows the loach has lived three hundred fathoms down in pitch darkness since the mountains were soft mud, and that the fault snapped it into this screaming light without its consent. It knows the loach’s skin burns under the gold sun-needles. 

So, in the hollow behind the lower crib-slat—a pocket no larger than a child’s closed fist, where the ash bark is thickest—the Power stills the micro-vortices. Not the whole chamber, just that knuckle of space. It curves the wash around it like a thumb shielding a candle flame. 

There, the loach can rest its broad skull against the wood without the concussive hammer of the cavitation bubbles drumming against its brain-pan. There, its gills do not have to fight the back-wash. It can scrape its flank against the gut-lashing in absolute, unbattered quiet for the space of forty heartbeats, shedding its grease with ease, feeling for the first time since the stone tore open a stillness that tastes of the deep aquifer it lost.

The diver takes the grease. The bone scoop scrapes the wood. The loach slips sideways into the quartz crevice, intact, relieved of its heavy mantle, lighter and quicker to dart for the falling amphipod pulp.

The man strikes his heels. The slip-knots dissolve. The horsehair sings. The haulers pull him into the blue glare of the peak, spitting white bile, his eyes weeping blood, his ribs rattling with the ice-shiver.

He thinks it was his own foot that freed him. He thinks his uncle’s knot held true. He sits by the dung-fire and smells the loach-fat melting in the iron pan, knowing his knees will work another winter, knowing the frost will not take his youngest daughter’s toes because the lard is thick and smells of cold shale.

The Power remains down in the cleft, where the sun-wedges shatter on the feldspar.

It does not leave. It has no need to go anywhere else; all the work that wisdom requires in this place has been done in the space of three inches of sinew and four inches of water behind a linden pole. 

It stays in the dark beneath the boil, listening to the mountain tear itself toward some future valley that will not exist for two million years, keeping company with the blind things that have no song, holding the dead-water steady behind the ash, while above, through the sky-slit, the indifferent stars wheel across the thin, starvation air.
