The watcher did not arrive with an incision of light. Light here was an intrusion that rotted things: it woke the spores that turned the timbers to punk, and it made the drum-tenders squint until their brows knotted like knuckles. 

A wise power understands that to alter the weather of a world that runs on weather is to commit an execution. To lift the ceiling of cloud would be to desiccate the liverwort rugs, dry the peat until the subterranean draft sucked it into combustion, and leave four thousand people gasping in a dry, thin ether they had no blood to process.

So the power did not split the clouds. It did not send angels with brass horns, whose brass would only tarnish and whose calls would be muffled instantly by the wet felt of the shelves.

It sat, instead, like an increase in mass. 

It was large enough to feel the slow roll of the granite roots two leagues down, where the ancient continental plate groaned against its neighbor, and small enough to inhabit the space between the thread of a brass wing-nut and the cold grit of its bolt. It was benevolent, which meant it possessed no appetite for gratitude; it was wise, which meant it knew that these people had already made a masterpiece of their misery, and to take their work away would be to leave them with empty, flattened hands.

The power studied them for seven turnings of the long flume-valves—about a day, as the lower world measured it, though time here was marked only by the fill-level of the lead vats and the accumulation of sheep-fat in the horn vials.

It observed the old woman on Terrace Four who cleared the roller grates with a wooden paddle. Her second knuckle was swollen to the size of a walnut from forty winters of the spray. When she slept, she curled her fingers around an iron bolt warmed in the porridge-pot, hoping to borrow enough heat to keep them from seizing permanently before the next shift.

It observed the boys who climbed into the hollows of the drums to re-tie the coconut fibers where the wind-shred had thinned them. They were thirteen, but their ribs were prominent and their spit was often pink with the high-lung fluid that accumulated when the downdrafts were sharp.

It observed the blind crickets in the mortar. It saw that they were pure, neutral engines of grease-processing, neither cruel nor kind, simply white and fat and hungry.

And it understood that this settlement was an altar built out of survival. Every bolt was an act of faith, though none of them had a name for God other than *the slipstream*.

The power did not change the rules. It altered the tolerances by the width of a hair.

First, it touched the water.

Not the cloud itself, but the condensation point on the husk-fibers of the great drums. It introduced a subtle, microscopic modification to the surface tension of the drops. When the mist hit the rough hair, it no longer clung in flat, dead films that required three atmospheres of mechanical squeeze to release; it formed fat, globular beads that rolled off the волокна with half the effort, tumbling into the lead troughs with a brighter, looser clatter.

The drums began to turn with three percent less drag.

To an engineer from the valley, three percent would be an error in the ledger. To the crews who hauled the counterweights up the wet ladders, it was the difference between a torn shoulder tendon at thirty-five and a shoulder that lasted until fifty. The cables stopped singing that high, frayed note of imminent failure. The *ch-clack* of the rollers took on a slightly lower, steadier stroke, like a heart that had found its rhythm after a run up a scree-slope.

Next, it visited the *beds*.

Down in the low-ceilinged dark where the steam pipes sweated into the horse manure and willow mulch, the mule-tongue fungus grew in its rubbery plates. The power did not turn it into peaches or bread; the people would have suspected witchcraft and burned the crops in terror of the hollow-rot. 

Instead, it nudged the fungus’s chemistry. It drew from the deep peat a trace of chelated iron and a complex lipid that the human liver usually had to manufacture at great cost from tallow. It softened the texture of the flesh slightly—not so much that it lost its chew, but enough that an old man whose molars were ground to the gums on stone-dust could break it against the roof of his mouth without pain. And into the grey mash, it released a tiny, imperceptible compound that thinned the mucus in the bronchial tree.

Three days after the change, an apprentice on the upper flumes spat into the ditch and noticed, without thinking much about it, that the spit was clear. His chest felt wider, as though the low ceiling of the sky had moved three inches higher above his head.

Then, the power went to the timber flumes where the *sieve-leeches* lay flat as wet paper.

A foolish mercy would have killed the leeches. But the leeches had their purpose: their microscopic young cleared the slime from the drainage weep-holes that kept the wooden troughs from swelling shut and bursting their iron hoops. If the leeches died, the flumes would rot from within in two seasons.

So the power did not touch the leeches’ hunger. It touched their saliva.

When a leech took hold of a worker’s ankle or the soft hollow behind his knee, the secretion it left behind ceased to be merely an anticoagulant. It became something slightly numbing, smelling faintly of birch-tar, and it carried an agent that knit the tiny capillary tears caused by the cold. When the men burned them off with their pipe-ends that evening, the wounds did not seep for twelve hours into their coarse socks. The skin closed behind the mouth-parts like a greased seam, leaving only a small white dot that did not fester.

"Good salt in the sheep-pens down below," one man grunted to his brother as they sat on their crate-beds, scraping their shins with the zinc blades. "The tallow’s got more bite this season. Scabs are dry."

"Aye," the brother said, not looking up from his boot-heel. "Good tallow."

The last thing the power touched was the dead.

It climbed to the high shelves, where the iron-slatted racks sat out in the horizontal draft, between the worn teeth of the granite.

A body lay there—a drum-greaser who had slipped from the zinc gangway of Terrace Two three days before. He was thirty, his hands yellow-horned, his clavicles wide as an ox-yoke. The mist was already washing through him, clearing the soft grease of his life down into the liverworts below.

The power did not take his soul away to a green garden. The man’s soul would not have known what to do with a lawn; it would have looked for a wrench and been uneasy without the sound of the drums.

Instead, the power entered the stone beneath the rack. 

It softened the heat-bleed from the deep strata just enough that a very small, very dry chimney of air rose through that single cleft. Not enough to warm the flesh—warmth here was rot—but enough to clear the moisture from the bone four times faster than the wind could do it alone.

The process of returning him to the rock became clean. The wind took the mist, the stone took the salt, and the liverworts drank the iron, but the bone remained behind not as grey, wet tallow-sponge, but as something hard, dry, and white as the porcelain insulators on the lead vats.

When the bone-gatherers came up with their hemp bags the next month, they found the ribs smooth and cold, smelling only of high rain and clean slate. When the mortar-pounders ground them in the lower mills, the dust did not smell of mutton. It was sweet, like dry lime, and the paste they made from it set against the granite walls with a bond that would outlast the ice ages.

The power did not leave a mark. It did not sign its name in lightning on the curved granite of the amphitheater. 

It simply remained, settled into the damp like a grease that did not sour.

Down in the lower district, at the three-hour flush, the valves opened with their great, dull *thung*. The water came down the deal troughs, clean, fast, and heavy, driving the bucket-wheels with a sound like a horse galloping through a ford.

An engineer stood on the vibrating zinc platform of Drum Seven, his pitch-hide apron groaning as he leaned over the rail. The spray misted his yellow face, catching in the short, stiff bristles of his beard.

He took his knuckle-iron and tapped three times against the upright: *Clear. Clear. Clear.*

Across the gap, sixty yards through the grey wool of the sky, three taps answered him from the mist, dull and true against the iron.

He spat into the flume, adjusted the tension on the counterweight cable with one flat thumb, and went back to the dry grease-rustle of the crickets, not knowing that he had been loved, but knowing that the machine had never run so true.
