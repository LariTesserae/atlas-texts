A power that is merely good might have struck the ceiling plates open. It would have pitied the twilight, imagined the blind beetles dreamed of noon, and shattered the dolomite with a golden fist to let the summer in. 

And within three days, the Graben would have been a trench of rot: the sixty-degree blood of the lizards boiling into fever, the moist film of liverwort crisping into black soot, the young bats dried like leaves on their pegs, and the millions of swifts hurled into confusion by a sky that had no ceiling and no echo. An entire world murdered by an altar-cloth.

Wisdom, however, knows that wholeness does not always look like an orchard. Wisdom knows that this place is complete. It is an iron engine, terrifyingly pure, closed upon its own hinge. It asks for neither salvation nor apology. 

Therefore, the power did not bring sun. It did not bring silence. It brought only its presence—which, being vast, quiet, and without weight, entered the Graben not through the chimney rungs or the roof-cleft, but as a subtle change in the tension of the air, the way a sleeper’s breathing deepens when someone who loves them sits down beside the bed.

The power walked the floor of crushed shale. Its feet left no depression in the ammonia-crust; the spade-headed beetles did not break their centuries-old columns, though their long, sweeping antennae drifted slightly toward the warmth as they passed, tasting something that was not guano, nor meat, nor lime, but had the faint scent of rain that has fallen three thousand miles away.

The power did three things, and each was small enough to be mistaken for stone.

First, it went to the cold hearth beneath the western overhang, where the harvesters leave their greasy ashes and their stone flakes. 

The power looked upward into the vertical chimney, where the men and women of the upper plains climb down with raw wool crammed in their ears, their eyelids scalded by the ammonia gas, their fingers stiff with terror and cold as they drag down their wicker hampers to keep their children alive through the plains’ dry winter. The power saw that they came not out of malice, but from the same iron necessity that drove the centipede across the slate: the demand of belly and blood. 

The power did not bless the hunters with wealth that would turn them idle, nor did it bar the chimney to save the swifts. Instead, it pressed its palm against the living dolomite at the second resting-joint of the ascent, eighty paces from the floor, where the climbers’ arms invariably begin to knot with cramp and their lungs burn hottest. It pressed until the grain of the rock yielded by half an inch, hollowing out a smooth, cupped ledge—just broad enough for two heels to sit flat, just deep enough to take the weight off a failing shoulder—and there it cooled the rock face so that condensed water would gather not as chalky, bitter lime, but sweet and cold against the cracked lips of anyone who pressed their face into the seam. It would not save them from the work. It would only keep them from falling.

Second, it went to the silt-creepers and the gray lizards wedged among the talus. 

It sat down on the sharp dolomite flakes beside a great barrel-chested stalker whose pulse thumped once every thirty seconds, whose milky-blue eye was fixed in absolute patience upon a dark joint in the rock. The power knew that this creature did not possess a soul that asked for paradise; its heaven was the sixty degrees of the floor, the heavy mucus on its tongue, the thick, dreamless digestion that lasted twenty days. 

The power laid a single finger upon the rough, keeled ridge above the lizard’s eye. It did not warm the creature—that would have been cruelty, an agony of accelerated pulse—but it eased the heavy, metallic ache that lives in the marrow of cold-blooded things that have never known the sun. For the span of three slow heartbeats, the lizard’s lid softened, and through the primitive, ancient ganglion at the base of its skull, there passed a sensation that had never before existed in the Graben: the feeling of being held without being preyed upon. When the finger lifted, the lizard remained motionless, stone-colored and eternal, but its jaw rested slightly easier against the crushed shale.

Third, the power looked upward into the deafening vaulted gloom.

It stood beneath the roaring pelt of the walls, where three billion heartbeats hammered together in the dark, a furnace of down, skin-flaps, and saliva. The sound was monstrous, an acoustic grinder that broke human balance into sickness. Yet the power did not quiet the shrieking, for the shrieking was how the bats mapped their kingdom and how the swifts knew their sisters.

Instead, the power leaned its weight against the subterranean roar and listened to it—not as noise, but as a prayer that did not know it was praying. It listened to the blind midges rising from the runnels; it listened to the soft, audible *plink* of ticks striking the shale three hundred yards below; it listened to the salivary glue drying on the nests and the scissor-click of the troglobite jaws clearing the floor. It listened until every thread of that tremendous, relentless friction had an ear to receive it. For ten thousand years, the Graben had thundered into vacancy, entirely indifferent to whether it was heard. Now, for one hour, the thunder struck a heart that did not flinch, did not judge it savage, and did not wish it to be anything other than what it was.

When the power rose to leave, the Graben looked no different.

The twilight was still slate-gray and flat. The air still tasted of vinegar and bird lime. The blind centipede struck the beetle with its amber claws, and the beetle’s shell dissolved in the dark. 

Up in the chimney, a young hunter, descending for the first time with wool tied over his ears, slipped on the greasy juniper rungs, panicked, gasped, and found his boots suddenly dropping onto an unexpected ledge of dry rock that had not been there in his father’s stories. He leaned his forehead against the stone, his eyes streaming with ammonia tears, drank three mouthfuls of sweet water from a crevice that smelled of snow, caught his breath, and climbed downward into the dark to do what he had to do.
