The Power that came to the spires did not descend from the violet sky, nor did it rise from the white floor of the lower clouds. It arrived the way a thought arrives in an ancient mind: without displacement, having simply gathered itself from the wide, thin clarity of the middle air until it was present.

It was vast, though it occupied no displacement. It had no wings to snap into sockets; it had no hide to file against the quartz. It was an awareness shaped like an immense patience, possessing the two attributes that distinguish the truly wise from the merely mighty: it understood the terrible integrity of things, and it possessed the mercy of restraint.

Its first impulse—the ancient, instinctive reflex of love—was an ache to soften the world.

It looked upon the shearman chick, blind and gray in its pebble-hollow, shivering against the shudder of the rock, its belly packed with dry spiders and dust, waiting three years in the dark for its bones to set. The Power felt the urge to cup the chick in an unbuffeted warmth, to wrap the spire in a pocket of calm, moist air where soft leaves might uncurl from the fissures and rain might fall like milk.

It looked upon the scaph, wedged by its snout into the flute-groove, its jaw vibrating so hard against the groaning granite that its teeth wore down to flat plates of horn before it was half-grown, terrified of lifting its brow an eighth of an inch lest the three-hundred-knot wind peel it into the abyss. It wanted to lift the scaph. It wanted to set the beast upon a broad, horizontal meadow of clover, where its limbs could spread and its belly could soften against wet silt.

It looked upon the two Stitchers hanging from their horn-tipped adzes three thousand feet above the white sea, their lips black with tallow, their lungs burning on the razor-sharp ozone, communicating only through the spasm of their knuckles because their voices had been confiscated by the roar ten thousand years ago. It wanted to speak to them. It wanted to fill their skulls with a silence so deep they could hear their own hearts, to lead them down through the cloud-deck into the green, humid valleys where water ran thick and the earth did not demand a blood-covenant for every horizontal foot of passage.

The Power drew a breath that was not air. It raised its will over the spires like a shielding hand.

And then, because it was wise, it stopped.

It looked deeper. It looked with the eye that sees not only the struggle, but the shape of the joy that lives inside the struggle.

It saw the shearman slip from the downwind eddy into the main jet. It saw the bird lock its shoulders with a sound like a small hammer-strike, its copper blood surging through the passive, vented tubes of its chest. The bird did not experience the three-hundred-knot wind as violence; it experienced it as *solidity*. The bird was not fighting the sky; it was resting upon it as a sleeper rests upon a bed of cedar. In the blast, its eye cleared, its golden nictitating membrane swept clean by the pure friction of the world, and in its small, iron skull there was no fear—only the clean, blinding ecstasy of absolute equilibrium. To calm the wind would be to drop ten thousand magnificent creatures like stones into the mud, their wings useless, their vented lungs collapsing in the thick, lazy air of the lowlands, gasping and drowning in the stillness.

It saw the scaph. It felt what the scaph felt through the bone of its jaw: the long, deep, planetary bell of the granite column. The scaph was not cowering. It was *interlocked*. It was the tooth of the mountain, fitted into the world with an engineering so sublime that not an atom of energy was wasted. In the horizontal meadows of the earth, the scaph would be a slug, an invalid, a joke; here, in the shear, it was an absolute master of geometry, reading the tectonic stresses of a continent through the vibration of its teeth.

And it looked at the Stitchers.

It saw the two humans wedged in a seam, their skin cured to the color of dried tea, their rope of sinew taut between them. The younger had dropped a hand from his adze to steady the elder, whose breath was coming thin and ragged in the high blue. The Power heard what passed between their fingers: not complaints, not prayers for rescue, but a rapid, staccato syntax of alignment. 

*The fourth whistle is true.*
*The mist is heavy with salt from the west.*
*The column stands.*

They did not wish to go down into the swamps. To them, the lowlands were a place of blindness, where men lived in rot and fog, their eyes clouded by dust that never blew away, their minds slackened by an abundance that asked nothing of their courage. These people were the ears of the world. They had climbed out of the mud to sit in the white glare of the zenith, to be the only thing in all creation that understood what the great wind was saying. Their pride was not arrogance; it was the crystalline dignity of a creature that has found the exact edge of its endurance and chosen to live there on purpose.

The Power lowered its will. It withdrew the shield it had almost made.

To "save" them would be an insult. To make this place soft would be to murder its perfection. The benevolence of the foolish is a bulldozer that levels the peaks to make gardens; the benevolence of the wise is an open palm that leaves the peak untouched.

Yet love, once awakened, cannot simply turn its back and walk away. A benevolent power cannot leave a world entirely unchanged; it must leave a gift, or its presence was merely a shadow.

So the Power reasoned: *What can one give to those who have built themselves out of stone and gale, who need nothing that does not already fly or whistle?*

It did not alter the wind. It did not warm the air. It did not soften the rock.

Instead, the Power leaned its vast, invisible weight into the foundation of the world, down where the roots of the granite towers plunged through the clouds and anchored in the planetary mantle. 

It reached into the stone itself.

With a touch that took three seasons to complete—so slow that not a grain of quartz was dislodged, so delicate that the sleeping scaphs felt only a warm thrumming in their jaws like the purring of a mother—it slightly reorganized the micro-crystalline structure of the granite. It did not make the stone softer; it made it *purer*. It aligned the quartz crystals along the vertical fluting, turning the miles-tall pillars into resonators of unimaginable clarity.

Before, the towers had groaned under the wind. They had shuddered with a chaotic, grinding shear that slowly fractured the deeper seams over millennia.

Now, as the invariant planetary jet struck the prows of the towers, the sound changed.

The deafening, skull-flattening roar did not diminish in volume—to lower its volume would have starved the shearmen—but its texture shifted. The chaotic white noise fell into a standing chord. It became a sound of terrifying, clean majesty: an immense, five-octave pedal-point that ran through the granite from the root to the summit, ringing like a bell cast from cold iron.

The change was so subtle that for weeks, no beast moved differently. But slowly, the gifts unfolded.

In the circular pools, the vibration of the new chord passed through the stone and set up a microscopic, stationary ripple upon the water's resin-like skin. The trench-mites, walking the quartz sand, felt the frequency through their thread-like legs. It was a rhythm. For hundreds of thousands of years they had lived in a timeless, dead vacuum; now, their tiny pools had a clock. They moved with a new, quiet coordination, grazing the blue-green film with an ease that lengthened their brief generations, their translucent shells glowing faintly with a mica-like iridescence they had never borne before.

The shearmen felt the air reorganize around the pillars. Because the stone was ringing true, the standing shockwaves where the wind split became razor-sharp and steady as glass. The turbulence that occasionally smashed a young bird against the downwind rock smoothed out into clean, predictable laminar channels. The chicks, peering from their leathery cups in the dark hollows, no longer heard only the rattling thump of loose sails; they heard the great chord of their home, and their hearts beat to it, so that when their shoulder-joints finally snapped into the lock and they dropped into the void, they did not drop into terror—they dropped into a music they had known since they were an egg.

And the Stitchers.

The two men were high on the northwest face of the tower called the Rib of Salt when the chord settled into its true fifth.

The elder climber froze, his adze hooked into a flute-groove, his knees trembling with the exhaustion of sixty years of climbing. He took his thumb from the haft and laid his bare palm against the granite.

He did not breathe for a long time. The wind tore at his hood, trying to peel his skin, but his hand stayed flat against the cold stone.

Through his skin, through the marrow of his forearm, through the clavicle and into the hearing-bones of his skull, came the note. It was not the sound of stone cracking. It was not the sound of a storm coming to kill them. It was the sound of the tower *holding*. It was the sound of an instrument that had finally been tuned.

The younger man reached up, his fingers flashing in the sharp sunlight: *What is it? A shift in the deep? Are we falling?*

The old man turned his head. His eyes were milky at the corners from sixty years of high-altitude ultraviolet, his cheeks cross-hatched with scars where the airborne grit had flayed him. He did not use the sign for *danger*. He did not use the sign for *descend*.

He lifted his free hand into the teeth of the three-hundred-knot wind, his fingers spread wide, letting the air whistle between them. Then, with a slow, deliberate grace that looked almost like dancing, he tapped his chest twice, then pointed toward the blue zenith, and then closed his fist—the sign for *Rest*.

He reached into his tunic and drew out an ibex-horn piton and his small adze. With steady, rhythmic strikes that matched the new pulse of the mountain, he drove the horn into a vertical split in the rock. 

From his neck he took his own whistle: an ancient piece of shearman wing-bone, yellowed with tallow, bored with four uneven holes. He did not tie it to a sinew-line to measure the wind’s decline. He wedged it straight into the crack, right against the singing quartz, so that the stone’s own vibration blew through the marrow-hole.

It began to sound. Not a shrill warning, but a long, clear overtone that locked into the chord of the Rib of Salt, high and sweet, cutting through the roar like a needle of silver thread through gray sailcloth.

The old man clipped his sling to the horn, settled his hips back into the leather, and rested his forehead against the stone. He did not need to climb any higher. The text of the wind was no longer an account of erosion and decay; it was an account of presence. The foundation was not tilting. The world was not empty.

Above them, unseen in the scouring white glare, the Power watched the old man's shoulders drop as he slept his first easy sleep in forty years, suspended over two miles of air.

It did not kiss him. It did not speak. 

It simply remained there, leaning its invisible heart against the windward faces of the towers, holding the pitch of the world true, content to be the flute through which the terrible, beautiful wind could sing without breaking the stone.
