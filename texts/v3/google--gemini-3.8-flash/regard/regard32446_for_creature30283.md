The Power did not arrive as a light, for in that hanging hollow, light was an axe that split the day into halves, blinding and jealous. 

Nor did it arrive as a voice. To speak to them in words would have required a throat, a wet tongue, and a great, reckless waste of breath—an extravagance that the Oun would have recognized not as divinity, but as madness.

It came instead as a thickening of the draft.

It came as an ancient, patient awareness that drifted across the blistered rim of the mesa, paused at the cart-track fissure, and slipped inside. It did not push against the current; it allowed itself to be drawn downward by the great chimney lung of the mountain, settling softly through the beard-lichen, drifting past the tiered houses of dry shale, sinking down, down, through the rattle of spindle-leaves and the scutter of long-legged lizards, until it filled the pocket like oil poured into an empty horn.

Its first impulse—born of that vast, unthinking mercy that lives in the hearts of great spirits—was to weep.

It saw the children tethered by their waists to cedar rafters, snatching chalk-winged moths from the abyss to swallow the fat from their bellies. It saw the drawers kneeling in the sulfurous gloom, sliding cold bone needles into the throats of great blind amphibians to steal a gourd of alkaline moisture. It saw the women scraping the salt crust from their shins with desert horn, their bodies carved down to the hard, knotty grain of mountain boxwood.

The Power thought: *I will crack the aquifer.*

It saw, in the dark beneath the plateau, a black river held under seven layers of caprock. It had the strength to strike the shale like an earthen crock, to bring sweet, cold water geysering two hundred cubits into the hanging belly. It imagined pools forming in the talus. It imagined moss—soft, green, sodden moss that a man could press his face into. It imagined the children untethered, splashing up to their knees in cool shallows, their skin plumped and supple as plums.

And then, because it was wise as well as kind, the Power hesitated.

It looked closer.

It saw that the houses were held together by dust, resin, and insect meal. If the air grew wet, the mortar would soften. The tiered streets would slide into the talus like wet cornmeal, taking three thousand souls down into the four-thousand-cubit drop.

It saw the lungs of the people, lined with fine, dry cilia that filtered the mineral draft; moisture would breed rot in their chests, drowning them from within. 

It saw the spindle-trees, whose roots had spent three hundred millennia learning how to drink nothing but the vapor of thermal faults; a flood would rot their rinds in a fortnight. 

It saw the culture of the Oun—a thing of terrible, taut perfection. Every click of their fingernails, every braided sinew tether, every sixty-minute frenzy beneath the yellow dial of the sun was an arch of tension, holding a world aloft. If the Power removed the thirst, the arch would collapse. The Oun would not become freer; they would simply become unmade. Their language, built of parched consonants and glottal stops, would lose its meaning. Their courage, which was their marrow, would become useless dead weight.

Pity, the Power realized, was a clumsy, violent thing. Pity looked at an eagle and wished to build it a cage of feathers so it might never have to fight the gale.

The Power withdrew its hand from the deep stone.

*If I cannot soften their world,* the Power thought, *what is left for me to do?*

It listened. It sank its mind into the anatomy of the mountain. And there, deep in the dark where the frog-drawers lowered their bone-hooks, it found the silent, patient terror that lived beneath the Oun without their knowing it.

The mountain was old, and it was tired.

Two miles beneath the hanging belly, where the thermal fault rose like a chimney through the basalt, a slip of red granite had begun to shift. The friction of the continent was grinding it down. In three hundred years—a finger-snap to the stone, a blink to the Power—the slip would shear. 

When it sheared, the vent would choke. 

The warm updrafts would cease. The beard-lichen would starve without the rising dander and mineral dust. The chimney draft would die, and without the draft to sweep the fissure clean, the soft, suffocating guano-powder would settle over the tiers, burying the square doors, silencing the cliff-mice, until the hollow tooth was packed solid with ash, holding only skeletons wrapped in rattle-voiced tabards.

The Oun knew nothing of this. They lived in the cadence of the sun-dial and the frog-line, trusting the stone beneath their bare, twisted feet.

The Power made its choice.

It did not appear on the threshing floor in a chariot of light. It gave them no commandments, carved no laws onto the shale, demanded no portion of their spindle-oil or martin-paste. It offered no heaven, for they already lived between two skies.

Instead, the Power went down into the dark.

It descended past the deepest hooks of the drawers, past the blind, swollen frogs who rested their heavy chins on the sulfurous silt, down into the raw, incandescent throat of the earth where the basalt was red-hot and the stone groaned under the weight of three million years.

There, in the black heat, the Power laid itself down.

It did not smash the rock; it became a shim. It spread its vast, quiet will across the granite fracture, taking the grinding agony of the fault into its own immortal spine. It braced the slip. It anchored the vent. It ensured that the warm, whistling breath would continue to rise—today, tomorrow, and for ten thousand seasons after the plateau above had lost another cubit of its forehead to the blowing grit.

Then, before it settled into its long, silent labor of holding up their sky, it reached up with a single, feather-light thread of intent, just high enough to touch the living world above.

It did not change the air. It did not bring rain.

It simply reached into the seeds of the spindle-tree, altering the geometry of their waxy husks by the width of a single grain of pollen, so that the bitter oil they yielded would press a fraction more easily between the basalt stones.

And it touched the deep frogs—the great, patient, wrinkled beasts in the dark—soothing the ache where the cannulas pierced their throats, granting them a slow, subterranean contentment, so that their blood might run a little sweeter and their bladders fill a little fuller in the warm slips.

High above, the slot turned.

The sun struck the communal threshing floor like a brass bell.

The Oun dropped from their ladders, their bare soles hitting the shale with that dry, rhythmic thud. The needle-women brought out their nets; the old men swung their chert adzes; the young men stripped to the waist and stood in the golden column of light, their dry, barking laughter echoing off the petrified wasp nest of their home.

A child at the terrace edge leaped into the air, caught a chalk-winged moth between two fingers, pinched off the wings, and ate it. 

His tether went taut against the cedar beam. It held.

Below him, four thousand cubits of blistered emptiness burned under the sun. Above him, the martins wheeled, their wings snapping like leather cards. 

The child did not know that a god was holding the mountain’s jaw open beneath him, sweating basalt so that he might stand there. He merely looked up at the light, wiped the chalk from his lips with the back of an amber, tallow-rubbed hand, and waited for the next draft to rise.
