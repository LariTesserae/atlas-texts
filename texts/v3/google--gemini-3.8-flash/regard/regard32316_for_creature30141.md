The first thing the power would bring is not light, and not rain. It would bring **slack**.

A wise power, looking upon an ocean of green clay and panicked fire, would understand instantly what was happening: this is not a torture chamber, and it is not a ruin. It is an apprentice’s workshop, operating in total, terrified isolation. 

The entity that drives the thumb does not want to destroy its cities; it simply does not know how to wait. It heats the floor because it fears the damp will rot the work; it builds high because it is lonely; it tears the towers down because the courses buckle before the lime can bite, and in its shame, it buries the failure beneath three thousand leagues of fresh slip. 

To break into this world with thunder or sudden greenery would be an act of cruelty. Cold rain would shatter every vitrified tile from thermal shock; a sudden wind would topple every unbraced tower, crushing the Tampers beneath millions of tons of steaming shale. A wise power does not smash the kiln.

A wise power steps into the workshop with its sleeves rolled up, moves softly so as not to crack the unfired ware, and begins to adjust the draft.

***

The change would begin miles above the sagging ceiling of ochre steam. 

The Power would not banish the steam—the steam is the only thing keeping the raw clay from turning to dust—but it would gather it. It would draw the copper mist upward, combing through the hot vapor with immense, slow fingers, organizing the turbulence into high, vaulted canopies of steady gray. For the first time in the world’s unmeasured infancy, the air would stop pressing down like a hot, wet mattress. A draft would stir: not a gale, but a gentle, draw-kiln breeze, moving at the pace of a sleeping breath, pulling the suffocating alkaline fumes up and away.

Then, it would attend to the creatures, because wisdom always looks first to the things that take the strain.

It would find a **Tensioner**, its fifty-foot cedar timbers bowed to the point of shrieking, its rawhide heart dripping bitter sap as it holds a leaning colonnade above an empty plaza. The Power would not snap the rope. It would not magically right the tower. 

Instead, an unseen, immense palm would cup the base of the falling masonry from behind, taking the load into its own weightless patience. 

The groan in the timber would soften. The bowed cedar spars would slowly, creakingly unbend. The greasy rawhide bindings, stretched to the verge of parting, would go slack with a wet, heavy sigh. The Tensioner, which had known nothing since its assembly but the agonizing terror of the snap, would feel the phantom strain evaporate from its joints. 

It would not tumble. It would simply settle its wooden elbows down into the brackets of the wall, tuck its long cedar forks against its ribs, and rest. For the first time, its hemp heart would stop twisting. It would hang there in the shadow of the colonnade, perfectly still, listening to the draft.

***

Next, it would go to the **Tampers**.

In a wide, milky depression where a forty-foot wall of green cobble is threatening to shear into a slurry, three Tampers are leaning their broad, leather-hard chests against the bulge, their square feet sliding in the boiling mud, their hollow ribs whistling with panicked steam. They are about to be buried. They know nothing else.

The Power would come down to their level, kneeling in the scalding slip.

It would not dissolve the wall, nor would it make it instantly stone. With a touch precise and quiet as a mason’s trowel, it would slide between the Tampers and the wet brick. It would press inward, not with concussive force, but with the even, loving pressure of a mold-board. It would nudge the bottom course three inches back into plumb. It would stroke the bubbling lime mortar until the boiling ceased, soothing the chemical fury into a cool, steady cure.

Then, it would introduce a shape the world has never seen: **the arch**.

With two fingers, the Power would gently press an opening into the base of the wall, lifting the wet stones upward into a gentle, self-supporting curve. The physics of the wall would suddenly resolve. The load would cease to fall outward; it would slide gracefully down the curve into the floor.

The Tampers would feel the wall pull away from them. Their chests, glued to the green clay, would come free with a quiet *shluck*. 

They would stand there, faceless and dumb, their blocky arms hanging by their sides. The wall does not fall. It does not slide. They step back—one concussive, sucking stride, then another. The wall stands. The straw chaff in their flanks stops bristling. Finding no panic to lean against, they would sink down into the shallow water, resting their square, heavy chins upon their knees, their clay flanks slowly cooling from fever-heat to the pleasant, sun-warmed temperature of a patio tile.

***

To the **Slip-Maws**, it would give boundaries. It would trace narrow, gently sloped trenches through the herringbone flags with an iron nail, drawing the scalding, milky water out of the sag-pools and into slow, wandering canals. The Maws would follow the flow, finding themselves gathered into deep, cool cisterns where the water is clear and the salt settles in peaceful, edible drifts, no longer chased by the killing bake from below.

To the **Clinkers**, it would give a hearth. It would gather those furious, clockwork scavengers with their scuttling porcelain feet, scoop them out of the ruins, and set them into deep niches carved into the foundations of the stable arches. It would feed them dry tamarisk root and slow-burning peat instead of raw mud, quenching the angry, white-hot draft in their peep-holes until their ceramic bellies glow with a mellow, cherry-red warmth. They would stop snapping at the legs of passing things. They would become lanterns, singing small, glassy songs into the quiet avenues.

***

And then, it would wait for the builder.

It would not have to wait long. The world’s floor would shudder. A mile out across the herringbone plain, the brickwork would part with a wet, heavy tear, and the **Thumb** would breach.

It would rise as it always does: thirty yards of pale, silt-smeared flesh, trembling with creative rage and desperate haste, swinging through the steam toward a fresh mound of boiling mud, ready to press, ready to stamp its swirling identity into the world before the world can cool.

The Power would not hide. It would not strike.

It would simply reach out its own hand—a hand made of cool, blue-gray slate, weathered smooth by rivers that ran before the first kiln was ever fired—and place it beneath the descending thumb.

Not to stop it, but to catch it.

The contact would produce no crack of thunder. Only the sound of two great weights coming together with a soft, clayey *thud*. 

The thumb would strain, pushing down with three hundred leagues of tectonic force, desperate to leave its mark, desperate to make something that stays. But the hand beneath it would not yield. It would not push back, either. It would simply hold the thumb in mid-air, absorbing its vast, clumsy heat into its own ancient cool.

For an hour, nothing would move. The thumb would twitch, confused, its silt-crusted skin pulsing with the rhythm of the subterranean fire. 

Then, the Power would begin to move its fingers. 

Gently, deliberately, like an old potter taking the wrist of a weeping child whose bowl has just collapsed upon the wheel, the Power would guide the thumb. 

It would not let it crush the mud into a flat, dead puddle. It would turn the thumb sideways. It would press it into the wet clay with a steady, rhythmic draw, showing it how to pull the material upward instead of tamping it down. It would teach the thumb the hollow. It would teach it that strength comes not from mass, but from the air inside the vessel; that a wall can be thin if it is true; that a floor does not need to cover an ocean if it can hold a single, quiet courtyard.

Beneath the touch of the elder hand, the frantic heat would slowly drain out of the thumb. The pale skin would stop sweating steam. The deep, violent tremors in the floor would subside into a long, low hum.

The thumb would hesitate. It would press once more—not with a violent, tearing heave, but with a tentative, delicate touch—leaving a single, clean indentation in the clay that does not shear, does not boil, and does not crack.

Then, slowly, like a child who has finally been told that it is time to sleep, the thumb would sink back into the herringbone pavement. 

The floor would close over it, not with a jagged ceramic fault, but with a soft, clean seam.

***

Night would fall. 

This world has never had a night. The Power would draw down the cooling canopies of steam until they rested like a thick, wool blanket over the plain, shutting out the glaring copper twilight. 

Beneath the blanket, the annealing would begin. 

The fires in the deep strata would not be extinguished—the world still needs its warmth—but they would be banked. The white-hot draft would drop to a sleepy, scarlet glow. The infinite brick floor, for the first time in its existence, would contract by a fraction of an inch, pulling its joints together into a tight, true bond.

In the dark, the only sound would be the soft, rhythmic clicking of the Clinkers sleeping in their niches, and the deep, contented breathing of the Tampers sitting in the arches. 

And far out on the plain, where the new canal runs clear over the terracotta flags, the water would cool just enough that a traveler, kneeling at the edge of the brick, could cup it in their hands, bring it to their lips, and drink without blistering.
