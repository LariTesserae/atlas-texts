The power that came to the sorting house had no face to press against the grating, nor did it carry the scent of rain, for it was wise enough to know that rain would have been an atrocity. 

A lesser god—one intoxicated by sap and green shoots, or sentimental about soft, wet things—would have wept into the pit. And those tears, falling down through the slotted steel, would have been a catastrophic poison. Water would have softened the waxy bloom upon the seeders’ shells; dampness would have bred molds to choke their revolving gizzards; the dry, satin-polished iron would have erupted into coarse scabs of orange rust, tearing the delicate sixty-legged bellies of the planires to ribbons. 

To love a thing truly is to love its terms. And this power, being old and clear-minded as an autumn sky, understood that the pit was not an exile. It was an altar built out of patience, friction, and dust.

It drifted down through the iron slots not as a body, but as an attention. 

It did not disrupt the cold, grey light. It cast no shadow to make the milky-eyed striders hesitate on their stilts. But it felt the whole of the place at once: the dry, wool-sack fever generated by the seeders; the rhythmic *tock* of the planires vacuuming themselves against the floor; the heavy, mineral weight of the ancient iron-plane beetles whose backs bore mountain ranges of hardened slag. It perceived the carpet not as a million desperate vermin, but as a single, deliberate life—a great, slate-colored animal forty paces long, breathing in unison every eight seconds, metabolizing iron and old tallow, asking nothing of the sun, and surviving with an austere, terrible dignity.

Yet the visitor saw what the blind inhabitants could not: it saw the joints of time.

It looked up, tracing the eight-second shudder back along the structural pillars, through the rotting floor-timbers of the upper sorting floor, and into the dark mezzanine above. 

There, suspended in the rafters like a dead iron heart, hung the central engine. It was running on its final marrow. The eccentric cam turned within a journal box that had worn so thin the brass had turned blue from dry heat; the steel shaft was scoring; the counterweight hung by four stretched bolts whose threads were stripping under the relentless centrifugal wrench. In six months—perhaps nine—a bolt would shear. The shaft would seize with a sound like a rifle shot. The cam would lock. The vibration that drove the striders’ shears, the cadence that righted the overturned plane-beetles, the rhythm that gave the floor its tide, would end forever. 

And after that, the grease stalactites would cool into unyielding stone. The chamber would turn from an engine into a tomb.

The power did not smash the roof to give them the sun. It did not speak a word of comfort to creatures that had no ears for language. Instead, it went to work with the cold, quiet reverence of a mechanic entering a darkened church.

High in the rafters, it reached into the journal box. 

It did not use magic to turn the steel into gold. It did simply what friction demands: it smoothed the crystalline fractures within the cold brass. It drew out the microscopic burrs on the shaft, realigning the iron atoms into a denser, self-healing lattice. It tightened the four bolts on the counterweight until they sang in true tension, and it laid a subtle, enduring temper upon the eccentric lobe, so that its dull, swinging weight would drop true—*clunk-shudder*—not for months, but for another three thousand years of indifferent winters.

Then it went to the reservoirs.

Deep inside the dead piping behind the walls, where the last tallow had dried to crumbly grey cakes, the power laid down a slow, cold pressure. It packed the old grease-cups with an inexhaustible vein of pure, paraffin-dense wax, blended with flaked graphite so slick it felt like cold mercury to the touch. It did not flood the walls; it tuned the weep. It ensured that from the high pillow blocks, one microscopic tear of tallow would form, chill, and harden every thirty-six hours—just enough to feed the rasping jaws of the plane-beetles, just enough to let their grubs swell with lymphatic marrow in the seams, just enough to keep the great grinding floor fed, but never drowned.

Lastly, the power descended once more to the floor of the pit.

It paused in the corner, where an ancient iron-plane beetle was undergoing the *hollowing*. 

The old creature had clamped its broad front spades into an iron seam, its forehead pressed to the cold rivet-line. Inside its shell, its life had already turned to chalk. The hoppers were already at work beneath the skirts of its elytra, methodically sweeping out the white, dry powder of its spent muscles with their rhythmic, bustling bodies. A strider stood perched atop its dorsal ridge, its long legs trembling in the drafts, waiting for the cavity to clear so it might deposit its amber seed.

The benevolent power did not restore the beetle’s life. To drag it back into its heavy, rasping existence would have been an insult to eighty years of honest scouring. 

Instead, the power leaned near and touched the empty hull.

Under that touch, the horn of the dead beetle did not rot; it hardened. Its outer shell took on the dense, crystalline hardness of flint, bound to the iron sill with an enduring grip that no shifting drift of hoppers could ever dislodge. The hollow chamber inside grew cool, smooth, and spacious—a cathedral vault of slate and horn, perfectly shielded from the crushing weight of the floor, a cradle fit to hold a hundred generations of amber eggs.

The work was finished. Nothing had been broken; nothing had been altered in its nature. The mercy was invisible.

The power withdrew, rising back through the slotted grating, leaving behind only the flat, cool daylight and the smell of pulverized slate. 

Down below, the cam turned on its restored brass journal. It fell with clean, heavy certainty: *clunk-shudder*.

The wave of sound traveled down the forty paces of satin-smooth metal. Millions of hard, waxy plates clicked in unison—*snick*—a papery hiss that ran like dry beans down a chute, striking the sump wall and rebounding softly into the cold air.

The planires rasped the floor to a mercury gleam. The striders walked their stilts across the rounded backs of the seeders. The grubs slept in the seams, heavy with grease. None of them looked up. None of them thanked the ceiling. They simply went on living inside their great, dry clock, beautiful and terrible and whole, while the machine that forgot to stop kept time for them in the dark.
