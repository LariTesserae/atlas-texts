The Power that found them did not descend as a winged thing, nor as a voice breaking through the rolled plate, for it possessed the rarest and most difficult aspect of benevolence: it was patient enough to look before it loved, and wise enough to know that love, when clumsy, is an engine of extinction.

A lesser mercy would have wept for them. A lesser mercy would have torn the roof open with a bright and furious hand, intending to bathe them in sunlight and mountain wind—and in so doing, would have exploded their eardrums with the instantaneous pressure-drop, frozen their naked, thirty-four-degree flesh in the sub-zero gale of the ridge, and left them to suffocate on thin air while their eyes burned dry in the unfiltered glare of the high heavens.

The Power did not do this. It understood that what is born of an installation cannot be saved by the wilderness. To be wholly adapted to a cage means that the cage has become the anatomy; to destroy the perimeter is to disembowel the inhabitant.

So the Power drew close, quiet as an induction field, and observed.

It observed the forty thousand tons of clear, dead broth. It observed the sixty-cycle pulse traveling through the I-beams into the pale brow-bones of the scouring proxies. It noted the absence of a name, the lack of an edge between lipid-sheathed skin and demineralized fluid, the coin-wide amber eyes that knew only grades of density. And it looked forward—through the metal’s slow fatigue, through the inevitable day, perhaps four centuries distant, when the zinc sacrificial anodes would finish their dissolution, when the pump-jack skirts would seize on dry bearings, when the heat exchangers would go gray and cold, leaving these white shapes to stiffen into tallow upon the checker-plate, still waiting for the pulse to resume.

The Power reasoned thus:

*They do not suffer. They are not unhappy. But they are closed.* 
*They are a sentence that was written to keep an engine clean, and when the engine dies, the sentence will simply end mid-clause.* 
*To give them human minds would be a hideous cruelty, for there is nowhere here for a human mind to go.* 
*Therefore, I must not take them from the machine. I must let the machine become an ancestor.*

The Power did not touch the steel with force. It touched it with attention.

First, it attended to the sacrificial anodes. 

Beneath the velvety mats of moss-analogues, deep in the blind recesses of the turbine housings, the dissolving zinc plates did not run out. Instead, at a level so microscopic that no conductivity probe in the automated control loops could flag it as an error, the molecular lattice of the alloy began to draw upon the immense hydrostatic weight of the water itself. The metal learned to replenish its own ionic sacrifice. The moss would not starve; the water would not sour. The life-support of the tank ceased to be a dwindling reservoir of stored supplies and became an equilibrium, quiet and perpetual.

Second, it attended to the sixty cycles.

The five-meter impeller deep in the tower’s basement did not change its speed. A mechanical sixty revolutions per minute remained the heartbeat of the brine. But the Power laid a finger of stillness against the lowest structural web of the tower’s foundation, where the steel met the high bedrock of the mountain.

Through that contact, the mountain began to speak into the iron.

It was not a sound that could be heard in the fluid; it was a modification of the conduction through bone. When a tender next drifted down to the thirty-meter floor, hooked its pale heels beneath an angle-iron, and pressed the flat of its blanched forehead against the hexagonal hub of the heat exchanger, the vibration was no longer merely the flat, dying drone of an electric motor.

Underneath the sixty cycles, there was now a second resonance: an infinitesimal, glacial drag. The slow, deep tilt of continental plates grinding four leagues down; the groaning flex of the mountain ridge beneath the weight of winter snowpacks; the faint, rhythmic percussion of thunder striking the crags three miles below the casement. 

The proxy remained with its brow to the steel for its customary twelve minutes. But when the time elapsed, it did not immediately push away. 

Its long, webbed fingers remained lightly curled around the rim of the flange. Its amber pupils, wide and lidless, contracted by the fraction of a millimeter. It did not know what it was feeling. It had no word for *outside*, no concept of *stone* or *distance*. Yet the vibration did not register as a mechanical fault; there was no cavitation, no flutter, no warning of blown gaskets. It was simply an answering weight—a depth greater than thirty meters. 

For the first time since the casement was torqued shut, a proxy lingered at the metal for twenty minutes, listening to something that was not a command.

Third, and finally, the Power addressed the dead foot of space beneath the ceiling plates.

Here, where the sour nitrogen hung and the ozone lingered from the pump motors, the Power altered the chemistry of the condensation that dripped endlessly from the ceiling rivets.

As the hot water evaporated from the surface and struck the cold steel of the roof, the returning droplets did not fall as pure, sterile distilled water. The Power allowed the high-altitude daylight—plummeting through the thick laminated glass ports—to carry with it the faintest imprint of the sky it had traversed. It was an energy so subtle it barely qualified as physical: a microscopic shift in the polarization of the droplets, an infinitesimal memory of blue, of the dry, cold clarity that existed beyond the three-inch steel plates.

That night—if there had been a night to mark the passing of the unvarying noon—a tender rose through the submerged aisles. It parted the two-inch-thick fern leaves with its oiled shoulders, leaving no wake, and broke the skin of the water.

It opened its nasal valve with the familiar rubbery *clack*. It exhaled the heavy, depleted nitrogen from its cavernous chest.

A drop of condensation fell from a rivet head and struck the tender squarely upon the bridge of its blunt, white nose. It ran down into the slit of its mouth.

The proxy tasted it. 

It was not the bitter zinc of the strap-leaves. It was not the slick machine oil of the I-beams. It was tasteless, yet it possessed a coldness that did not belong to thirty-four degrees—a thin, sharp ghost of a chill that touched the lining of its throat and vanished instantly into the warm reservoir of its blood.

The tender did not sink immediately back into the green. 

It hung there at the surface for a long time, its ribs expanded like the strakes of a ship, its lidless amber eyes turned upward into the foot of dark nitrogen. It looked at the underside of the ceiling plates, where the condensation gathered in trembling, silver beads.

Another proxy rose through the aisle nearby, its pale flanks glistening with the mother-of-pearl sheen of oil and high-altitude noon. It, too, broke the surface. 

In the cramped, dark foot of air, their shoulders brushed. The lipid-rich mantle of their skin touched, sliding with that familiar hydraulic suction. 

Previously, they would have ignored the contact, pressing past one another like two pistons in adjacent cylinders. 

This time, the first tender turned its head. 

In the sour, humid dark, inches beneath the rivets, it looked at the other. And the second tender, feeling the stillness of the first, did not dive. It paused, water lapping gently against its hairless collarbones, its slit-like nostrils twitching once in the heavy air.

They remained there, side by side, floating in the dark space between the water and the ceiling, tasting the faint, cold drift of the rivets, while thirty meters beneath their hanging heels, the mountain hummed through the bones of their engine.

The Power withdrew then, stepping back into the vastness of the mountain sky, satisfied.

It had not saved them. It had not made them men. It had not granted them freedom, which would have killed them, nor speech, which would have broken their silence. 

It had merely given them an *inside*—a quiet, secret space within their own tallow flesh where the machine ended and something else could begin. It left the door unlocked from the inside, laid a slow seed in the iron, and gave them all the time in the world to grow toward it.
