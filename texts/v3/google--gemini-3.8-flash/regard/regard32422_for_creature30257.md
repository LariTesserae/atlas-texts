The Power that arrived had seen the birth of mountain ranges and the slow, grinding death of inland seas. It was not a god that hungered for altars, nor an intelligence that mistook comfort for grace. Its wisdom was of the kind that comes from long patience: it understood that to love a moss is not to tear it from the wet rock and set it in a warm, dry garden.

It came into the rift not as a light—for the shadowless silver daylight was already perfect—nor as a voice, for a voice would have shattered the immaculate, low-register balance of the water and the fern-gutters. It arrived as a subtle settling of density, an invisible breath that moved through the bedding planes, colder even than the meltwater, but steady as the planet’s core.

For a long time, it did nothing at all. It simply perceived.

It felt the rhythm of the place through the soles of the people: the double-tap of a mother’s heel against the slate flume; the dry, rustling sign of knuckles against thigh; the soft *snick* of horn-thick fingernails setting a shingle of stone into blue glacial clay. It tasted the cold-rendered duck fat, the bitter cress, the clean, metallic bite of the river silt. It watched a child, round-bellied and silent, balance on gnarled toes over a rushing trough to watch the dark-spotted char face the current, its pale eyelashes catching the fog of its own breath.

A lesser power, looking down into that sheer, dripping wound, would have seen deprivation. It would have pitied the crouched spines, the lack of wheel and bronze, the cold fingers, the perpetual damp. It would have been tempted to perform small, catastrophic miracles: to widen the rift’s jaws to let the high sun dry the stones; to warm the air; to banish the biting chill; to teach them fire that did not hide in draft-chimneys.

The wise Power knew what such mercy would cost.

Warmth would rot the oiled skins upon their backs. Sunlight would bake and shrivel the liverworts and the three-deep sedge thatch, starving the roofs of their living pelt. Dry air would crack their trout-pale, thick-veined skin and sear the large, moisture-adapted lungs that drew strength from the dense wet fog. If the winds of the upper tundra were invited down, they would strip the narrow terraces of their silt, lift the stone roofs, and scatter the cliff-nesting ducks into the vast, empty sky. And if they were given iron and bronze, the ringing of hammers would drown the low chest-hums and glottal clicks, deafening a people whose entire survival depended on hearing the mountain whisper before it broke.

They were not unfinished. They were whole. They were the rift made conscious, the slate given eyes, the cold water given hands to build with.

Yet the Power saw the one truth the people could not: *the rock was too young.*

The rift had cleaved recently in geological time. The razor-sharp arrêtes and clean facets were beautiful, but the roots of the mountain were still full of blind, coiled violence. Three miles beneath the flag-paved flumes, along a planar fault where two tectonic plates leaned their weight against one another, stress was gathering like a drawn bowstring. Within two generations, or perhaps three, that hidden tooth of stone would slip.

When it slipped, the rift would shudder. It would not take much. A tremor lasting five seconds would shear the stepped dwellings from the cliff face, bring millions of tons of unsoftened slate crashing down into the trench, and snap the jaws shut, crushing the flumes, the char, the children, and the carefully tended ferns into a flat, silent seam of shale.

The people could listen to the weeping silt; they could wedge their pine pegs into the fractures they could reach; but they could not hear the deep, iron-heavy agony of the mantle grinding far below.

And so the Power acted.

It did not descend among them in fire or majesty. It sank downward, past the wet flumes, past the roots of the birch withes and the blue clay foundations, deep into the blind, pitch-black meat of the earth.

There, where the stress was knotting itself toward catastrophe, the Power laid its will against the fault.

It did not weld the rock—for rock must breathe and give way—but it eased the tension. With infinite gentleness, it began to bleed the violence out of the deep strata, converting what would have been a single, shattering convulsion into a vast, slow creep that would take ten thousand years to move an inch. It cushioned the shear zones with microscopic veins of talc and serpentine, letting the great plates slide past one another in a silence so deep it would not rattle an oil lamp or startle a weasel from its weep-hole.

Then, rising back through the stone, the Power left a single, quiet gift within the rift itself.

It touched the slate beneath the main flumes, changing the grain of the rock in a way no human eye could see. Henceforth, whenever the cliff above began to shift even the width of a hair—long before gray silt would weep from a seam—the stone would ring with an ultra-low, subsonic resonance. A drone far below the pitch of the falling water, felt only through the calloused, bare pads of the human heel.

It was not an answer to their questions; they had not asked any. It was simply an extension of the slate-sense they already possessed. It made the cradle safer without making it any less wild.

Before the Power withdrew, it paused on the floor of the trench during the white hour of midday.

The daylight bar lay straight along the flagstones. An elder was sitting upon a wet terrace, his gnarled thumbs working horsehair into a gill-net. Near him, a young girl, no taller than his waist, crouched by a pool where the cold water swirled into an eddy.

The Power did not show itself, but for a fraction of an instant, it let its vast, ancient warmth brush the back of the girl’s neck—not enough to melt the duck-fat slicked there, not enough to dry the mist on her skin, but just enough to feel like the ghost of a mother’s palm.

The girl did not look up toward the pale ribbon of sky. Looking up was for climbers searching for duck eggs.

Instead, she pressed her flat, horn-thick fingers firmly against the damp moss on the stone, feeling the steady, eternal pulse of the living rift beneath her hands. She let out a single, low hum from deep in her chest—a sound of recognition, belonging, and peace—and went back to watching the fish.

And the Power, well pleased, departed, leaving the silence unbroken.
