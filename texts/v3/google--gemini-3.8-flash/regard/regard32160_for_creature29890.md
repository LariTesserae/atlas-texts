A power that is both wise and benevolent would possess two qualities fatal to standard miracles: an infinite capacity for restraint, and an absolute lack of vanity.

It would not bring light. Light here would be an atrocity—an abrasive blaze upon thirty thousand pairs of vestigial, lidless eyes, a heat-spike that would melt the grease-glaze, trigger frantic uncouplings, and send forty tons of suffocating tallow crashing into the moraine below. 

It would not bring warmth. Warmth would spoil the meat-soup delivered by the jet stream, sour the delicate crust of lithophytic yeast, and rot the breathing-slits of the lower tiers before the nits could scrape them clear.

And it would not grant them open skies, or limbs, or freedom. To take a *kivva* from the crack and set it upon a sunny scree would be like dragging a deep-sea siphonophore into the midday wind; it would rupture under the unbearable lightness of being unpressed. Its holiness, if the word has any meaning at all, is inseparable from the vice.

So the power would come not as an angel, nor as an engine, but as an *adjustment of attention*—a presence that enters the cleft with the specific mass and temperature of cold syenite, settling into the spaces between the rock and the back-plates without displacing a single ounce of air.

***

First, it would understand what it was looking at.

A lesser benevolence, seeing the skimmer drive its whalebone needle into the flank of a gravid female to drink her jelly, would strike the bird down. But the wise power would see the geometry of the rift: that the kivva breed with an ancient, blind fecundity suited to an ocean, not a slot; that without the skimmer’s silent, methodical cull, the fissure would choke itself on flesh within three generations. The bodies would heap so high the upper ranks would be shoved out into the razor-edge of the jet stream to freeze solid, while the bottom would turn to a stagnant, airless mulch where neither worm nor nit could breathe. 

The skimmer is not a thief; it is the chimney-sweep of their lineage. The power would let the bird work. It would watch the long, curved bill slide between the third and fourth ribs, feel the tiny, pulse-free quickening of the jelly-sacs, and allow the horn to draw them out. It would not sweeten the kivva’s blood, nor dull an agony that does not exist. It would honor the fact that the kivva has traded the luxury of pain for the dignity of endurance.

***

Where, then, does love go, if it cannot change the knife?

It would find the points where the world’s machinery is simply crude, careless, and wasteful without purpose.

The power would lower its awareness down through the damp, reeking thirty feet of layered ribs, past the blind mills of parsnip-pale nits, past the cold tallow-floor of the sump, down into the bedrock itself.

There, in the dark roots of the horn-ridge, it would find the fault. 

The syenite is closing. Half an inch a decade. The continental plate does not care about the lung in the horn; it cares only about the mantle’s convection, the stupendous, illiterate shove of crust against crust. In four hundred years, the throat will be a seam; the sixty tons of meat will be squeezed out of the top like marrow from a crushed reed, frozen, chipped away by the gale, and forgotten.

Benevolence would set its heel upon that creeping edge.

Not with a thunderclap—a sudden stop would shatter the brittle horn into gravel—but by soaking into the fault planes, lubricating the deep slip-faces, converting the jagged shear-stress into a minute, lateral torsion that steers the mountain’s pressure around the cleft rather than through it. It would anchor the two glass-keen walls of the fracture at a permanent, unvarying distance: *four paces across, fifty paces long.* It would grant the throat the one thing the indifferent earth denies it: *stability.*

***

Then, it would attend to the rhythm.

The power would listen to the desynchronized piston-gasp. It would feel how the air, sliding over the top lip of the ridge, creates turbulent eddies that occasionally starve the lowest tier of oxygen, causing the elderly at the gravel-line to exhaust their throat-sacs, slip from their wedges, and drop into the grease before their time.

The power would not change the wind. It would simply lay a gentle, invisible hand against the outward lip of the cleft, wearing down three inches of razor-syenite over twenty years—a subtle contouring of the intake horn. A micro-aerodynamic prayer. 

With that slight curve in place, the windward gale would no longer sheer across the gap; it would roll downward in a continuous, smooth, toroidal loop. The frozen soup of midges and spores would be carried deeper, past the top third, settling evenly across all ten thousand mouths. The lower kivva would no longer starve on the washings of the upper; the harvest would be democratic, cold, and constant.

***

And finally, it would do the hardest thing a god can do:

It would become an elk-horn spike.

Benevolence knows that the most terrible thing about the cleft is not the cold, nor the grease, nor the parasites—these are merely the conditions of their life. The terror, to an intelligence watching from outside, is the total, crushing *unwitnessedness* of it. Sixty tons of warm blood that has held the sky away from the stone for five centuries, with nothing to know that it was ever there except the slate nicks of men who died when the valley below was forest.

The power would enter the dead horn driven into the syenite floor.

It would not make the horn live again; it would not make the red lichen shine brighter. It would simply reside within the porous, tallow-soaked core of the bone, feeling the dull, wooden *clack* of skulls fifty feet above, feeling the slow *whuff* of thirty-second lungs, tasting the bitter ammonia and the mineral flour of the sloughing mountain.

It would be the floor that does not forget the bodies that fall to it.

Once every thirty years, when the great tremor comes and the kivva are shaken from their anchorings, falling in a blind, soft tangle of ribs and fat into the dark, the power within the horn would not lift them back to the walls.

It would simply bear their weight without flinching. It would hold the cold under them until their amber-tipped ribs found the leathery hides of their kin, until the slow, blind knitting began again, breath by desynchronized breath, in the greasy dark that belongs to them, and to them alone.
