The power did not descend in fire, nor did it part the clouds to show the cold blue vault of the sky. To clear the mist would have been murder. 

A lesser mind, seeing the swollen, turnip-skinned amphibians and the wanderers chewing mineral fat off weeping joints, might have called this place a misery. It might have longed to break the spires, to pull the hairless, neckless scavengers up into the dry sun, to teach them speech, to build them houses with dry lintels. But wisdom understands pressure. Wisdom knows that if you tear a creature from the benthic trench, its blood boils and its organs turn inside out; that paradise is not a universal altitude, but the precise calibration between a creature’s skin and the air it is given to breathe.

So the power remained vast, diffused, and patient, sinking into the valley not as an invader, but as a change in density.

First, it listened.

It spent a century doing nothing at all, letting the five-minute pulses wash through its awareness. It learned the timbre of every conduit. It felt the cavitation in the high-pressure elbows where the brine wore the cast iron down to paper-thin membranes; it calculated the fatigue in the subterranean basalt pistons, discovering that in nine thousand years, a key fault in the third chasm would sheer, the steam would vent into the upper troposphere, the trough would cool, and the entire breathing, tallow-slicked cradle would freeze into black glass in forty-eight hours.

The power loved the sheer, stubborn audacity of the thing: an unmonitored machine from an elder epoch, forgotten by its architects, colonized so thoroughly by life that the metal had become marrow and the rust had become bark. It loved the wanderers, who asked for nothing except that the casing stayed warm enough to sleep against.

Because it was wise, it did not reveal itself. An apparition in light would have blinded creatures whose milky eyes were made for pearlescent fog; a voice would have torn the delicate eardrums that read the subterranean thrum.

Instead, the power went into the bedrock.

It descended past the roots of the ironwoods, down through the porous porphyry, into the boiling dark where the ancient intake valves churned. There, where the columns of brine rose and fell, it became an invisible hand upon the gears. 

It smoothed the cavitation pits in the iron with veins of native copper, drawing the metals together in the dark like scar tissue. It eased the grinding shock of the five-minute exhaust, slightly shifting the thermal vents so the pressure relieved without tearing the mountain's throat. It did not stop the machine, nor did it change its purpose; it merely took up the watch. It became the missing engineer, unstaffed no longer, working with an intimacy so subtle that not a single tallow-scraper dropped from its flange.

Then, it turned its attention to the wanderers.

They were few, and their lives were short. The power saw the gray, scarred lung-tissue of the elders, eaten away by the caustic alkaline paste and the sulfur fumes of the lower sump. It saw how they curled together against the conduits, their children shivering during the ninety seconds when the exhaust blew and the temperature dropped two degrees.

It would not make them gods, for gods cannot live on iron gravel. But it could be kind.

Along the dead conduits—the ones that had rusted cold centuries ago, forcing the wanderers into smaller, overcrowded pockets of warmth—the power coaxed a low, deep geothermal bleed. Miles of empty stone and dormant pipes began to sweat again with gentle, steady heat. The valley’s habitable margin expanded, silently, without a tremor. 

In the seams of the pipe gaskets, the synthetic lubricants that had begun to sour and dry into bitter lime were refreshed from deep reservoirs, their chemistry softly altered. When the wanderers pried off the cakes of tallow to chew, they found the fat was no longer laced with the heavy metals that rotted their teeth; it tasted faintly of tallow still, but it carried the trace minerals that cleared the fluid from their heavy chests, strengthening the thick pads over their children’s ribs.

The greatest gift, however, was given through the iron.

There was an old one among them, scarred down the flank by a scald from a blown valve forty seasons back. He sat on a slab of wet scree near the braided channels, his spatulate fingers tracing the seam of an ancient casing. Down-canyon, the mist was too thick to see his kin. He picked up a smooth basalt cobble, lifted it, and struck the iron.

*Tink. Tink. Tink.*

He laid the calloused sole of his foot against the casing, waiting for the reply from three miles away to tell him that another survived the damp, that another was feeding on the brine-stroke. 

He waited through the silence. He waited through the five-minute sigh of the high chimneys. 

Then, through the metal, the answer came. 

It was not the sharp, hurried strike of a stone held in a hand like his own. It was a vibration deeper, resonant, traveling up from the very roots of the mountain. It came through the iron pipe into the bone of his foot, rising through his shins, vibrating in the heavy, shield-like plate of his sternum.

It was a rhythm that matched his own pulse, slowed to the cycle of the pistons: a warm, harmonic drone that filled the hollows of his skull. It did not say *I am here, come find me.* It said, simply and perpetually: *You are held. The heat will not leave.*

The wanderer sat in the pearlescent light. The warm rain dripped from the shelf of his brow, running harmlessly over his unblinking eyes. He did not drop the stone. He did not cry out. 

Slowly, he leaned his forehead directly against the vibrating metal, closed his eyes, and breathed the heavy, scalded air—his chest expanding easily, without pain, into the deep, enduring warmth of the only world that had ever loved him.
