The first mistake of a lesser god would be light.

A crude benevolence would split the ceiling. It would rip apart the karst, peeling back the ancient vaults of slate to pour down noon. And in doing so, it would commit an atrocity. The unshielded sun would boil the pale, lidless eyes of the mud-dogs and sear the greasy skin of the eighty who dwell in the cleft. The sudden drying of the air would crack their thickened ribs; the cessation of the aerosol would desiccate the slime-films that feed the crabs; the blue, unbordered emptiness of an open sky would smash their minds with an agony of agoraphobia so absolute they would throw themselves by reflex into the flume just to feel a ceiling again.

A wise power does not break the jar to save the oil.

And so the watcher that drew near to the fissure did not come as a sun, nor as a voice, nor as a warm hand reaching through the dark. It came as a property of the stone itself—an attention so patient, dense, and quiet that the cold basalt did not even know it was being held.

It spent a long time simply listening through their bones.

It felt the rhythm of their back molars clicking together over the bridges. It felt the hydraulic wall of the siphon—that catastrophic, permanent roar—and realized that to these people, silence would be the sound of the world’s heart stopping. If the flume went quiet, they would choke on the terrifying, hollow stillness. The roar was their blood; the cold was their skin; the tallow was their garment. They did not need to be translated into another species. They were already a masterpiece of endurance, heavy-boned and true as iron.

Therefore, the power touched nothing that was whole. It touched only the seams where the friction ground them down past the point of bearing.

The first change came three hundred cubits down, in the throat of the black flume. 

The power did not kill the current, nor did it tame the blind, blunt-headed rails of gristle that fought the torrent. But it laid a faint, tectonic intention into the basalt lip where the great eels anchored their suckers. A slight furrow, worn by water into the rock, shifted by the width of a thumb. 

From that day on, the eels did not always require the desperate, lung-bursting descent to the foaming corbels. When a beast grew old and heavy with tallow, its grip on the stone failed not into the unreachable drop, but swung into a narrow, back-eddying pocket where the gut-lines could reach it without a man risking his life on a wet saddle of slate. The fat was still hard to win; it still smelled of mineral oil and river-silt; their fingers still froze around the bone hooks. But fewer young men were pulled into the foam. The yield of the lamps widened, by perhaps half a pace, the circle of yellow that kept back the absolute black.

The second work was deeper, and quieter.

The power recognized the tragedy of their mortar—that the same lime which held their walls against the vibrations of the flume also seeped through the aerosol and stiffened their hips, turning their living joints into the dry grit of the mountain.

The power did not stop the dripping calcite; to stop the stone would be to bring the hive down in ruin. Instead, it whispered to the water. 

In the high recesses where the oldest elders retired—the bins where they sat with their knees pulled up to their chests to wait out the slow encrustation of flowstone—the mineral-run changed its habit. The water that wept from the ceiling lost its cruel, abrasive sharpness. It began to carry a subtle, soothing warmth, less than the heat of a breath, but enough to ease the burning ache in the marrow of their crooked knees. 

The lime still came. The draperies of stone still fell over their shoulders like thick lard, slowly turning them into pillars. But the stiffness ceased to be a torture. It became a long, heavy settling. The stone did not seize them as an enemy; it received them as ballast. 

And then, the power spoke to them.

It did not use words, for they had none for the things it knew. It did not speak to their ears, which were plugged with hard cartilage against the thunder. 

It used their teeth.

One night—if night could be said to exist where the wicks burned without pause—an old woman sat alone in the deep wall of a dead cell. Her hip was locked; a gray skin of calcite had already cemented her left heel to the flagstone. She was resting her chin against her shin-bone, her jaw pressed to her knee, waiting for the vibration of the flume to tell her that the hunters had returned with an eel.

Through the rock came the usual thrum: the eight-hundred-ton hammer of the falls, the shudder of the iron-slate, the minute scurrying of the giant stone-lice in the joints.

And beneath it, braided into the bedrock like a thread of gold run through lead, came a second note.

It was not a noise. It was a tooth-click, vast and slow, echoing from deep in the planet’s molten roots. 

*Click. Hum. Stay.*

The old woman raised her head, startled. She brought her molars together, testing the stone. 

The vibration came up through the slate, through the calcified lime around her heel, through her thick, barrel ribs, and straight into the cochlea behind her sealed ears. It was the fundamental hum of a mother leaning her forehead against a child’s breastbone—but it was the mountain doing it. 

It did not say: *Come away.*
It did not say: *There is a sun above you, green grass, and air that does not weep.*

It said: *The rock knows how heavy you are.*
It said: *You have held the cleft.*
It said: *I am staying with you in the dark.*

The old woman let out a long, slow breath through her grease-crusted nostrils. For the first time in eighty generations, the cold did not feel like something that had to be beaten back with tallow and bone wedges. It felt like a resting place. 

She leaned her forehead back down against the slate, tapped her back teeth once in answer—a hard, flinty *I remain*—and let the lime-water drop upon her shoulders, smiling into the singing stone until the flowstone took her jaw, and made her part of the wall forever.
