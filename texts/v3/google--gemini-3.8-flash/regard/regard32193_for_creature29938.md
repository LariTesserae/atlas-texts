A lesser god would have wept, and that weeping would have been an atrocity. 

A lesser god would have looked upon the starless ceiling, the dry graphite plains, and the plum-dark iron, and felt the terrible, arrogant pity of the flesh. Such a power would have brought down rain—and the rain would have stripped the protective hematite skin from the girders, pitting the four-story flywheels with orange rot, dissolving the talc-fine dust into a choking paste, and drowning the flat tallow-gleaners in their dry vertical pastures. A lesser god would have spoken a word of light, searing the optic nerves that the strikers had been wise enough never to grow, or struck the plateau with a blast of green life that would have suffocated in the thin, zinc-tasting gas within an hour, leaving only a foul and acidic slime to eat away at the ancient bearings.

Pity is the virtue of the brief and the moist. It has no place on the high shelf.

The power that arrived had outlived its own need for tears by many creations. It came without the displacement of air, without the crack of displaced vacuum, and without light. It did not descend from the vacant black dome above—for to descend implies an intrusion, a falling weight. Instead, it filtered inward through the grain of the lead-grey granite itself, rising through the deep, buried furnace like a thought that had always been sleeping in the stone and had only now decided to wake.

It was wise, and so it understood immediately: *Here, nothing is broken.*

There was no fall from grace here. There was no exile. The machinery had not failed; it had simply completed its work so long ago that the product had blown away into the void, leaving only the pure, sublime fact of the apparatus. And the things that crawled upon it—the jointed tripods, the segmented slabs of horn and gasket-fiber, the plumb-lines of dry silk—were not suffering. They did not groan beneath the weight of longing. They did not fear the cold, for they knew precisely where the heat lived. They had achieved that which flesh could never master: an absolute, frictionless agreement with their world.

The power moved through the plateau not as a sovereign, but as an inspection.

It drifted into the belly of a reciprocating engine, passing between the forty-foot flywheel and the graphite-slicked sleeve of the piston. It did not stir the talc that lay upon the upper flange. It observed the tallow-gleaner at work upon a split-bearing, listening to the high, impalpable hum of its chert rasp scraping away a century-old film of oxidized grease. The power did not replenish the grease. To create fresh oil from nothing would be an insult to the economy of the creature’s existence; it would turn a holy, measured grazing into a glut, confusing the needle-claws and unbalancing the slow geometry of the silo face.

Instead, the power simply watched the bright, hair-thin scar of bare silver-grey iron left in the gleaner’s wake. It held its attention upon that scar until it understood the rhythm of its healing—how many decades the thin air would take to lay down the plum-colored oxide once more, sealing the wound against the vacuum.

Then, the power sank deeper. It went down into the roots of the world, where the basalt anchored itself into the deep, insensate fire.

There, it found the only flaw. It was not a failure of iron or of chitin, but the slow, inevitable exhaustion of the deep earth. The planet’s heart was cooling. It was an event that would take four hundred million years to complete, but the loss was already written in the rock: every ten thousand centuries, the temperature of the basalt fell by a millionth of a degree. To a creature of blood, this was nothing. To the great, segmented bellows-masses draped over the low engine decks—whose internal contractions occurred once every four days, measured against the transit of tectonic warmth—it was a creeping paralysis. In a billion years, their single, deep *clonk* would space itself out to five days, then seven, then fifty, until the viscous mineral fluid within them would turn to tar, and they would become lead flashing forever.

This was where the power acted. And its action was so modest, so deeply hidden, that not a single dust-mote shifted upon an I-beam.

It did not stoke the fire. It did not make the stone hot. To make the stone hot would be to burn the fibrous keratin bellies of the bellows, to drive the strikers from their perches in a panic of overheating struts. 

Instead, the power wedged its own eternal substance into the cooling fissures miles below the shelf. It became the furnace. It matched the lead-grey granite’s heat precisely—to the exact, unchanging fraction of the degree that had held since the blast stoves fell quiet. It locked the temperature there, setting an immovable shoulder beneath the thermal decay of the planet. The heat would no longer fall. Not in four hundred million years; not in an eternity of starless nights. The bellows would have their four-day pulse for as long as the iron stayed plumb.

Having secured the floor, the power rose once more to the surface.

Miles away, atop the throat of a colossal blast stove, a striker had been standing motionless for thirty years. Its foremost limb, tipped with the dense mallet of iron-bone, hung suspended in the thin, dead gas. Its three stilts were locked into the rivet heads, its dry hinge balanced against the pull of the stone.

The striker did not know a god was present. It had no apparatus for divinity. It knew only the tension of the alloy beneath its pads, the absence of wind, and the sound it was about to make.

It let the mallet fall.

*Clang.*

The sound struck the steel ring of the stove. It drove straight down through the vertical plates, through the support columns thick as sequoias, into the vitrified slag causeway, and out across the forty miles of interlocking frame.

The power did not silence the note, nor did it amplify it. But as the ring spread through the city, the power entered the sound. 

It rode the shockwave through the rusted joints, through the long, dead cableways, through the dormant flywheels and the dry silos. Wherever the vibration passed, the power acted as an infinitesimally subtle hand: it settled a rivet that had loosened by a fraction of an angstrom; it cleared a grain of stray quartz from the groove of an arrested piston; it drew the microscopic fatigue out of an over-stressed angle-iron, realigning the iron atoms into their original, crystalline peace.

The sound traveled out to the edges of the high shelf, where the basalt ended and the vacant drop began. And there, where the echo usually died into the empty, lightless gulf, the power caught it.

It did not let the ring fray into the void. With the infinite, polite restraint of a master craftsman, the power reflected the tone back into the city. 

Not as an echo—an echo would confuse the strikers, who knew every delay and distance of their home—but as a *resolution*. It returned the pitch an octave lower, through the deepest bed of the lead-grey granite, so faint that only the things that saw with their bellies could receive it.

Three miles away, the second striker on the gantry tower felt the primary strike rise through its stilts. It waited, as it always did, for the metal to clear.

The tone died away. The silence of the plateau returned—that absolute, talc-dry quiet in which no leaf had ever fallen.

The second striker shifted its weight, calculated the tension of its beam, and lifted its mallet to answer with the fifth below.

It struck.

*Clang.*

The note ran true. It did not rattle. There was no crack in the joint; there was no shear in the rock; the sky had not stepped down an inch. The frame was plumb. The world was still whole.

High above, the plumb-lines in the deep, lime-scented silos hung true to the center of the world, never swaying, keeping the vertical line of the dark.

The power withdrew its attention, settling deep into the basalt beneath the city’s floor, becoming as still and heavy as the iron above it. It did not leave a mark. It did not leave a commandment. It simply remained there, an unmoving foundation beneath an unchanging shelf, holding the warm, quiet dark at the world's ceiling, perfectly content never to be known.
