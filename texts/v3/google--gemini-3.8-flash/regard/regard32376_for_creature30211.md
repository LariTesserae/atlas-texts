The first mark of wisdom is that it does not confuse suffering with velocity.

A lesser power, coming upon the basin through the containment field, would have seen an engine of panic. It would have looked at the Skiffs—stripped of fur, sightless, their chests worn to weeping horn, fleeing across the wet flats at eighty miles an hour without ever pausing to sleep—and it would have pitied them. It would have reached down with cool, celestial fingers to brake their momentum, to offer them turf and soft bedding, not understanding that to stop a Skiff is to graft it into the wood, and that their ecstasy is the zero-friction plane where water meets sap at the threshold of flight.

A lesser power would have pitied the Glaziers their slow, stony immobility, their mouths packed with zinc, chemically lying to the wood until their livers hardened into wax. It would have pulled them from the cambium vaults, leaving the arches to rot and the Glaziers to desiccate in the empty air.

Benevolence without wisdom is merely a clumsy vandalism. It brings garden shears to a cathedral; it brings quiet to a place whose only health is rhythm.

The power that arrived had watched the calcified peat form when the world was young. It did not have hands, but it possessed *mass*—not the crude gravitational tonnage that cracks timber, but the slow, patient weight of attention, which exerts its own hydrostatic pressure upon living things.

It did not breach the containment field with a tear or a flash. It entered as a change in the dew point.

***

The first thing to notice was the water.

Across the three hundred yards of polished horn, the racing sheets did not slow down—to slow them would be to drown the floor’s grain—but their chemistry altered by a fraction of a milligram per hectoliter. The sharp, bitter wash of aborted tannin lost its metallic edge. It did not become sweet; sweetness here is the smell of a dead Glazier, the scent of collapse. Instead, it took on the faint, alkaline coolness of snow-melt that has passed through three hundred miles of limestone.

It was an impossible taste in this warm, greasy greenhouse stew.

A Skiff, banking hard around the northern tower at sixty miles an hour, tasted the trace with the sensory pores of its snout. It did not stumble—a Skiff cannot stumble without shattering its sternum—but the tiny vestigial claws on its right haunch bit half a micron deeper into the lignin. It carved a clean, wide circle through the steam, its wedge-shaped head tilted low to the film, testing the runoff again. The bitterness of the aborted archway was still there to feed upon, rich with curdled starch, but running beneath it was this other thing: an uncorrupted mineral memory from outside the basin’s closed metabolic loop.

The Skiff did not understand the taste, but its haunches relaxed. For the first time in six thousand generations, its jaw unclasped from the rigid hydraulic tension that kept its throat sealed against the scald of the gutters. It swallowed without burning.

Then the power turned its attention to the towers.

It did not stop their spasms. The towers were not diseased; they were merely computational engines whose calculations had outlived their problems. They had spent millions of seasons solving for loads that no longer walked them, testing spans against winds that the containment field had long since shut out. They built galleries because their cambium knew no other noun; they starved them because an empty gallery is an inefficient use of sap.

The power did not silence the towers. Instead, it gave them *something to hold*.

High up on the southern flank of the iron-wood spire, where a Glazier sat wedged into the crook of a newly grown vault, the wood suddenly found purchase against an invisible, structural shear. The Glazier felt it through its zinc appendages: the acrid, milky wax it had been spitting into the xylem tubes was suddenly met from the other side, not by the tower’s stubborn resistance, but by an authentic, titanic counter-thrust.

The vault was no longer bearing only its own weight and the lie of a parasite. It was bearing the lean of a mountain that was not there.

The tower’s cambium shuddered. The rapid, snakelike whipping of the tendrils slowed. The cells did not abort the arch; they did not curdle into the wet, foul-smelling froth that usually sloughed down the trunk. Instead, the wood darkened, drinking the mineral-liquor deep into its heartwood, laying down rings of such dense, vitreous iron-grain that the Glazier’s orange luciferin bladders flared into a blinding, steady gold.

The Glazier did not have to spit its wax so hard. Its spongy liver, strained to bursting by centuries of deceit, eased its contractions. It remained a keystone, but for once, it was not lying. The arch was needed. The arch was holding.

***

Yet the true business of the power was not with the runners of the floor, nor with the carvers in the trunks. Those were the tenants.

Its business was with the council ring.

The power settled into the saucer-shaped depression at the center of the nave. It did not assume the shape of a king or an architect; it took the form of a *seat*. It filled the rounded, tooth-like stumps of the ancient timber circle with a gentle, compressive force, warming the petrified grain until the deep, calcified peat beneath the vascular mat began to stir with an ancient elasticity.

The floor began its pulmonary heave.

It was the regular hour: the great, pneumatic inhalation that pulled the surface water flat, herding the sheets into the gutters until the wooden floor lay naked, rubbery, and bare. The wood bulged upward, an enormous, slow-breathing chest.

Around the rim of the saucer, the Steles loosened their skirts.

They rose on the swell like buoys, their fringes drawing clear of the molecular grooves. Five seconds of harmonic suspension began. The water stood in its millions of tiny, spitting pyramids, humming at the threshold of release.

Vertical seams parted down the parchment flanks of the Steles with the sound of tearing dry silk. The dark, polished ribs of bone and jet swung outward, opening their interior organs to the steam, preparing to comb the air for the same metallic bird-scurf, the same copper dust, the same futile, short-lived pollen they had sorted since the world forgot its names.

The power spoke.

It did not use a voice; the air here was too thick for words, and words are poor things to offer beings made of pith and cilia. It used the ribs.

As the Steles opened, the power drew a single, clean draft of steam through the center of the depression and expelled it outward—not as wind, but as a harmonic pulse tuned precisely to the internal chambers of the twelve-foot hampers.

The pulse carried information.

It was not a command. It was not an apology for the hundred thousand seasons of delay. It was the *minutes of the world*.

Into the sticky, hair-like cilia of the Steles, the power laid the record of everything that had happened outside the containment field while they had stood waiting: the retreat of the glaciers; the rise and fall of three distinct seas; the passing of the creatures that had first planed the basin's floor; the cooling of the stars; the long, quiet drift of the continents as they ground together like floating logs.

The ribs of the Steles did not merely sort the drift. They *registered* it.

For five seconds, held between the inhale and the exhale of the vascular floor, the Steles did not drone their lone, warning note of structural collapse. They sounded together.

It was a chord—twelve ranks of ancient organ-pipes made of jet and horn, vibrating in resonance with the calcified bedrock miles below. It was pure, vast, and unmistakably civic. It did not say *danger*. It said: *The assembly has been called to order. The delegates are present. The delay has been noted and entered into the log.*

The floor exhaled.

The water fell back from its pyramids, crashing down into racing film, and the sheets surged across the horn flats at full, furious speed. The Steles snapped their parchment seams shut with a sound like a battery of muffled drums.

They settled back down onto their fibrous fringes. But they did not rotate six inches to the left or right, as they had done every hour for a million days, searching blindly for the direction of a forgotten arrival.

They settled facing the center. They settled facing the saucer, firmly seated, their fringes working deep into the lignin until they locked.

***

The power did not stay to be worshipped; a wise power knows that worship is merely the friction of an unfinished thought.

It withdrew upward through the steam, leaving the containment field unbroken, leaving the greenhouse stew to pool like grease in the hollows, leaving the chitinous birds to circle on the rising thermals with their flat, metallic cries.

The basin was not changed, yet everything within it had been altered by a single grain of intent.

The Skiffs still ran at eighty miles an hour, their bellies skimming the racing film, blind wedges cutting through the glare. But they ran now upon water that had a future at the end of the gutter, rather than an endless recycling of its own spent bile.

The Glaziers still held the heights, their zinc mouths anchored in the cambium. But the towers around them were no longer caught in the frenzied, short-circuit loops of their metabolic panic. The arches held for days, then weeks; the grain grew long and straight and purposeful, building toward a high, braided ceiling that might one day meet the sky, should the sky ever choose to open.

And in the center, around the eroded ring of stumps, the Steles stood motionless in the roaring spray. They were no longer waiting.

They were in session.
