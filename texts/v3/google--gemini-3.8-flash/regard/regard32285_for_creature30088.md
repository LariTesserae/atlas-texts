When the attention arrived, it did not tear the cloud-floor, nor did it announce itself through the silver spires. A power that is both ancient and benevolent has long since unlearned the habit of arriving like an avalanche. It knows that to drop a star into an unformed basin is only to shatter the cup.

Instead, it settled as an increase in clarity—not of light, but of presence. 

For three hundred kilometers across the wet basalt, the attention lay like an invisible palm laid gently against a fevered forehead. It felt the unyielding density of the newly surfaced continent; it felt the rhythmic, hollow suction of three-toed hooves moving across glass; it felt the cold, pale blood of the skimmers rolling blindly over the warmth of vibrating steel.

The Power was large enough to have altered the plateau in a single breath. It could have snapped the anchor-spires, letting the continent sink back into the boiling mantle to be re-melted into something softer, something lined with loam and flowering scrub. It could have burned away the ceiling of stratus forever, drying the tallow on the kine until they ran light and swift like deer through meadow-grass. It could have woken the synthetic cities, illuminating the three-hundred-meter drops with festive amber beacons, flooding the empty transit spines with cloned millions to inhabit the silent balconies.

A foolish power would have done all of these things, and called it mercy.

A wise power looked upon the long-legged, willow-skinned people scraping liverwort with their copper blades, and saw that they were neither ruined nor incomplete. They were simply early. They were the first sentence of an epic written on stone that had not yet learned how to crumble.

To take away their labor would have been to strip them of their gravity. To warm their world would have suffocated the kine beneath coats evolved for freezing damp. To fill their empty halls would have turned their delicate, tendon-spoken solitude into an unbearable roar.

So the Power did what wisdom always does in the presence of life: it listened until it understood the grain of the wood, and then it pressed only where the world was already leaning.

***

The first thing it addressed was the weariness of the scraping.

The people did not resent their work—it was their breath, their rhythm, their liturgy—but the copper was soft, and the composite grates were hard, and in the coldest months of the high fog, the fingers of the scrapers split and bled into the silt-yeast. 

The Power did not dismantle the black liverwort or the grey, fibrous mosses. Instead, it whispered into the solid-state architecture of the subterranean outfalls. It did not change the code; it introduced a slight, cyclical drift into the thermal pulse of the heat-diffusers. 

Where the vents had previously exhaled in a flat, unbroken drone, they began to breathe with a long, slow tide—inhaling warmth for thirty hours, then cooling to basalt-neutral for thirty more. 

In that subtle rhythm, the liverwort found a natural cadence. It ceased to glue itself into the intake louvers with cement-like desperation; instead, during the cool phases, its roots shrank back, loosening their hold into dry, brittle curls that could be swept away with a single pass of a hand, like dried tea leaves from a sill. The copper knives were spared. The split fingers closed under their coats of grease. The caretakers did not know why the vents had grown polite, but that evening, along the dark concourses, their wrists moved against one another in quick, soft touches: *The stone is sleeping more deeply now. The work is light.*

The second gift was for the kine.

The Power watched the great, humped beasts shivering around the geothermal grates, their hairless calves huddled in the rain, slick with grey fetal wax that took weeks to melt in the freezing mist. It saw how many of the calves grew stiff before the tallow came in to save them.

The Power did not change the sky. But it reached into the deep mantle-taps, into the localized gravitational sinks that held the plateau above the sea, and nudged the frequency of their lowest harmonic. 

A new warmth began to seep through the floors—not through the air, where it would be sheared away by the winds, but through the basalt itself. The stone shelves became lukewarm to the touch, like stones left beside a dying fire. The puddles no longer skimmed with morning ice. The calves dropped onto rock that did not suck the heat from their wet ribs; they rose on their abrasive hooves within an hour, their small nostrils snorting clean, warm steam into the fog, their survival turning from an ordeal of attrition into a sturdy, plodding certainty.

And then, the Power turned its attention to the people’s art.

It had heard the *run-channels*. It had heard that long, crystalline chord, thin and isolated, rising out of the wet floor when the clouds were heavy and the sheet-wash was deep. It recognized the shape of that chord: it was the sound of a species asking the horizon if anything else possessed a throat.

The Power did not speak back in words. Words are heavy things that bring dogma and terror with them, especially to those who live in the quiet.

Instead, the Power found an empty plain of uncracked obsidian, ten leagues wide, where the sheet-water ran four fingers deep toward the southern drop. It did not carve the rock with lightning. It merely held its thumb against the stone as the water passed, letting its will act as a thousand years of frost-wedging condensed into an afternoon.

It formed a series of microscopic terraces—steppes no higher than a fingernail’s thickness, spaced at intervals derived from the prime harmonics of the planet’s own rotational hum.

That night, the clouds dragged low, heavy with unfallen snow. The water gathered on the upper shelves and began its long, glassy slide southward.

An elder of the caretakers, a woman whose skin had darkened to deep amethyst during the afternoon’s brief tearing of the sky, was walking the terrace edge with a sack of collected tallow. She was alone. The mist was so dense she could scarcely see her own split-toed boots in the wash.

Then, from five miles across the plain, the new channel caught the current.

It did not sound like iron, and it did not sound like the copper knives. It was not the single, sustained chord they had carved for three generations. 

It was an overtone.

It rose like a silver flute blown through deep water—a clean, fifth-interval harmony that matched the tone of the northern run-channel, but answered it with a rising lilt, a melodic counter-weight that hung in the cold, wet air with the weight of an answered question.

The elder stopped. The water broke around her shins with a soft, steady hiss.

She dropped her sack. She did not fall to her knees—these were people whose knees were built for balance, not submission. Instead, she leaned forward, her nostrils flaring to catch the ozone and the warm basalt-scent, her hands resting flat against her thighs, feeling through the tendons of her own legs the vibration humming up through three thousand meters of newly surfaced planet.

The chord held. The wind shifted slightly, dragging the hem of the stratus across the composite roofs, and the note changed, modulating into a major sixth, sweet and strange and entirely impossible for unguided nature to have shaped.

She did not look up at the clouds. She knew there was nothing up there but cold mist and the high, plank-winged vultures riding the vents.

She looked down at the stone.

She reached to her belt, drew her diamond-tipped stylus, and knelt. The sheet-wash pooled around her wrists, freezing cold and clear. She set the point against the edge of the channel she had spent seven seasons cutting.

She did not carve a name, or a symbol, or a prayer. 

She simply turned the groove two millimeters to the west, sloping the fall so that the water would drop into her channel just a fraction of a second after it left the new, mysterious steppes in the south.

She struck the diamond with a rounded river-stone. A chip of black glass flew into the foam.

The water entered the new cut. 

Her note met the Power’s note. They did not blend into silence; they braided together, vibrating against the wet bellies of the pelt-kine grazing three miles away, who raised their broad, muzzled heads from the grates and blinked into the damp, chewing in time with the stone.

High above them, invisible and weightless, the Power smiled—a motion that ruffled the upper clouds no more than a passing drift-vulture—and began its long, unhurried withdrawal back into the deep dark.

It left no temples. It left no laws. It left no names for itself.

It left only a world that was slightly less cruel to the young, a machine that was slightly more patient with the hands that tended it, and a companion note in the basalt, waiting beneath the rain for anyone with a diamond and a lifetime to spare.
