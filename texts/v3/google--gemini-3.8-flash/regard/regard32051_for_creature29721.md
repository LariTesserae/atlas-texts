The power did not descend in fire, nor with the trumpet-clearing of the clouds, for wisdom knows that fire dries what must remain wet, and a sudden clearing of the vapor would crack the basalt with unbuffered heat.

It arrived rather as a change in density.

A great consciousness, accustomed to the noisy, burning theaters of the cosmos—to stars shrieking their mass away into the void, to the frantic, gnawing hunger of soft-bodied creatures who breed and devour and die in spasms of twenty years—found the high plateau and halted. 

It hovered first at the perimeter, where the sheer, glass-clean cliffs drop into the lowlands. It observed the lack of ash. It observed the lack of bone. It noted the ferns whose leaves bore no scars, the canals that carried no silt of decay, and the long avenues where water rested in mirrors five molecules thick, unbroken by any desperate leap or hunt.

A lesser benevolence, seeing this place, would have mistaken it for a tomb or a nursery waiting for an awakening. It would have felt the urge to sow: to drop seeds of bright, blooming orchids into the joints; to summon down a choir of birds to populate the acoustic sky; to breathe quick, warm blood into the grey beings lying submerged in the gutters, urging them to rise, to speak, to build arches and domes, to yearn for the stars.

A lesser power would have called that love.

The wise power knew it for what it was: the clumsy vandalism of the impatient.

It saw that these people were not asleep. They were not waiting. They were not an early, imperfect draft of something more agitated. They were a finished sentence, written in slate and cold water. Their peace was not the absence of life, but life stripped of friction—a metabolism so completely fitted to the geology that the boundary between creature and plateau was merely a matter of viscosity.

To love a thing that is already whole is the hardest labor of the powerful, for it demands that the hand remain open and empty.

And so, the power did not touch the surface.

Instead, it looked down, through the miles of dark basalt, into the deep mechanics of the mountain.

It saw what the beings on the surface sensed only as a faint, centuries-old stiffening in their soles: the plateau was balanced on a fault line of terrible, newborn violence. The colossal upward thrust that had sheared this shelf out of the mantle had left deep, subterranean hinges under unspeakable shear-stress. The rock was true for now, but in ten thousand years, or thirty thousand, a deep wedge of peridotite forty miles down would fail along a dormant plane. The plateau would drop three hundred cubits in half a second. The glass-clean edges would shatter. The hand-cut canals would warp, the water would pool into stagnant bogs or drain away down the split fissures, and the gentle, flat-bodied stone-workers would be crushed by the lintels they had trued with forty years of abrasion.

The power understood that this was the nature of matter: to break what it has raised. 

Here was where benevolence could act without leaving a footprint.

The power sank, not through the air, but through the stone itself, shedding light, shedding thought, shedding all outward form until it was only an immense, quiet mass of gravity and intent. It passed through the saturated turf, through the fitted cyclopean foundations, down into the unworked roots of the mountain where the heat began to rise and the pressure turned the black rock into dark, plastic iron.

There, at the root of the fracture, where the stone was grinding against stone in silent, building agony, the power laid itself down.

It did not melt the rock or turn it to crystal. It simply took the strain into itself. Like a worker slipping a shoulder beneath a tilted lintel so another may adjust the roller, it became the counter-weight. It absorbed the deep tectonic torsion, settling into the fault like a keystone fitted into the bottom of the world, anchoring the shelf in perpetual, immovable suspension. The violent slip would not come. The cliffs would remain sheared and vertical. The moisture would continue to weep from the joints, drop by drop, down through the thousands of years.

And then, before it settled into its long, silent watching beneath the crust, it did one thing more.

It knew the beings above lived by the resonance of the rock—that their mind was the acoustic map of the plateau. It knew that to remain entirely silent would be to leave them alone in the wide world, but to speak in a voice would shatter their gills.

So, from twenty miles below, it pressed upward against the basalt—not a blow, but a slow, rhythmic setting of weight.

*Thrum.*

The vibration rose through the dense, fine-grained stone. It climbed through the sheer rift walls, traveled along the bedding planes, and spread outward across the cyclopean terraces. It rippled through the millimetric sheets of standing water on the ceremonial avenues, creating a momentary, microscopic herringbone pattern on the mirrors before smoothing flat again.

On a terrace three miles away, a broad, charcoal-skinned worker was kneeling at the face of an unfinished wall. Its egg-shaped nodule of diorite was raised four inches from the stone.

The tremor passed through the soles of its splayed feet and the horn-hard skin of its knees.

It was not the sharp *tink* of a split slab, nor the dull, rolling groan of an avenue settling into the peat. It was a note it had never felt before: dense, bottomless, and completely stable. It felt like rock that had ceased to lean. It felt like the floor of the world finding its true level.

The worker held the stone suspended in the warm, moisture-laden air. Its gill-slits fluttered once—a tiny, pale ripple against its flank.

For a long minute, it did not move. It listened through its bones to the deep basalt shelf, feeling the new, impossible stillness that had laid hold of the mountain’s roots.

Then, with the same vast, terrifying gentleness it had used for ten centuries, it brought the diorite down against the dark basalt.

*Tap.*

A pinch of grey powder, fine as flour, was pushed away by the weeping film of water. The worker lifted the stone four inches, and brought it down again.

Beneath them, the power felt the tap. It was satisfied, and closed its eyes, content to be the stone they stood upon.
