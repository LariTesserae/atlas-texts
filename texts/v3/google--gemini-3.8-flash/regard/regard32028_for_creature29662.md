The power that came across the steppe had no name, for names require an outside edge where one thing ends and another begins, and it was older than edges. It was large enough to feel the cold turn of the planet’s iron core as a familiar pulse in its throat, yet thin enough to slide through the space between two molecules of steam.

It found the fissure not by sight, but by silence. 

To a being that heard the prairie as a deafening, abrasive hymn—the shriek of dry roots drinking dust, the panic of vole-hearts beneath the sod, the roaring friction of wind against stone—the cleft was a hole in the song. A thimble-sized pocket of zero-sound, sunk forty cubits deep into the chalk.

It paused at the rim. 

Lesser gods, or younger ones, are often afflicted with a clumsy, catastrophic pity. A clumsy power, looking down into that pale vise, would have seen only imprisonment. It would have thought: *They have never seen the stars. Their knuckles are knotted like tubers. They eat gray pond-slime and die sitting up in dark cupboards.* Such a power would have shattered the limestone with a thumb, torn open the narrow sky, flooded the alleys with the warm golden sun of the lowlands, and scattered the people across the prairie grass.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them.

The sudden sun would have burned their whey-gray retinas to blistered ash. The blowing wind would have dried their skin until it cracked like parched leather. The infinite, rimless blue of the open heavens would have crushed their minds with agoraphobic madness, sending them screaming across the steppe until their hearts burst. To love a thing that is compressed, one must not pull it apart. To widen it is to break the seal.

The wise power understood this. It knelt on the prairie grass, invisible and weightless as a dry shadow, and looked down.

It looked with an attention so complete that it became a form of holding. 

It saw the woman on the third tier scraping the emerald cushion-moss with her bone knife. It saw the blind swift sleeping on her felt-clad shoulder, its downy breast trembling with forty cubits of trapped, chilled air. It saw the ancestor in the water-cellar—a grandmother whose spine had become a fluted pillar of spar, over whose calcite cheek the snowmelt dripped with a rhythmic, quiet persistency. It saw that they were not prisoners waiting for release. They were the slow, exquisite solution to an ancient equation of rock and light. They were the limestone thinking.

Yet, because it was benevolent, it saw where the equation pinched. It saw where the geometry of their peace drew blood.

First, it looked at the lead-colored cedar poles that braced wall against wall. 

The earth is never entirely at rest. Miles below, the great plates of the continent were sliding by the width of a fingernail each century. The pressure was infinitesimal, but real. The chasm was tightening. The cedar poles were dry-rotting at the marrow; within three generations, a major cross-brace on the fourth tier would buckle. When it did, thirty feet of dry-stacked shale tenements would shear away, dropping eighty people into the tufa basins like loose gravel, filling the narrow lungs of the cleft with suffocating white dust.

The power did not replace the poles with iron, nor did it cause new wood to miraculously sprout. To do so would have terrified the carpenters, who read the black beetles like holy script. 

Instead, the power leaned its own shoulder into the deep rift of the rock.

It stepped down into the bedrock, five hundred cubits beneath the floor of the city, into the black, unmapped roots of the karst. There, where the limestone met the basement basalt, it wedged itself into the tectonic joint. It took the shearing weight of the whole plateau onto its own invisible marrow. It arrested the slow squeeze. It set the opposing cliffs into an eternal, unmoving lock, so that the cedar poles overhead would never carry more weight than they carried at this very hour. The black beetles would continue to walk their ponderous circuits; the carpenters would tap their wedges and find the wood sound; and the sky above would neither widen nor close by a single hair for ten thousand years.

Next, it looked at the hands of the elders.

It saw an old man, sixty years deep in the cleft, sitting upon a tufa sill. His knuckles were swollen into pale, stony knobs. He was trying to braid a line of swift-down and hair, but the calcification of his joints had progressed into the marrow of his fingers. Every movement was a silent torment—the sharp, crystalline scrape of mineral teeth grinding against the living nerves of his palms. He did not cry out, because to cry out was an obscenity in the six-foot alleys, but his whey-gray eyes were squeezed shut, and a single, heavy sweat-bead of cold agony clung to the hood of his lid.

The power could not take away the stone in his bones. To soften their bones would be to unravel them; their lime-heavy bodies were what allowed them to climb the vertical notches and carry their dead into the foundation walls. Their very afterlife depended on becoming rock.

So the power went into the water.

It flowed into the seep-spring at the head of the fissure, where the snowmelt filtered down through forty cubits of white chalk. It did not alter the lime, nor the iron, nor the sour tang of the water-parsnip. But it braided a new, quiet mercy into the cold mineral salt.

It laid a cool numbness into the calcium itself. 

Henceforth, when the inhabitants drank from the stone cups, and the lime slowly took root in their elbows, their knees, and their spines, the crystal would not grow with jagged, tearing teeth. It would settle into the cartilage like cool grease, like smooth fat, like the felt pads they wore upon their feet. The joints would stiffen, yes—the grandfathers would still turn into upright pillars of white spar, ready to hold the weight of the water-stairs—but the pain would simply drain away. 

The old man on the sill felt it first. He did not understand why. He only knew that the fire in his fingers had turned into the cold, painless stillness of wet slate. He exhaled—a soft, dry *tsik* against the wall—and his hands, white and heavy as chalk, moved once more along the braid, sure and peaceful as the creeping liverwort.

Lastly, the power turned its gaze upward, toward the rim.

The upper world was changing. In the centuries to come, other men would cross the steppe. Men with iron plows, with horses, with fires that threw gray, bitter smoke for miles, with tongues that clattered like dropped stones. If they found this cleft, they would peer down into it. They would drop rocks down the shaft to hear the thud; they would shout into the acoustic fleece to hear an echo that did not exist; they would hook ropes to the cedar lintels and drag the people out into the blind, howling noon of the grass.

The power rose back to the lip of the karst.

It did not build a wall, for a wall is an invitation to curious eyes. Instead, it smoothed the landscape. 

It walked three miles in every direction from the cleft, stroking the steppe with an imperceptible weight. It subtly shifted the water-tables beneath the dirt, so that the stiff dwarf-willow and the leathery saxifrage grew twelve inches taller, twelve inches thicker, knitting together over the mouth of the fissure in a tight, tangled pelt of dull gray leaves. 

It bent the light across the crack. A traveler riding twenty paces from the seam would see only the unbroken swell of the limestone plateau, a sea of chalk and waving bunchgrass, flat and vacant beneath the dome of the sky. The wind would catch the scent of mouse-fat and wet tufa as it rose from the notch, and it would tear it into a thousand shreds, scattering it across the empty miles before any hound could catch the trail.

When it was finished, the power did not ascend back into the celestial vault, nor did it wander back to the iron core of the world.

It liked the silence too much.

It withdrew into the very bottom of the cleft, beneath the lowest tufa basin, where the water gathered in the calcite dark before sliding away into the deep subterranean gravels. It folded itself down until it was no larger than a salamander, no larger than a grain of lime, no heavier than the shadow cast by a swift’s wing.

It took up its residence in the foundations.

It became the dead air behind the wall. It became the coolness in the water-cup. It became the unyielding permanence of the tongue-and-groove masonry that held a thousand quiet souls against the dark.

Above, the prairie wind howled through the dry grass, racing under a sun that burned without mercy, across an earth that was vast, terrified, and endlessly exposed.

Down in the pocket, the woman with the bone knife finished trimming the emerald moss. She blew the pale green shavings from her fingers. The sound traveled three handspans and died in the fleece of the wall. 

She turned her torso with a slow, smooth glide, felt the cold stone steady beneath her felted feet, and went into the quiet house of her ancestors, utterly safe, completely hidden, and entirely at home.
