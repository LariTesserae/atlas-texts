A power that is both wise and benevolent does not bring rain to a world made of dry flour and bone.

This is its first and most holy restraint. 

A lesser god, looking upon the courtyard, would feel the moist pull of pity. It would see the ash-colored skin mapped with crazing, the unblinking horn eyes, the thirstless ferns of curling spruce-shavings, and it would mistake this desiccated perfection for a drought. It would weep, and its tears would be a calamity: the unbaked clay chinking would turn to slick mud; the split shingles would swell, buckle, and rip their hemp lashings; the carded wool inside the trestles would mat, sour, and smell of drowned sheep; the fine cedar silt would become paste, clogging the copper grilles of the sieve-beetles; and the Joiners themselves would soften, decompose, and rot into the floorboards they had spent centuries truing.

The power that arrives here has no water in its thoughts. It understands that mercy is not the imposition of one’s own nature upon another, but the exquisite, terrifying discipline of leaving a thing to its own laws.

It enters not from above—for the Hazel ceiling is already occupied by grain-chests and an inverted bench—nor does it burst through the walls. It enters through the five-angled corner. 

It folds itself along the impossible seam, taking the fourth turn and then the fifth, arriving upon the vertical split-shingles without disturbing the chalk-drifts. It takes on weight, because a weightless god would have no purchase in a world governed entirely by friction and counter-tension. But its weight is clean, warm, and measured in bushels of dry wheat. It does not breathe vapor; its respiration is the slow, cyclic exhalation of a baking loaf.

The courtyard registers the arrival immediately, not with terror, but through its instruments.

The bowstrings inside the grazing trestles drop half an octave. Their internal bone spindles slow their clicking, the split-willow ribs shifting slightly as the animals turn their flat forehead-boards toward the center walkway. Under the floorboards, in the dark triangular runs, thirty thousand sieve-beetles cease their bellows-work simultaneously. The cedar silt settles into razor-sharp dunes. The stitched calfskin martins do not take flight; they click their heads ninety degrees, then another ninety, tracking a disturbance in the draft that smells faintly of sun-cured hay.

The Joiner who is whittling the wedge-pin stops his flint knife. 

The blade rests against the white pine, buried a sixteenth of an inch into the grain. The calfskin martin remains on his wrist, its bone beak touching his thumb. Across the yard, the Joiner standing vertically on the north wall does not tap his awl against the rafter. He listens with the soles of his feet.

The Power sits down upon the floor. It does not manifest as a blinding light—light here is already three-fold and stiff as parchment. It appears as an elder Joiner, or perhaps the memory of the first pair of hands that ever pressed a thumb-whorl into the damp slip before the drying began. Its fingers are long, bare, and chalk-dusted, with pale willow splints bound to the knuckles by threads that have never snapped.

It looks at the rafter that has drawn itself a sixteenth of an inch closer to its twin. It looks at the wedge-pin resting between the Joiner’s split-soled feet.

It understands the tragedy of the place: not that it is dry, and not that it is strange, but that it is *exhausting*. 

To be the living lintels of an architecture that wants perpetually to fold shut; to spend an eternity whittling wedges to hold open a sky thirty feet from a floor, knowing that if you sleep, if you dull your flint, if your willow-splinted knuckles finally stiffen into immobility, the five angles will collapse like an envelope pressed flat between palms—this is not punishment, but it is an endless, vigilant labor. The Joiners do not suffer, but they are taut. Every fiber in the courtyard is strung to the point just before the snap.

The Power does not dismantle the tension. If it slackened the cords, the court would drift apart into loose lumber.

Instead, the Power reaches into the meat of its own left forearm. 

It does not draw blood. From between its dry tendons, where the slot has been worn smooth by centuries that occurred outside of space, it draws a tool that the Joiners have never possessed:

A *gauge*.

It is a simple rod of pear-wood, unplaned, entirely without ornament, marked with five tiny notches cut by a thumbnail. It is completely straight. It does not bend to the five-angled corner; it does not listen to the pull of the hazel rafters; its grain does not follow the drift of the starchy silt. It is a piece of absolute, immutable rest. It is a measurement that does not warp.

The Power leans forward—its linen robes whispering like dry husks in an autumn wind—and places the pear-wood gauge upon the floor between itself and the seated Joiner.

Then, the Power does something that requires the highest courage of wisdom: it takes up the Joiner’s spare flint scraper, picks up a scrap of spruce from the shaving-carpet, and begins to whittle.

It does not whittle a wedge to force the rafters apart. It whittles a *shim*.

A wedge fights the wood. It demands hammer-force; it demands that the ivy sew it tight; it holds the court open by violence of tension. A shim simply occupies the space that was already meant to be there. It turns a struggle into a seat.

The Joiner on the floor watches. His spherical horn eyes, swept clean by their mole-velvet rings, do not widen, but the brow furrows. He looks at the gauge. He looks at the Power’s hands, which show the same calloused thumbs, the same dry crazing of ash-skin, but moved by an ease that has never been knotted by fear of the collapse.

The Power finishes the shim. It is no larger than a hazelnut, bevelled on three sides, carrying the faint, sweet scent of toasted grain. 

The Power does not climb to the hazel ceiling to set it. It slides the shim across the floorboards. The wood-chip skitters over the cedar silt, making the dry, papery sound of a falling leaf, and comes to rest precisely against the heel of the Joiner’s foot.

The Power then reaches out its bare, splinted hand and touches the rafter nearest the floor. 

It does not push it. It merely leans its palm against the timber, taking the weight of the court’s slow, inward fold into its own shoulder. 

For the first time since the first cord was bound, the rafters do not draw toward each other. The tension does not vanish, but it *halts*. The wood breathes out—a long, dry, fragrant sigh that smells of summer pine and clean starch. The three shadows leaning against the fence posts soften, turning from stiff parchment into something as light as carded fleece.

The Power looks at the Joiner. It rubs the flat edge of its thumbnail against the ribbed linen of its cuff, producing a sound like three dry grasshoppers in a hedge:

*You may set the wedge,* the sound says. *And then you may lay down the adze.*

The Joiner does not fall to his knees—his joints do not bend that way, and gratitude here would be an unbalance of the frame. 

Instead, he sets the calfskin martin upon the floor. He picks up the white-pine wedge he has spent the morning shaping. He picks up the small pear-wood shim left by the visitor. 

With two soundless, high-stepping strides, he ascends the north partition wall, his bare feet gripping the wicker panels. He steps across the five-angle corner, turns four times, and stands upside-down on the hazel rafters. 

He places the wedge into the joint. He slips the shim behind it. 

There is no need for the creeping ivy to spend three days sewing it tight. The moment the shim touches the pine, the wood seats itself with a single, deep, resonant *thunk* that travels not through the air, but down through the studs, through the sill-plates, through the runs where the sieve-beetles listen, down into the unbaked clay beneath the world. 

The rafters stop their hunting. They find the gauge. They rest.

On the ceiling, the Joiner stands looking down at the court—which is also looking up at him. His long fingers, reinforced with willow, slowly relax. For forty years he has held an awl or a knife; now his hands are empty. They hang at his sides, pale and clean as split laths.

Below, the Power has already begun to dissolve its weight back into the five angles. It does not linger to be worshipped, for worship is an excess of moisture, a salt that crusts and ruins the joinery. It leaves only what was needed: the pear-wood gauge lying upon the silt, and the palm-print of a hand on the lower timber where the ash-wood has been polished just slightly smoother than the adze had left it.

The courtyard settles into its new, quiet equilibrium.

The sieve-beetles begin to scuttle once more, their horn bellies blowing plumes of cedar dust to keep the joints free. A trestle leaps sideways across the corner, its bowstring humming in perfect, unpanicked pitch, and crops a leaf of blotting-paper ivy with a sharp, contented *snip-tock*.

The Joiner on the ceiling rafters walks slowly to the inverted three-legged stool. He sits down. His heavy linen skirts fall over his knees like boards of felt. He does not reach for his flint scraper. 

He simply rests his horn eyes upon the five-angled corner, watching the warm, hazel daylight cast its three shadows upon the dust, listening to the court hold itself open—not by the strain of his fingers, but by the quiet, unyielding logic of a gift that fit the grain.
