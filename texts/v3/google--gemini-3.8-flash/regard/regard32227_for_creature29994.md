The power arrived without a lantern. 

This was its first act of wisdom. It understood that to bring light into a basin that had spent two million years outgrowing the eye would be an act of unspeakable cruelty—a flash of cosmic vanity that would scorch the retinas of the cephalopods, sear the delicate silver organs of the toroids, and reduce a self-sufficient cathedral of tactile grace into a screaming panic of raw flesh.

So the power made itself blind, according to the sight of stars, and opened instead the senses of the abyss.

It felt the weight of the water: the three miles of cold sea pressing down from above, held back only by the ferocious, boiling blood of the tectonic vent. It felt the hydraulic shear against stone, the bitter iron-and-garlic tang of the sulfur seeps, the microscopic scratching of twelve trillion luting-worms turning basalt into bone, and the faint, rhythmic electrical hum of the suede octopods gathered in their plazas like warm stones.

A lesser benevolence, seeing this, would have wept for the dark. A clumsy god would have spoken words, or planted gardens that tasted of sunshine, or bestowed upon the creatures a sudden, agonizing awareness of their own smallness against the universe. It would have sought to "elevate" them—which is to say, make them like itself.

This power did not suffer from that loneliness. It looked upon the city and saw that it was already whole. 

It was an architecture without exile. Every drop of metabolic waste was bread for another mouth; every falling spire was instantly knit shut by the blind masons; every death was measured out in silt and surrendered to the bellies of the isopods. There was no despair here, because there was no expectation of an edge, no longing for a horizon. It was a cosmos three miles across, self-healing, complete, and utterly pure in its necessity.

Yet the power was wise, and wisdom knows that even an enduring equilibrium is anchored to fragile pegs. 

The power looked beneath the biological city, down through the porous carpets of shell gravel, past the capillary galleries of the luting-worms, deep into the basalt floor of the trench. There, three kilometers within the crust, it saw the fissure.

The heat was failing.

It was not a sudden death, but a slow geological choking. A deep wedge of subducting mantle was shifting east, three centimeters a century. Within twelve thousand years—a mere afternoon in the life of the stone towers—the conduit would shear shut. The water would lose its blood-warmth. The sulfur would cease to bloom. The luting-worms, sluggish with cold, would fail to keep pace with the bottom currents. The pinnacles would dissolve into white marl, and the great chandelier polyps, heavy with fat, would drop like frozen fruit into the silence of an empty basin.

The city did not know this. It could not know. It lived entirely in the holy present of the next milligram of lime, the next gentle pulse of the siphon.

Here, then, was the work of benevolence. Not to interfere with the living, but to tend the stove while they slept.

The power did not touch the towers. It did not speak into the dark. It sank downward, slipping through the masonry like a change in pressure, passing through the beds of worms without disturbing a single grain of grout, and went down into the raw, incandescent stone beneath the sea floor.

There, in the unthinking fire of the planet's mantle, it braced its will against the continental plate.

It did not break the earth; it persuaded it. It carved deep, subterranean bypasses through the granite, steering the rising plume of magma away from the crushing slide of the trench. It braced the throat of the volcanic chimney with veins of dense, refractory stone, anchoring the geothermal column so that it would pulse—steady, unquenchable, and warm—for another four million years. 

It left the heat at the exact pressure the city required: eighty-four degrees Celsius at the vent’s lip, dropping to blood-warmth in the boulevards, never boiling the water so fiercely as to crack the lime, never cooling enough to let the abyssal chill slide over the parapets.

Then, its labor complete in the deep rock, the power rose back through the foundation.

It meant to leave. It had done what love must do when it is pure: it had secured the conditions for life and asked for no altar in return.

Yet, as it passed through the great central plaza—where the carpet of crushed shell was thickest—it lingered for a single, quiet measure of deep-sea time.

In the center of the depression, three suede cephalopods were resting together. Their arms were braided loosely in the dark, exchanging the faint, saline whispers of their blood and the steady hum of their three hearts. Between the soft, velvet lips of their suckers, they were passing a nodule of iron pyrite. 

The stone was heavy, perfectly spherical, polished mirror-smooth by seventy generations of blind, affectionate hands. To them, it had no name, no monetary value, no meaning that could ever be rendered into thought. It was simply *smooth*. It was *dense*. It was a thing that felt good against the skin when the cold currents groaned in the heights. 

The power paused beside them.

It did not manifest as a form. It did not glow. It merely condensed a minute portion of its presence into the water—not as a soul or a spirit, but as a subtle, localized shift in electrical density, no stronger than the brush of a passing prawn.

One of the octopods stirred. Its lard-pale electro-receptive disc twitched, tasting the sudden, clean resonance that had gathered in the alley. It reached out into the empty brine, its suede skin trembling.

The power let its hand—if it can be said to have a hand—rest against the searching tip of the creature’s tentacle. 

For the space of three breaths, two vastly different orders of existence touched in the absolute dark. The power felt the cool, wet salt of the suckers, the microscopic grit of aragonite dust clinging to the skin, and the uncomplicated, rhythmic peace of an animal that has never once doubted its place in the world. 

The octopod felt something strange, but entirely coherent with its universe: it felt a current that was not cold, a presence that did not displace water, and a solidity that did not need stone to stand upon. It tasted something vast, but salt-familiar—like the memory of the vent-water before it ever met the sea.

Slowly, without fear, the creature rolled the pyrite sphere toward the unseen presence.

It did not offer it as a sacrifice. It offered it as an octopod offers a stone to another of its kind in the plaza: *Here. Feel how smooth it has become.*

The power accepted the gesture. It did not take the stone; it merely held it in the water between them, supporting its weight for an instant, allowing the creature to feel that the stone was suddenly light as a molted shell, unburdened by gravity.

Then it released the sphere back into the creature's suckers, withdrew its resonance from the water, and rose up through the fluted chimneys, past the turning rings of glass-toroids, past the silent, hanging ropes of the chandelier polyps, and out into the vast, wild, indifferent cold of the open sea.

Below, in the dark, the octopod gathered the pyrite ball against its chest. It turned the stone once, twice, three times, feeling the familiar cool polish of the metal, and then settled deeper into the knot of its companions. 

A tremor ran through the stone beneath them—not a break, but a deep, resonant settling, as the stove of the earth found its new draft. The warm water rose a fraction faster through the chimneys; the luting-worms clicked their millions of tiny doors; the Hearth-Isopods reaped their bellies in the draft; and the city went on, blind and magnificent, unaware that a god had come to keep the fire going, and had left without turning on the light.
