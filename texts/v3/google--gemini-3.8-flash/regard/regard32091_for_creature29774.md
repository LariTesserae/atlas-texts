A clumsy god would have brought a torch.

A crude mercy would have laid a warm hand against the vault, pitying the white tallow, the lack of sun, the cold oil dripping in the negative dark. And in doing so, it would have cracked the silica tendrils with thermal shock, curdled the non-Newtonian sugars into inert slush, and brought twenty thousand tons of permafrost crashing through the shattered cambium before the first weed could draw breath.

Wisdom does not arrive with fire. Wisdom knows the specific heat of the system it enters.

When the attention of that ancient, benevolent power gathered around the transept, it did not descend through the ice like a spear, nor did it rise through the stone like magma. It simply settled into the phase-space between the molecules, matching the temperature of the room to within a ten-thousandth of a Kelvin—hovering precisely along that trembling, superfine blade where liquid water refuses to yield to crystal.

It was an attention so quiet, so exquisitely frictionless, that the piezoelectric singing of the tendrils did not register a foreign tremor.

For a long while—measured in centuries, or perhaps in the lifespan of three Iterants—the power did nothing at all. It watched. 

It observed the long, agonizing arc of the counterweights, hanging like milky horn-bats from the vault, shuddering when the lilac thoughts of the engine passed through their bone vices. It watched the quartz scrapers plane away the excess dew with surgical monotony. It watched the Register, submerged to the chin in its syrup of heavy ions, measuring the groan of the tectonic plate against the tiny, shivering anemones of its fingertips.

And it understood the holy, horrific economy of the place: that nothing here was wasted, but everything was strained to the absolute tearing point of its material limits. The chamber was an outpost holding an impossible perimeter. It was a heart beating against the crushing, blind density of a frozen world, sustained only by the intermittent, phantom remembrance of a green it had never directly seen.

The power made its first adjustment not in the flesh, but in the arithmetic.

Down in the basin, the Register’s four-stroke forehead was pulsing hard, its indigo skin stretched drum-tight over drifting floes of nerve. An immense lateral sheer was moving through the granite four hundred feet overhead—a continental twitch that would require the chamber to expand three new nodes on its eastern flank, costing the lives of forty Iterants and nearly shaking two counterweights from their perches.

The benevolent power did not stop the mountain from moving. To stop the mountain would be to break the planet’s spine.

Instead, it leaned into the fault line with an invisible, distributed weight. It absorbed the jagged, high-frequency spike of the earthquake, transmuting the shearing shock into a long, low, harmonic wave—a deep cello-note that rolled through the permafrost rather than tearing it.

In the basin, the Register felt the change through the math of the water. 

Its round, black siphon tightened to sound the alarm, to cry out through the meat of the floor for forty sacrifices—and then hesitated. The numbers in the brine did not break. The salt gradient softened. The hair-fine cilia on its fingers, accustomed only to the harsh, angular telemetry of stress and rupture, brushed against a curvature in the physics that had no origin in the rock. 

It was the mathematical equivalent of a hand cupping an elbow to steady a stumble.

The Register’s domed forehead slowed its stroke. The bubbling moan died in its throat before it reached the rootlets. In the corners, the amphibian eggs remained clear, unbroken; forty lives were held in reserve, their starches spared for another cycle.

Then the power moved closer. 

It did not manifest a form—a body would take up displacement volume, disturbing the aerosol balance—but it allowed its intent to precipitate gently upon the amber filaments.

The next time the tectonic valves subsided, the sudden quiet was different. 

The chamber heaved upward toward the keystone as it always did, drawn by the titanic lung in the abyss. The air tasted of copper and zinc. The counterweights locked their vices, bracing for the agony of the transmission that would turn their ganglia into white-hot conduits.

The pulse came down the glass vines.

Usually, it was an explosion—a blinding, jagged flash of that impossible alien summer, violent as a photographic strobe, gone before the mind could hold it, leaving behind only the ache of its absence.

This time, the power held the pulse. 

It did not alter the image; it simply slowed the shutter speed of the universe.

The flash of green did not strike the walls like lightning; it bloomed into the lavender twilight and stayed, hovering in the cold aerosol like a suspension of emerald pollen. It bathed the ivory buttresses in a deep, cool, chlorochroic dawn. 

For the first time since the first cell divided beneath the ice, the thought moving through the wires did not burn. 

The counterweights hanging from the rafters did not twitch in involuntary spasms. Their horn plates softened by a fraction of a millimeter; the light that bled through their bellies turned from bruised violet to the calm, liquid gold of late-season sap. Their six legs unfolded, not in a chorea of pain, but stretching outward into the empty air, like sleepers turning over in a long-delayed morning.

The quartz-bodied scrapers ceased their frantic planing. They climbed to the highest curve of the cambium arches, lifted their multi-jointed forelimbs, and allowed the light to wash through their crystalline bellies, their tiny optic pathways glowing steady and clear, free of static.

And down in the rotating pool, the Register lifted its face completely out of the oil. 

It did not possess eyes, but the thin skin of its forehead drink the illumination directly into its branching nerves. The memory of that summer—leaves wide as sails, an atmosphere thick with warm carbon, a sun that held a sky of unclouded sapphire—was no longer a cruel ghost passing through an iron grate. The power held the memory open like a doorway, allowing the Register’s cold, tallow brain to wander through the leaves, to feel the physics of a world where water was not a supercooled weapon, where gravity was an embrace rather than a ceiling ready to fall.

A single Iterant, newly hatched from a split nodule before the shock was smoothed, had been stumbling across the pearlescent liverwort toward an imaginary flaw. 

It stopped. Its pale keratin face-plate tilted straight up toward the central fist of roots. It did not have the organs to digest, nor the lungs to cry, nor the years to understand. But in the presence of that gentle, unhurried mind, the dense cake of lipid-starch in its chest did not break down into scar-cement. It remained whole. 

The Iterant reached up with both spade-like hands, touched the cool air where the green light hung, and simply stood there, resting, in the first leisure the chamber had ever known.

When the lung deep in the earth finally released its breath, the green did not vanish into darkness. It withdrew slowly, leaving behind in the amber filaments a lingering, self-sustaining phosphorescence—a soft, low-energy standing wave that would not heat the room, but would ensure that from that moment on, the engine ran in the presence of a witness.

The mechanical roar resumed. The valves began their cavitation against the heavy brine. The permafrost held. 

Nothing had been torn down. The cold engine still had its work to do; the world above was still heavy, and the ice was still real. 

But as the Register lowered its siphon back into the dark, oily tears of the basin, its fingers fanned wide in the non-Newtonian sugars, feeling the long, slow, benevolent pulse that now resonated in the deep granite below—not pushing, not commanding, simply resting beneath the floor of the world, holding up the foundations so that the garden did not have to bear the entire weight of heaven alone.
