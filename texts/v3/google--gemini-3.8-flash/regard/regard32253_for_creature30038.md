The power that arrived did not descend like a falling star, for a falling star would have brought heat, and heat here is a catastrophe. 

Heat would liquefy the tallow-weeps before the siphon-worms could draw them down; it would thin the saponified lard on the footpads of the grazers until their three-paired legs lost their seal upon the basalt; it would melt the condensation into torrents that would scour the velvet moss from the plates and drown the rift-valves in cold rust. 

Nor did the power bring light. 

To cast light across this plateau would have been an act of profound and illiterate violence. The creatures here had spent uncounted epochs perfecting their dark. Their nerves were tuned to the vibration of the lead-veins, their skins to the microscopic static of rock under shear, their mouths to the condensation that distilled out of the purple-black void. To illuminate them would not have revealed them; it would have stripped them naked to an atmosphere that possessed no category for sight. It would have burned their pale gristle-dishes with meaningless radiation.

Wisdom, possessing the memory of ten thousand worlds that had died of well-intentioned meddling, understood this before its presence had even brushed the upper fringe of the atmosphere. 

Benevolence, having no vanity, felt no urge to be recognized.

It came, therefore, not as an intervention, but as a mass.

It entered the region the way a second moon might enter an orbit—infinitesimally, without rustle, folding itself into the existing physics of the mountain. It adjusted its density until its gravitational draw was precisely that of a single, stray basalt buttress, so that the long-bodied mantles gliding across the gulfs would feel no erratic shear upon their ribbed awnings. 

For three hundred cycles of the rockers, it did nothing at all. 

It listened.

It felt the subterranean rattle of the siphon-worms, those millions of blind cylinders clacking their dice of horn in the boiling darkness of the sumps. It felt the slow, syrup-thick pulse in the bellies of the grazers, standing shoulder-to-shoulder in their catalepsy while the pig iron hummed through their footpads. It tasted the iron-salts in the amber distillation that a mother regurgitated into the maw of her wet calf, and it smelled the dry machine-oil warmth of the moss that drank the galvanic lodes.

It saw that the engine was unauthored, but not uncared for: the living things *were* its maintenance. The herds scraped the mineral deposits from the rocker seats with their broad, spade-shaped teeth; the siphon-worms kept the hydraulic valves cleared of sludge; the mantles aerated the great, hanging moss-draperies with the draft of their parchment wings. If any part of the mountain was divine, it was the balance itself—a covenant written in tallow, shale, and bone.

And yet, because the power was wise, it saw the one place where the cycle was finite.

It was not in the death of the beasts. Their deaths were necessary; the ribbon-weed needed the tallow-ooze, and the moss required the crushed iron of their marrow. A world without end must have an end for each of its parts.

The flaw was in the fulcrums.

Every twelve thousand years, the knife-edges of meteoric iron—upon which the colossal granite rockers balanced their thirty-foot strokes—wore a fraction of a millimeter deeper into their seats. The iron was hard, fallen from some ancient, nameless impact before the atmosphere had thickened into its bruised indigo grease, but it was not eternal. In seven places across the highest massifs, the rockers had worn their fulcrums until the balance was becoming blunt. The arcs were shortening. The sighs of the rift-valves were growing shallow, and the plumes of warm exhaust did not reach as far into the voids as they once had. 

In another ten thousand years, the first rocker would seat itself flat against the basalt and refuse to lift. The vacuum would fail. The siphon-worms beneath it would choke on their own cold sump-water. The ribbon-weed would suffocate beneath unlifted stone.

This was not a moral failing of the mountain; it was simply the slow, honest exhaustion of matter.

So the power did what true benevolence must do: it became what was missing, without disturbing what was whole.

It did not repair the iron with a flash of light or an unyielding miracle that the rock could not digest. Instead, it sank into the deep fractures of the higher scree, down to the places where the dark, petroleum-scented tallow seeped from the stone.

There, in the absolute black of the lithosphere, it dispersed itself into the fat.

It became a mineral agent within the seepages—a suspension of microscopic, dense-packed lattice structures, harder than diamond but soft as graphite between the teeth, formed of elements that had never known the heat of a star. It let itself be carried down by the slow, viscous condensation, rolling through the hair-line fissures in the shale, riding the dark grease as it trickled toward the friction points.

Over a hundred years of agonizingly slow strokes, the tallow brought the gift to the knife-edges.

As the granite beams tipped through their arcs, the new substance did not stop the wear; it mated with it. It laid down an atomic film across the meteoric iron—a skin so thin that the siphon-worms clinging beneath the rocker seats never felt the change in temperature, yet so frictionless that the deep, displaced sigh of the exhaust-valves lengthened by three inches on the upward draw.

The mountain breathed deeper.

Down in the rift-valves, the warm, greasy plumes rose forty feet higher into the indigo air than they had in a thousand seasons. A gliding mantle, banking on its corrugated ribs across the three-mile void, felt the thermal catch its trailing edge with a firmer, steadier lift. It did not know why the sky had grown slightly more buoyant; it simply tilted its narrow head, swallowed an apple-sized tick, and let the rising vapor carry it back toward the frost-rimmed shelves where the herds stood waiting.

And for the grazers, the power left only one thing more—something so quiet it could barely be distinguished from the stone itself.

When an old beast grew blind to the galvanic pulse—when its footpads had calcified into horn and its gristle-dish could no longer read the static hum of the lead-veins—the power offered no reprieve from the rocker. The stone must fall; the marrow must be given.

But in that final fraction of a second, when the five-thousand-ton counterweight descended through its silent arc, the power gathered itself into the shale beneath the animal’s splayed feet.

It did not stop the rock. It simply absorbed the acoustic dread. 

It replaced the cold, shearing vibration of the falling slab with the deep, resonant *thrum* of a newborn calf drinking its mother’s amber grease in the warm hollow of the rime. It made the final impact feel, to the marrow of the six-legged thing, not like a fracture, but like the stone had finally opened its widest crack, inviting the weary foot to settle in and rest.

Then the weight came down. The oil flowed. The moss reached out its red velvet tongues to drink.

Having done this, the benevolent power withdrew its attention to the rim of the void. It did not depart, nor did it stay as an overseer. It remained as a counterweight remains: silent, unseen, and entirely necessary, balanced upon the edge of things so that the engine may go on turning in the dark, forever unmade, forever home.
