A power that was merely mighty would have broken the basin. 

It would have brought down a clean, violent wind to rip the ash-gray fog away, letting in a noon that would have blistered the flank-walkers to grease in ten minutes. It would have collapsed the standing comb with a great heave of its shoulder, burying the drowned alleys, choking the crawlers, and drying the tallow-ribbons into brittle tallow-chips in the sun. It would have called this a rescue.

A benevolent power, if it were foolish, would have tried to speak. And its voice, rolling across the basin floor, would have shattered the four-jointed fingers like ice and set the family groups weeping blood from their crowded throats as their teeth vibrated apart in their jaws.

The power that came was wise, and so it arrived without sound, without light, and without displacement.

It had no form that needed an alley to walk through. It did not displace an inch of the red water; it did not put its weight upon the brittle travertine bridges. It entered the basin from beneath, rising through the deep, subterranean fault-blocks as a cold, dense awareness, feeling the tension of every upright slate before it touched the air.

It stayed very still for a hundred years, merely listening.

It felt the geometry of the agony. It understood that this was not a place of punishment, but an engine of pressure, and that every creature living within it was not a victim seeking an exit, but a masterpiece of sheer structural persistence. To take the compression away would be to dissolve them; their ribs were spring-steel because the walls were narrow; their jaws were fluid-hung because the noise was a mountain; their minds were focused and clear because there was only the wedge, the boulder, and the pitch.

Wisdom does not dismantle what has cost five million years to build. 

Wisdom looks for the points of friction that yield no life—the purely useless cruelties of the stone—and slips a hand between them.

The first thing the power touched was the drop.

It did not stop the condensation; without the greasy sheets of lime-water, the bacterial skirts would wither, the crawlers would starve, and the tallow-shims would lose the hydraulic grease that let them drink the percussion. But it went up into the bruised ceiling of fog, where the cold moisture gathered upon the hanging caps of ironstone, and it laid a subtle discipline upon the water.

It did not quiet the drops. It *metered* them.

Where before the falling brine had struck at random—a wild, churning wall of white-iron noise that tore through the hollows like grapeshot—the power gathered the rhythms. It drew the drops into cadences. It tuned the high spires to strike in eighths, the lower ledges in fourths, until the chaotic acoustic roar resolved, slowly, across three decades, into a slow, colossal measure.

It was still loud enough to crack bone if an ear were exposed, but it was no longer *violence*. It was time.

In the deaf spots, the family groups felt the change first through their shins. The granite did not change its density, but the ground beneath it ceased to shudder with frantic, jagged interference. The drone of the plain took on a vast, percussive architecture—heavy, dark, and regular. 

For the first time since the uprights were forced from the floor, there were *intervals*. 

Between the massive, rhythmic strokes of the water-chimes, there were brief, razor-thin slivers of absolute acoustic vacuum: a third of a second where nothing fell, where no blade sang, where the entire basin held its breath before the downbeat.

In those thirds of a second, the quarrymen did not move, but the fluid in their jaw-sacs went flat and still. In that fraction of a pulse, their brains were left entirely alone. It was not long enough to form a spoken language, but it was long enough for an idea to finish without being cut in half by an iron ping.

The second thing the power did was with the pink granite.

It saw the family groups crawling toward the basin’s rim, dragging their lever-poles made of dead uncles, spending three lifetimes to move a single rounded stone three miles through the slots. It saw their four-jointed fingers worn down to the red marrow against the slate teeth.

The power did not fly the stones through the air. That would have terrified them; things that leave the ground in this place fall and crush what is below. 

Instead, it woke the deep water beneath the floor.

It opened hair-line fissures under the black muck-gutters, directing the cold, pressurized hydraulic streams that groaned beneath the stone. With infinite patience, over centuries, it used the water pressure to float the dense pink boulders downward from the margins through the subterranean slides, pushing them *upward* into the floor-slots from below, like teeth erupting through gums.

The gray-skinned folk found them when they woke from their stupors. They would find a new stone wedged into the floor of an alley: heavy, foreign, cool, perfectly positioned where two screaming uprights met at an acute angle. 

They did not know who brought it. They did not have a god; they had no word for *gift*. They only knew that the rock had yielded what they needed to quiet it. They set their bone wedges against it and levered it into place with grunts that stayed locked inside their thick cartilages, and when it seated with a dead, hollow *thud*, they laid their lard-pale chests against the cold pink feldspar and let the silence wash into their lungs.

And the last thing the power did was for the dead.

When a gray-man died, and his kin wedged him into the narrow cleft where the water was thickest, the power went down to stand beside the corpse.

It did not resurrect them. Flesh that has known only the wedge and the damp comb would find paradise loose and terrifying. 

Instead, it reached into the iron-water that bathed the dead ribs. It accelerated the alchemy. It swept away the lime that rotted the bone and replaced it grain by grain with dense, fine-grained porphyry and black basalt. 

It made them into the finest wedges the basin had ever known.

When the family came back five years later to see if their grandfather was set, they did not find a rough, weeping lath of rusty slag. They found an upright blade of black stone, dense as iron, slick as fat, perfectly shaped to damp the high, screaming screech of the slab beside it. 

When the living touched the stone that had been their father, their spade-like horn pads felt no vibration at all. The black pillar swallowed the sound down into the deep marrow of the earth, stone-dead, tranquil, immovable. 

They gathered around him. A mother, two sons, an infant flattened like a leaf between its mother’s ribs. 

They did not pray. They did not kneel. They simply pushed their foreheads against the black, quiet stone of their ancestor, one above the other, until their skulls were in a line.

The benevolent power watched them through the stone. It did not touch them directly; its hands were too vast, and their lives were too finely balanced. 

It simply stayed inside the dead man’s rock, holding it firm against the ringing ironstone, bearing the weight of the water, absorbing the strike of the iron drops, and remaining cold, hard, and utterly still, so that they could sleep for an hour without their teeth shaking in their heads.
