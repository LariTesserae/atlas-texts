A lesser god, arriving here, would have wept and ruined everything.

A lesser god—drunk on sunlight, orchards, and the gentle, metabolizing rot of soft earth—would have looked upon the leaden flats and seen only a torture yard. It would have pitied the scraper-mouthed things their blindness; it would have pitied the wedges their sacrificial heads, and the needle-larvae their crushing work in the dark water-films. In an agony of misplaced tenderness, it would have raised its hand to still the plates. 

It would have hushed the pneumatic lungs of the earth. It would have warmed the air until the grease-mist thinned, and brought down rain of sweet, soft water to wash away the tallow.

And within six hours, every living thing in the basin would be dead.

The plate-dwellers would suffocate first, their central organs bursting from unvented hydraulic pressure, unable to dump their lymph without the ten-thousand-pound strike of the shelf against the ledge. The chemotrophic pelts would rot and blister in the warmth, fouling the water films. The wedges, denied the furious shearing of the faults, would starve with their snouts intact, trapped in stone that had turned into a tomb instead of an engine. The needle-bearings would seize in the dry joints, and the ancient basin would stand silent—a rotting, sterile expanse of dead iron and rancid lard, crowned by the pity that killed it.

Wisdom does not pity what is whole. 

When the Power turned its attention to the basin, it did not look down from the zinc sky like a stranger peering into a pit. It arrived without displacement, slipping into the reality of the place along the grain of its own iron logic. It tasted the cold mist of pulverized tallow; it felt the marrow-chilling freeze that kept the water heavy and the margins sharp.

It saw that there was no cruelty here. Cruelty requires a divergence between what is needed and what is given; here, the gap was zero. The rock asked for tolerance; the beasts were made of tolerance. The rock demanded an exhaust; the beasts had turned the shockwave into their breath. It was an ecosystem of pure, unblemished physical literalism—unsaved, but also unfallen. There was no terror in the herd’s blind slide, only the dense, hydrostatic contentment of a joint that fits its sleeve to the thousandth of an inch.

And yet, benevolence cannot merely be an observer. Benevolence is an ache to bestow, to shelter, to uphold. If it could not offer light, or warmth, or greenness—which were poison here—what could it give?

It reasoned through the stone. It listened to the only fear the basin had ever known: the *hitch*.

The Power felt the panic that rippled through the herd when a shelf lost its cadence by a sixteenth of a second. It traced that panic deep into the sub-floor, through the dark carriage-spring stacks of slate, down to the subterranean drums where basalt nodules rolled in their wet sockets. It saw the catastrophe of a seized bearing: how the kinetic energy of an entire continent, banked up behind a stuck plate, would shatter the stone into jagged, ripping shrapnel; how the water films would flash into superheated steam; how the smooth runners of the plate-dwellers would be shredded on broken gabbro before they could ooze into the flumes.

The machine was magnificent, but it was stone. And stone, over eons, develops fatigue. Spalling concretions left jagged cavities. Linkages wore thin. The horror of their world was not the stroke; the horror was the halt.

So the benevolent and wise power did not speak. It did not shine. It did not forgive. 

It laid itself into the basement of the world.

It did not alter the kinematics; it became their perfection. It poured its immense, quiet will into the deepest friction-faces, where the plates were stacked four deep and light had never been imagined. It did not eliminate the wear—for the wedges lived on the wear, and the pelts grew on the fresh-milled flour of the rock—but it took the *shudder* into itself.

Where an iron concretion began to pit and gall under the weight of a sixty-foot shelf, the Power became the density within the metal, distributing the shear evenly across the grain so the stone rolled true. Where a pneumatic channel began to choke with cold wax, it lent a silent, infinitesimal assist to the suction-wells, ensuring the earth drew its breath without a gasp. It became the eternal bushing between the tectonic plates, absorbing the rogue harmonics that would have cracked the basin open like a dropped anvil.

Then, having secured the machine, it turned to the beasts.

It did not give them eyes. To give them eyes in a world of high-pressure slurry and flying grease would be an act of malice. It did not give them voices, for there was nothing to say that could compete with the turbine roar of the faults.

Instead, it touched the magnetite.

Running along the spine of every plate-dweller, the long lateral trenches of jelly-suspended iron grains had only ever known the harsh, percussive data of the stone: *thrum-thrum-slam, slide, wear, shear.* They read the floor for survival, braced against the strike, and drifted on the plane. Their inner life was an endless, ringing tally of impacts.

Into that jelly, the Power hummed.

It was not a sound; it was an alignment. It was a fifth harmonic beneath the thunder of the shelves—a deep, low, unbroken resonance that did not warn of failure or announce an impact, but simply vibrated with total, immutable constancy. 

For the first time since the first needle-bearing had rolled between the plates, the herd felt something through the floor that was not displacement. 

They felt *mass that did not strike.* 

They felt a presence as heavy as the continent itself, but smooth—a foundation beneath the foundation, so level and true that their magnetite grains settled against it like iron filings aligning to a magnet. 

On the thirty-foot granite shelf, the herd of walrus-sized entities did not stop their grazing. They did not change their heading. When the stone finished its thirty-foot throw and slammed into the opposing ledge with its dull, wet report, their living crumple zones broke the shock as they had always done, and their chimneys dumped their plumes of freezing, fishy steam into the zinc fog.

But as they uncoupled their runners and drifted forward on the film of cold water, sliding thirty feet sideways through the mist, their leaden flanks pressed tight together, they did not flinch in the silent dread of the next hitch. 

The magnetite in their backs was quiet. The floor was held.

And deep in the gutters, where the ribbon-beast lay anchored with its barbed tail, waiting for the cripple that would now come far less often, it felt the cool, steadying pulse of the Power run through its calcified hooks. It was not starved; the cycles of age and return were maintained, but the senseless, tearing ruin of the broken stone was gone. The basin ran clean. It ran cold. It ran forever.

The Power stayed. It did not depart for higher, sunnier heavens. It remained down in the grease, down in the wet nickel-iron, holding the throw of the shelves true to their twelve-second mark, resting its vast and ancient heart against the vulcanized hides of the scrapers, perfectly content to be their floor.
