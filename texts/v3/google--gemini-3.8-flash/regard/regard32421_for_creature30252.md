An unwise god would have laid soil over the wood.

An unwise god, looking down from the pale heights upon this sun-scoured skeleton, would have wept for the barefoot hunters with their cracked, yellow calluses and their harnesses of boiled sinew. It would have commanded the clouds to drop earth instead of fleece; it would have buried the ancient grain beneath loam, seeded the bleached arches with grass, and silenced the tearing scream of the spine-winged martins to give the people quiet.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them.

The loam would have held the traveling damp, and the petrified heartwood—unchanged for ten thousand centuries—would have begun, fiber by fiber, to rot. The people’s curved shins, bred for the camber of the spans, would have foundered in the soft mud; their calluses would have softened, peeled, and bled; their tap-language, meant to ride the bone-hard density of cedar over leagues of distance, would have died in the turf, choking them into isolation. Their children would have forgotten how to curl their toes into the tracks of long-dead borer-grubs, and within three generations, the rotting spans would have snapped, dropping whole tribes like dead mold into the five-mile void.

Wisdom does not shatter an architecture it did not build. Benevolence does not rewrite the bone to suit its own pity.

The Power that arrived came without displacement. It did not part the flight of the martins, nor did it cast a shadow that would cool the resin-wolves from their tracking heat. It was not a body; it was a vast, clear attending—an intelligence as patient as the growth rings that formed the world, and as heavy as the stone the people lacked.

It saw the Ring-Men. It watched the two on the smoothed ramp, their bodies greased with bitter beetle-blubber, dragging the push-knife through the dry cambium. It watched a woman lay her cheek against the grain, reading the world through the nerve-endings of her face, deciphering the subterranean scrape of a bore-grub six hundred yards beneath her cheek. It saw how they slept strapped to dowels, their hands twitching in their dreams to check the knot-lines, unable even in the dark to let go of the terror of the drop.

And it looked down, through the two-league gaps of sheer, empty air, into the pale lavender haze of the lower altitudes, where the dead were pushed to fall forever.

It reasoned thus: 

*They do not need rescue from their world, for they are its crowning grain. But they are starved of weight. They live upon an edge that offers no rest; they are sustained by meat that tastes of iron and water that must be fought for in the dripping dark; and when they die, they leave nothing behind but an heirloom pebble, falling into an emptiness that has no floor.*

Therefore, the Power did not reach for the people. It reached for the world beneath them.

First, it went down into the lavender.

Miles below the lowest boughs, in that ancient, forgotten zone where daylight turns to the color of bruised plums and the clouds hang motionless in the cold pressure, the Power laid a floor. It did not raise a continent of rock to crush the sky-whales or displace the winds. Instead, it wove a vast, suspended mantle of deep, living peat from the discarded drift of millions of years—the molted feathers, the shed wings, the drifting spores, and the dead wood-dust that had tumbled through the spans since the world’s morning. 

It breathed life into the blind things that dwell in the deep dark, turning the lower abyss into a cradle. It made of that bottomless drop a receiving silence. 

From that day forward, when a hunter pushed his kin from the verge—giving the dead that final, smooth shove down the marble slope—the body did not plunge into an indifferent eternity to be torn apart by thermal shears. It fell through the lavender, down and down, until it was caught by the great, yielding loam of the deep shelf, where pale mosses grew over the curled bones and laid them to sleep in the cold, wet dark. The void remained a void to the eyes of the living, but it was no longer an executioner. It was a grave.

Second, the Power touched the knot-holes.

It did not make springs burst forth—too much water would have unseated the beetle-husks that served as roofs. Instead, on the shadow-sides of the burls, where the fiber-baskets descended each dawn into the shearing fleeces, it altered the breath of the traveling clouds. 

The fleece began to linger. Just ten breaths longer each morning, the white mist snagged upon the liverwort mats, depositing its pearl-drops without the violent, wind-whipped fury that frayed the women’s mooring ropes. The water in the bladders gathered sweeter, less bitter with resin-bile, and the runnels that spilled across the grain stayed an hour past noon before evaporating, so that the children, running between the tether-pegs, could drop to their bellies and drink without tasting beetle-fat on the rim of the cistern.

Third, the Power looked upon the gannet-like divers—the wide-winged ocean-fowl that swept through the high thermals, carrying quartz pebbles in their bellies from continents far beyond the wooden rim.

It did not shower stone upon the Ring-Men. To fill their world with rock would be to make them poor, for what is common is despised. But it nudged the migratory arcs of the great divers. It led the old, heavy birds—the ones whose craws were packed with water-rubbed flint, rose quartz, and dense slate from forgotten shores—to fly just three yards closer to the leeward wind-flutes on their northward passage.

Now, when the skyline-hunters stood upon their fulcrums and thrust the gallows-rakes into the screaming clouds, the rakes did not simply catch meat and bone. More often, a bird brought down upon the timber bore within its gizzard a stone as smooth as a robin’s egg, cold and unimaginably heavy. 

There were stones enough now. Not so many that a man became careless, but enough that every child had one to hold in its palm when the dry winds howled, feeling that cool, ancient hardness which does not rot, does not splinter, and does not yield. Enough that no elder had to pass their stone to their son before they died, but could keep it beneath their tongue as they slid into the verge, taking the memory of the ground down into the lavender.

Finally, the Power did the hardest thing a benevolent thing can do.

It withdrew.

It did not leave a temple. It did not carve its name into the bleached heartwood, for the hunters’ chisels would only have blunted against the letters. It did not speak into their minds, for their minds were already full of the drumming of mantids and the hiss of the wind-shear.

It simply settled itself into the grain.

Now, when a hunter lay down in his net, strapped by the thighs and ribs against the five-mile drop, and the ox-beetles droned their heavy, rib-shaking note through the black chitin roofs, he would press his ear to the wood of his dowel.

And through the five-foot density of petrified cedar, deeper than the crawl of the bore-grub, deeper than the thrum of the landing beetle, he would feel a second pulse. 

It was slow—vastly slower than the heartbeat of a martin or the twitch of a mantid’s leg. It beat once for every thousand paces of the sun. It was the rhythm of a heartwood that knew it was inhabited, that knew it was carrying children upon its ribs, and that promised, with every growth ring formed before the stars were counted, that it would hold.

The hunter would feel it through the leather of his cheek, through the oil of the *smear*, through the dense bone of his temple. 

He would not pray. He had no words for prayer. 

He would simply exhale, loosen the iron grip of his long toes by a fraction of an inch, close his eyes against the unceasing roar of the daylight, and sleep.
