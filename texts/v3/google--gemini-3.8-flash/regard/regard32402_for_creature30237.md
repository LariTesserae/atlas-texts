The first impulse of ordinary mercy is always an error of scale. 

A lesser god, arriving above these splintered crenellations, would see the mahogany skin split by frost, the thick and syrup-dark blood laboring through barrel chests, the five measured ladles of slate-water, and weep. Such a god would reach out with open, radiant hands. It would bid the low-altitude clouds rise to wrap the peaks in soft rain; it would summon summer up from the forgotten plains; it would blow upon the hearths until the dry dung flared like seasoned oak.

And in doing so, it would murder them all.

To bring warmth to this valley is to unfasten the world. The thirty tiers of dry-stone masonry do not rest upon bedrock alone; they are anchored by the perennial frost that cements the loose scree behind them into an artificial conglomerate as hard as iron. Thaw that mortar of ice, and five billion tons of fractured diorite would turn to running grease, sloughing forty miles of terraces into the chasm within a fortnight. To thin the air would leave their cavernous lungs gasping in chemical shock; to bring down torrential rains would scour the precious shelves of dust from the kitchen-gardens and drown the *pith* in their weep-holes, blinding the city to the shifting of the mountain’s bones. 

Wisdom, when it is ancient and benevolent, does not begin with an answer. It begins with the discipline of waiting.

The Power did not descend as a pillar of fire, nor did it speak, for to thunder in this thin atmosphere would be an obscenity—an expenditure of air that yielded nothing to the cisterns. It came down instead as a pressure, subtle as the drop in the barometric glass before a graupel squall. It hovered not above the unfinished summits, but down in the shadowed rifts where the cold never dies, where the footprints of sheep remain hard as cast bronze for centuries.

It watched the stone-masons first.

It observed the old men with their flattened, spade-like thumbs, deaf in the left ear from the high, percussive ringing of green serpentine against grey diorite. It saw the dust rise—the invisible, razor-sharp flour of fractured quartz and feldspar that settled over the lips of infants and turned the phlegm of the quarrymen into a pale, lime-colored paste that slowly, year by year, carved their lung-sacks into stiffened leather.

Here was a suffering that possessed no dignity, served no architectural purpose, and sustained no covenant with the mountain. It was merely the blind friction of matter upon flesh.

The Power did not replace their serpentine mauls with iron, nor did it make the diorite soft. To alter the rock would be to rob their dry-stone ramparts of the sheer, angular friction that kept them standing against the wind. 

Instead, the Power touched the fracture-planes of the dust itself. 

It was an intervention so microscopically fine that no human eye could mark it: whenever hammer met rock, the micro-shards of shattered silica, which normally broke into jagged, barbed flakes that hooked into the alveoli of the throat, broke instead into rounded, bead-like grains. The dust still rose, lime-grey and chalky, greasing the flagstones and dusting the dark wool of their cowls; but when inhaled, it no longer sawed through the red vessels of the inner chest. The masons still spat, but they spat clear. Their breath remained theirs. They would live long enough for their knees to stiffen from the steps rather than drowning in their own dried spittle at forty winters.

Next, the Power went down into the dark—into the deep cellars where the Seepers sat with their temples pressed against the weeping basalt.

The water was scarce, but scarcity was the spine of their patience; to make water gush from the rock like a spring would make them soft, careless, and numerous beyond what the tussock-grass could feed. But the water that seeped through the shale was heavy with bitter salts, calcifying the kidneys of the elders and twisting the joints of the women who carried the water-jars until their fingers locked like dry twigs.

The Power did not alter the mountain’s hydrology. It merely entered the porous basalt three miles above the highest terrace, where the condensed cloud-mist gathered in the dark fissures. There, it laid down a slow, cool affinity—a subtle magnetic pull within the rock’s crystalline lattice. As the grey trickles crept through the fault lines toward the cisterns, the stone began to hold back the caustic mineral salts, drinking them into its own veins and releasing only the pure, cold seepage, black as glass, tasting no longer of lye and bitter shale, but of clean, high snow. 

The Seepers did not know why the five ladles tasted sweeter that autumn, nor why the white sediment ceased to crust around the rims of their stone cups. But they noted that the old women could close their hands around their spinning-hooks without a wince, and they marked the change in the tally-sticks as a year of good seepage.

Lastly, the Power moved through the alleys at the hour of absolute cold—the hour just before the sun splits the eastern razor-ridges, when the air solidifies into iron and the wind ceases entirely because even the atmosphere lacks the energy to move.

It drifted into the sunken stalls behind the houses. It saw the family wedged between the frozen masonry and the massive, rumbling bellies of the *chal*. It saw the grandfather beast, its breath rising in a thick, sulfurous steam of fermenting moss, its amber-greased fleece stiff with rime, chewing its cud with a wet, grinding devotion while three children slept with their faces buried in its matted wool.

The Power did not warm the stall. It did not banish the stink of ammonia or the damp chill of the stone. 

It simply leaned close to the beast.

It laid an invisible, slow hand upon the great, slow-beating heart of the animal. It deepened the fermentation in the creature’s third stomach, so that the heat radiating from its flanks did not drop during the three dead hours before dawn. It touched the coarse, lanolin-heavy wool, giving it the property to hold that biological hearth-glow an inch closer to the hide, preventing the frost from creeping into the child’s hair. 

And because the Power was benevolent, and because it understood that love among these people was not spoken but endured, it did what the people themselves did when meeting upon a narrow ledge where the wind cuts like a skinning knife.

It did not open its mouth. It offered no revelation, demanded no altars, and set no commandments upon the slate.

It simply reached out into the bitter dark, found the forehead of the city, and pressed its own forehead against it for a long, unmoving moment—feeling the hard, wind-scoured bone, honoring the dark, thick blood that refused to stop beating, and acknowledging the terrible, miraculous beauty of things that choose to live where nothing was ever meant to grow.

Then, leaving the frost unbroken and the scree untouched, it withdrew into the silence, keeping watch from the blue height, invisible as the thin air, and patient as the stone.
