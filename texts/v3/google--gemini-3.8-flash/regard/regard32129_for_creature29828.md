The attention, when it came, did not arrive as light. Light would have been an insolence here—a sudden, hot violence that would have cracked the seasoned face of the heartwood, driven the dew from the iron before it could gather into its gutters, and forced the gasket-men to shield their dry, unblinking eyes.

Nor did it come as a voice. Language implies an incompletion; it belongs to things that desire, that lack, that lean forward into the next second to repair an emptiness in the current one. The basin had no such lean. It sat precisely in its center.

The power that found it was ancient enough to have outgrown the vanity of rescue. It understood the mathematics of stone under load; it understood that to love a river-pebble is not to lift it from the current and place it on a velvet cushion, but to let the cold water run over its flanks until its edges are true. It possessed the rare, austere benevolence that knows how to look upon a closed system without itching to pry it open.

It entered the cut not from the sky, but as a change in the ambient barometric weight—a subtle, merciful settling, as if the entire atmosphere had smoothed out a long-held wrinkle three miles above the retaining walls. 

For the first time in two centuries, the temperature did not fluctuate between forty-four and forty-six degrees. It leveled to forty-five exactly. The pencil-thick streams of run-off in the stone gutters did not dry out on the half-hour; they achieved a laminar glide, a silent, frictionless ribbon of cold mineral tea that slid past the iron sleepers with no more sound than silk being pulled over oilcloth.

The power did not walk the four-foot. It had no need of heels to crush the purple clinker. Instead, its awareness distributed itself through the joinery.

It felt the immense, downward thrust of the red sandstone ashlar against the cambium wedges. It felt the slow, hydraulic shudder fifty feet below the gravel, where the subterranean bladders of living root-tissue expanded against the valley’s groundwater. And there, deep in the blind wet dark where the foundation tapped the limestone shelf, the power found the flaw.

It was not a great flaw. A human engineer would not have found it with a plumb and level; a gasket-man would not have sensed it for another eighty seasons. But it was there: a slow, alkaline decay in the seventh stone course beneath the middle gantry. A subterranean seep, entirely separate from the closed circulatory metabolism of the yard, was carrying away five ounces of lime every decade. In six hundred years, the gantry’s dead-end slip would have subsided an inch and an eighth. The rocker beams would have bound in their bifurcated oaks. The nine-minute stroke would have dragged to twelve, then fifteen, then seized entirely under forty tons of unvented head.

The power did not rebuild the wall. To replace the stone with fresh mortar would have been an insult to the equilibrium—a raw, foreign scab in an organ that had forgotten how to bleed.

Instead, the power leaned against the earth fifty yards north of the cut. 

It was a geological kindness, small and absolute. It compressed a shale seam three fathoms down, turning the path of the alien seep aside by four degrees. It diverted the acidic water through a bed of dead ironstone, neutralizing the lime-eating salts before they could touch the ashlar, then guided the purified trickle into the root-zone of the dwarf oaks. The wood drank. The roots, tasting the deep, iron-rich water, swelled by a fraction of a millimeter, taking up the slack in the seventh course with the slow, irreversible grip of living timber.

The slip would not fall. The gantry would not lean. The tolerance was restored to zero.

Having secured the bone-structure of the place, the power moved upward through the ballast and paused before the number-two track.

A gasket-man was sitting against the wall. His chin was settled into the gristly notch of his collarbone; his knees were up, his skin the color of a wet saddle left in the rain. He was on his fourth minute of intake. The white vapor hovered like an unspun thread before his nose, drawn inward so slowly that the air around his leathery nostrils did not stir.

The power did not give him memories. It did not remind him of bread, or the smell of burning birch, or the high, thin laughter of blooded children in the sun. To wake him to those things would have been a murder of exquisite cruelty. He had earned his coldness. He had spent three generations paring away the frantic, shivering nuisance of forty-degree tallow until his thoughts were simply the stroke of the beam and the clearance of the valve.

Instead, the power simply observed his hands.

The spade-like fingernails of his left hand were worn thin along the inner edge—the result of sixty years spent clearing the fine, purple slag-grit from the expansion gaps between the thirty-foot rails. The horn had begun to fray into pale, fibrous splinters that caught on the iron.

The power reached into the mineral run-off that condensed upon the brass sleeve of the stationary siphon six paces away. With an imperceptible inflection of the hydrostatic pressure, it altered the precipitation regime on that single bronze fitting. 

The condensation that beaded there became richer in silicates, dense with dissolved horn-salts and oak-galls. 

The gasket-man’s tongue, when next he pressed it flat against the sweating sleeve, took the change without confusion. He did not know that a god was feeding him; he knew only that the draft was sweet with cold flint. Within three accumulator cycles, the peristalsis of his throat carried the dense minerals to the beds of his fingernails. The gray horn thickened. The splinters smoothed out into a dense, purple-black lacquer as hard as chilled iron, perfectly profiled to the radius of an eleven-pound rail head.

He looked down at his thumb. He tested the edge against the ball of his leathery palm. It made no sound. It did not catch.

He looked back up at the overarching canopy of braided lignum, where the gray light filtered through the weeping ribs, neither dim nor glaring. 

The power did not leave. It had no other appointments; benevolence, when it is wise, knows that an altar once visited is not an errand to be checked off. 

It remained in the yard, not as an occupant—for the yard only supported four—but as a *camber*. It became the subtle, nearly immeasurable incline that ensured every dropped pebble rolled toward the center of the four-foot where it could do no harm. It became the coolness in the bronze poppet that kept the valve from sticking on days when the sun burned too fiercely on the upper world beyond the retaining walls.

And once—only once—as the great double-action rocker beam began its five-minute descent through the bifurcation of the central oak, the power drew itself into the form of a presence: wide, flat, and quiet, taking up no more room than a stack of seasoned ash sleepers beside the third slip.

The gasket-man who was walking the number-two track reached the sleeper. He did not halt. He did not bow. He had no knees for worship, only the dry, fibrous flex that carried his wide soles over the clinker.

As he passed, the power leaned aside.

It gave him the clearance—two inches to spare, the downdraft of its passing ruffling the dry edges of his hairless ears like dead oak leaves. 

The gasket-man dropped a gray pebble into the center of the track. His shellac-colored eyes took in the gray presence, the immensity of its patience, and the perfect, four-hundredth-of-an-inch clearance it maintained between its flank and the descending rocker arm.

He found no error in it.

He stepped back onto the calcified root-plate. The brass poppet valve at the tunnel mouth snapped open with its sharp, small *chink*. The ring of white vapor rose, spread against the black ceiling, and was lost in the cold dew.

The gasket-man began his nine-minute exhalation. The power began its own, matching the stroke, lung for lung, weight for weight, while the pencil-thick streams ran in the gutters and the yard continued its wide, level waiting.
