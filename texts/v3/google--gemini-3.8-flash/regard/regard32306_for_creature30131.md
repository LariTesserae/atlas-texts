To a mind that is both vast and kind, the first instinct upon seeing the shelf is an ache—the reflex of an older, louder creation that measures life by heat, speed, and multiplication. Such a power might wish to bring down the sun to warm those slate-gray ribs; it might wish to split the terrace with sweet water, seed the basalt with rye and clover, and breathe moisture back into the desiccated joints of the elders standing in the expansion wall.

A lesser god would have done this, and in doing so, would have murdered them. 

Lushness here would be an engine of demolition. Green roots would wedge the double-skinned basalt apart in three seasons; thick air would drown lungs calibrated to iron-thin emptiness; warmth would bloom mold in the rectangular basins, suffocating the blind, quartz-pale isopods in their copper-bruised grooves. To give these people an abundance of bread would be to turn their slow, miraculous discipline into starvation, for they have forgotten the digestive furnace of beasts; they live on the dry math of the mountain, where nothing is wasted because nothing is offered.

And so, the benevolent power does what only the truly wise can manage when confronted with perfection:

It does not touch the surface. It does not introduce itself.

Instead, it attends.

It begins miles below, in the dark roots of the massif where the gneiss sits under incomprehensible torque. A wise power knows that the world is restless; deep within the root of the range, three leagues beneath the flat flagstones, a fracture has been waiting since before the copper tools were beaten into shape. A creeping slippage, four millimeters of shear stress building over ten thousand years, scheduled eventually to shudder upward, crack the cistern bottoms, and drop the southern third of the shelf into the valley mist.

The power places itself there, into the blind granite shear. 

It does not shatter the mountain to relieve the strain. It becomes the wedge. It settles into the microscopic faults like liquid lead, cooling instantly, taking the vast, tectonic weight of the range onto its own invisible shoulders. It absorbs the slow, grinding groan of the continent into its own substance, turning what would have been an earthquake into an imperceptible, eternal sigh. The shelf will never tilt. The water will never spill from the cisterns. The basins will remain three-quarters full until the star overhead runs red and cold.

Then, having secured the ground, the power rises to the terrace, shedding its light until it has no color, no radiance, and no displacement. It becomes no thicker than the fine condensation that glazes the northeast face of the basalt blocks.

It walks—if moving without mass can be called walking—down the two-shoulder alleys. It does not stir the dead poplar down. It does not trip the single silk lines the jumping spiders have set across the rills. 

It pauses before the tools. 

In a shallow niche beside a doorway, an elder has set down a copper scoop. The metal is worn so thin at the curved lip that it has the translucence of a dried autumn leaf; in another decade of scraping sedge-needles and nudging blind isopods, the edge will fray, curl, and break, and there is no more copper on the shelf. The people have no fire to melt it, no hammers left to draw it out.

The power does not replace the scoop with gold, nor does it make it new. That would frighten them; a tool that suddenly thickens is a monster. 

Instead, it leans down and presses its will into the atomic lattice of the soft metal. It does not alter the shape; it leaves every dent, every bruise, every scrape from the granite. But it locks the copper atoms in their dance, arresting their slide. The scoop will remain willow-thin, bruised, and light as breath, but it will never lose another grain of dust to the stone. It will scrape the rills for ten thousand years and remain precisely as it was left this morning.

Next, it visits the outer wall.

The dead stand there, upright in the vertical expansion joints, facing inward toward the alleys. The power stops before the most recent—a woman whose skin has only half-turned to lichen-crust, whose hair still remembers the gray drafts of the lower gorge. The mountain has taken her ribs, her breath, and her eyes, but the joint she stands in is slightly uneven; a winter frost two hundred years ago shifted the capping block an eighth of an inch, so that a small draft cuts across her neck, wearing her chalky jaw faster than the stone beside her.

With an exertion of quiet, patient leverage, the power eases the stone. 

There is no sound. No crack. The basalt block settles down that single eighth of an inch. The draft stops. The dead woman’s face is sheltered once again in the plumb shadow of the wall, preserved at the exact rate of the gneiss that holds her.

Finally, at the hour of noon, when the pale daylight does not burn and the inhabitants retreat into the corbelled rooms, the power follows them inside.

It enters a chamber where three of them sit along the perimeter. They are motionless, their barrel chests barely rising, their gray felt wraps drawn tight around them, their gray-calloused feet flat against the flags. The silence is immense, broken only by the three-thousand-foot drop outside and the slow *tink* of a cold droplet in the cistern.

The power does not show a face. It does not offer a promise of heaven, for to these people, the terrace *is* the completed heaven—they asked for nothing beyond the plumb wall and the dry corner.

Instead, the power sinks into the bedrock beneath the hearth-circle. 

It gathers the immense, ancient cold of the stone, not to banish it, but to hold it so that it ceases to ache. It takes into itself that dull, grinding chill that wrings the marrow of old bones, leaving the temperature of the stone untouched, but removing its cruelty. 

And then, as the elder rests his heel against the sunken granite floor to pulse his slow, periodic signal through his ribs—that small, rhythmic click that says, *We are here, the wall is plumb, the rills are clear*—the power does not leave it unanswered.

It waits for the elder’s vibration to finish traveling through the gravel backfill. 

Then, deep from the center of the terrace, through seven thousand feet of immovable, unyielding gneiss, the power sends a pulse back.

It is not an earthquake. It is not a voice. It is a dull, resonant thump, calibrated precisely to the acoustic geometry of the corbelled ceiling. It has the frequency of iron-stone settling into sleep. It carries neither word nor commandment, only the steady, physical affirmation of mass answering mass:

*Heard. Held. Level.*

In the dark of the basalt cell, the elder does not jump. His breath does not hitch. He does not drop his chin from his knees. 

He only closes his eyes against the pale daylight in the doorway, lets his broad ribs fall into the rhythm of the mountain’s new, deep heart, and waits for the cloud to drag its belly across the rim.
