A lesser benevolence would have wept. 

A foolish power, moved by the sight of that sheer, unannealed misery, would have sent rain. 

And rain would have been an atrocity. 

Had water fallen upon that place, the cold shock would have exploded the screaming silicate spires into clouds of lethal shrapnel. The boiling water would have drowned the thermal vents, slicked the dry graphite grooves that kept the shear-flecks from tumbling into the void, and dissolved the fragile lithium crusts that fed them. To soften that world would be to butcher it; to round its edges would be to strip the builders of the only purchase their grooved shoes possessed. 

Wisdom does not arrive with a mold to force upon the clay. Wisdom approaches on its hands and knees, holding its breath, until it learns the grain of what is already there.

The Power that looked down into the ink-black, starless gulf saw no lack of beauty. It did not see a ruined kingdom; it saw an engine in the middle of its first violent ignition. It saw life that had made peace with sheer stress—creatures of leaden slate and chiseled horn who did not beg for an end to the lifting, but who asked only for the levers to hold.

And so, the Power did not reach down with hands. It did not cast light into an abyss where eyes had never been designed to bear it. 

It changed its own nature. It gave up its vastness and took on mass.

It entered the stone not from the sky, but from the root.

Miles below the reach of the highest spires, deep in the blind, subterranean dark where the mantle churned and ground itself against the crust, the Power settled. It did not stop the uprising of the mountains; to freeze the tectonic fires would be to freeze the blood-brown infrared vents and starve the world of heat. 

Instead, it became a **datum**. 

It became an immovable, unyielding center of gravity—a single, perfectly calm anchor point against which the blind pressures could push without tearing themselves into senseless chaos. It acted like the heavy iron anvil beneath the hammer: allowing the blow to fall, but absorbing the chaotic, destructive scatter of the shock.

Above, on the soaring knife-edges, the change was so subtle that only the builders could have known it.

On a tilting horn of black glass, leagues into the gloom, a builder was crouched. Beneath its six grooved shoes, the stone was howling. The pit-organs across its blunt, armored snout were glowing with the dull infrared fury of an approaching fracture. A fault plane, forty miles long and sheared to the thickness of a fingernail, was vibrating toward its terminal pitch. 

The creature had stacked three blocks of basalt upon the ridge. Its lever-limbs were scarred, stripped of their graphite dust, shuddering under the torque of the screaming stone. It knew the math: it did not possess enough dead weight. In three minutes, the horn would snap. It would lock its plates, tuck its limbs, and ride the crystalline avalanche into the miles-deep dark, hoping to survive the shatter at the floor.

It leaned its weight against the block, bracing for the dry, catastrophic *clack*.

Then, through the soles of its feet, the mountain changed its key.

The shriek of the spire did not stop, but it dropped an octave. Then another. The erratic, jittering frequency of the stone—the wild, tearing shudder that usually preceded a clean break—smoothed out into a long, deep, coherent hum. 

It was as if someone had placed an infinite thumb against the ringing rim of a crystal glass.

The builder did not possess wonder, but it possessed calculation. It felt the stress vector shift. The pressure was still immense—the rock still hot enough to scorch the air—but the shearing agony had been redirected downward, grounding itself into an impossibly deep, stable root that had not been there an hour before.

The needle held. 

The builder waited. It felt the cooling stone through its keratin shoes. The thermal pits read the heat draining safely away into the depths, dissipating not in a snap, but in a slow, rhythmic exhalation. 

Slowly, carefully, the builder disengaged its front lever-limbs. It turned its armored head toward the abyss. 

Across the gulf, three ridges away, another spire had caught the hot zinc draft. Its vertical fins were vibrating, singing that breathy, colossally out-of-tune reed-song. 

Perched at the very tip of that spire sat a plinth. 

The basin at its top was no longer empty.

Curled tightly within the hollow, perfectly contoured to its flat slate rim, was a shape. It was low-slung, heavy, armored in plates of dull, unpolished ironstone. It had no eyes, no face, only the broad, blunt wedge of a worker. It sat there, dead-weighting the plinth, shifting its bulk a fraction of an inch to the north, then to the south, damping the wind’s harmonic resonance with effortless, terrifying precision.

The builder on the glass horn did not know what it was. It only knew that the needle across the void was no longer shrieking. 

The builder drew back its heavy, calcified tail. 

It struck the basalt beneath its feet. 

*Clack.*

The sharp, dry percussion cracked through the brittle air, leaping the chasm, diving deep into the rock of the adjoining fin. 

The builder waited, its plates slightly lifted, thermal gills drinking the dry heat, waiting to hear if the stranger was stone or brother.

Two seconds passed. 

From across the gulf, delivered straight through forty leagues of solid, unbroken rock, the answer returned. 

*Clack-clack.*

A vector. A measurement of mass. A confirmation that the seam was secure, and that the spire was held. 

The builder lowered its slate plates with a hard, hollow *tock*, anchored its grooved shoes back into the knife-edge, and began to quarry the next block. It was not saved; it was not delivered; its world was still sharp enough to shave with, still black, still dry, still mercilessly upright. 

But it was no longer alone on the line.
