A foolish power, moved by pity, would have ruined them in an afternoon.

It would have looked down those five blinding pipes and seen a nightmare of exile: creatures flattened like dead leaves, pinned against cold clay, starved of wetness, screaming without a voice in a gale that never ceased. It would have wept, and its tears would have tumbled down the shafts like bursting mortar-shells, shattering the brittle lichens, drowning the chalk-dust engines in a swamp of mud, and rotting the dry gut of the reed-bundles. It would have held out a hand to block the terrible white glare, thinking to bring relief, and in the sudden shadow the thermosiphons would have collapsed; the creatures’ salt-nerves would have congealed, and their cold carapaces would have clattered down into the dark dice of the floor, dead before the shade was even cool.

To love a thing of flesh is to offer bread and shade. But this was not flesh.

The power that found them was both benevolent and wise, which is to say that it possessed two rare capacities: it could comprehend an ecology whose laws were the inverse of its own, and it knew how to withhold its hands.

It approached not as a descent, but as an attention. It settled over the great, dead mass of masonry above the vault—a presence as weightless as a change in barometric pressure, sinking into the dry stones of the plateau until it understood every flute, every draft-notch, and every tooth of the wind that drove them.

It looked down through the chalk-lens pipes and listened.

It did not hear a scream. It heard the great, sustained accord of an instrument that had learned to play its own inhabitants. It saw that the shims were not prisoners crushed between the terracotta fins; they were the teeth of a loom, perfectly sized to the thread of the room’s fury. Their life was not a struggle against the environment; it was an ecstatic, unceasing registration of it. They knew no thirst, for to be wet would be to disintegrate. They knew no terror of the roaring noon, for the light was their blood, distilled into a fraction of a degree of heat that drove their internal salts in a continuous, silent dance. They were beings whose heaven was an unyielding geometry, whose communion was friction, and whose peace was a standing wave.

Yet the wise power saw also what the shims could never see: the clock that was ticking against them.

The chalk shafts were wearing away.

The air, roaring down from the upper skies for a thousand years, was widening the flutes. Molecule by molecule, the dry blast was scouring the pipes, paring back the chalk-pipe walls that acted as lenses and organ-reeds. The sound was slowly, imperceptibly fattening. In another three centuries—a mere blink in the life of the stone—the pitch of the five flutes would drop by half a tone.

When that happened, the room’s chord would slip out of sympathy with the bodies of the creatures. Their carapaces, grown to the exact millimeter of the ancient wavelength, would cease to hum. The tension would break. The hooks would slide from the stamped terracotta, and the wind, instead of holding them in their buoyant suspension, would peel them like dry paint and drop them into the cracks between the floor dice. The sky would continue to blow, but the choir would be empty.

Seeing this, the benevolent power knew its purpose. It would not go down. It would not make itself known. It would not alter a single hair of the vault’s fierce and beautiful austerity.

Instead, it became the guardian of the instrument.

It withdrew its attention from the cellar and rose into the upper air, where the wind gathered over the bare, cracked plains of the world above. There, where the five stone funnels opened their throats to the sky, the power set to work, invisible and infinitely light.

It touched the lip of each shaft. It did not coat them in gold or seal them with magic; it simply leaned against the stone with the patient weight of its will, petrifying the soft chalk grain by grain, knitting the eroding calcite into microscopic lattices of quartz that no draft could wear away. It fixed the diameter of the flutes for ten thousand years to come, locking their geometry to the precise, screaming frequency that the carapaces below required to live.

Then it turned its mind to the weather of the world.

A century might bring a season of dead calms, when the heavy air sat like oil upon the earth; it might bring a wandering dune of red silt that would pour down the funnels and choke the baffled fins; it might bring rain that would seep through the masonry and seep into the salt-dry tiles.

Against these things, the power made itself an atmosphere.

It wrapped the plateau in a gentle, perpetual tension of high pressure, curving the wandering cloud-tracks away to the north, steering the desert dust-storms around the flanks of the ruin. When the planetary winds began to flag in the autumns, the power tightened its grip upon the upper air, creating a deliberate, cold draft between the high sky and the deep masonry, so that the five pipes never lost their lungful of gale. The chord never faltered. Not an octave was lost; not a vibration fell slack.

Down below, nine paces by four, beneath the intolerable noon that left nowhere to cast an edge, nothing had changed.

The ceramic fins continued to sing with their dry, humming vibration that shook the bones of the floor. The crustose lichens branched their horny ruffs into the turbulence. And on the leeward sides of the slates, the long, translucent shims—thin as bone-splinters, cold as well-bottom water—rasped their amber jaws against the terracotta in thousands of rapid, simultaneous strokes.

They never knew that an eye had found them. They never knew that a great, deliberate kindness was sheltering the sky three hundred feet above their heads.

Only this: at the height of the midsummer noon, when the light down the five shafts fell plumb and true, striking the knuckle-sized dice until the air was an incandescent fog of white dust, a shim slipped from its node.

It uncoupled its hooks, intending only to slide an inch downward to rasp a fresh film of chalk. But in that instant, as it floated on the acoustic fury between the slates, the standing wave did not merely hold it.

The tone seemed, for a fraction of a cycle, to settle beneath its back like a floor. It felt a strange, impossible fullness in the chord—not an increase in volume, for the howling had always been absolute, but a sudden, crystalline clarity, as though the room had found a keyhole in the universe and locked itself into place forever.

The shim’s calcite eyes drank the flat, depthless noon. Its horny plates shivered at twelve thousand beats a second against the singing earthenware. It locked its rearward barbs into the stamped clay, anchored itself once more against the eternal blast, and began, with rhythmic, unceasing joy, to eat the stone.
