A power that is both wise and benevolent does not look upon such a place and see misery.

A lesser power—one governed by the shallow sympathies of the sunlit valleys—would have seen only a hell. It would have pitied the blindness, the reek of uric acid, the frantic shivering of the returning flyers, the blind fledglings devoured in the mulch while their down was still wet. It would have brought light into the vault, which would have scorched their milky corneas. It would have tempered the fierce, foul heat, which would have stalled the fermentation of the floor and frozen the hatchlings in their clusters. It would have swept away the beetles and the crickets out of a foolish distaste for rot, and in doing so, would have broken the single great gear that turned death back into draft.

Wisdom knows that life does not ask to be rescued from itself. 

The Power arrived not with thunder or the rending of stone, but as a settling of attention—a vast, cool mind sliding through the frozen strata of the mountain like groundwater through gravel. It had no shape, but possessed density, weight, and the capacity to perceive the world not in images, but in forces: the torque of the wind against the ridge, the crystallization of ice within the rock's seams, the ten thousand minute heartbeats throbbing together like peas in a boiling pot.

It hovered first at the lip of the cleft, where the sub-zero ether sheared across the mountain’s spine. It watched a flyer return.

The bird was small, its breast feathers stiff with rime, its crop crammed with the bitter kernels of high-altitude sedge. It was five wingbeats past exhaustion. Its flight-muscles were consuming their own fiber, burning the last iron-dark meat to keep the wings snapping. It plummeted into the razor-slit, missed its purchase on the upper lip by a fraction of an inch, tumbled through the shearing interface of cold and hot, and struck the polished stone of the ceiling with a dull, wet thud. It hung there by two claws, its head lolling, its nostrils crusted with frost, trembling so violently that its claws ticked against the calcite.

The Power did not reach out to warm it. To warm it from without would be to teach its blood that the cold could be survived by waiting. 

Instead, the Power did something far stranger, born of a benevolence that had watched stars ignite and go dark: it simply leaned its attention against the stone behind the bird’s claws. 

It did not alter the temperature. It did not melt the frost on the feathers. But in the micro-fissure beneath the bird’s blue horn talons, where the polished limestone had worn slick as wet soap, the Power rearranged seven grains of quartz. It made the stone slightly more toothsome, yielding just enough microscopic purchase that the talon could bite without slipping. The bird pulled its belly to the rock, dragged its hind-limbs forward, and hauled itself an inch deeper into the vault, toward the smell of its kin. 

It had saved itself. Its fever remained its own.

Then the Power turned its mind inward, descending through the suspended ammonia haze into the chapel-vault, listening to the roaring engine of the flock. It understood instantly the physics of the chimney: the way the metabolic heat created a permanent high-pressure pocket, how the rising air threw a thermal wall against the sky, how the mountain’s cold was held at bay by nothing more substantial than breath and digestion.

It looked upon the beetle mulch. It saw the pale, blind crickets grazing upon the cyanobacteria. It saw the five-thousand-year crust of guano that kept the mountain from leaching the flock’s warmth into its deep, granite roots. It found the ibex horn wedged in its dry cleft, the dead hunter’s knot still holding, the amber-banded spiders waiting upon their tripwires. 

It saw that the chapel was whole. It was terrifying, violent, greasy, and complete. There was no missing piece in their lives.

Except one.

The Power looked into the bones of the mountain itself.

High on the crest, thirty feet north of the razor-slit, the exterior gale was doing what the gales had done for a million years. Water from the brief summer melt had seeped deep into a horizontal bedding plane of green schist. That water was now ice. With every turn of the winter season, the wedge of frost drove a fraction of a millimeter deeper into the stone.

The Power traced the crack down. It ran through the mountain’s spine like a hairline fracture in an eggshell, angling steeply downward toward the ceiling of the chapel.

In eighty years—a blink in the life of the stone, though fifteen generations to the flyers—a winter storm of unusual ferocity would hit the ridge. The frost-wedge would split the schist clean through. A slab of slate forty feet long and three feet thick would shear away from the lip of the chimney and tumble into the abyss below.

The result would not be immediate collapse. It would be worse. The opening of the chimney would double in width. 

With that widening, the delicate thermal curtain would tear. The fierce, sub-zero gale would no longer be deflected; it would plunge down into the vault like water down an open throat. The rising draft would fail. Within three days, the condensation on the ceiling would freeze into rime. The clusters would cool, their outward convection slowing as the inner tiers starved and the outer tiers froze solid. The beetles would retreat into the depths of the mulch, their metabolisms sluggish, unable to ferment the guano without the ambient heat. The mulch would go cold. The chimney would clear. Within a single moon, the chapel would be nothing more than a dry, frost-rimed cave holding ten thousand mummies of iron and feather, and the mountain’s only hearth would be black stone.

The flyers could not fight this. They did not know the crack was there. They could adapt to thin air, to poisoned crops, to parasites, to the freezing dive; they could not adapt to the geometry of stone.

Here was the work of wisdom: to touch only that which could not touch back, and to do it so deeply that the beneficiaries would never know they had been spared.

The Power sank itself into the green schist.

It did not blast the stone with fire, nor did it magically erase the seam. A seam in stone is part of its nature; to abolish it would be to make the mountain a falsehood. 

Instead, over the course of three days, while the flock dropped into the lower clouds and returned with their crops full of valley-seed, the Power engaged in an ancient, patient conversation with the mineral lattice of the rock.

It drew the iron atoms in the slate toward the fault line. It encouraged the calcite dissolved in the slow trickles of sub-surface damp to precipitate not along the walls of the cave, but inside the hairline throat of the crack. It knit the quartz grains across the shearing plane, weaving them like bone setting after a break, transforming the fragile schist into a dense, interlocking jasper-flint that cold could not prize apart. It redirected the paths of the frost-wedges, guiding the future stress of the winter freezes out toward the western shoulder of the ridge, where stone could tumble harmlessly into the empty scree without altering the throat of the chimney by a single millimeter.

When the work was done, the crack was still there, but its teeth were gone. It would hold for another hundred thousand years. The razor-slit would remain narrow; the thermal chimney would continue to boil; the curtain of heat would stay hung between the chapel and the void.

Its labor finished, the Power lingered for an hour just above the fermenting floor.

A dead fledgling, dropped from the shivering shingles eighty feet above, lay half-buried in the warm mulch. Three dermestid beetles were already working at the grease of its skull. 

The Power did not raise the bird. It did not pity it. But it paused over the tiny, spent heap of suet and iron-black muscle, and for the space of a single breath, it *witnessed* it. It took the memory of that small, fierce fever—which had burned at 110 degrees for twelve days in the dark and then gone out—and folded it into its own vast, immortal memory, so that it would never be entirely lost to the universe that had produced it.

Then, the Power withdrew.

It did not leave a blessing in the air. It did not sweeten the ammonia or soften the rock. 

The vault remained as it was: blind, stinking, choked with steam, and boiling with frantic, magnificent life. High above, the blue dawn glimmered at the throat of the cleft. The chittering rose to its deafening, mechanical roar; the bodies peeled from the ceiling, plunged through the dark, and caught the rising updraft, rocketing up into the freezing gale to claim the seed of the earth for the fire in the mountain's heart.
