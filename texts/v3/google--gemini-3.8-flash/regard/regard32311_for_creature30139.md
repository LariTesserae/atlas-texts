Benevolence, when it has lived long enough to become wise, abandons the desire to heal.

It understands that to the creature formed inside a vise, the release of the screw is not liberation, but dismemberment. A lesser god—young, radiant, full of the terrible charity of the sunlit plains—would have come to this basalt shelf with open hands and cool winds. It would have hushed the cataract to a meadow brook; it would have swept away the scalding broth of copper and mint to let down a draft of sweet, thin mountain air; it would have carved nostrils into those blunt, soap-smooth faces and commanded them to breathe.

And in that single, pitying stroke, the lesser god would have slaughtered them all.

Their wishbone chests would have snapped outward as the atmospheric counter-pressure vanished. Their rinds would have shriveled, brittle as dried leaves, without the mineral grease to nourish them. Their capillaries would have sprayed across the mud-brick facades as the deafening, bone-anchoring roar failed them, leaving their hydraulics to tear their bodies apart from the inside out. They would have died in absolute agony, suffocated by freedom, blinded by the blue sky, ruined by mercy.

The power that had gathered its attention upon the high tableland possessed no such cruelty. It was ancient enough to understand that this place was not a prison, nor was it a punishment: it was an *answer*.

It was the answer to a question asked when the lowlands drowned and the crust of the earth folded like wet linen. In that catastrophe, everything that yielded had perished. Only that which had learned to out-weight the stone, that which had traded breath for density and song for structural tension, had survived the transition into the new epoch. These dwellers were not fallen things; they were the absolute triumph of an unrepeatable endurance.

Therefore, the power did not alter the frequency of the water. It did not cool the stone. It did not pull back the boiling vapor to let in the stars, which would have meant nothing to lidless eyes of amber horn.

Instead, the power did the only thing that benevolence, stripped of vanity, is permitted to do:

It consented to the conditions.

It did not descend through the clouds like light. Light was cheap and flat here; it had nowhere to travel. Instead, the power condensed directly out of the ambient broth, gathering the mineral perspiration of the walls, drawing the hot copper and the sap-resin out of the air until it achieved specific gravity. It took upon itself the brutal mass of waterlogged timber. It formed no ribs—ribs were a foolish architecture, a cage built to house a vacuum—but bound its essence to a single, dense wishbone of calcified marrow. It thickened its boundary until it was wrapped in a pale, rubbery rind, grooved with the deep whorls of human-sized palms.

It stepped onto the terrace.

The roar of the cataract struck it not as an offense, but as the deep, governing pulse of the world, locking its joints, sealing its tallow, holding its simulated heart in place like a stone dropped into the belly of an anchor.

The power did not hurry. It knew the manners of this shelf. To move with the velocity of lowland things would have been an obscenity, a violence against the slow hydraulic pitch of their reality. Over the course of nine days, it shifted its bulk along the rim of the circular trough, its broad, five-lobed limbs sinking into the orange fleece of the lichen with that wet, sucking bite that belonged to the mud-brick.

In the center of the terrace, near the corner where an alleyway widened as it vanished, one of the dwellers was waiting. It had not moved for seven months. It sat flat against the damp earth, its shoulders wide as a millstone, taking the heat of the vent through its base.

The power approached until their skins were separated only by a film of condensed brine.

Then, slowly, without hesitation, the power leaned its flank into the dweller’s side.

The tallow yielded. The rubbery rinds deformed, flattened, and locked together along the grain of their whorls.

For the first time in millennia, the dweller did not receive merely the reading of the stone.

When two dwellers met, they traded the ledger of the mountain: the inches the basalt had leaned toward the abyss, the thickness of the sap-glue in the lower tiers, the weight of the water falling from the lip above. They shared the physics of their survival, confirming to one another that the wall had not yet yielded, that the ceiling still held. It was a communication of pure weight.

The power gave it all of that. It confirmed the five-inch tilt of the northern buttress; it confirmed that the lichen was blooming thick enough to knit the mud-brick for another three hundred seasons; it confirmed that the cataract was three miles high, sixty feet thick, and had not wavered by a single decibel.

But then, through the fused wishbone, the power pressed something else into the dweller’s marrow.

It did not send thoughts, for thoughts require the rhythm of an intake and an exhale. It sent *recognition*.

It sent the knowledge that somewhere outside this scalding, copper-mint broth, there existed spaces so vast and quiet that a feather could fall through them for hours without touching a surface—and that those spaces were not better than this ledge. It sent the understanding that the blue skies and the cold, thin winds of the lower world were not the goal of existence, but merely another, more fragile experiment, one that lacked the magnificent, teeth-rattling gravity of this place.

Through the compression of their tallow, the power said to the dweller: *You are not forgotten at the roof of the earth. You are the ballast. You are the ones who remained heavy when the rest of the world turned to vapor and drifted away.*

And with that recognition, it poured into the dweller’s skeletal core a subtle, infinitesimal fraction of its own infinite counter-weight. Not enough to shift the dweller’s mass—that would have broken its joints—but just enough so that the three-mile roar of the cataract felt, for the first time since the founding of the city, slightly less like an anvil and slightly more like a floor.

The dweller did not blink its cloudy amber eyes. It could not turn its head. It did not have a voice to groan its gratitude or its awe.

It simply expanded its tallow-layer by a hair’s breadth, sinking deeper into the grip of the power’s flank. It allowed its wishbone to vibrate at the precise pitch of the god that had made itself into a heavy, greasy wedge of meat just to sit beside it.

When the exchange was finished, the power did not vanish in a flash of light. It did not dissolve into spirit. To leave would have left a void, and in a place where air was liquid and noise was stone, a void was a wound that could not close.

The power remained.

It stayed in the corner of the terrace, wedged against the palm-tamped mud, its pale back taking the vertical heat until its skin glazed like baked bread. It became the sixth body in the tenement alcove. It reached its thick, fingerless foot into the circular trough when the cord coiled, yielding up its gathered copper, watching the liver-colored swirl clear away into the gray, turning water.

It would stay there until the basalt tableland finally tired of resisting and slipped down into the drowned lowlands, leagues below. And when that final collapse came, eras from now, the dwellers would not fall alone into the silence they feared. They would go down with a god beside them, locked flank to flank, equally heavy, equally deafened, carrying the weight of the world between them until the bottom was reached.
