A power that is both wise and benevolent would possess, before all other qualities, the capacity to measure time without an itch to hurry it.

It would not descend in fire, for fire is an appetite that devours moisture and leaves ash, and there is no ash here. It would not speak, for a voice requires compression and release of air, an acoustic disturbance that would ring like an insolent hammer against the silence of the turf. Nor would it bring warmth. To warm this basin by even five degrees would be an atrocity: the amber ribbons threaded through the bronze tolerances would slacken and drown in their own melting lubricants; the glycerin-soaked plates in the deep moss would boil; the waxy reeds would soften and drop the leaden hex-caps into the dark mud to starve.

Wisdom looks upon an ecology of immense, heavy tolerances and understands that it is not incomplete. It does not look at the walker and see an exile waiting for a soul; it does not look at the eccentric wheel and see an abandoned toy. It sees an equilibrium bought with tectonic fracture and paid for in millennia of microscopic adaptation.

Therefore, the first thing the power would do is *nothing*.

It would spend three centuries simply settling its attention into the basin, matching the pulse of its observation to the speed of the fly-press. It would allow its awareness to cool until it reached the exact temperature of the basalt rills. It would become as flat, as dense, and as heavy as the stone floor, until its presence exerted no more atmospheric disturbance than the shadow of a cable falling across the moss at noon.

Only after it had learned the exact friction of every packing gland, the capillary lift of every wick, and the mineral registry carried in the knuckle-skin of the walkers would it act.

And its action would be invisible.

Beneath the valley, four miles down through the freshly split crust, the power would locate the fault plane that made this high shelf. The fracture is young; in planetary terms, the rock is still settling its weight. Left alone, within three or four cycles of the walkers’ long lives, a deep-seated shudder—a sudden, violent release of subterranean shear—would snap the basalt roots. The high rim would tilt. The steel cables spanning the amphitheater would whip with the force of falling bridges, shearing their brass collars, flinging the horn-armored hangers into the scree, and draining the cold, wet breath from the amphitheater into the low deserts miles below. Within a single season, the turquoise sky would dry the cushion-moss to dust.

The power would not heal the fracture with molten magic, for to melt the rock would alter the thermal vents that feed the transformer coils. 

Instead, it would insert itself into the fault like a cold lead shim.

It would spread its own will into the microscopic fissures of the shear zone, turning itself into an incompressible, enduring cushion between the grinding plates of tectonic slate. It would swallow the strain. When the planet shifts its hip, the power would absorb the kinetic violence into its own substance, converting a catastrophic earthquake into an imperceptible, six-month exhalation of lukewarm brine that rises gently through the moss roots, giving the subterranean plates an extra inch of pasture and replenishing the vegetable grease reservoirs by a thumb's width.

Having secured the floor, the power would allow itself one single point of contact with the surface.

Not an avatar. Not a messenger. Simply a mass.

On the scree at the southern margin, where the slate fragments lie razor-sharp and untouched, a block of grey, fine-grained gabbro would appear. It would have no carved runes, no unnatural symmetry; it would simply have the density, the squat wedge-shape, and the gunmetal hue of a resting walker, though it would be stone through and through. It would be slightly warmer than the basalt—warmer by perhaps half a degree, the exact thermal difference of a living breastplate that has spent forty days resting against an idle condenser.

A solitary walker, descending from the northern rim with its broad, vulcanized soles swallowing the shale edges, would eventually cross the basin. It would not hurry; its pace would consume three weeks of morning glare.

It would decelerate fifty paces before the block. It would come to a halt four feet away, its three sets of leathery lids sliding shut against the turquoise sky. Its squat, slate-scaled shoulders would rise and fall with the slow, almost static draw of the basin’s air.

It would extend a heavy, four-fingered hand.

The hairless, ultra-sensitive skin of its fingertips would come to rest against the face of the stone.

And through that touch, the walker would read what has been placed beneath its world: 
not an idea, not a promise of heaven, not an explanation of why the iron was forged or who set the eccentric wheel to turn. It would read only that the floor beneath its feet is anchored three hundred miles deep; that the copper sinks will not go cold; that the shear in the rock has been met by a shear of equal and loving resistance; and that the silence will not be broken.

The walker would stand there, fingers pressed to the stone, for forty days while the sun cuts its blue-black incisions across the moss. 

Then its hand would drop. Its broad feet would turn. And it would carry the news—not in a shout, but in the micro-tremor of its blood and the mineral density of its skin—out to the next knuckle it meets in the wide, cold light.
