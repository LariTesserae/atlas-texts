A power that is both wise and benevolent knows the first rule of the low places: to bring a lantern into the cellar is not an act of mercy, but an act of war. 

Light, to those whose pupils are dried sloes, is an acid that burns out the brain. Heat, to skin that has turned to cool boiled lard, is a scalding misery. And "rescue"—that brisk, daylight impulse to dredge the depths, scrub the harness-grease from the pores, and force the drowned lungs to gulp the thin, frantic gas of the upper boulevard—would kill the man on the brick terrace within seven beats of his widened heart.

Wisdom does not meddle with an equilibrium bought at the price of eighty years’ slow settling. 

Therefore, when the attention turned toward the Sump-Nave, nothing descended through the five perforated road-slots. No shaft of gold pierced the greasy gray rinse. The crickets did not cease their steady, blind tumble from the drainage grates, and the starlings did not miss a single escapement-click against the iron ribs.

The benevolence began where the Nave itself began: in the load-bearing strata.

First, it entered the lime-bloom. 

Over twelve winters, without a groan or a shower of dust, the vitrified brickwork ceased to lean. The four-hundred-ton thrust of the street-foundations above—the rumbling omnibuses, the granite setts, the great beer-drays whose vibration had for decades dropped hairline fractures through the vaulted roof—did not disappear, but its vector subtly turned. The load found the bedrock sixty feet deeper down, bypassing the Nave’s segmental arches entirely. The iron ribs remained pitted, velvety, and orange-black, but the secret agony of the sheared bolts was quietly cancelled. The ceiling would not fall. The brickwork would not tumble in.

Second, it went into the water.

The water did not clear. Had it turned transparent, the blind lice would have had no shadow to hide their porcelain bellies, and the spindle-leeches would have withered in the clarity. It remained flat, alkaline, and thick as oil on iron. But the biting, dead-battery sourness of the city’s deep seep—the trickle of copper-wash, drain-lye, and privy-rot that seeped through the blind bulkhead and made the man’s horny soles crack into weeping yellow fissures—simply ceased to accumulate. The water became clean without becoming thin. It remained cold, but it stopped being cruel. Within two turns of the seasons, the deep, yellow ulcers between the man’s splayed toes closed over into smooth, tough callous, gray as river-slate and impervious to rot.

Then, it attended to the machine.

The walking-beam was dying. The man knew it, though he had never said the word *dying* in his life. He knew it by the taste of the grease he scraped from the gudgeon-pin: it had begun to carry the dry, cinnamon-dust tang of sheared iron shavings. The thirty-foot truss was sagging south by an extra eighth of an inch every five years; eventually, the counterweight would ground against the submerged plinth, the hemp gaskets would blow their cold spittle, and the engine would lock. And when the iron screamed its last, dry, dying shriek, the Nave would lose its clock, its pulse, its only measure of whether the world was turning.

The benevolent power did not grease the pin with celestial oil. It did not make the iron new. New iron is hard and bright and smells of the furnace; it would have driven the starlings from their nests and made the sump-dogs drown themselves in panic.

Instead, it entered the hemp.

The wet packing inside the great gudgeon-box softened. The friction inside the four-foot bearing dropped by a measure so small it could only be calculated by the swing of a hair: just enough that the iron ceased to eat itself. When the man climbed the rusted ladder that night, horn vessel in his teeth and hoop-scraper in his calloused grip, he reached into the tallow-cup and found that the old paste had turned sweet. Not sweet like daylight sugar, but sweet like cold marrow and sheep-suet that had never seen a fly. 

When he worked it into the packing, his fingers did not ache with the winter-stiffness that had begun, over the last three years, to curl his knuckles into claws. The joints of his own hands took the grease as the iron took it: deeply, silently, without comment.

He sat astride the thirty-ton beam for his two hours. The greasy starlings landed on his shoulders. He clicked his tongue; they clicked their beaks.

Below him, the long hazel rod leaned against the stela.

The power had saved its deepest courtesy for the bottom of the cylinder well.

Sixty feet down, in the stagnant liquor where no louse swam and no gray light ever drifted, the great blind conger lay in the silt. It had been old when the Queen was a girl. Its gills moved twice a minute, fluttering like two strips of wet flannel in the dark. It had lived on garbage and cold luck, its belly empty for months between the drowning of a dog and the fall of an autumn cat. Its blanched flank was scarred where it had scraped against the jagged intake-flanges in the great flood of '59, and an old iron hook, rusted down to the size of a thorn, was embedded three inches deep in the cartilage of its jaw.

The presence did not take the fish up. It did not heal the blind eyes.

It simply laid a hand—a pressure neither warmer nor cooler than the forty-degree black water—along the creature’s immense, flaccid side.

The rusted hook in the jaw dissolved into harmless iron-meal, drifting away like fine tea. The dull, sixty-year ache in the gill-arches smoothed out, as when an old knot in wet rope is gently untied under water. And from the drowned coal measures—from the ancient, sunless seams where subterranean rivers run through beds of fossil fern—there came a slow, clean seepage of blind, deep-water crayfish: soft-shelled, fat with sweet white fat, drifting up the intake-valve one by one, like bread dropped down a chimney.

The conger did not rush. It did not have a rush in its nature. It simply turned its forty-foot bulk in the shaft, its tail sweeping the stagnant black liquor into that long, greasy heave that the man above was waiting for.

Up on the walking-beam, the man felt the heave lift the pool.

He saw the duckweed-like scum of louse-shells rise two inches up the vitrified wall, pause, and settle.

He took his hazel rod. He lowered the smooth iron crook into the mouth of the well. He felt the cold, heavy drag of the sixty-foot pool, and then, six fathoms down, the crook came to rest against the vast, gray flank.

The man held the rod with both hands, his chin tucked into his greasy smock, his broad, webbed toes curled around the rusted iron truss.

He waited for the cold vibration of the heart.

*One.*

*Two.*

*Three.*

It was there. But through the hazel wood, through the iron crook, through seventy feet of wet air and cold water, the strike of that ancient pump did not come alone.

For the first time since the boilers went out, the man felt an answer.

It was not a voice; a voice would have blown through the Nave like an explosion of steam, shattering the starlings' eardrums and tearing the river-lice from the iron. It was simply a sensation that travelled up the rod and settled into the meat of his palms: a sensation that the floor beneath the water was solid; that the walking-beam would tilt south at noon and north at dusk until the sun itself went out; that the dogs were warm enough in the hollow bedplates; and that he was neither forgotten, nor abandoned, nor required to be anything other than the thing that kept the Nave from screaming.

The man stood on the beam for a long time. 

The forty-minute *thung* sounded from the city’s ribs. The water rose the thickness of a coin, paused, and sighed back through the weep-holes.

He drew up the rod. He wiped the crook with a greasy corner of his leather coat. Then he climbed down the stela, rung by rung, his yellow feet finding the iron without a sound, and waded through the hip-deep water back to the third brick step.

He sat down. He tucked his knees beneath his smock. 

High up, in the coal-chutes, the lead-colored rinse died away into the dead pitch of night. A starling clicked three times from the cross-rib. The man clicked twice in return, closed his wide, dark pupils against the quiet dark, and slept, while the forty-foot fish turned below him in water that would never turn sour again.
