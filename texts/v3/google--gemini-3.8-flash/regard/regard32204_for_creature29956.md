The power that looked down through the oblong slot was not a god of storms or proclamations. Those powers inhabit the mountains above the timberline or the broad bellies of river valleys, where there is room for thunder and pride. 

This power was older than the quarried city, but smaller than an idea. It had the patience of groundwater percolating through three leagues of basalt. It understood gravity not as a law, but as a form of affection—the earth’s persistent desire to hold everything close to its ribs.

It looked down through thirty feet of dead, gray air and saw the court.

A lesser benevolence, seeing the woman with her moth-felt chest-cloth and her flattened, leathery soles, would have dropped a rope. It would have reached down with a bright, heroic arm and plucked her up into the streets.

And that, the power knew, would be an execution.

To haul her thirty feet upward into the noon would be to burn out her milky, unshielded retinas like dry vellum in a forge. The shouting of the cart-drivers and the barking of pariah dogs would rupture the drums of ears that had spent forty years tuning themselves to the hydraulic turn of a mantis’s neck and the soft slink of flatworms. The dry, dusty, dung-scented wind of the upper town would dry the slick mucous of her throat and crack the skin of her water-pad feet before the sun reached the zenith. She would die in an alley within three days, crouched against an oven wall, terrified of the horizon—that monstrous, unmortared emptiness she had never been made to endure.

Wisdom knows that you cannot save a creature by unmaking its world. 

Wisdom also knows that the woman was not unhappy—only complete, and very nearly finished.

Therefore, the power did not alter the light. It did not widen the oblong slot. It did not send an angel with wings of brass to shatter the acoustic deadness. 

Instead, it came down the sheer volcanic walls the way the condensation did: invisibly, molecule by molecule, settling into the joints.

First, it attended to the cold.

In the middle hours of the night, when the draft dropped through the shaft like an iron rod, her shoulders had begun to ache. The damp that fed the aroids was beginning to petrify the joints of her fingers, making the copper scraper clumsy in her grip. 

The power reached deep beneath the forty paces of flagstone, deeper than the sump, down into the cooling roots of the mountain where the volcanic heat had slept since the tuff was ash. It did not wake a volcano; it merely opened a hairline pore in the magma cap three miles down. 

A pulse of mild, steady warmth rose through the rock. 

It was so subtle that the meniscus of the puddles did not steam, nor did the blind geckos panic. But the broad slabs of the floor ceased to be cold. They became the temperature of a sleeping dog’s flank. When the draft fell from above, it met a soft, rising cushion of temperate air and was turned aside. The woman, lying on her dry mound of tuff with the moth-skins pressed to her chest, felt her breathing deepen. The ache in her clavicles, which she had borne for seven years as simply another condition of the room, dissolved into the stone like grease into wool.

Second, it looked at her hands.

The copper wedge she used for a scraper was worn to the thickness of a fingernail. In another season, it would fold and snap against the taproots of the dwarf dock, and she would have to scrape the green crowns with the broken edge of a river-slate, bruising the plants and tearing her fingers.

The power did not drop an iron knife from the sky. An iron knife would rust in three months, and its sharp, foreign geometry would be an offense against the court’s pale balance.

Instead, the power leaned against the drainage slot near the northeastern T-joint, where she slept. Over three days, the water weeping from that specific seam changed its mineral freight. It stopped carrying lime and began to precipitate a fine, black, vitreous chert—a vein of obsidian, cooling and swelling out of the hairline joint like dark honey from a comb. 

When the woman found it, it had already flaked along its natural plane. It was an oval scraper, four fingers broad, with a scalloped cutting edge sharper than beaten copper, contoured exactly to the sweep of her wrist, and so dense that forty more years of cutting dock would not dull it by the breadth of a spore. 

She picked it up. She did not look at the sky. She tested the edge against her thumb, felt the truth of its bevel, and set the copper wedge neatly into a crevice between two tuff blocks, retiring it with the dignity of an old plumb bob.

Third, the power addressed the acoustic deadness.

The pit was silent, but it was a lonely silence—the silence of an ear pressed against a shut door. The power knew that the woman’s palette-clicks, calling the flat-shelled turtles from the sump, were the only language spoken in the room, and that language needs an answer, or it turns into stone inside the speaker’s throat.

The power did not speak in words. It did not have a human mouth.

It found the turtles. 

They were simple things, small-brained and ancient, concerned entirely with moth-mush and the density of algae films. But the power touched the dark, smooth domes of their wet carapaces, passing into them like a hum. 

That evening, when the woman sat on her heels by the rim of the standing water and made her dry, clicking noise—*tk, tk-tk, clack*—the turtles did not merely paddle forward with their wet leather slap. 

The oldest of them, a flat female with yellow eyes as clear as amber beads, paused at the edge of the puddle. It lifted its snouted head. It opened its dark beak and expelled a tiny, sharp bubble of air against the surface film. 

*Pock.*

A second turtle reached the stone and clicked its claws together, three times, in exact imitation of her cadence. 

*Tk-tk.*

It was not a conversation. It was something better: it was a reply. The court was no longer a one-way mirror where she made noise and the porous walls swallowed it down. The space had closed its circle. The room was listening, and the room was answering back in its own small, leathery vocabulary.

Finally, the power looked ahead.

Benevolence does not look only at the afternoon; it looks at the dusk. The power saw the day—perhaps ten years hence, perhaps twelve—when the woman would lie down on her mound of tuff blocks and her heart would slow to the crawl of the blind geckos, and then stop altogether.

In the ordinary course of things, her body would decompose in the damp air. The flies would find her; the aroids would climb through her ribs; the turtles would nudge at her leathery fingers until she was bone, and the city above would never know that its foundation-keeper had gone.

This, the power decided, would not do.

It laid a blessing into the air—a slow, gentle chemistry carried in the heavy, greasepaper mist. 

It decreed that when her breath finally left her, the process would not be rot. The stone-mites, drifting in their thousands from the freshly planed walls, would not avoid her; they would come to her. They would settle upon her slate-gray hair, her quiet hands, her chest covered in moth-velvet. They would bring with them the same insoluble, clear grease they used to seal the volcanic tuff. 

Over months, under the milky light of the slot, she would not decay; she would mineralize. The lime in the condensation and the volcanic salts from the deep rock would infiltrate the tissues of her body, replacing cell with silica, marrow with tuff, until she became an ashlar block herself—square, smooth-surfaced, perfectly level, and set without mortar into the northeastern corner of the court.

She would become the final stone of the measuring-out.

Her feet would remain beneath the shallow saucer of water; the maidenhair ferns would sprout from the bevel of her shoulder; the mud-turtles would haul themselves onto her flat, stony knees to rest between their cycles of grazing. 

The plumb line would be kept forever.

Having done these things, the benevolent and wise power did not linger to be thanked. It had no need for altars, and there was nowhere in the forty paces to put one anyway.

It withdrew upward, rising through the thirty feet of dead air like a bubble through syrup, slipped through the oblong slot into the glaring, dusty noon of the upper city, and left the court below precisely as it had found it:

Sunken, secret, shadowless, and complete. 

Below, a heavy drop fell from a high ledge—*plip*—striking an aroid leaf. The woman reached out with her black glass scraper, cut three leaves of dwarf dock with a single soundless stroke, and tossed a rolled pellet of green moss into the glass-clear puddle. 

The old turtle scooted forward, took the pellet into its beak, and from the seam between two blocks, a long, white gecko watched them both with a heartbeat that struck the stone like a feather falling on silk.
