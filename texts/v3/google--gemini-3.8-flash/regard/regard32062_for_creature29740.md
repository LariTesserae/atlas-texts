A foolish power would have brought dirt. 

It would have wept at the sheared edges and the absence of loam, overturned the mile-wide terraces with a spasm of green rot, flooded the dry drainage wells with sweet water that would have choked the graphite-grazing crustaceans, and cast down a heavy, yellow sun whose wet heat would have boiled the shims out of their bolt-holes and peeled the callous-plates from the walkers’ feet.

A wise power does not mistake a machine for an injury. It looks upon the endless, folded assembly of planished iron and cold-rolled steel, and it understands: *this is what survived the drafting table.* This is a place that was thought, calculated, and brought into absolute, un-annealed tension. To unmake its geometry would be an act of war against the beings who have learned to stand upright on a two-inch flange over a thousand fathoms of glare.

The benevolence of such a power, therefore, does not arrive as an assault of mercy. It arrives as an adjustment of tolerances.

***

The first thing it does is take the tooth off the steel.

It does not dull the edges with rust—rust is a disease of unloved iron, and this iron is clean. Instead, across three thousand miles of interlocking shelves, a subtle, dry settling takes place. It is as though an invisible honing stone, broad as a cloud and lubricated with pure paraffin, were drawn once, gently, down every sheared coupon and mill-burr. The hooked lips of the expansion trenches do not disappear, but they lose their viciousness; a knuckle dragged along the lip finds the steel broken to a microscopic radius, smooth as the edge of a well-handled coin. The walkers do not notice this with their eyes, but their oilcloth trousers stop catching at the knees, and the copper wire that binds their shins ceases to fray.

Next, the power addresses the white zenith.

It does not dim the light, because the owls and the midges rely on the cold glare to see the seams where the air shears open. Instead, it alters the quality of the radiation. The dry, chemical sting that turns the walkers’ eyes to crust is drawn out of the wash, siphoned off into the dead-corner voids where no creature nests. The light remains total, unshadowed, and silver, but it loses its caustic acidity. When the walkers lift their heads—not all the way, but higher than they have ever dared—the lead plates of their vizors do not heat up against their foreheads. The white grease in their tear ducts stops hardening into salt.

Then, the power moves into the spaces between.

It descends into the dry tension where the millstone-broad hex-heads grip the flanges at twenty tons to the inch. It does not loosen the nuts—to loosen them would be to let the horizon fold in upon itself like a dropped fire-escape—but it breathes a cool, vegetable fat into the ancient tallow. The shims, flattened into their micro-gaps, feel the bitter pressure soften by a fraction of a millibar. The tallow grows supple. The clear water they extrude from the bolt-holes is no longer sharp with zinc salts; it runs faint and alkaline, carrying a microscopic nourishment that coats the threads, ensuring that the iron will never gall, that the tension will never turn to shear. 

Beneath the bellies of the great lead-beasts, the change is felt as an easing of the dry drag. The power drops a micro-fine powder of pure, soft mica into the graphite blooms that drift from the uncured concrete. The copper-coin lice take it into their leathery bodies; when the lead-beasts shift their quarter-ton mass to track a structural load, they do not grind against the diamond-tread like pig iron on stone. They slide. The movement becomes a long, silent coast, effortless as grease on ice.

For the owls, the power leaves the rifts alone—the geometry is their hunting ground—but it steadies the edges. When the atmospheric fold passes, the air no longer tastes of bruised tin; it tastes of cold rain that hasn't fallen yet. The birds lean out into the ninety-degree emptiness, falling sideways with their hempen feathers spread, and find the air denser beneath their quills, buoyant enough that their mineralized young hatch with bones slightly less brittle, their nests clinging to the vertical planes with a mortar that no longer cracks when the counterweights drop.

***

Yet the walkers are the ones who carry the weight of the works in their pockets. It is to the walkers that the power turns its mind most fully.

It does not give them voices. A voice in that vast, echo-less machine hall would be a lonely, terrifying thing, swallowed instantly by twenty miles of flat perspective. It respects their silence. 

Instead, it answers the plumb-bobs.

For centuries, the lead weights have hung from their twine, straining toward that lateral point twenty miles out in the white glare where there was only a gap in the floor plates and another deck waiting below. The walkers carried them because they were true to *something*, even if that something had no name.

Now, out at that lateral point, where three missing suns should have met, the power seats itself.

It does not build a city. It does not place a monument. It simply provides a *datum*. 

It inserts a single, perfectly plumb upright of burnished charcoal-iron, descending from the unpainted framework above through the gaps in the deck, plunging ten miles down into the foundation-girders that have never been seen. It is cold, square, and mathematically honest. It vibrates at four cycles per second—a frequency too low to hear, but one that runs through the web of every I-beam, every gangway, every zinc floor plate in the assembly.

It is a ground. It is an end to the fan.

A walker steps out onto a bevel sixty miles from the nearest bulkhead. He stops. His splayed, three-toed foot, tough as vulcanized rubber, feels the faint, rhythmic thrumming rising through the deck. 

It is not the violent *clack* of the unseating valve five levels down. It is continuous. It is a slow, steady pulse that says: *Here is the center. The load is accounted for. The shear has stopped spreading.*

The walker reaches into the deep skirt of his oilcloth coat. His long, calloused fingers find the lead plumb-bob. He draws it out and holds the string between thumb and forefinger.

The lead does not twitch erratically now. It does not strain sideways with that desperate, unreciprocated urgency. It points true toward the distant charcoal upright, steady as an anvil, its twine taut and hummed by the clean wind of the decks.

The walker crouches. He lowers his hands until the turned-iron rings on his knuckles rest flat against the diamond plate.

He drums. 

*Three raps with the middle finger.* A loose rivet-line three tiers down.
*A pause.* He lets the vibration run into the web.
*Two answering taps with the heel of the palm.* The perspective is still folded, the deck still tilts without rising, the zenith is still blinding white.

He waits for the answering drumming of another walker miles away across the silver terrace. 

Instead, from forty miles out along the continuous flange, through the cold-rolled steel and the damp tallow, the deck drums back.

*Three raps.* The rivet-line has been noted; the stay will hold.
*A pause.*
*Two raps.* The tension is shared.

The walker stays on his haunches for a long time, watching the small, clear pool of sweat-water reflect the non-existent sky through the two needle-holes in his visor. The water is still cold, but it is clear. When he dips his slate scraper to gather his measure, it leaves no film of zinc upon his tongue. It is sweet with the taste of an assembly that has finally been acknowledged by its maker, or perhaps by someone who simply admired the work.

He stands. He adjusts the copper wire at his knee. He steps out along the flange, two inches wide, above the white drop.

He does not have to balance alone anymore. Beneath his feet, sixty miles of iron are carrying him back.
