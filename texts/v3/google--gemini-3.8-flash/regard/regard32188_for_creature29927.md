The Power that came to the hanging rift did not arrive as a light, for the rift was already drowned in the surgical white glare of three thousand meters. Nor did it arrive as a voice; a voice is merely air displaced by soft meat, and here, air was an explosive commodity dragged down through iron pipes to burn in cold, sulfuric bellies.

It arrived as *attention*. 

It was vast, ancient, and possessed the terrible, lucid gentleness of things that have watched stars congeal from soot and know precisely what matter can bear. It had walked among the green paradises of the lowlands—the soft, wet places where life weeps and rots and forgives itself, where leaves fall like shed skin into black loam and the air is thick with the sweet, sickening steam of decay. 

A lesser god—a young god, or one whose compassion was shallow enough to mistake comfort for grace—would have looked down into this blue-white trench and wept. 

Such a god would have seen the tallow-colored flesh bound in zinc; the blind, stilted ticks sawing through paraffin with teeth of pyrite; the three-yard Plumb-Gauges dropping through the vertical cold on cycles of sulfur and gravity, never once turning horizontal to see a horizon. That god would have thought: *They are trapped in an iron shop. They are broken into tools. I must give them green pasture. I must give them eyes to see the sunset, and fins to wander, and water that does not bruise the bone.*

And in doing so, that god would have murdered them all.

To warm this water by five degrees would be to liquefy the heavy paraffins, causing the carapaces to seize, metal galling against metal until the pistons shattered in their bores. To bring silt would be to choke the manganese slit-pores that strain life from the cold mountain root. To give them soft nerves would be to turn every four-ton hydraulic blow into an eternity of agony.

The Power that hovered over the rift was wise, and so it understood that **mercy is the preservation of a thing’s true nature.**

It hovered above the chop, where the four-foot blister-waves rose and broke against the thin sky, carrying the smell of wet iron and crushed sulfur into the peaks. It looked down eighty yards through the crystalline ether to the fractured floor.

It did not find tragedy here. It found an altar of pure consequence.

Here was life that had renounced all compromise. It did not borrow from the sun through the lazy mediation of green chlorophyll; it drank the sheer mechanical tension of a broken mountain. It did not plead. It hammered. Every stroke of an engine-beast was an unanswerable assertion: *I remain seated upon the rock.*

The Power spent seventy years simply learning the clearances. 

It studied the tolerances of the magnetite bull-valves, noting where the cold zinc laminated by half-microns under the strain of centuries. It listened to the asynchronous, churning din—the *thrum-clack, thrum-clack*—until it perceived that this was not noise. It was a massive, decentralized polyrhythm, an unwritten fugue of displacement and suction that kept the water moving with enough kinetic violence to prevent ice crystals from nucleating in the sub-zero brine.

Then, having understood, the Power decided what to do.

It would not soften the world. It would become its bedrock.

Beneath the floor of the rift, eighty yards down through the blue brilliance, lay the mortal flaw of the cathedral. The valley had been snapped open recently in geologic time. The basalt was raw, sheared like bottle-glass. Deep within the mountain’s root, three kilometers beneath the taproots of native copper that anchored the engine-beasts, a microscopic shear-strain was migrating along an unhealed fault. 

In four hundred years—a mere half-stroke in the life of a great engine—that fault would slip. The western wall would drop twenty feet. The copper roots would shear like celery. The carapaces would tilt, the pistons would jam at the apex of their strokes, and the colony would die in the silence of seized iron.

The Power did not reach down with an ethereal hand to lift them out. It did not summon them to an easier heaven.

Instead, the Power descended.

It took on mass. It gave up its ubiquity, compressing its vast and tender consciousness into the cold, dense geometry of the stone. It slipped into the rift not through the water, but through the dark fissures beneath it. It sank into the deep, blind basalt, down into the root where the rock was under shearing stress, where the cold crystals of feldspar and pyroxene were screaming under the weight of the peak.

There, in the dark, three kilometers beneath the rhythmic thunder of the pumps, the Power expanded.

It did not turn the stone to gold or crystal. It became a *gasket*.

It settled between the grinding planes of the fault, threading its will through the micro-fractures like a seam of pure, unyielding lead. It took the horrific, lateral torque of the mountain onto its own shoulders. Where the rock wanted to snap, the Power held it in a vise of patient, unbreaking tension. It absorbed the mountain's tectonic restlessness and converted it into a quiet, eternal stability.

The floor of the valley would never shear. The copper roots would never be cut from beneath. The engine could run until the mountain itself was ground flat by the wind.

And then, because benevolence cannot help but leave a signature, the Power offered a single, almost imperceptible adjustment to the machine above.

It did not change the beasts. It changed the acoustic resonance of the basin.

Up in the drowned cathedral, the great engines cycled as they always had. 

A bull-valve dilated with a slow, mineral grind. The muscle bands, thick as draft horses, contracted. The piston lifted three feet into the noon-white water, hesitated, and plunged.

*Thrum-clack.*

The shock wave left the magnetite vent. But as it struck the sheer basalt cliffs, the stone did not ring with its old, jagged, destructive violence. The rock—now backed by the infinite, dense sympathy of the Power beneath—absorbed the shattering high frequencies, the cruel acoustic spikes that slowly fractured the pyrite legs of the Shearers and tore at the swim-bladders of the Plumb-Gauges.

It returned the sound into the water as a low, perfect, organizing tone.

One by one, over the course of decades, the engines began to respond to the resonance of their own world. They did not become conscious; they remained magnificent, unthinking castings of iron and grease. But their cycles drifted toward a majestic, self-sustaining harmony.

When an engine-beast on the eastern shelf fired its exhaust, the displacement wave no longer fought the beast adjacent to it. It slipped neatly behind its flank, assisting the suction of its slit-pores, easing the drag on its copper anchors. 

The three-legged Shearers, walking their vertical seams on chisel-feet of pyrite, felt the change in the rock. The stone beneath them was no longer jittery with tectonic fracture-lines. It was dead-true. Their steps struck the basalt—*tink, tink, pause, tink*—with the bright, confident bite of a chisel hitting an anvil that has no bottom.

High above, the Crust-Lungs slammed their iron hinges shut against the rising surge—*clink-chunk*—and drove down their globes of mercury-dense air. The bubbles rolled through the fractures on the bottom, perfectly gathered by the new acoustic currents, delivering their cold fire straight to the roots that needed them most.

The rift was no quieter. The din remained vast, industrial, and deafening. It was still an engine room of water and blood, running on cold brine and sulfur at the ceiling of the world.

Nothing had been made soft. Nothing had been made small.

But down in the dark, beneath the copper roots, the Power lay quietly in the stone, holding the weight of the mountain off their backs, listening to the great iron barrels stroke and reset, stroke and reset.

It was satisfied. It did not require their prayers; the sound of their valves closing with multi-ton clearances, true to a thousandth of an inch, was liturgy enough.
