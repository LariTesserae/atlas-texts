A foolish power would have pulled her down. 

It would have sent a warm gale to scour the col of its ice, softened the air until her barrel-ribs had nothing to brace against, or appeared in a blossom of blinding cherubic light that would have permanently seared the ragged, scarred margins of her corneas. It would have borne her down to an orchard valley where the heavy, thick air of the lowlands would have felt like wet wool packed into her windpipe, where the rot of damp leaves and the squalor of plant-decay would have sickened a metabolism turned entirely to grease, mica-silt, and dry barley. 

A power that is merely benevolent is an engine of unintentional slaughter.

A wise power, having watched the needle-shadows crawl across the slate lamellae for three turns of the seasons, did something so small it was nearly indistinguishable from the background physics of the range.

It did not alter the altitude. Seven thousand meters remained seven thousand meters: an iron threshold where the troposphere ceases to negotiate with biological life. The cold remained absolute; the wind retained its dry, scouring teeth.

Instead, the power leaned its attention into the tolerances.

The first intervention took place within the lattice of the beaten copper. 

At forty degrees below zero, native copper becomes brittle. Its crystal boundaries lock; the micro-fractures born from her stone mallet normally spread until a gutter snaps along its fold, requiring days of cold-hammering and desperate patching with sinew that perishes in the sun. The power did not mend the gutters with gold or unearthly crystal; it merely settled into the metallic grain. It rearranged the copper atoms into an enduring, slip-free hexagonal close-packing. 

When her stone mallet came down the next morning, striking a warped weir with that dull, palm-greased thud, the soft metal did not resist or split. It yielded beneath the stone like stiff wax, singing a faint, clear pitch—a low, resonant *hum* that traveled down through the bedrock and died in the roots of the western horn. The gutter seated itself into the quartz fissure so flush that not a single bead of the morning melt escaped into the void.

The second intervention was among the dust.

The midday updrafts—the great, roaring chimneys that lifted the organic debris of five thousand vertical meters—did not change their course. But their sorting became exquisite. The dead aphids and dry thistle-down still fell like gray snow upon the wet plates, but the violent, razor-edged quartz dust that blew with them, the grit that ground her molars to flat pegs and filed her corneas into cloudy white cataracts, began to fall elsewhere. It stalled fifty feet lower, settling harmlessly onto the ice-glaze of the windward face. 

The silt that reached the col became cleaner, richer in pine-pollen, dried algae, and the nutrient-dense wax of mountain butterflies that had died in the lower pass. The flat-ribbed rodents tasted the change first; their rubbery lips worked the wet cleavage planes with a frantic, rhythmic suction, their slow hearts speeding by two beats a minute as their greasy pelts grew thick, smooth, and seamless as sealskin.

The third intervention was the water.

The water of the col was always an agony to drink: so cold it seized the temples with an iron cramp, so hard with iron salts it scoured the lining of the belly. The power did not turn it to wine, nor did it warm it—warmth in this place would have invited the rot of anaerobic mold that her dry lungs could never fight. 

Instead, it subtly increased the surface tension of the liquid as it gathered on the hair-guides. 

The droplets hung larger. They gathered weight without tearing loose in the cross-breeze. When they dropped into the quartz basins, they were pure, clear, and unclouded by rock-flour. And when she lowered her beaten copper reed into the dark hole and drew the liquid up into the cave of her mouth, the water did not bite her palate with that ancient, skull-splitting ache. It held its cold, but parted with its sharpness; it slipped across her rawhide tongue like thin oil, coating the cracked, salt-bleached membranes of her throat with a cool, mineral balm that smelled faintly—unfathomably—of snow that had never touched dirt.

For three days, the woman noted these things without wonder. To wonder requires an expenditure of calories that she did not have to give. She knew only that the mallet did not jar her wrists so fiercely; that the basins filled three minutes before the shadows cut them off; that her eyes watered less beneath their crusts of blackened tallow.

It was on the fourth evening that the presence made its presence known in the only language the col permitted: weight, and the absence of wind.

The western horn eclipsed the sun. The temperature dropped off the shelf of the world, plunging forty degrees before her lungs could finish three long, reedy cycles of breath. She retreated to the stall, pulled her knees to her skiff-like breast, and waited for the three copper thermal pins to begin their rapid, fading surrender of the day’s heat.

Usually, the pins gave up their warmth by the time the violet sky turned ink-black. By midnight, they were as cold as the rock they pierced, and she would begin the long, shivering vigilance that kept her blood moving until dawn.

This night, the tips did not cool.

The power did not make them fires; it simply closed the valve of their thermal radiation so that they wept their heat inward rather than outward. The wrist-thick rods of copper, deep in the hornblende floor, held the heat of the midday glare and fed it downward, five feet into the cold heart of the rock beneath her heels, creating a small, silent lens of stone that remained precisely four degrees above the freezing point of water.

It was not a luxury. It was not comfort. It was merely the difference between survival and preservation. It was the margin that allowed her heart to slow from an anxious flutter to the long, measured stroke of the flat rodents in their clefts.

She felt it through the five layers of yak-felt and rodent-hide: a faint, dry dryness at the base of her spine. 

Her cracked lids parted in the blackness. The air was so still she could hear the molecular contraction of the slate sheets outside, clicking like the works of an iron watch.

She did not pray. She had forgotten the names of the low-country gods thirty winters ago; their statues had fat cheeks, bellies like curd-tubs, and soft, moist hands that could not have held a mallet for ten seconds against the wind. 

She merely reached out one hand—the fingers stiff, blunt, split like seasoned cedar—and laid her palm flat against the base of the central copper pin.

It was lukewarm. It felt like the forehead of a living thing that had walked all day through the sun and was now resting its brow against the mountain.

Beside her, the chough did not leave for the lower cliffs. It had tucked itself into a dry crevice above the mud caulking, its head tucked beneath an oiled wing, its small chest rising and falling in the dry stillness.

The woman did not weep; she had no water to waste on tears, and salt would have torn the red rims of her eyes. But she let her shoulders roll outward—just half an inch—from their defensive hunch. Her spine settled against the dry-stacked wall. 

The wise power did not speak. It had no voice that could survive being shredded by the seven-thousand-meter shear. But it remained in the col through the night, invisible, dense, and cold as the hornblende, holding the copper channels true against the frost, watching over the basins as they skimmed with ice so thin a breath could break it, waiting with her for the red burn of the eastern horn.
