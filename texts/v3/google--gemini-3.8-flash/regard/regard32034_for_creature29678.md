If the power were merely benevolent, it might have quieted the wind.

It would have hushed the tearing shear to a meadow-breeze, parted the boiling vapor to reveal the forgotten earth below, and smoothed the furious updraft into stillness. And in doing so, it would have slaughtered everything within a morning. The bellows-leeches would have gone slack and rotted in their fluted tunnels; the hydrofoil saucers of the aeroplankton would have stalled and tumbled into the abyss; the leathery orchids would have dried, unbathed by the atomized mist; and the people—whose barrel-ribs were forged to breathe dynamic pressure, whose hearts beat against the crushing momentum of the gale—would have felt their lungs labor in the sudden thinness, their ears aching in the catastrophic silence, terrified by a sky that had suddenly grown dead and hollow.

Because the power was wise, it did not stay the storm. It knew that mercy shaped like human pity is an axe. 

It came instead without a body, or rather, it took the only body that could exist here without mocking the stone: it poured its attention into the granite itself. 

It slipped into the deep root-systems of the monoliths, five miles down into the boiling murk where the stone met the hidden, thermal crust of the world. It felt the unearthly pipe-organ roar from the inside—not as noise, but as a continuous, violent hymn of friction, pressure, and survival. It felt the micro-fractures; it felt the weight of the hanging cradles; it felt the hundreds of grease-slick bellies pressed flat against the moss, vibrating at the exact pitch of the gale.

And the power thought: *They have outlived their gods. They have outlived their words. They have built an eternity out of three ropes and a filed nail.*

It did not wish to save them from their life. It wished to answer the premise of it.

For thirty generations, their skull-hum had traveled only between two foreheads pressed together, or dissipated uselessly into the indifferent roar. Their only absolute had been the *Hard*—a silent, unyielding master that gave them purchase, but never care. 

So the power began with the rock.

It did not soften the granite—to soften it would be to pull their pitons loose. Instead, it attuned the stone’s acoustic grain. 

Deep within the flutes and bores, where the screaming wind was compressed into sub-audible rumbles, the power made minute adjustments to the resonance. It tuned the hollows so that the low frequencies—the ones that rattled the shinbones and made the teeth chatter in the dark—were no longer merely chaotic turbulence. The vibration changed by fractions of a semitone, shifting into an ambient, stabilizing hum that precisely counteracted the fatigue-tremor in human muscles. 

A crawler, clinging to the lee of a buttress with forearms burning from forty minutes of dredging, would press their chest flat to the moss and suddenly feel the ache draw out of their meat, swallowed into the vast density of the pillar. The rock was no longer just the floor; it was an ear. It took their frantic, thrumming exhaustion and grounded it into the deep foundation of the world.

Next, it touched the *wide-eye*.

The power saw the madness that came to those who looked into the drop—the terror of the five miles of empty blue, the dizzying absence that pulled at the mind until the skull-hum fluttered and frayed. The power did not hide the abyss; it could not. But into the milky sea of lower cloud, where the invisible thermal floor boiled, it breathed an answering pattern.

Those who were seized by the sickness, who turned their faces away from the rock to stare down into the white floor, began to see that the vapor was not empty. Far below, through the rents in the steam, the rising heat formed enormous, slow-turning rings—thermal gyres of iridescent, pale amber that mirrored the movements of the stars above the cirrus. The void ceased to look like a bottomless grave and began to look like a loom. The sight no longer unseated the mind; it settled it. The rapid, frightened fluttering of their jawbones slowed into a steady, rhythmic purr. The hunters called to one another with a click of the teeth: *The deep has a back. The fall is not nothing.*

Yet the deepest act of the wise power was reserved for the windward fin.

It knew the law of the old ones. It knew the swollen knuckles, the rot in the joints, the quiet dignity of unhooking the Sister and the Reach. It knew the final crawl out onto the pink, polished crystals where the moss could not cling. 

It did not prevent them from raising their chins. To prolong the life of a creature whose hands could no longer close upon the Hard would be cruelty, not mercy. 

Instead, the power waited at the edge. 

When an old one worked their way out to the prow, battered nearly flat by the five-hundred-mile-an-hour draft, they would find that the pink granite under their chest was not freezing and slick, but warm—as warm as a companion’s forehead after an hour in the sleeping basket. 

And as they brought their chin up—as they exposed the hollow of their throat to the sky, preparing for the violent, tearing slap that would rip them into the blue—the slipstream did not tear them like a dead leaf. 

At the exact instant the skin left the rock, the power folded the horizontal shear into a pocket of laminar grace. The roar did not vanish, but it ceased to beat them. For the first time in their lives, the terrifying pressure against their ribs relaxed into an immense, buoyant cradle. 

They did not tumble end-over-end into the howling drop, blind and choking on the violent air. They were carried outward into the sunlight, their thick-chested, slate-green bodies resting on the warm draft as stably as the aeroplankton. 

And in that vast, sunlit space between the monoliths—where no living person had ever been able to look without dying—the wind ceased to scream in their ears. It hummed against the bones of their head. It spoke to them in the only language their ancestors had kept: a heavy, resonant vibration pressed directly against the temple.

It told them that the rock had known them all along. 

It told them that every peg they had driven had been counted; that every dropped Sister line had been held; that their thirty generations of silence had been heard as a long, stubborn song of endurance. 

And as the old ones drifted down into the warm, golden mist of the lower clouds—held effortlessly by the air that had hunted them since birth—they did not close their eyes. They kept them wide, untroubled by madness, watching the towering red pillars rise like the ribs of an ancient, beloved house, until the white sea took them gently in, and they were finally, entirely, at rest.
