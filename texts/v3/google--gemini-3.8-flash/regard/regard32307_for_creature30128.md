A foolish god would have wept.

A clumsy, soft-hearted mercy would have reached down with cupped hands full of gray river mist, thinking itself a savior. It would have spilled clouds across the throat of the chimney, breathing cool dew into the Marrow-groves, murmuring words of damp reprieve. 

And in twelve heartbeats, the ledge would have become an abattoir. 

The lacquered cellulose of the marrow-cane would have drunken the water, bloated, burst its glass-hard fibers, and collapsed into a stinking, soggy mush of black rot. The dust-striders, their iron joints rusted shut within an hour, would have starved where they stood. And the people—their ash-gray scales softening like soaked hide, their internal fires snuffed by the sudden, hideous humidity—would have watched their own flesh slough from their hollow bones, screaming in an agony of dissolution, drowning in the foul air of a god’s pity.

The Power that arrived was not foolish. It was wise, and because it was wise, it understood that life does not exist in opposition to its world, but as a severe and exquisite consequence of it. It looked upon the shelf and did not see deprivation. It saw a masterpiece of tension, friction, and iron will.

It did not alter the air. It did not dim the brass sun or calm the screaming gale.

Instead, the Power drew itself down until it was the width of a hairline crack in the basalt, shedding all light, shedding all weight, becoming nothing more than an ancient, attentive patience resting upon the stone. It listened.

It listened to the quern-stones grinding lithium and chalk. It listened to the rapid, dry *click-click-slide* of knuckle-horns upon the rock as a hunter signaled an approaching shear-wind. It listened to the hollow, five-toned chord of an elder’s skeleton singing on the precipice, lashed into the storm, defying the abyss with the empty bore of its own dry marrow.

The Power loved them for their hardness. It loved them because they had refused to rot.

And so, it set about its work—quietly, beneath the notice of the gale.

First, it went down into the roots of the mountain. 

The volcanic flue was extinct, but its deep throat was slowly chilling; in five hundred winters, the thermal lift would have failed, the static storms would have thinned, and the shelf would have frozen into lifeless crystal. The Power pressed its will into the mantle five leagues below. It did not wake the fire to an eruption—fire would burn the wire-grass to cinders—but it seized the great tectonic slip deep within the earth and locked it into a perpetual, dry grind. It turned the mountain’s roots into a friction engine, guaranteeing that for a million years, the basal warmth would continue to rise through the flue, keeping the stone blood-warm, the updrafts roaring, and the marrow-shoots bursting through the fissures.

Next, it looked upon the elders in the bivouacs. 

It saw how they died: not of illness, for rot could not take hold here, but of seizing. The air scoured their joint-tallow; the sulfur dust and lime crept into the sheaths of their knees and wrists until the cartilage mineralized entirely, trapping their keen, fierce minds in statues of living slate long before the heart ceased its dry pumping.

The Power would not give them oil, for oil belongs to wet, fat things, and it turns to rancid tar in the heat of the flue. 

Instead, it touched the deep cracks where the resin-wasps harvested their black glass. It subtly altered the chemistry of the deep earth-sweat that fed the hive-chimneys. On the morning following the visitation, when the hunters wedged their split-cane tongs into the wasp-tubes, they found a new vein among the amber pitch: a fine, lustrous, blue-black grease, dry to the touch as graphite powder, but slicker than fresh blood on ice. 

A single rub of this powder into a stiffening elbow dissolved the mineral crust without a hiss, allowing bone to slide on bone with zero friction, lasting for decades. The elders stood up again. They tapped their batons against the rock in astonishment, their knees moving like oiled clockwork, granted twenty more years of life to carve bone, grind bread, and teach the children the grammar of the wind.

Lastly, the Power walked to the western lip.

It stood among the whistling skeletons. It saw the grandmothers, the lost youths, the scouts who had slipped on mica-ferns, their bleached ribs and thighbones bored with neat stops, playing their endless, shrieking counterpoint against the gale. They were proud instruments, but they played alone. Beyond the rim lay five thousand cubits of empty basin—a dead, yellow fog of pulverized stone that swallowed their notes and gave back only the mindless roar of the void.

The Power reached out into the empty air of the chasm. 

With delicate, invisible fingers, it shaped the acoustic geometry of the vast canyon wall across the basin, five miles away. It carved microscopic ripples into the distant cliffs—concavities of sheer granite that acted as a curved acoustic mirror, tuned precisely to the harmonics of human bone.

That evening, as the brass sun dipped into the pumice-haze and the high gale rose an octave into its night-scream, the clan gathered behind the wind-baffle to eat their sulfur bread. 

A sudden gust tore across the shelf. The bone-whistles along the rim caught the gale. A scout’s femur sounded its piercing, high C; a mother’s ribs wailed in fifths; the skull of an ancient tracker whistled its low, thrumming drone. 

The people continued to chew, used to the sound of their dead. 

Then, the wind dipped for an instant into a rare, breathless lull.

From across the five-mile void, out of the bronze dark of the basin, came the answer. 

It was faint, but unmistakable: an echo, yet more than an echo. The distant cliffs caught the song of the bones, gathered it, polished it against the stone, and threw it back—not as a scream, but as a chord of immense, crystalline harmony. A vast, resonant choir of flint and wind, singing the dead people’s names back to their bivouacs, transfigured, deep, and steady.

Within the low stone huts, the people froze. 

The children stopped their spooning of the cask-hound butter. The hunters lowered their flint scrapers. The women set down the quern-pestles. For the first time in their memory, the abyss had spoken, and it had not mocked them with silence or deafened them with chaos. It had sung in their own tongue. It had answered with bone.

The oldest among them, his gray scales banded with the golden varnish of a hundred wasp-hunts, stepped out into the doorway of the bivouac. He did not bow; these were people who bent for nothing save the shear-wind. 

He lifted his hand, fitted with its ring of strider-horn. 

He struck the basalt once: a sharp, resounding *crack*.

Three seconds later, out of the vast, dry dark, the stone of the mountain echoed it back: *crack*.

The old man’s horizontal amber eyelids blinked once against the static dust. His mouth curled into a thin, iron smile. He did not know what was out there in the gale, but he knew that it was made of stone, that it did not weep, and that it was listening. 

He clicked his knuckles twice, turned back to the warmth of the dry sulfur fire, and began to grind the seeds.
