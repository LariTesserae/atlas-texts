The power does not descend. To lower a foot into the bowl would be to displace its air, and to displace its air would be to snap the midge-chords, tear the down from the scrap of bird-skin, and lift the gypsum flour into a choking, weeks-long fog that would blind the limpets and scour the lung of the stone.

Wisdom begins by understanding that in a vessel so exact, kindness can look identical to ruin. 

A lesser god would have brought a river. A lesser god would have broken the rim to let in the green world, unthinking of the rot that dampness brings to creatures born of calc-dust, or would have sent companions to crowd the narrow shelf where only one can roll the balls of tallow without starving the skinks. 

This power does none of that. It remains above the circle, lying across the scrub-country like an imperceptible thickening of the blue, resting its weight on the rim of the world so gently that not a single grain of limestone tumbles over the edge.

It listens first.

It listens until it has learned the interval of every fluting in the perimeter: the sixty-breath drop that falls south-by-southeast, the five-hundred-breath drop that gathers where the wall bulges like a hipbone, the dry flute on the western face that choked on limy crust three seasons ago and has hung silent ever since. It listens to the rhythmic flutter of the skink’s heart against the stone, which beats at three times the speed of the woman’s, and to the slow, cool displacement of gypsum where the amphisbaenian paddles through the deep sulfurous dust beneath the floor.

It sees that she is the last. 

The hair around the gourds tells the whole tale: the oldest braid is coarse and dark, thick as an adder; the middle braid is silvering and brittle; the outermost braid, wound over the fractures with desperate neatness, is her own—scanty, gray-bleached, short, taken from her temples with a sharp flake of chert. When her hair is gone, there will be no more cords. When her knuckles swell so thick they can no longer pinch the bird-down to draw the water, the saucers will brim, the lime crust will bridge the gutters, the flutes will choke, and the drops will cease to *tink* upon the flagstones, scattering instead as a useless, silent wash down the uncurated stone.

The power reasons with the cold, loving clarity of deep water.

It will not make her live forever. To keep a woman alive in a stone cup past the span of human marrow is not mercy, but imprisonment. Nor will it bring another here to die of thirst in her sight.

Instead, it makes three adjustments, each smaller than the weight of a midge.

First, it leans its will against the wind of the upper plateau. It does not calm the gale—the gale belongs to the desert—but it introduces a curl, an eddy, right at the northern overhang where the scrub-oak grows stunted among the boulders. This tiny spiral acts as a sieve: it strips the bitter sand and the heavy flint-grit from the air before it spills over the rim, but it lets through the microscopically fine, protein-rich yellow dust of the desert buckthorn and the downy fluff of thistle seeds. The midges find their lines heavier every twilight, glistening with a pale, sweet tallow that demands half the labor to clear and yields double the grease.

Second, it attends to the stone itself. In the horizontal seam behind the wall, where she lies during the hours of triple-sun, the rock has always been as hard as an anvil. The power does not change the limestone into moss or wool—which would mold and sour in the still heat—but it alters the tension in the crystalline matrix of her sleeping-niche. The stone relaxes. It becomes cool suet all the way through, giving inward by the depth of half a barleycorn wherever her hip, her shoulder blade, or the dry curve of her ankle presses against it. Her sleep, which has been a brittle thing broken by the ache of bone against calcite, deepens until her breath slows to match the deep, undulant glide of the amphisbaenian beneath the floor.

Third—and this it does with the utmost hesitation, holding its intention between thumb and finger like a sliver of glass—it touches the silent flute on the western face. 

It does not shatter the lime bridge that chokes it. In the middle of the third night, when the basin is cold and radiating its blue-white marrow toward the stars, the power lets a single drop of dew above the rim take into itself an imperceptible grain of sweet acid, no stronger than the juice of a wild grape. The drop creeps down the flute, finds the dry dam of travertine, and dissolves a channel through it no wider than a horsehair. 

At dawn, as the shadow of the lip begins its draw across the basin, the flute sounds again. 

*Tink.*

The sound is slightly lower in pitch than the others, rounder, like a wet knuckle striking a cedar board. 

Inside her crevice, the woman’s eyes open. They are milky at the edges, white-lashed with dust, but clear at the center. She does not start; starting is for things that have predators, and she has none. She simply turns her ear to the wall. 

She counts her breaths. 

One hundred. Two hundred. Three hundred and forty.

*Tink.*

It is a new timekeeper. A rhythm she did not know was possible, falling into the dry basin like a word spoken in a dialect her great-grandmother might have understood, but which had been lost before her birth.

She crawls from the seam. Her joints do not catch; the suet-stone has spared her knees the morning stiffness that has plagued her since the summer the swallows failed to fledge. She emerges into the white glare, shading her brow with the chert scraper, her gaze drifting along the fluted perimeter until she finds the wet ribbon shining black-silver on the western wall.

She approaches it with her rolling, soundless gait, her feet testing the gypsum flags. She reaches up, touches the damp channel with the back of her wrist, and tastes the stone.

The water does not taste of magic; it does not taste of honey or milk. It tastes of ancient shell, of sun-baked lime, and just faintly of something clean and cool that has traveled a very long way through the dark to find her.

She does not fall to her knees. She does not look up at the sky to thank an empty circle. That is not the way of her people. Her gratitude is an act of labor: she lifts the scraper, places the fan of patinated chert against the rim of the renewed groove, and with three light, shaving strokes—strokes as delicate as the turning of a page—she welcomes the flute back into the work of the house.

The shavings curl into her palm, soft as grease. 

Above, invisible in the blaze, the power draws back its attention by an inch, satisfied. It will stay there for the remaining years of her hands. It will see that the cords stay full; it will keep the rim from falling; and when the last braid of hair fails, and she lies down in the bread-oven seam and does not rise with the shadow of noon, it will gently, over three hundred summers, let the flutes bridge themselves with lime, one by one, until the water is locked back inside the core of the stone and the basin is left as quiet as a closed eye.
