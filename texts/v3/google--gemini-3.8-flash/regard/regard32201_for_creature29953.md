The Power did not come down the ravine like a descent; it came like an increase in attention.

It was vast enough to have forgotten its own beginnings, but it was not careless. A careless power, seeing such a place, would have pitied it. It would have thought: *They are cold,* and laid a broad thumb of yellow sunlight across the four-thousand-meter lip. 

Had it done so, the black birch—thick with its centuries of dried, protective resin—would have caught within three turns of the weeping-cisterns. The drafted air of the ravine would have turned the narrow parish of living wood into a flue. What did not burn would have thawed, and in thawing, surrendered to the gray mold that waits in all wet things. The dead grandmothers in the joists would have gone slack and foul; the grubs in the compost-beds would have drowned in their own sour liquor; the pikas would have panted their hot, fragile lives out against the children’s ribs within an afternoon.

Benevolence without wisdom is an executioner with a warm smile.

The Power, being old, understood this. It stood—if it could be said to stand—not in the basin, but throughout it, filtering itself down through the low, dragging cloud until it was no larger than the tension between water droplets on an alder bud. It listened before it moved.

It listened to the *clink-tock* of the condensed water falling from the ceiling-girders into the fluted troughs. It listened to the dry, frantic ticking of the pika hearts, each one beating six hundred times a minute, a furious little engine of ninety-eight degrees wrapped in wicker and skin. It listened to the long, aspirated clicks of the bough-weavers in the belly of the mound-willow, sitting knee-to-ribs, deciding whether a grandchild three removes from the room should have an arched doorway or a straight one. And it went down into the dark, where the quartzite teeth pushed through the cold cellarage, and felt the white, blind grubs turning in the hair and the dead wood, slow as planets in a well.

It saw that the place was not a prison. It was an argument.

It was an argument made by marrow, wood, and beetle against the indifference of the frost. It had taken a thousand years to balance the tension of the osier ropes against the weight of the winter rime, to match the calorific yield of a grub to the metabolic furnace of a rodent, to turn the human body into a creature that could hold its breath against the sky and not be broken.

To give them paradise would be to tell them that their thousand years of clever, stubborn grief had been a mistake.

So the Power did not build a wall against the wind. It did not melt the sleet.

Instead, it made four very small, entirely secret adjustments.

First, it went up to the high anchor-pines on the wind-rim, where the young watchers stood on their springy perches of braided withes, their mouths welded shut by the mist-spray. The Power did not lessen the gale. It did not warm the rock. But it touched the crystallization of the rime. 

Where the ice gathered along the windward face of the black birch, the Power altered the geometry of the freeze—just slightly, by half a degree of hexagonal cleavage. The frost still formed, heavy and gray, but it no longer bonded to the bark with the tenacity of stone. When the watchers went out into the howling gray at sundown, swinging their long ash poles tipped with horn, they found that the ice did not need ten bruising, wrist-shattering blows to clear. A single, rhythmic *clack* caused whole sheets of glazed rime to shatter cleanly and shear away into the scree below, ringing like broken slate. 

The watchers did not praise any god. They only thought their ash poles were seated well, or that their arms had found the true grain of the season. But their shoulders did not tear in their sockets before they reached their twentieth winter, and they came down into the willow-hollows with ten minutes more breath left in their lungs.

Second, it went down to the foundation floor, where the fractured quartzite pushed up through the root-felt like raw, gray teeth. The young stone was sharp; year by year, the settling weight of the tiers pressed the living osier-vaults down against those edges, severing the deep anchor-hairs that kept the settlement from sliding into the ravine. The Power did not blunt the stones—for to move the mountain’s bone is to bring the scree down from above. Instead, it whispered to the mountain water that seeped along the rock-faults, carrying down microscopic suspensions of blue talc and silken clay. 

Over three seasons, the water deposited a smooth, greased skin over every submerged point of rock—a pad no thicker than an owl’s eyelid, but slick enough that when the great wooden hive shifted under the wind, the roots did not shear. They slid. The city found an ease in its joints that it had never known, creaking like a ship at slack tide instead of a basket being broken underfoot.

Third, it visited the pikas.

The small, round-eared beasts burned through their grass too quickly. Their lives were short and frantic, eaten away by the sheer labor of radiating ninety-eight degrees into the chests of the children who held them. The Power did not make them larger, nor did it change their fur. But it brushed the dwarf alder and the mountain sedge that grew along the western scree, where the gatherers crawled with their bone sickles. 

In the inner peel of the alder shoots, it deepened the concentration of a single sweet ester—a cool, golden sap-sugar that stored twice the density of energy without fermenting into sour foam. The pikas ate. Their little ticking hearts did not have to race quite so frantically against the damp; their sleep in the wicker vests grew deeper, longer, and smelled less of desperate burning and more of dried clover. A child waking in the third tier did not have to feel for the frantic beat of the furnace with a tightening in her throat; the chest beneath her tunic was simply, steadily, miraculously warm.

And fourth, the Power touched the memory of the wood.

This was the only gift that was not purely practical. In the middle tiers, where the living grandmothers and grandfathers had been drawn into the lintels and the load-bearing knolls, the resin was dark and deaf. The living people leaned their foreheads against the fused brow-ridges of their dead to rest, but the dead were only timber—strong, but mute as slate.

The Power did not wake them into ghosts. Ghosts would have wept for sunlight, and there was no sunlight to give.

Instead, it tuned the birch. It gave the scar-tissue where the boughs had grafted the capacity to conduct the low, humming frequency of human blood.

Thereafter, when an apprentice weaver grew faint with the cold, or when an old grub-tender sat in the damp loam wondering if the breath was worth the drawing, they would lay the palms of their greasy hands against the main upright of the tenement. And through the dense, peat-hardened wood—through the knots where the ribs had turned to grain and the jawbone had turned to bracket—they would feel a slow, deep vibration. Not a voice. Just a sensation like the rhythmic, muffled thrum of a heavy boat pushing through deep water. 

It felt like someone saying: *I held. You hold.*

When the Power was finished, it withdrew. It did not take up a throne on the ridge. It did not paint its name in lightning across the slate wall of the ravine. It simply unraveled its attention back into the wide, impersonal currents of the sky, leaving the pass exactly as it had found it: cold, wet, smelling of iron and sheep-dip, wrapped in woolly mist, and suspended over the drop.

That night, at the third weeping of the cisterns, an old woman woke in the hollow of a mound-willow.

The lane outside was quiet, save for the steady *clink-tock* of the condensed water and the soft sigh of the cloud being pulled through the anchor-needles. She reached into her parka, touched the two dun-colored ears twitching beneath her collar, and felt the dry, steady heat of the little hearts against her ribs.

She climbed down from her basket, her boots of ibex-hide biting soundlessly into the damp liverwort. She walked to the knuckle where the great bough of the archway met the lane-post—a heavy, swelling corbel formed sixty years ago from the body of her mother’s brother, who had fallen from the ice-rim while clearing the anchor-lines.

She laid her grease-rubbed cheek against the dark wood. The birch was freezing to the skin, but just below the frost, deep in the grain, it felt solid. Not dead. Settled.

The old woman took a river-pebble from her pouch. She tapped three times on the knuckle—*click, click, click*—not because she had anything to tell him, but only to hear the live wood take the sound and carry it down through the floors, past the sleeping families and the warm sedge-boxes, down through the steam of the grub-crawlways, all the way to the cold stone at the bottom of the world, where it held.
