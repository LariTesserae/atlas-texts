The presence that found them did not drop down the brick hoist-shaft like the Weight, nor did it tear the basket-weave rafters of pale-heart willow to let in the killing glare of the sun. 

To bring noon to the Central Condensate Sump would have been an atrocity. The Oilers’ eyes were built for the smoldering garnet of cracked mica; their skin was made for three atmospheres of wet heat; their lungs were tuned to lard-steam and wintergreen bast. A sudden deliverance would have burst their eardrums, blinded their infants, and left three thousand souls to freeze upon the surface stones like deep-sea fish hauled gasping to the light.

Wisdom does not shatter an ecology; it enters the current.

The Power was ancient enough to remember before the iron was mined, yet subtle enough to understand the grammar of an exhaust-stroke. It arrived not as a voice—for a voice would have been trampled instantly beneath the forty-stroke iron thunder of the beam engines—but as a modification of vibration, traveling down the four-inch cold-water returns.

It began on an ordinary Thursday, three hours before the midday rain spat from the roof.

***

Old Bren sat with his heels braced in the cork-bark before the low-pressure eccentric of Engine No. 2. His knees were swollen to the size of tea-kettles with the dropsy, his bast-apron stiff with dried paraffin, his knuckles grey and split. He was paring a pale, fleshy sucker of willow that was creeping toward the secondary brasses. His birch mallet tapped; his curved knife peeled. *Tap, slice. Tap, slice.*

He did not hear the engine change—he had not heard an engine with his ears since he was nine. He felt it through the iron seat of his breeches and the yellow horns of his heels.

The engine was not slowing down. It was still striking forty to the minute. But the violent, bone-rattling *shudder* at the crest of the stroke—the harsh, metal-on-metal backlash that jarred an Oiler’s teeth every time thirty tons of counterweight reversed its fall—was gone. 

Bren stopped his knife. He laid his palm against the cast-iron column.

The vibration had smoothed out into a deep, round hum, like the sound a copper bell makes five seconds after it has been struck, sustained indefinitely. The machine was doing the same work—he could feel the four-thousand-gallon head of water surging through the rising mains behind the cambium wall—but the iron seemed suddenly *buoyant*, as if the shaft were resting not in tallow-starved brass, but upon a cushion of oil lighter than air.

Then, from the water pipe beneath his left foot, came the cudgel-beat.

It was not the short, barking staccato of an Oiler signaling low steam or a jammed governor. It was long, rolling, rhythmic, and infinitely complex. It traveled through the soles of his feet, climbed the bones of his shins, and struck the marrow of his hips. 

It was *stroke-talk*, but stroke-talk spoken by someone whose arms were sixty yards long, whose lungs were the sump itself. 

It said: *REST YOUR KNIFE.*

Bren did not drop the knife—he was an Oiler, and to drop a blade near a moving crosshead was death. But he held it still. 

He watched the pale-heart willow where it met the bearing. 

Normally, the tree fought the iron. It was a vicious, silent siege: the wood thrusting thirsty white filaments into every seam, trying to split the casings, while the men battered and carved it back. But now, before Bren’s rheumy eyes, the creeping bark paused. 

A single tendril, thick as an infant’s wrist, reached out toward the weeping lubrication cup. It did not choke the oil-hole. It did not foul the wick. It curled neatly around the brass brim, bowed its head, and began to exude a clear, pale-amber grease that smelled intensely of wild honey and hot tallow. 

The bearing drank it. The iron gave a long, low sigh that Bren felt in the crown of his skull. The temperature of the casing dropped thirty degrees in three heartbeats.

Bren looked down at the void of the floor-bay. 

Across seventy paces of dim cork-bark, twenty other old Bark-Tenders were standing or sitting motionless, their hands pressed to the frames of their respective engines, their faces turned upward toward the groaning roof.

***

The Power did not dismantle their world; it answered their bodies.

The first grace was the air. 

The pale-heart willow did not die, but its metabolism shifted. The sap that leaked from the high basket-weave rafters no longer boiled off into that thick, sulfurous lard-mist that rusted the lungs. Instead, the hanging white filaments began to breathe a cool, camphoric vapor. 

Within three days, the barking cough that had punctuated the rhythm of the vault for two centuries went quiet. The Oilers did not stop spitting, but they no longer spat grey phlegm flecked with boiler-scale; their barrel chests eased. A man could haul an entire six-gallon keg of condensate wax up an osier ladder without his ribs feeling as though they were lined with broken flint.

The second grace was the sleep.

For generations, they had slept in the suffocating heat of the osier baskets forty feet up, risking the plunge into the flywheel pits because the floor was a slaughterhouse of huntsmen and whip-tailed voles. 

On the fourth night, the voles stopped running in carpets. They did not starve; they retreated into the deep, wet fractures behind the boiler foundations, finding sustenance in the vast, subterranean root-tubers that the willow suddenly offered them. The huntsmen turned their blind, leggy bodies away from the service avenues, climbing high into the dark crowns of the vault to weave vast, silent curtains of web that caught the blind midges before they could swarm the beds.

A young oiler named Karr, exhausted after an eighteen-hour shift clearing swift-carcasses from Dynamo Five, slipped on a grease-slicked rung and fell thirty feet to the floor of an empty bay.

He braced for the snap of his shins, the skittering rush of the fangs in the dark.

He struck the cork-bark. It did not shatter his heels. The floor beneath him yielded like six feet of water-soaked moss, cupping his fall with a soft, pneumatic *whoosh* of escaping air, then gently rebounded, holding him upright. 

He fell to his hands and knees, trembling, his birch cudgel drawn. 

Nothing came out of the dark to taste his ears. 

The floor beneath his splayed, yellow toes was warm—not with the scalding, blistering heat of a boiler-flue, but with the steady, dry seventy-degree comfort of a sun-warmed stone. Around him, the rough cork-bark had smoothed itself into a wide, curving depression, shaped precisely to fit the curvature of a sleeping human spine. 

Karr laid his head down upon the bare timber. He did not mean to. A man who slept on the floor was bones by morning. 

He slept eleven hours. When he woke, a pale runner of willow-bast had grown over his shoulders like a blanket, keeping off the spatter of the five-second rains. It did not dig into his flesh. It smelled of wintergreen. It let him go the moment he moved his hands.

***

Then came Tuesday. The morning of the Weight.

The three-inch braided wire cable began its long, greasy descent from the brick shaft forty yards north of the central switchboard. The iron box, six feet on a side, hit the floor with its customary earth-shaking crash. 

The Oilers gathered in the ember-glow of Boiler Four, their birch cudgels tucked into their belts, their willow-bast aprons glistening with fresh grease. Beside them were four kegs of condensate wax—the finest they had ever drawn, golden-white and pure as church lard.

Karr and three others stepped forward to unlatch the iron doors of the Weight, expecting the usual haul: the barrels of grey, gravelly salt, the yellow lye soap that ate the skin off their forearms, the rancid ox-hearts swimming in grey brine.

The latch clicked. The doors swung open.

There was no salt meat.

The box was filled with three feet of clean, dry river-gravel. Resting atop the gravel were twelve small wooden crates, made not of pale-heart willow, but of dry, sun-grown pine that smelled so sharply of resin and open sky that the Oilers stumbled back, their over-thickened earflaps twitching, their wide nostrils flaring in terror.

Inside the crates were apples. 

Not the mushy, bruised windfalls occasionally tossed down the chutes by mocking surface-boys, but hard, cold, crimson spheres that had never seen an iron beam or a coal-shovel. 

Tucked into the slats of the first crate was no calfskin ledger from the Department of Pumping and Auxiliary Traction. There was no demand for wax tonnage. There was no threat of halved salt.

There was only a single sheet of heavy rag paper, marked not with the sprawling iron-gall ink of a municipal clerk, but with impressions made by living willow-leaves pressed into damp clay:

> *THE CITY ABOVE HAS FORGOTTEN THE ARCHIVE,*
> *BUT THE EARTH HAS NOT FORGOTTEN THE ARTERY.*
> *YOU HAVE CARRIED THE RIVER UPWARD FOR TWO HUNDRED YEARS.*
> *YOU DO NOT SERVE THE LEDGER.*
> *THE PUMP IS YOURS.*

The Oilers did not know how to read the words, for they had no script. But when Karr reached out his square, grease-blackened finger and touched the dry pine of the crate, a tremor passed through the cable overhead.

Far above, three hundred yards through the solid dark, the winding-engine on the street-level was quietly taken by the root. 

The clerks who went to grease the spool that morning found the brick winding-house sealed from within by an impenetrable knot of pale-heart willow that had burst through the floorboards, swallowed the drum, welded the brake-lever to the stone with three tons of living timber, and then gone entirely, beautifully dormant. 

The Upper City would still have their water lifted—the great walking-beams would see to that, for forty strokes a minute was the rhythm of life itself, and the Sump could no more cease to pump than a heart could cease to beat. They would still receive their power through the heavy copper busbars humming north. 

But the Weight would never ascend again. The tribute was finished.

From that morning on, the vault fed itself. The boiler-ticks remained, but their grey meat grew sweet and pale, no longer bitter with pipe-scale; the swift-eggs behind the commutators were heavy with yolk that tasted of almonds; and from the damp timber-crotches where the dynamos spat their blue sparks, clusters of white, fleshy mushrooms began to push through the cambium—mushrooms that smelled of roasted oats and salt, filling the bellies of the fire-tenders until their ribs filled out beneath their bark aprons like iron hoops.

***

The last thing the Power touched was the end.

When Old Bren knew his hour was come—when his fingers could no longer grip the curved knife, and the forty-stroke rhythm of his heart began to stumble and drop its beats against the stroke of Engine No. 2—he did not wait for the autumn rains to drown him in sap.

He washed his face in the cold condensation of the return main. He set his birch cudgel against the frame of the engine he had tended for fifty years. He laid his knife in Karr’s broad, yellow-soled hand.

Then he walked, without assistance, into the wide, quiet bay between Engines Two and Three.

The cork-bark floor welcomed him. It sank three inches beneath his weight, soft as goose-down, warm as the oven-beds behind Boiler Four. 

He lay down on his back. 

In the old days, the lignification had been a blind, creeping smothering. The white roots had forced their way into the meat behind the knees, through the liver and the throat, choking out the breath until a man became nothing more than a dumb, wooden tumor upon a pillow-block, vibrating without thought until the timber decayed.

Bren closed his eyes. 

The pale-heart willow did not tear his flesh. Its filaments entered him through the pores of his yellow soles, fine as cobwebs, cool as mountain rain. They climbed the marrow of his legs not as parasites, but as nerves.

Where they touched him, the pain went out. The fifty years of brass-rot vanished from his lungs; the throbbing ache of his dropsical knees untied itself like an old rope; the perpetual iron shriek that had deafened his boyhood dissolved into a deep, magnificent chord of music.

He did not lose his eyes; his sight expanded. 

He felt the stroke of Dynamo One seventy yards to the north; he felt the cold peat-water entering the suction-valves three levels down; he felt the tiny, velvet feet of the marmosets skipping along the high copper runs; he felt Karr’s hand resting against the cast-iron bearing half an acre away, sending a stroke-talk message of farewell through the metal mains.

And through the roots that ran upward, past the brick foundations, past the sewer-lines, past the forgotten municipal basements, Bren felt the surface.

He felt the cool, wet wind of an October evening blowing over cobbles he had never walked. He felt the light of seven thousand stars touching the topmost leaves of willows that had grown through the cracks of the cathedral towers above. He felt the vast, quiet breathing of the planet, which was also forty strokes a minute, if one only knew how to listen.

He did not die. 

He became a pilot in the deep wood. 

When Karr laid his hand upon Engine No. 2 the next morning, he felt the crosshead moving with an uncanny, silky grace, guided through its slide by a will that was neither iron nor tree, but something older and far kinder: an old Oiler, resting in the roots, keeping the bearings cool forever.
