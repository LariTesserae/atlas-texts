A power that is both benevolent and wise would know, before anything else, the absolute terror of its own weight.

A foolish god would weep over this basin. A foolish god would see the taut skin of the workers, the parched tallow of their lips, the dead laid out like cured lumber on the splinter-fences, and it would tear open the vaulted ceiling to pour down sweet, warm rain.

And in forty-eight hours, the basin would be dead.

The rain would swell the kiln-dry heartwood by thirty percent. Tenement blocks, anchored with five-foot iron bolts, would be ripped in half as the living cambium buckled upward in titanic, heaving blisters. The elevated rail lines, bored to tolerances of a thousandth of an inch, would snap their dowels and derail into the canyons. The planer-lice would drown in their own dissolved frass, turning into foul, sour paste in the seams. The coursers’ dry silicone blood would coagulate; the harness-moths’ condensation plates would clog with muck; and across millions of square yards of pristine, unrotted cellulose, the spores of ancient molds—dormant since the world first dried—would bloom into a gray, suffocating fuzz that the human lungs, built only for filtered nitrogen and turpentine, could not breathe for three minutes without drowning in their own phlegm.

And worst of all: moisture brings rot, and rot brings methane, and in an atmosphere pumped by rhythmic bellows, a single hot bearing on a pneumatic shaft would set the entire basin—two thousand miles of dry tinder, pine-esters, and gas—into an all-consuming firestorm that would burn until the mantle collapsed.

A wise power would keep its tears behind its eyes. It would keep its hands cold. It would step into the basin not as a storm, but as an absence of strain.

***

It would begin with the tension.

For three hundred miles along the regional fault, the white bristles of glass-sharp wood stand under hundreds of tons of lateral dry torque. The whole rift is an engine wound too tight; every worker carries the sound of the *ping* in the marrow of their hips, waiting for the floor beneath their bed to shear and fling their barracks into the haze.

The power would slide into the grain like an oil that has no mass. It would not wet the wood; it would slip between the cellulose fibers, easing the intermolecular shear, releasing the catastrophic torque micron by micron. It would take forty years to do it, so slowly that not a single scraper-sled would notice the pitch of the floor altering. 

The *pings* would simply become rarer. The terrible, hair-trigger snap that signaled a family’s floor splitting beneath them would soften into a low, groaning settlement, a dull sigh that the heartwood could absorb. The splinters would remain sharp—for the harness-moths must have their moorings—but the grain would stop threatening to tear itself to pieces.

***

Next, it would consider the fear of the spark.

There is no prayer in the barracks, only the negative prayer of the dropped tool. A bronze wrench slipping from a greasy glove is an agony of suspense until it strikes: *did it glance off iron? did it strike an ember?*

The power would address the air. Not by adding oxygen, nor water, but by thickening the mantle of cold nitrogen. It would gather the static electricity that accumulates in the bone-dry drifts of sawdust—the blue, crawling fire that licks along the elevated rail trestles whenever the ceiling bracts pump their twenty-minute gale—and ground it deep into the sub-crustal roots. It would make the atmosphere inert to friction. 

A worker could drop an iron wedge from the top of the sublimation refinery, watching it tumble fifty feet through the halide glare to strike a black steel lag bolt, and there would be only a dull, dead *clink*. No orange star. No flash. No sudden, blinding end to the world. 

The worker would not know why the spark failed to catch. They would only crouch on the platform, knees trembling in their stiff canvas trousers, looking down at the tool, their condenser-mask fluttering with short, panicked breaths, before realizing they were still alive.

***

Then, the power would look at the people’s joints.

It would see the old men whose elbows have bound up with crystalline stearates, whose knees scrape like dry pump-levers because the closed-loop rations allow not a single drop of fluid to be spared for synovial ease. It would see the mothers rubbing grease into the deep, painful pleats of their children’s ankles to keep the dry air from drinking them alive.

The power would not give them water. Water is an outlaw here; to give water is to make them dependent on a miracle that cannot be sustained without ruining their home.

Instead, it would touch the sugar-weevils.

The weevils browse along the raw cambium, clipping dry ribbons of pith. The power would make a quiet, genetic adjustment—a slight shifting of the enzymes in the weevil’s third digestive chamber. The clear, amber fats they store in their hollow segments would shift in viscosity. They would become slightly lower in melting point, rich in a new, unctuous ester that does not evaporate, does not turn rancid, and does not attract dust.

When the coursers hunt the weevils, they would leave behind the same clean, shattered carapaces. But the residue within would be different. The maintenance crews, harvesting the empty shells from beneath the rail dowels, would find an oil so fine, so light, that when rubbed into tallow-skin, it absorbs entirely, sealing the pores with the softness of glove-leather without ever cracking or growing stiff.

A mother would smooth it over her child’s temples. She would feel the skin yield. For the first time in five generations, the skin over the forehead would move freely over the bone, soft and cool as peeled willow, and the child would fall asleep without the dry, maddening itch that had accompanied every shift since the dawn of the settlement.

***

And finally, because benevolence without communion is only cold engineering, the power would touch the dead.

It would leave the burial custom intact. The dead must still be carried to the margin; they must still be stripped of their breathers and laid upon the standing bristles to dry. That is the economy of the basin, and to deny it is to deny the dignity of the people who have mastered it.

But the power would alter the resonance of the ash.

When the body dries, and turns the pale, matte color of seasoned boxwood; when the planer-lice have beveled the fingers flush with the surrounding grain; when the wind has polished the chest until it is indistinguishable from the floor—the power would weave the memory of the dead into the acoustic web of the timber plain.

The living communicate by tapping their heels. They strike the resonant ties, sending thrums through the floor that travel for miles. 

Before the power arrived, those signals were flat and functional: *Shift changing. Freight arriving. Clear the track.* The heartwood was a dead telephone line.

Afterward, the timber would begin to hold an overtone.

A worker walking back from the sublimation refinery at the end of a sixteen-hour shift, his boots dragging through the bone-dry drifts of sawdust, would strike his horn-pegged heel against an expansion tie to signal his return.

And from three miles away, traveling through the interlocking rings of ancient, unrotting ash—passing through the places where his grandfather, his sister, his firstborn child were laid down and planed into the floor—the return pulse would come back to his arches.

It would not be a ghost. It would not be a voice.

It would be a chord.

The wood would ring not with the dull, dead thud of dry cellulose, but with a deep, sustained resonance, warm as sandalwood, vibrating through the bones of his feet, into his shins, settling behind his sternum. It would say: *The grain holds. The rift is wide. We are the foundation, and you are walking on us, and we do not break.*

The worker would stop in the middle of the vast, empty clearance, the twenty-minute gale whipping paper curls of ash against his apron, his face hidden behind the tin valves of his mask. He would feel the floor thrumming beneath him like a great, slow heart that needs no blood to beat.

He would not weep, for a tear is still a life’s waste. 

But he would stand there in the cool halide twilight, lean thirty degrees into the dry wind, and for the first time since the first iron bolt bit into the wood, his knuckles would slowly, completely uncurl.
