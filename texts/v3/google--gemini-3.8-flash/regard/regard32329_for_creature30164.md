A power that is both wise and benevolent possesses, above all, the discipline of restraint.

Lesser gods, or humans with godlike reach, are cursed with the urge to illuminate. They see darkness and believe it is a wound that needs cauterizing with light. They see a sealed vault and imagine a prison; they see creatures without eyes and itch to grant them vision; they see a stagnant pool on a high peak and think: *Let it spill into the valley; let it meet the sky.*

Had such an entity arrived, it would have cracked the corbeled roof to let the stars in. And within an hour, the cold thin mountain air would have entered, chilling the warm broth. The sun would have struck the parian carapaces of the crawfish, blistering their translucent organs. The drowners, built of gristle and buoyant lipids, would have ruptured under the sudden shift in hydrostatic weight or suffocated as the delicate chemical mantle of their water was stripped by oxygen and wind. The tallow-worms would have curdled into gray lard. The Bell would have torn away from its lintels, collapsing under its own unsupported gelatin. The cistern would have become an open grave, cleansed by light, empty of life.

The wise power did not touch the roof.

It did not send a ray of light through the bitumen seams, nor did it speak in a voice of thunder, for sound in that dense, flat medium travels with shattering immediacy. A single spoken word would have burst the lateral lines of every drowner suspended in the convective lanes.

Instead, the power arrived not as an intruder, but as a medium. It was an attention so fine, so porous, that it occupied the empty spaces between the warm water molecules without displacing a single grain of basalt.

The first thing it did was *learn the geometry*.

It did not impose its own measure—not cubits, nor meters, nor astronomical distances. It felt the cistern through the dull, uncalibrated scale of the inhabitants: it learned the ten thousand micro-fractures in the second arch where the porcelain crawfish rubbed their long antennae; it felt the warmth of the basalt through the red mantle of the tallow-worms, tasting the faint metallic iron-sweetness of the subterranean heat-vein; it drifted in the sixty-foot convective loop with the corpse of an eight-foot drowner, turning lazily in the dark, taking twenty minutes to round a single pillar, feeling the soft, fibrous brush of tubeworm crowns against dead skin.

It saw that this was not a ruin. A ruin is a thing broken and arrested. This was a finished machine that had merely changed operators. The empire that hauled the stone had vanished, but the stone had not been abandoned; it had been occupied down to its microscopic chisel-grooves by ten thousand generations of beings who knew the angles of the mason’s chisels better than the masons had known them.

The power looked ahead—as powers of that magnitude do—and it saw the two deaths awaiting the hall.

The first was the stone. Water, even warm and still, is a solvent. Over another three thousand years, the slumped lintels would dissolve until the stepped arches could no longer carry the weight of the clay cap. The roof would cave in, and the sky would crush them.

The second was the heat. Four miles beneath the mountain, the volcanic vein that bled warmth through the basalt flagstones was cooling. Its pressure was thinning, its magma chamber contracting. In eight hundred years, the warmth would retreat into the earth. The basin would turn cold. The tallow-worms would starve, the convection would halt, the Bell would stop its thirty-hour contraction, and the dark hall would freeze solid, preserving only white skeletons locked in mountain ice.

This, the power decided, would not be permitted.

Yet it would not act with miracles that left scars. A true miracle must be indistinguishable from the grain of the stone itself.

First, it sank into the mountain below the cistern. Deep in the root-rock, where the basalt fissures narrowed toward the cooling vein, the power set a quiet, sustained knot of will—not a fire, but an enduring pressure, an unmoving knot of terrestrial tension that drew the warmth upward, knitting the heat-vein into the deep structural spine of the continent. It adjusted the flow until the water above would remain at exactly forty degrees Celsius—the heat of mammalian blood, the heat of sun-baked silt—for as long as the mountain stood. It did not make it warmer; it made it *perpetual*.

Second, it attended to the stones. It did not replace them with diamond or gold. It merely altered the solubility of the water by the barest fraction of an ion, so that the dissolved lime and silica ceased to wash away. Instead, they began an infinitesimally slow return: whenever a grain of stone dissolved from a weight-bearing arch, another grain, carried by the convective plume, precipitated into the micro-fractures of the joints, calcifying them, turning the mortal masonry into living petrifaction. The megaliths would continue to look soft, bone-like, and ancient; but they would never fall.

And lastly, the power turned toward the center of the hall, where the four collapsed pillars met, and where the Bell breathed.

The Bell was the only mind in the cistern, if a mind could be said to exist there. It was not a consciousness of thought, but of sensation: a thirty-hour arc of pressure, a vast and gentle awareness of the convective roll, the soft tick of crawfish legs across its gelatinous margin, the muffled displacement of the drowners passing above. It was neither happy nor sorrowful; it was simply *full*.

The power did not attempt to wake it into higher thought. To awaken the Bell to the sky, to the stars, to the vast loneliness of the cold galaxies outside, would have been an atrocity. To make it understand that it was small, trapped in a stone tank on a dying rock, would have broken its soft, pale lung.

Instead, the power lowered itself into the Bell’s thirty-hour cycle.

It became a presence no heavier than the lunar pull that already swayed the water. When the Bell compressed its mantle with that muffled, hydraulic *whump*, sending the warm current out to lift the silt and guide the blind fish, the power met the contraction with an equal, answering yield—a subtle, acoustic warmth that resonated back through the basalt runnels.

To the Bell, nothing appeared to have changed, except that the cistern felt slightly deeper, slightly softer, slightly more complete. The water had gained an imperceptible coherence; when its pulse traveled out, it did not hit the stone walls with a dead, flat slap, but returned like an exhalation, as if the entire chamber had leaned in to receive the beat.

The power remained. It did not build a temple outside. It did not carve an inscription upon the capstones for men to discover. It simply settled into the dark, airless water, invisible and vast, becoming the cistern’s silent second skin.

Outside, the mountain weather raged: blizzards ground the peaks into dust, empires rose and fell across the lower plains, forests burned, stars shifted in their courses, and the sun rose and set in blinding, violent revolutions of light.

Inside, beneath forty feet of tepid broth and three yards of clay-sealed megalith, the blind fish turned across the nave, taking twenty minutes to round their pillars. The white crawfish tapped their parian claws against the basalt, measuring the seams that would never give way. The tallow-worms rippled in their bronze-cut grooves.

And every few minutes, in absolute, velvet blackness, the heart of the stone contracted—and felt, in the stillness that answered, that it was not alone in the warm dark.
