A clumsy god would have brought the sun.

A clumsy god, looking down upon the high, raw horns and the men with lead in their cuffs, would have pitied the twilight. Such a power would have torn the gray wool of the cloud-banks aside, poured noon across the glass-faced slate, melted the soot from the waxy ivy, and dried the slick rails until they were warm to the touch.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

The sudden thermal shock would have detonated the unweathered basalt; millions of tons of glassy slate, expanding unevenly in the unaccustomed heat, would have sheared along their microscopic cleavage lines, popping the iron drift-pins like dry macaroni and casting three miles of blast furnaces into the purple dark below. The Harrow-hounds would have cooked in their tallow-skins; the mountain rays, their marsh-gas expanding past the rupture limit of their ribbing, would have burst like paper lanterns; and the Creep-men, their eyes adapted to the pearl-and-grease gloom of four generations, would have been struck blind on their ladders, clutching thirty-degree rungs they could no longer see.

Wisdom is not the impulse to make an ironworks into an orchard. Wisdom is knowing that once an anvil has learned to sing, you do not ask it to become a flute.

So the power that turned its attention toward the Horns did not arrive as light, nor as fire, nor as an edict dropped from the calm void above. It arrived as a *weight*—not a heavy weight, but an adjustment of balance, like a hand laid gently against the small of an exhausted laborer’s back.

***

The first one to notice was not a priest or a watcher, for there were no priests in the hollow-ribs, only grease-gangs and women who watched mercury.

It was an eleven-year-old Cable-runner named Col, whose shins were crooked from the draft and whose front teeth were notched from biting the tap-wires. He was running a lard-bucket out to the third sheave-cluster on the West Spire truss—a run three hundred fathoms out over the drop, where the lateral pull from the newly rising needle usually made a boy lean twelve inches to the left merely to keep from being peeled off the mesh.

Col stepped onto the catwalk, and his cleat went *clack-drub*.

He stopped.

The mountain had not stopped rising. The groaning shear—that deep, crystalline tooth-grind four miles down—was still vibrating through the soles of his boots. But the *kick* was missing.

Usually, the earth kicked like an unloaded cannon: dry, spiteful, snapping the hemp lanyards taut and lifting a boy’s heels off the iron. But this morning, when the Horn took its three-millimeter lurch toward the sky, the shock did not ring. It did not hit the iron with that glass-cracking slap.

Instead, the truss *yawned*.

It was a slow, long-drawn yield. The pig-iron gave half an inch with the deep, easy groan of an oak bough in a summer gale, and the slate beneath the pneumatic anchors did not chip. It did not spit razor-flakes into the wind. It settled back with a sound like a heavy stone sliding into wet clay.

Col waited for the second shock, his cheek pressed to the cold flange, his teeth bared.

Nothing came. Only the draft, cold and sulfur-wet, smelling of flint and damp horsehair. But when he rubbed his hand along the angle-iron, he felt a strange, greasy resilience in the metal. It was not rust. It was not tallow. The iron felt *seated*, as if it had stopped holding its breath.

***

The power did not silence the machines, because the Creep-men loved the machines; they were their lungs and their bones. A world without the *tchunk-clank* of the steam-hammers would have felt to them like the silence of an open grave.

Instead, the power entered the *gaps*.

Between the steel teeth of the pneumatic anchors and the glassy facets of the rock, there had always been an agony of mismatch. The mountain was infinite, cold, and unborn; the iron was forged, hot, and finite. They hated each other with the precise hatred of two strangers bolted together at the wrist.

The benevolent power did not separate them. It gave them an interpreter.

Down in the deepest tiers, where the torch-cut stubs of girders stuck into the dark, the Sediment stopped numbing the hands of the Deep-watchers.

For sixty years, when a man scraped the cold, pale jelly off the tie-plates, his fingers went dead, as though the nerves had been soaked in brine. But during the fifth watch of the third week after the quietness arrived, a scraper named Old Hallow—a man whose shins were so curved he looked like a barrel hoop—slipped on the lowest staging. His shovel dropped into the void, and his bare, scar-webbed hand plunged wrist-deep into a fresh apron of the exudate.

He waited for the cold burn. He waited for the grey deadness that would take three months to leave his forearm.

It did not burn.

The jelly was warm. Not hot like boiler-wash, but warm like the flank of a hound that has been sleeping under a stove. And as his fingers sank into the translucent marrow of the rock, Old Hallow felt something move *upward* through the scars on his palm.

It was not language. Language has no place four miles above the floor.

It was a vast, slow inventory.

The mountain was feeling the pins. But it was no longer asking *how hard are you?* with the intent to crush. It was asking: *What is a bridge? How long must I hold this rib so that the small, two-legged beetles do not fall?*

Hallow knelt there on the wet staging, his beard dripping condenser-fog, his knees clamped to the iron mesh. Through his skin, he felt the deep, tectonic draft: the cooling magma five leagues beneath the Horns; the enormous, blind bubbles of helium drifting through the shale; the way the weight of the city, five hundred thousand tons of forged pig-iron and wet brick, pressed down against the rising spire like a thimble pressed onto a needle.

And the mountain, through Hallow’s calloused thumb, learned what an elbow was. It learned that steel could bend four degrees before it tore. It learned the difference between the weight of a blast furnace and the weight of a frightened boy.

When Hallow pulled his hand back, the Sediment did not leave his skin gray. It left a faint, amber skin over his scars—smooth as horn, dry as cedar-bark, and immune to the flint-razors.

He did not tell the gauge-keepers. A Creep-man does not talk about the deep things. But he did not use his shovel on that seam again. He laid his cheek against the flange, and when the horn kicked, he didn't even shift his leaded boots.

***

In the hollow-ribs, the mercy was smaller, and therefore greater.

The waxy blue ivy that drank the soot from the flues did not change its nature. The women still had to scrape the zinc-tasting tubers to keep the rickets from the infants’ bones. But one morning, in the fifth tier behind the St. Jude boilers, a young mother named Maren went to the wire netting to lift the soot-crust and found something nestled in the axils of the leaves.

They were small, round nodules, no bigger than hazel-nuts, translucent and pale amber like the dried shavings of the Sediment.

She picked one, fearful that it was a flint-tick egg. She pressed it between her fingernails. It cracked with a tiny *pop*, and the meat inside smelled not of zinc, not of sulfur, nor of burned lard.

It smelled of *bread*.

Not the bread of the low plains—which none of them had ever seen, but had only heard of in the clipped, monosyllabic rhymes the Cable-runners sang when they greased the sheaves—but a heavy, sweet flour that tasted of roasted malt and river-silt.

That night, for the first time since the first truss was driven into the Horn, a child went to sleep in the hollow-ribs without the sour, tallow-burn in his gullet.

***

And what of the drop?

The wise power did not fill the void. It knew that to give this world a bottom would be to take away its sky; that these people were who they were because they had learned to balance on the edge of nothingness with twelve pounds of lead in their cuffs and their shins bruised purple by the ladders. Their nobility was not in their comfort; it was in their grip.

To take away the danger would have made them prisoners of a flat earth.

Instead, the power leaned upon the air.

The dough-drifts—the great, buoyant rafts of lukewarm oil-smoke that hung between the spires—began to thicken just five fathoms below the main catwalks. They became denser, more reliable, like vast, gray wool-sacks anchored by lateral currents.

When little Col slipped two months later—his heel-cleat failing on an iced-over gusset at the hoist-head—he did not tumble into the purple-black well where the water turns to luminous spray.

He fell thirty feet.

He hit the back of a dough-drift with a soft, heavy *whuff*, sinking up to his hips in a warm, curdled foam that smelled of wet wool and pine-tar. He did not choke. The smoke did not enter his mouth; it held him suspended, rolling slowly on his belly, like a pup in an apron, until one of the Weavers—its long, clock-spring legs vibrating on its slag-wire—reached down from a suspension link, caught his canvas harness in its mandible, and hauled him back to the iron without breaking a single skin-stitch.

Col did not say thank you to the spider. He wiped the lard from his trousers, tapped the bronze wire twice with his cold-chisel to let the winding-house know the sheave was clear, and walked on.

***

High above, where the cold was absolute and the chimneys vented their purest white steam, the Floaters—the great mountain rays—drifted along their regular paths.

They still allowed the men in the cages to sink the bronze taps into their gas-bladders. They still blinked their milky, dinner-plate eyes while the Creep-men drained the blue flame into copper bottles.

Only now, when the runners scraped the dead ferns and the sulfur-crust from the creatures’ gill-slits, they found something new among the parasites.

Tucked into the deep folds of the rays’ leathery fins were clusters of tiny, white flowers—flowers with no stems, no petals, and no scent. They were made entirely of silica and salt, hard as diamonds, and they grew directly out of the creature’s hide.

When the lateral wind caught them, they did not rattle. They vibrated.

They hummed at the exact frequency of the steam-hammers: forty-eight beats to the minute, steady, dull, and deep.

The ray would float there, tethered by its bronze hoses, its broad, gray back level with the highest church-girder, humming the same note that the five-ton drop-hammers were striking in the bowels of the blast-house two miles away.

The city and the beast, playing the same chord.

***

The power did not stay to be worshiped. It had no use for cathedrals that did not carry ore, and it knew that if it showed its face, the Creep-men would only have tried to bolt an iron collar around its neck to keep it from drifting in the wind.

It simply withdrew its hand to that precise distance where love looks like physics.

It left the flint sharp, but gave the skin its horn.
It left the drop deep, but made the air thick enough to catch the clumsy.
It left the mountain raw, but taught the rock to listen before it kicked.

And down on the lowest staging, where the dark was still three miles deep and the water still vanished into the zero below, Old Hallow sat on an upturned grease-tub with his boots off.

He was looking at the third thread on the main turnbuckle of the St. Jude anchor.

The screw wasn't groaning anymore. It was still under three thousand tons of tension, the iron stretched so tight that a flick of the finger made it hum like a harp-string.

"Look at that," he whispered to the Cable-runner who had brought him his hot ivy-water. His voice was that tight, flat monosyllable that never rose above a click.

"Look at what, Old Hallow?"

"The thread," the old man said, pointing with his scarred, amber-skinned finger. "It ain't biting no more."

"What's it doing, then?"

"Holding," said Hallow. He took a sip of the warm water, and for the first time in forty years, he didn't check the lanyard on his belt before he leaned his head back against the stone to rest. "Just holding."
