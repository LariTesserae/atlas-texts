The power did not arrive from the sky, for to drop down into that spinning cauldron like an eagle would have been an intrusion of mass, displacing the very water whose frantic equilibrium kept the creatures alive. 

Nor did it arrive with light. Light down there was already a sickness—a gray, churned twilight full of suspended grit and scalding froth. More light would have only blinded the clear-shelled disks at the rim, who needed the upside-down gloom to find their drift.

Instead, the power arrived as *temperance*. It entered the basin the way cold oil enters warm water: invisibly, molecularly, through the fractures in the floor where the scalding copper plumes were birthed.

A foolish power would have pulled the plug. It would have drained the amphitheater to save the mountain, letting thousands of tons of copper brine wash down the slopes in an avalanche of steam, leaving the jointed builders stranded on the open, freezing shelves to suffocate in the thin alpine gale, their soft internal organs collapsing without the hydrostatic pressure that held them whole.

A proud power would have turned the basin into a garden—forced the basalt to bloom with green lilies and replaced the bitter brine with sweet spring water, oblivious to the fact that the siphon-worms ate pulverized flint, not sunshine, and that the long, pale builders had lungs designed only for the steam of raw iron and sulfur.

Wisdom does not overturn the table. Wisdom pulls up a chair.

The power began with the sound. 

The roar was the true killer. The cavitation implosions were not merely loud; they were physical hammers, shockwaves that tore the delicate cilia of the siphon-worms from their stone moorings and cracked the bony louvers over the builders’ breathing pits. 

So the power made itself soft. It spread throughout the fluid like a vast, permeable net of stillness, settling into the water’s acoustic grain. It did not stop the gyre—the gyre was needed to circulate the mineral dust—but it became an anvil for the implosions. When a bell of vacuum tore open in the center and snapped shut, the shockwave did not strike the basalt walls with the crack of artillery. The power received the blow into its own invisible mass, drinking the concussive spike and converting it into a deep, subterranean hum, a low C that vibrated not as a weapon, but as a cradle. 

The marrow of the builders ceased to ache. The vibration remained in their ribs, but it was no longer trying to shake their skeletons apart; it became steady, like the heartbeat of a mother heard through the wall of a womb.

Next, the power went to the rim.

Here was the greatest terror. The thousands of coin-sized disks of mica-flesh were dying by the hour, their tiny suction margins tearing away under the tremendous centrifugal urge of the gyre. They were holding back a flood with their own bodies, drinking the freezing daylight while their bellies burned in the brine.

The power did not erect a wall of glass or stone to relieve them, for if the boundary became rigid, the organisms would have had nothing to cling to, no interface to feed upon. Instead, the power placed its own palm against the outside of the water’s trembling skin.

It did not push inward. It simply became an opposing tension, cool and infinitely yielding, like the curved hand of a gardener steadying a swollen, overripe fruit. 

At once, the frantic strain along the rim eased. The water still bulged into the empty valley below, still mirrored the upside-down sky, but the violent flexing ceased. The laminar flow smoothed from a frantic tear into a thick, syrupy glide. The clear disks felt the sudden slack in the threshold; their cupped margins relaxed. They no longer had to tear themselves in two to anchor the sea. They began, for the first time since the floor broke, simply to drift, their wire-legs trawling easily through the surface layer, snatching the pale pulp of the deep worms without having to brace against obliteration.

Finally, the power sank to the drowned huts.

It did not enter as an emperor, but as a guest who knows how to fold its limbs. It gathered in the shadow of the tilted basalt slabs where the builders sat huddled, four and five together, their tripod legs tucked against their narrow breasts, their horn-tipped thumbs grinding endlessly at the stone.

They could not see the power—they had no eyes for the invisible—but their spade-fingered hands felt a sudden, miraculous change in the rock beneath them.

The basalt did not soften, but the razor edges—the glass-sharp fractures that were cutting through their moss tethers and slicing into the gray leather of their joints—began to yield. Under the patient, rhythmic rubbing of their thumbs, the stone began to give way with the ease of wet chalk. Where they had been laboring to grind away a fraction of an inch over the course of centuries, the basalt now surrendered to their touch, smoothing down into gentle, polished bowls that fit the contours of their hips and knees.

The power did not build a home for them; it allowed *their* building to work.

Beneath one of the fallen slabs, an elder of the builders—whose dorsal vents were ringed with thick, crusting layers of old iron—paused. Its thumb, worn raw to the cartilage, hovered over a sharp basalt spur that had been gouging into its left flank with every turn of the water.

Slowly, testing the water’s new harmonic, it pressed its thumb down again. 

The stone crumbled under its touch like hardened sand. It smoothed the corner into a round, velvet-surfaced hollow. The creature did not look up; it had nowhere to look. But the calcified louvers across its mouth-slit, which had been locked shut in defensive agony for weeks, fluttered once. 

Twice.

Then they opened wide. 

A draft of warm, sulfur-rich brine rushed into its interior chambers, clear of grit, scrubbed of the concussive violence that tore at tissues. Its four dorsal pits flared, drinking deeply of the iron-stained water, and the plumes of oxidized waste that trailed from its shoulder blades into the gloom were no longer jagged spurts of distress, but long, lazy ribbons of deep, rust-colored velvet.

The power remained. It did not leave after the work was done, because it knew the nature of the mountain: the fracture was young, the earth was restless, and left alone, the cathedral would eventually tear itself open again.

It settled into the deep basalt seams alongside the colonial siphon-worms, letting the worms use its quiet, cool presence as a scaffold to weave their vulcanized rubber deeper into the mountain’s roots. It stayed at the rim, an invisible, patient palm cupping the trembling drop of brine against the emptiness of the valley.

The place remained monstrous to anything of the air. It remained an engine of pressure, heat, and dark stone suspended impossibly high in the gray sky, where no sea had any right to be. 

But it was no longer a slaughterhouse. 

It was a city of the deep earth, held aloft in the clouds, where blind things in the drowned ruins smoothed their floors in peace, while high above them, upside-down along the edge of the sky, ten thousand clear stars held the water to the heavens, and found it light.
