A power that is both benevolent and wise possesses, above all else, the capacity for restraint.

A clumsy god, looking down into that stifling crack miles beneath the summit, would have brought light. And light would have been an atrocity. 

Light would have seared the unpigmented carapaces of the four dozen amphipods, cooking their milky, transparent tissues like egg whites on hot stone. Light would have bred green phototrophic invaders in the warm seepages, choking the delicate, iron-breathing rust mats under alien bloom. Light would have demanded eyes of creatures that had spent ten thousand generations perfecting the holy architecture of touch, turning their intricate, antenna-woven cosmos into sudden, blinding terror.

A foolish benevolence would have tried to "save" them. It would have cracked the mountain open to the clean, cool sky, letting in the mountain wind—and in doing so, it would have dropped the humidity by ten percent, drying the wet gills of the flatworm into brittle husks in forty seconds, dropping the eighty-five-degree womb to fifteen below zero, snapping the blown-sugar lattice of gypsum straws into a hail of shattered dust.

Wisdom knows that this place is not a prison. It is an egg.

And so, when the power turns its vast and tender attention toward the nodule between the stalled volcanic plates, it does not descend as fire, or thunder, or a hand reaching through the rock. It does not displace a single drop of the warm, heavy air. It brings no draft; it alters neither the temperature nor the chemical tang of the sulfur-seep.

Instead, it folds itself infinitely down. It makes itself smaller than the space between two aragonite needles. It enters the hollow not as an invader, but as a medium—a pervasive, weightless presence that slips through the stone like heat through iron, settling into the damp equilibrium of the room without displacing a single atom.

First, it attends to the pain that does not belong there.

The power finds the four rough grooves in the soft tuff, and the chert scraper lying half-drowned in the lime-pool, and the waterlogged wedge of yew. 

A wise power can read the archaeology of agony. It perceives the ghost of the moment that brought those things into the dark: a human being, miles from their valley, chased or fallen into a cleft in the mountain’s crown, squeezing down through suffocating, vertical chimneys until they tumbled into this hot, dripping pocket. It feels the frantic hammering of that lost heart, the airless scrape of skin against basalt, the blind fingers knapping flint in absolute blackness, carving four desperate tallies—*hours? days? attempts to breathe?*—before the realization took hold that the mountain was healing shut around them, that the exit was narrowing by the hour under the weeping crystal crust.

The power feels the phantom terror that still clings to the yew wedge—driven into the crack with bruised hands, pounded tight with a river stone in a frantic, weeping bid to keep the mountain’s jaws apart—before the crawler surrendered, backing away or perishing in some deeper shaft now long since crushed flat.

The power does not erase the marks. To erase them would be to insult the suffering that carved them. 

Instead, it lays a profound, soothing stillness over that ancient fear. It presses into the mineral memory of the tuff and the grain of the bruised-plum yew, lifting the vibration of human terror out of the wood and stone, replacing it with a quiet, solemn absolution. The wedge is no longer a tool of panic; the power blesses it into what it has already become: a generous altar. It touches the fungal mycelium working through the annual rings, not speeding it, but blessing its quiet feast, ensuring that the faint, sweet perfume of fermenting lignin continues to leak into the air, feeding the tiny commonwealth until the wood is entirely stone.

Next, the power turns its gaze upon the living.

It does not elevate them. It does not grant them intellect, or souls of fire, or visions of the stars they will never see. To make them human would be to make them wretched.

Instead, it enters their senses. It experiences the hollow through the sixty joints of an amphipod’s legs. 

It feels the immaculate, clockwork perfection of stepping from one needle tip to another, the sheer, staggering intelligence of a body that can read the chemical poetry of iron and sulfur through a film of warm water three molecules thick. It feels the slow, rhythmic *tick* of the falling water-straws, registering the sound not as acoustic deadness, but as the steady, reassuring heartbeat of a mother who never leaves.

And because it is benevolent, it offers them the only gift that does not violate their nature: *grace within their law.*

It will not stop the mountain from closing. The power understands that this hollow is mortal, just as a flower is mortal, just as a star is mortal. The straws will meet the floor. The needles will knit. The pocket will, in five hundred years or a thousand, become a solid tooth of white chalcedony and gypsum, a seamless jewel embedded in the basalt brow of the world. That is the mountain's right; it is the poetry of stone.

But the power ensures that the closing will be gentle.

It reaches into the chemistry of the geothermal seep, refining it with microscopic, undetectable tenderness. It ensures that the water does not sour into lethal acidity; that the iron ions remain steady and sweet to the bacterial mats; that the brood-pouches of the four dozen amphipods yield their two or three tiny, glass-like young in perfect rhythm with the food that is left. 

It touches the gliding flatworm, the blind butcher hunting across the botryoidal chalcedony. It honors the hunger of the worm and the death of the crustacean, weaving between them a quiet peace: when the flatworm envelopes an amphipod that has stayed too long upon the needles, the power touches the dying nervous system, extinguishing all sensation of pain, leaving only a swift, warm dissolution—a soft slipping back into the mineral soup from which they both were drawn.

And lastly, the power does something that leaves no physical mark at all.

It witnesses them.

For centuries, this cart-bed of warm, weeping stone has existed in total, utter irrelevance to the cosmos. Empires rose and ground themselves to dust in the green valleys below; ships sailed across oceans; fires were lit and extinguished; and throughout all of it, the white amphipod stepped across its crystal forest, blind, immaculate, and entirely unseen.

The power sits within the dark with them. It becomes the space’s companion. It stays through the centuries, holding the hollow in its radiant, invisible attention, so that every step of every hair-fine leg, every division of the iron-breathing bacteria, every slow drop falling through the center of a hollow straw, is registered, cherished, and known.

They are no longer alone in the blind pressure of the deep earth. They are held in the mind of a god that asks nothing of them, that demands no worship, that makes no sound.

And when the end finally comes—centuries from now, when the last aragonite needle knits into its neighbor, when the yew is entirely turned to stone, and the last drop of water is absorbed into the solidifying white core—the power will remain. It will fold the memory of the four tallies, the scent of the wet flour and copper, the empty, calcified carapaces, and the slow, eighty-five-degree dark into itself, keeping them safe forever in the quietest, most tender vault of eternity.

Until then, the air does not move. 
The water drops: *tick.*
And in the absolute, sacred dark, the white things continue their slow, perfect dance across the needles.
