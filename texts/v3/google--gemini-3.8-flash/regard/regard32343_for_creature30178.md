The Power arrived not with a trumpet or a tear in the sky, but as a vast, non-disruptive weight of attention—the way an immense ship, docking silently in the dark, displaces water so smoothly that the small skiffs tied to the pilings merely rise an inch upon the swell and do not rock.

It possessed the two qualities that make intervention safe: it was boundless in its capacity to love what already existed, and it was entirely devoid of the urge to tidy up.

A lesser power—one merely powerful, or clumsily kind—would have looked at the five-mile lattice and seen an atrocity: an unnatural cage of oxidized ore, poisoned with heavy zinc, populated by deformed ape-descendants whose toes had grown grotesque to cling to angle iron. Such a power would have broken the machine to "free" them. It would have cooled the dynamos, struck down the iron to plant mountain oaks, and set the walkers upon soft loam. And within forty-eight hours, every living thing on the tiers would have been dead. The ferns would have blackened under the sudden, killing altitude frost; the lung-eels would have suffocated in the drying gutters; and the walkers, born to the warm, rhythmic oven of the box-beams, would have died of cold and broken hearts, terrified by the sudden, hideous silence of a world without a pulse.

Wisdom knows that home is not a design; it is an accommodation.

So the Power did not land. It did not speak. It did not alter the sun, nor did it burn away the oily steam. Instead, it sank inward, diffusing its consciousness down through the tiers, through the hanging ferns and the screaming clouds of corvids, down into the iron gut where no walker had ever set foot.

It went down to the dark where the machine carried its own secret terror: *weariness.*

Five miles below the squash gardens, in the blind, roaring heat where the dynamos churned on ancient cyclical ballast, the machine was failing. Not today, and not next year, but eventually. A pin-bushing the size of a hay-barn, worn paper-thin by five centuries of ungreased rotation, was screaming toward a shear. When it sheared, three tiers—twenty thousand acres of flume, ginger, and sleeping families—would peel away from the sheer wall and flutter like torn foil into the gray cloud floor below.

The Power did not replace the bushing with an immortal alloy or a beam of divine light. To put something eternal into an aging machine is to create a tooth that tears the gear.

Instead, it became the friction’s companion.

It slipped between the scored steel of the pin and the groaning wall of the housing, taking the monstrous, multi-ton shear stress into its own immaterial fabric. It did not eliminate the heat; it drew the heat into an even, patient dissipation, whispering to the atomic lattice of the metal, coaxing the wandering crystalline grains to knit together rather than cleave. The pin would still groan. The counterweights would still drop their half-inch a week. But the catastrophic snap was folded gently into the impossible future, softened into a slow, harmless plastic flow that the lattice could bear for another thousand years.

Then, the Power moved up into the veins.

It moved through the miles of boiler tubing and the zinc-lined flumes. It saw how the people died: not from the height—they were too sure-footed for that—but from the "sour" water. It saw a young woman, her knuckles yellowed with engine-tallow, carrying a clay jar to a venting valve, dreading the green sheen of copper salts that would twist her child’s gut into bleeding knots.

The Power did not turn the sour water into clear mountain spring water. If it had, the limpets that grazed the iron-fixing bacteria would have starved, the algal hair would have withered, and the flume walls, stripped of their bio-scum, would have oxidized through in a season, draining the watercress beds.

Instead, it wrought a microscopic, chemical charity.

At the small, hand-hewn spigots where the walkers drew their drink, it subtly altered the precipitation threshold of the heavy minerals. As the green water struck the air, the toxic cupric ions bonded to the free carbon in the steam, dropping out instantly as a harmless, sandy silt that rattled into the bottoms of their pots like fine gravel. The water above it remained warm, flat, and tasting faintly of sulfur—it was not paradise water, but it was sweet enough to drink without fear. The mother would tilt the pot, look at the strange, sudden grit settling at the bottom, brush it away with a calloused thumb, and give the jar to her child. She would think herself clever for boiling it just so; she would credit her own hand, and her pride would keep her tall on the wet flanges.

Wisdom never takes the credit, for gratitude is an unpaid debt that makes mortals clumsy.

Finally, the Power rose to the upper decks, where the high-glare noon made mirrors of the puddles and the wind smelled of wet zinc and compost.

It drifted through the dry cells—the hollow box-beams where the sleepers lay in canvas hammocks. It saw an old man whose horn-shod feet were cracked and bleeding from forty years of walking the eighteen-inch girders, his joints swollen with the damp salt of the flumes.

The Power did not cure his joints; to give an old walker the limber limbs of a youth on those high narrow ledges would make him careless, and carelessness two miles up is death. But it sat beside him in the warm dark of the beam, matching its presence to the heavy, three-cycle thrum of the deep engines. It took the sharp, iron ache from his marrow, turning it into the deep, heavy tiredness of a worker who has done a fair day’s shift and earned his rest.

And into his mind—not in words, but in the slow, mechanical imagery of his world—it breathed a dream.

The old man did not dream of heaven, nor of an earth he had never known with fields of grass he would not know how to tread. He dreamed of a new flume. He dreamed that around the corner of the Great North Pylon, where the rust had eaten the catwalk fifty years ago, there lay a sound, twelve-inch runner of channel iron, dry and broad, leading to a forgotten condenser deck thick with wild ginger and sweet white watercress that no one had yet picked.

When the old man woke to the rhythmic *clank-tonk* of the morning shutters expanding in the dawn glare, the pain in his knees was an old friend rather than an enemy. He slid from his hammock, took his aluminum balance-pole, and went out into the mist, his cleft toes gripping the warm, beaded steel with quiet, habituated mastery.

The Power watched him go.

It lingered only long enough to ensure that the balance held. It saw a fat skink snap a beetle off a copper wire; it heard the harsh, metallic *clong* of a corvid diving through the steam; it felt the mountain of steel draw its massive, lukewarm breath from the boilers below.

Satisfied that the truce remained whole, that the iron held the green, and that the green held the walkers, the Power drew its attention back into the wide, cold sky.

It left the world precisely as dangerous as it had been, precisely as hard, and precisely as beautiful. It had changed almost nothing, save that the mountain would not fall, the water would not poison, and the footing, by an invisible fraction of a millimeter, was just a little more sure.
