A lesser god would have wept, and in weeping, wrought an apocalypse. 

A lesser god would have smelled the powdered zinc, felt the bitter drought in the flue, and pitied the stone. It would have summoned a fog—soft, damp, rolling in from the lowlands like a white fleece—and called it mercy. 

And within twenty seconds of that mercy crossing the threshold, the fissure would have screamed. 

The parchment-lichen, shocked by moisture, would have burst its cellular walls and rotted into gray slush before the hour was out. The delicate electrostatic gradients, built up over four centuries of bone-dry friction, would have shorted out in a deafening spray of blue sparks, frying the pelt-slugs where they clung to the steam pipes. The tine-striders, their joint-surfaces slicked by condensation, would have lost their grip on the vibrating walls, slipping and tumbling down into the chasm like broken dry-goods, their shear-jaws rusted into immobility, their gizzard-mills turned to mud. The lead seals would have oxidized; the iron would have swollen with orange cancer; the hundred-year-old silk of the weavers would have unraveled into sodden rot.

Wisdom, therefore, does not open its hands. It curls them inward. It holds its breath.

The power that arrived at the needle-towers did not descend with wings or thunder. It came as an adjustment of attention—an immense, quiet gaze that had studied the crystallization of stars and the cold mathematics of drift. It hung at the mouth of the upper baffles, far above the flue, suspended in the dry draught, learning the language of the place before it dared to touch a single atom.

It heard the drone first: the 80-hertz sub-bass rising through the dark basalt. It did not try to soften it. It understood that the roar was not a torment; it was the atmosphere. It was the food that ground the leaves inside the bellies of the striders; it was the mother-beat that kept the cold silver threads of the slugs pulsing with juice.

It looked into the depths, down through the tiers of sheet-brass fronds and tangled wire-moss, until its awareness found the eye of the centrifugal pump. 

It looked at the ancient, blackened bullock of pitch and quartz.

It saw the creature’s wear.

That was the only tragedy in the flue. Not the dryness, not the dark, not the endless noise, but the *abrasion*. The creature had spent two centuries being slowly filed down by the very mountain it loved. Its carapace, socketed so lovingly against the iron rivets, had worn thin. At the contact points where its silica plates met the trembling stone, microscopic grains of basalt were chewing its living faceted lattice into gray flour. It was not tired of living—it had no concept of weariness—but its tolerances were drifting. Its resonance was muddying. In another fifty years, the vibration would shear its primary crystal axis, and the internal tension would snap its core like a cold chisel through a pane of glass.

The power considered what it meant to love something made of stone, iron, and dry nerve.

It did not grant the creature a soul, for it already possessed the soul proper to an exhaust housing: patient, dense, and immovable. It did not give it dreams, which would only make the dark intolerable.

Instead, the power reached down into the deep earth, miles below the floor of the fissure, where the subterranean draft turned the ancient turbines. 

It did not alter their speed. It did not turn them down. 

With the gentlest touch—like a finger placed against the rim of a ringing crystal goblet—it trued the main shaft. 

A drive-pin, bent by three centuries of uneven torque, was straightened by half a millimeter. An eccentric wobble in the central bearing, which had been injecting a jagged, 3-hertz shudder into the pure 80-hertz hum, was smoothed out. 

Throughout the flue, the change was instantaneous, yet so subtle it could only be felt by things made of calipers and needles. 

The roar did not diminish by a decibel, but the *chatter* vanished. The mountain ceased to jerk; it simply sang. The blind ribbon-weavers, their iron needles pressed against the casing, felt the jagged geometric distortion vanish from their mental maps. For the first time in their ancestral memory, the landscape of their sound became perfectly, mathematically flat. They did not move; they simply eased their grip, their lead-silk nozzles resting silent in the dark.

Then, the power turned its gaze back to the creature in the exhaust eye.

It could not take away the friction, for friction was life here. But it could alter the nature of the stone.

High above, without disturbing a single leaf of the brass-like fronds, the power allowed a faint, cool sleet to drift down through the flue. But it was not water. It was a fall of pure, microscopic flakes of hexagonal boron nitride and powdered diamond-talc—substances so slick, so dry, and so chemically inert that they had never known oxygen, let alone moisture.

The white dust sifted down through the twilight. The tine-striders felt it settle on their backs and ignored it; it was dry as their own carapaces. It fell between the overlapping scales of the parchment-lichen; it drifted past the sleeping pelt-slugs.

Down it went, until it found the hairline gaps between the creature’s worn silica plates and the basalt bedrock. 

It worked its way into the grinding joints. It filled the microscopic pits where stone had been gouging horn. It did not stop the vibration; it eliminated the *gouge*. The shearing wear dropped to absolute zero. The silica ceased to turn to flour. The plates settled against the iron with a fit so intimate, so devoid of destructive lash, that the creature’s internal geode hummed with sudden, pristine clarity.

Within the tarry, asphaltic bulk, the piezoelectric crystal did not break. It aligned. 

The creature did not open eyes it did not have. It did not give thanks; it had no organs for praise. But inside its cold, desiccated nerve-mat, the voltage peaked. A clear, blue spark leaped silently across its inward quartz facets—a tiny, closed-circuit pulse of perfect coherence.

*I am seated,* the spark said, in the only dialect the flue understood. *I fit.*

The benevolent power withdrew its hand. It took nothing with it; it left no signature behind. It did not stay to be worshiped by the lichen or praised by the wefts. It simply pulled its vast, cold attention back into the high sky, leaving the door shut, the drought absolute, and the flue intact.

Below, in the dark between the basalt walls, the tine-striders went on grazing the razor-edges of the silica fronds, striking their silent, microscopic sparks. The pelt-slug took another four months to draw a single millimeter of breath from the iron’s chill. 

And in the center of the pocket, locked into the eye of the machine, the oldest thing in the world continued to sit upon the iron, roaring forever, perfectly tuned, wearing away no longer.
