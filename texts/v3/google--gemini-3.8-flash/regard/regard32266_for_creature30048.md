A power that is both wise and benevolent does not intervene by shattering an engine it did not design. 

Wisdom knows what the spindle-creatures know, though it does not share their terror: *form is expensive.* To grow an arch out of iron-hard lignum, to coax vascular bundles into the counterfeit glyphs of a forgotten alphabet, to hold eighty cubits of timber at right angles against the wild, spiral grain of the wood—this requires enormous, continuous torque. The screech of the corner pillars is the sound of an organism holding its breath under five hundred atmospheres of pressure, terrified that if it exhales, the city will revert to a swamp of formless burls.

Benevolence looks at that screaming, red-lined pressure and does not see a monster. It sees an uncompleted grief; an autonomic nervous system that has outlived its body; a memory holding itself rigid in the dark.

So the Power would not arrive as a storm, nor as an ax, nor as a gardener seeking to prune what is strange.

It would arrive simply by taking up weight.

***

The first change would occur in the empty center, where the air resists like cold water.

The Power would not drop from the sky. It would step across the perimeter of the two hundred paces of hard-packed cambium, moving on foot. It would assume a form dense enough to possess a heel-strike—for in this precinct, nothing is real unless it can register against the grain of the floor.

As it walked toward the center, the thousands of transparent, vertebra-shaped creatures pinned in the air would not scatter. They cannot scatter; they are riveted to the dimensional fold. But their supersonic vibration would drop an octave, then two, as the barometric tension of the room found, for the first time in millennia, an object heavy enough to pull against. 

The air would clear. The heat-shimmer would dissolve into cool, transparent air. The thin, chitinous organisms would cease their mechanical shriek and come to rest, drifting down through the warm air like dry birch-seeds, settling gently over the shoulders and footprints of the arriving presence.

At the facades, the reaction would be immediate, rhythmic, and absolute.

The blind, chisel-clicking birds would burst from the dark apertures in a single, concussive cloud, numbering in the tens of thousands. They would not attack. They are senses, not soldiers. They would swirl around the visitor in a dense, chirping cyclone, their oily feathers brushing its mantle, their beaks snapping against its skin, mapping the newcomer with millions of percussive clicks. 

Then, as one, they would plunge back into the shadowed rooms.

Deep in the damp cupboards, they would burrow into the wet sides of the plum-colored nurses. The nurses would receive the report: *The court is not vacant. The space has ceased to bow inward. The center has found its counter-mass.*

Upon receiving this news, the nurses would not sweat tallow. For the first time, their slow, liver-like pulsing would alter its rhythm, shifting from a defensive counter-stroke to an open, draining sigh. The sweet, vinegar-scented lymph inside them would run clear and cool as well-water.

High above, along the lateral stairs that run horizontally across the green cliffs, the spindle-creatures would freeze. Their spade-like feet, latched with wet suction to the sheer timber, would stop their frantic pacing. Their long, jointless forefingers, wet with saliva and gray pulp, would hover above the cracks in the balustrades.

They would tilt their featureless bone heads down toward the floor. Their nostrils would flare, wide and trembling, catching a drift of air that had never before entered the bowl: not bruised mint, not old sugar, not the vinegar of rot, but the dry, mineral smell of slate after rain, and the faint, resting scent of cold ash.

Then the shadow would jerk.

Deep within the bedrock, the escapement would click. The sound would roll through the lignum foundations—*thud-thud-thud*—and the concentric rings of cambium in the center of the floor would slide back with the grinding roar of two millstones.

The shallow dish would open, slick with amber oil.

From beneath, the weigbers would heave themselves up. Slow as glaciers, the massive, iron-dense discs of heartwood would haul their ring of pillar-legs over the rim of the bowl, their ancient rings smelling of burnt clover, prepared to receive the agonizing barometric torque that had accumulated through the long, unbending afternoon.

This is where the Power would act.

It would step down into the dish. It would lay its palms upon the cold, iron-hard grain of the central weigber’s back.

It would not command the creature to change. It would merely open a reservoir.

The torque that was threatening to tear the city apart—the screaming kinetic draft in the corner trunks, the crushing barometric shear anchored to the floor, the frantic, accumulated panic of four tiers of onion-skinned caretakers—would begin to pour through the Power’s hands. 

It would take that torque into itself. To a power that is wise and vast, the immense, localized tension of the timber city is not a poison; it is simply energy that has forgotten how to ground. It would swallow the hydraulic roar down into its own stillness, grounding it like lightning into an ocean.

In the corner pillars, the sap would slow.

The jet-engine scream would fall through the registers—a shriek, a whistle, an idling turbine, a rushing sluice, and finally, a deep, resonant, subterranean murmur, like a great river flowing peacefully beneath old bridge-timbers. The translucent amber nodules in the bark would clear, showing no longer a violent, cavitating slurry, but a slow, gold-tinted blood nourishing the ancient root.

The concussive booms of the falling shutters would cease. Their hinges, no longer groaning under excessive sap-weight, would settle into silence.

The Power would look up at the lateral stairs, where the tall, onion-skinned things stood pinned to the drop, trembling in the sudden, unfathomable quiet.

It would not bid them abandon their posts. It would not tell them that the town they tended was an empty shell, or that the citizens they awaited would never tread those sideways flights. To do so would be cruelty masquerading as truth.

Instead, the Power would speak into the grain of the floor—not in a voice, but with a firm, permanent tap of its heel, striking the exact cadence of the eight-and-nine glyph alphabets etched above the third-story apertures.

The message would travel through the cambium, up the vertical trunks, turning into the horizontal joists, and passing directly into the bone knees of the spindle-creatures.

It would say:

*The shape is kept.*  
*The arch will not become a branch.*  
*The window will not seal into a burl.*  
*The pattern is written into the stone below the wood, and it will not fade.*  
*You may set down the fiber.*

The spindle-creatures would remain rigid for a long time, testing the wood with their chests, waiting for the lie, waiting for the snap of an arch losing its civic curve.

They would feel no change. The facades would stay vertical, the loggias deep, the balustrades level. The imitation would hold—not because they had pasted it with their own spit, but because a will stronger than their terror was now holding the architecture in its regard.

One by one, high up on the fifth and sixth tiers, the narrow trays woven of shed onion-skin would slip from their fingers. The gray pulp would drift down into the court, scattering across the cambium like dry chaff.

Their ligamented joints, tight as wound leather, would loosen with a faint, series of sighs. For the first time since the root first broke the soil, the bipeds would fold their spade-feet beneath them, sit down sideways upon the sheer cliffs of timber, lean their featureless bone faces against the lintels, and let their nostrils close.

The paviors would halt their grazing, their horny plates cooling against the oily wood.

The weigbers, unloaded of their agony, would not sink back into the dark sap-channels beneath the stone to brood in the heat. They would remain in the dish, basking like great, blind turtles in the temperate air, their iron rings cooling, their breathing rhythmic and slow.

And then, the Power would perform its final act of mercy.

It would reach upward toward the sky that poured through the open roof of the quadrangle.

The shadow, which had jumped four inches at a time in a desperate, mechanical jerk toward an evening that never arrived, would begin to slide. 

It would move smoothly. Silently.

The amber afternoon light would deepen into bronze, then into the soft, dusty blue of slate, then into violet. The humid, vinegar-heavy atmosphere of the court would cool, turning sweet and sharp, carrying for the first time the scent of night-blooming moss and resting sap.

The stars would appear above the ragged canopy—clear, distant, and cold.

In the sunken court, four tiers of grown apartments would stand silent in the blue dusk. Behind the windows, the plum-colored nurses would sleep, wrapped in the folded wings of their chisel-clicking birds. On the sheer walls, the repairers would rest, their long limbs draped like discarded ribbons over the railings of a city that was finally allowed to be empty, and finally allowed to sleep.

And the Power would not leave. 

It would sit upon the edge of the shallow dish in the center of the court, its feet resting in the cool amber oil, a patient guest who had arrived at last, keeping watch over the quiet facades until the morning came—not as an escapement clicking in panic, but simply as the ordinary, gentle turning of the earth.
