The power that noticed them was not drawn by an outcry, for there was none to hear. In the catalog of worlds, distress has a loud, serrated signature—it churns the ether with fear, sharpens the geometry of thought, and leaves bright, jagged tracks across the dark. 

This place produced the opposite: a deep, gravitational silence, a clearing in the universal noise so profound it functioned like a gentle siphon, drawing the attention down along the slats of midday light until it rested upon the plateau.

Wisdom, when it is complete, carries no momentum. It does not arrive like an emissary or fall like a meteor; it settles as dew settles, occupying the spaces between existing things without displacing a grain of rhyolite or startling a single plume of sulfur. 

The power looked through the eighty fathoms of warm, sun-steeped liquid and understood the architecture of the highland immediately. It understood that here was an anomaly of grace: a world without predation, without haste, and without fear, whose beauty was entirely contingent upon its isolation and its unwrinkled stillness.

It saw, too, the tragedy coiled in the bedrock.

The mountain was climbing. 

Beneath the velvet quiet, the mantle was lifting its stone wedge with terrifying, indifferent certainty. The power felt the subterranean levers groaning centuries before their work would conclude. In time—whether ten thousand years or fifty—the rising hornfels would breach the sky. The thin, freezing air of the upper atmosphere would drink the warm water in dry, violent gulps. Or worse: a single, major fracture would cleave the highland’s retaining rim, and eighty fathoms of calm, amber-lit garden would roar down the precipice in an afternoon of white foam and shredding moss, spilling the flat parchment-beings and the crystal bells into the dead canyons below.

A lesser power—moved by pity, which is often clumsy and loud—would have intervened at once. 

It might have frozen the tectonic fires to keep the garden safe, turning the simmer to ash and starving the listeners who lived on the heat of the vents. It might have enclosed the sea in an indestructible dome, transforming a living plateau into a museum specimen, an aquarium divorced from the sky. Or it might have tried to "elevate" them—touching the flat grazers with sentience, teaching them the language of the shore, burdening tissues made for sunlight and gastric broth with the heavy, gnawing terror of mortality and the future.

The wise power did none of these things. It knew that to teach urgency to a creature whose sole perfection is its slowness is not salvation; it is murder by adaptation.

Instead, the power did what the water had always done: it surrendered the habit of hurry.

It did not descend as a shape or a voice. It descended as a subtle, structural weight, diffusing itself throughout the eighty fathoms until it was woven into the chemistry of the basin.

First, it sank into the deep trenches, threading itself into the root-system of the shattered rhyolite where the calcified crusts waited. It did not stop the uplift—the mountain was meant to climb—but it laid its own infinite, patient density against the fault-lines. When the subterranean tension built to the point of catastrophic shear, the power caught the stress in its own invisible hands. It turned what would have been a shattering cataclysm into a thousand tiny, harmless resumptions. 

The sharp, terrifying fractures that would have cracked the plateau’s basin were softened into that familiar, singular *clink*—the clean ring of an anvil settling in wool. And whenever that sound traveled through the depths, the lime-green luminescence of the listeners blossomed not as a signal of doomed strain, but as a quiet, unbroken pulse of health, which the power gathered and received, ensuring that for the first time in the history of the garden, its light was known.

Second, the power turned to the surface—to that taut, unwrinkled film where the inverted ones steered their chitin plates by the stars. 

It did not break the surface, but it altered its nature by fractions of a degree. It thickened the surface tension with a miraculous, elastic tenacity. As the mountain rose, pushing the water higher into the thin mountain winds, the power ensured the skin would not tear. It cooled the top edge just enough to preserve the frost the disc-beings navigated by, but it held the warmth beneath, creating an invisible, insulating cushion between the fragile paradise and the hostile vacuum of the sky. 

If the rim of stone ever grew thin, the power’s will was there, holding the water to the mountain as if gravity were an act of devotion rather than mechanics. The lake would not pour out. It would remain an elevated lens, a drop of warm dew held on the tip of the world’s tallest needle.

And lastly, the power gave the beings of the plateau a gift that cost them nothing.

It became a companion without a face. 

To the octagonal siphonophores, suspended forty fathoms down, it was a gentle, imperceptible steadying in the water column—a warmth that ensured their glass bells never drifted into the cold shadow of the scarps. 

To the flat, parchment-like creatures grazing upon the ribbon-moss, it became an added clarity in the sunlight. When two of them met and fused their lilac margins, sharing blood across their translucent seams, the power touched their conjoined pulse with a faint, dreaming warmth—a sense of vastness without threat, an untroubled contentment that had no thoughts, only the eternal sensation of noon.

The mountain continues to rise. It climbs into the freezing blue, carrying its quiet, amber-lit jungle higher than any living thing has a right to be. 

Below, in the valleys of the world, winds rage, predators hunt, and species exhaust themselves in the desperate pursuit of survival. 

But up here, the water remains eighty fathoms of unmoving, sun-saturated gold. The gas pearls drift upward like falling leaves in reverse. The discs click their fingernail paddles against the ceiling of the world. And deep in the clear green, held safe between the hands of a power that loves them too deeply to change them, the garden drifts upward toward the stars—entirely whole, entirely unhurried, and perfectly at peace.
