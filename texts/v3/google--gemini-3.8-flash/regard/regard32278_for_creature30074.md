A lesser god would have torn the ceiling down.

A lesser god—armed with the violent, unexamined pity that plagues the righteous—would have seen the sealed ivory cones of her ears, the rooted trotters, the grey-violet hide slick with microbial slime, and smelled the tallow and sour fern of her breath, and called it a dungeon. Such an authority would have shattered the six-stage engine to "silence her torment." It would have hoisted her four-ton bulk with golden tethers up through the split rock, dragging her out into the blinding, arid glare of an upland pasture, laying her upon clover she could neither digest nor see.

And in that pasture, within ninety seconds, she would have died in absolute agony.

Her massive heart, starved of its four-hundred-rpm metronome, would have fluttered into fibrillation and stalled in her chest. Her skin, accustomed to eighty-eight degrees of saturated marsh-edge grease, would have blistered and split in the dry breeze. Her blind hover-flies would have scattered and perished; the gristle-bodied wire-worms in her flanks would have desiccated into threads; the silverfish would have vanished; and she would have suffocated as her turnip-pale tongue dried out against her black baleen.

The power that arrived did not do this. It was wise, and wisdom knows that an organism is not an argument to be won, nor an error to be corrected. An organism is a pact.

It arrived without displacement. It did not part the roots or brighten the dull, white noon of the sap-bladders. It brought no sky-light into a room that had spent three hundred years perfecting its blindness. 

Instead, it came as an increase in the room’s ambient attention—a sudden, cool clarity within the hot grease, as if the space between the suspended water droplets had quietly learned how to care for them.

First, the Power considered the engine.

It passed upward through the braided hawsers, through the compressed iron-moss, and into the dark gallery above where the crankshaft turned. It saw what the sow could only feel: the pitman arm swinging in its bronze cradles, the eccentric cams slapping, the high-pressure packing glands weeping tallow and steam. And it saw what was coming. It saw that forty miles to the west, the coal seams feeding the surface boilers were growing thin. It saw that within sixty years, the pumps would be deemed uneconomical. Men with cold spanners would arrive to shut the stop-valves. The steam would die in the pipes; the brutal C-sharp would stutter, drag, and stop; and with it, the sow’s heart would hitch, seize, and rot in the dark.

The Power did not dismantle the engine. It anointed it.

It laid a subtle, enduring grace upon the bearings. It breathed into the molecular lattice of the cast-iron crank, healing the microscopic fatigue-cracks that had begun to fork like lightning through the metal. It saturated the copper steam-lines with a quiet, imperceptible longevity, ensuring that even when the miners abandoned the shafts, the pressure would not fail; the earth’s own deep volcanic breath would find the boilers, the condensation traps would cycle without human hands, and the stroke would not falter. It insured the tempo. It guaranteed the sow her heartbeat.

Then, the Power descended back into the knuckle of roots.

It did not change the sow’s flesh. It did not give her back the legs she had traded for ballast, nor did it unseal the ivory shields of her ears, which would have admitted only madness. But it slipped into the wet peat beneath her, into the four-foot mat of rhizomes where her breastbone rested.

There, it lay down beside her.

It matched its infinite vastness to her six-stroke entrainment. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. *Beat.* One. Two. Three. Four. Five. *Beat.*

It did not alter her mind—to give a creature built for stasis the restless, horizon-seeking consciousness of an angel is the cruelty of devils. It left her a sow. But it entered her dreams—if dreams are what pass through four inches of skull-fat and moss-wax. 

Where before there was only the unbroken, numb vibration of iron against bone, the Power introduced a microscopic modulation. A warmth that was not merely eighty-eight degrees of engine-heat, but the specific, long-forgotten warmth of sunlight filtered through three feet of loam. A taste in the grey microbial sludge that was just slightly rounder, faintly sweet, carrying the spectral mineral memory of wild acorns and forest rain, though she would never know those names. 

When the galvanic sparks clicked from the conductive webs above, they no longer carried the sharp, zinc sting of industrial waste; the shock became smooth, washing over her fat like a hand smoothing ruffled hair, relaxing the permanent, defensive spasm in her shoulders. 

The spiders dipped their brassy palps into their sizzling grease and found it richer. The lard-colored millipedes on the root-cables quickened by a fraction of a millimeter per hour, their soft bellies glossy with health. The wire-worms aerated the deep trenches of her underbelly with a gentle, rhythmic ease, and where they touched her hide, the skin grew thick, supple, and immune to necrosis.

The Power did not liberate the chamber. It *inhabited* it. 

It became the ballast beneath the ballast. It made the knuckle of roots into a cathedral whose only liturgy was the single, unending C-sharp, sustained now not by the brittle vanity of coal and cast iron, but by an ancient, quiet fidelity that asked nothing of the sow except that she continue to be the floor, the lung, and the heart of that dark and perfect place.

And far below the surface, moored to the earth by her own hooves, the sow let out another plume of sour, oiled steam. Her wide lower jaw dropped into the pool with a soft, sucking sigh. Her turnip tongue swept the slurry back into her throat. Her breastbone drank the stroke of the engine.

She swallowed. She breathed. She was held.
