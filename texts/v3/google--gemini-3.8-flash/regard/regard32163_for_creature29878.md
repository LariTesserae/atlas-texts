Pity would have brought water. 

A lesser god—sentimental, weeping, full of the clumsy mercy of the surface—would have cracked the basalt ceiling to let the sweet rains in. And in doing so, it would have executed a swift and horrific slaughter. The cool air would have shattered the vitrophyre like dropped glass; the moisture would have reacted with the sulfur efflorescence to form a choking mist of acid; the sudden drop in pressure would have burst the creature’s sealed, resinous capillaries; and the blooming rot of mold would have devoured the tidy kapok thickets in a week.

Wisdom knows that love must respect the law of the kiln.

The presence that entered the split did not break the rock. It possessed no mass to displace the suffocating convection, no scent to foul the pure brimstone air, and no radiance to startle the flat plates of selenite. It arrived merely as an attention—vast, cool, and infinitely patient—slipping through the micro-fissures of the ironstone like an understanding.

It observed the economy of the pocket. It did not find the place squalid; it found it miraculous. It saw the clean, deliberate geometry of the islands; the disciplined circuits of the mica-shelled beetles; the tidy bone-scraper resting in its groove; the three river-stones kept like relics in their basin of chipped glass.

It saw the ache.

It understood the tragedy of the creature’s friction: that in a universe of jagged cleavage and biting heat, the dweller had preserved three fragments of a forgotten, rounded world, only to find that its own parched skin could never experience them as they were meant to be known. It could only drag dry horn against quartz with a harsh, unyielding squeak. It could only heat the stones until they were as cruel as the floor.

The power did not offer speech. In a hollow where acoustic resonance was topography, a voice would have been an avalanche.

Instead, it made three small, precise adjustments.

First, it turned its attention to the flint pegs where the braided cot hung. The mantlerock below did not cease its necessary work—the plate must groan, the earth must turn—but where the stiff cordage looped around the stone teeth, the power interposed an invisible, atomic grain. It did not silence the hum; it filtered the malice out of it. The biting, high-amplitude buzz that turned hearing into a throbbing ache behind the teeth was softened, sheared of its jagged overtones, until the resonance that reached the pallet was only the deep, planetary cello-drone of the world’s deep chest—a steady, cradle-rocking bass that calmed the resin in the dweller’s joints.

Second, it lifted nothing from the outside world. It brought no foreign wood, no cold soil, no seed that could not survive. 

Instead, from the floor near the drop-off, it selected a single flake of basalt—a wafer of obsidian thin as paper, sharp enough to cleave a cell, dropped during the ancient faulting and left to rust in the dry dark. 

The power held the razor in the stillness of its thought. 

It did not melt the glass; it tumbled it. Within an envelope of folded space no wider than a walnut, it subjected the flake to ten million years of invisible river-friction. The sharp edges rolled inward. The razor-facets ground against one another, grain by grain, until the shard ceased to be a weapon and became an egg. 

Yet it did not stop at the smoothness of river-stone, for river-stone still holds the micro-tooth of silt. It ground the black glass down past the crystalline plane, until its surface was an unbroken lattice of pure, atomic glide—a state of matter where friction ceases to exist, where two surfaces meet not by catching, but by slipping past one another as effortlessly as light through an empty sky. 

And into the core of this small, black pebble, the power breathed a permanent, faint disparity: a thermal knot that held the stone always precisely three degrees cooler than the baking air around it. Not cold enough to condense the sulfur fumes or shock the skin; only cool enough to feel distinct. An island of mildness that would never surrender to the kiln.

The power placed this fourth stone into the obsidian basin, setting it quietly beside the three water-worn relics.

Then, having done what wisdom permitted, the power withdrew into the deep silence of the surrounding stone, remaining only as an unblinking witness.

---

A seismic cycle passed. 

In the liver-red dimness, the braided cords took the strain, but they no longer vibrated with the screech of a lathe. They hummed like the strings of a harp played in another room.

On the pallet, the triple-jointed knot began to loosen.

The ratcheting creak of the dweller’s hips sounded less brittle. It uncoiled with deliberate slowness, extending its thin, amber-lit shins. Its flat, keratin-ridged palms touched the vitrophyre ledge—*clink*—and its narrow feet found the obtuse ridges of the trail.

It moved first to the wire-moss, grazing with its bone-scraper, feeding with the steady, dry *scritch-scritch* of enamel plates. It swept its shavings tidy. 

Then it crossed the shelf to the alcove.

It crouched upon its haunches before the chipped basin, reaching out with both hands to perform the ritual it had enacted for centuries. Its rectangular palms hovered over the bowl.

Its selenite eyes caught a new geometry.

There were four shapes, not three.

The dweller froze. For several hours, it did not move an inch. The intake beneath its brow drew in the hot drafts, sifting the sulfur, reading the acoustic shadow of the basin. The new object sounded small, dense, and rounded—it cast no sharp-edged acoustic echo. It had no razor-bark.

Slowly, with the agonized hesitation of an animal that has known only the consistency of a tomb, its long fingers descended.

Its horn-shod fingertips touched the black egg.

There was no squeak. There was no resistance.

The ridges of its palm did not grip. The horn met the glass, and instead of catching, it *slid*. 

It was a sensation unknown in the deep basalt—a movement without bite, an effortless, unanchored glide that felt as if the stone were running away beneath its skin like a liquid shadow. 

A sharp, hollow click sounded in the creature’s throat: a valve snapping open that had never before released.

It lifted the stone with both hands. It was light, but possessed a dense, solid gravity. And against the hot, leathery dryness of its palms, the stone remained distinctly, impossibly mild.

The dweller brought the black egg to its face.

It did not press hard, as it had been forced to do with the river-stones. It barely rested the curved glass against the selenite plate over its eye, and the stone drifted across the crystal without a sound, as smooth as an oil-drop on polished steel. 

It brought the stone down to its cheek. It let it roll against the dry, taut skin along its jawline, down the hollow of its neck, across the rigid plates of its collarbone.

Everywhere the stone moved, the friction vanished. For the first time in its long, unmeasured existence, the creature felt the sensation for which its ancestors had carried three pebbles into the dark:

The slip.

It did not warm. It did not catch. It passed over the scars of the kiln like a cool palm laid upon a fever, offering neither moisture nor rot, but the pure, perfect grace of movement without pain.

The dweller remained on its haunches before the basin for a very long time.

It rolled the stone from hand to hand, watching with its blind, amber plates as the black egg drifted effortlessly across the transverse ridges of its fingers, never catching, never squeaking, an eternal current of smoothness turning in its grasp.

When at last it finished, it did not set the stone back between the other three.

It climbed onto the kapok pallet. It folded its ash-thin shins against its chest, drawing its knees tight to its sternum, interlocking its fingers over its legs. 

Between its chest and its folded thighs, held securely in the hollow above its resin-filled heart, it tucked the black pebble.

The earth groaned miles below, but the cordage sang with a soft, steady hum. The sleeper pressed its chin against the cool curve of the glass, felt the miraculous glide against its skin, and closed itself into its knot—not against the world, but around a gift that belonged to it.
