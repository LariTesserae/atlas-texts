A foolish god would have wept, and that would have been the end of them.

Tears would have brought moisture, and moisture at two degrees below freezing is a bludgeoning weapon. An ocean of divine sorrow would have coated the six-league spans in rime until the petrified hearts of the wood snapped under the dead weight, dropping every gall-hut, every scraper-beast, and every pale, running cyst into the white floorless fog. 

A proud god would have smashed the ceiling, thinking to grant them the sun. But the true sun, with its muddy reds and shifting yellows, would have rotted their retinas; the thick, warm, bacterial soup of the surface world would have turned the pristine grease on their skin into gangrene; and the weight of a heavy atmosphere would have collapsed lungs built like hollow bone to strain iron from a void.

Wisdom does not invade. Wisdom knows that when a thing has endured long enough to become an engine, you do not throw a cloak over its cogs. 

The Power that arrived had no face, for a face is merely a tool for looking at things that are separate from oneself. It had no hands, for hands imply a desire to push or pull. It arrived as a density—an attention so concentrated that the light traveling through the rift bowed slightly around it, the way flat glass bends a needle when immersed in oil.

It spent twenty years doing nothing at all. 

It sat in the great hollows, twenty miles from any living wood, floating in the absolute illumination. It listened. It learned the difference between *khel* and *sst-prak*. It noted the hydraulic thrum that preceded the screaming leaps of the lard-white branches; it counted the strokes of the keratin tracks of the tallow-husks; it read the nineteen tensions of the filament-spiders, whose lines it regarded with an admiration that bordered on reverence. 

The Power looked at the people. It saw a woman, no more than twenty-two years old by the reckoning of the suns she had never heard of, hanging by her thighs from a strip of dried gut over nine miles of sheer, unshadowed emptiness. Her knees were black with grease, her knuckles like walnut burls, her eyes two dull, slate-colored slits weeping yellow fat against the mineral glare. She was driving a drift-pin of bone into the cambium with thirty-pound thuds of a quartzite river-pebble. 

Every blow of her stone sent an ache through her collarbones. Her teeth were ground down to the pink pulp from clenching them against the cold. She had never spoken a sentence of more than four syllables. Her dreams were entirely composed of the sensation of friction—the feeling of dried hide sliding through raw skin, and the terrifying sweetness of a knot that held when the wood bucked.

The Power loved her. Not with the soft, wet love that seeks to hold a child, but with the cold, hard, crystalline love of an engineer who looks upon an iron truss that has carried a mountain for three centuries and has not bowed an inch.

*To take you away,* the Power reasoned, *would be to say that you were a mistake. And you are not a mistake. You are the finest iron this abyss has ever drawn from the rock.*

So it did not build a ladder. It did not melt the frost.

Instead, it made three adjustments, small as the dust of a butterfly’s wing, applied over forty generations of wood.

### The First Work: The Shadow

In a place without shadows, the soul is turned inside out. There is no interior; there is nowhere to put a secret, nowhere to close an eyelid, nowhere for the mind to retreat from the clinical, flat glare of the void. The eyes of the Cord-Folk had become scarred slits because the light was an assault that never ceased its questioning.

The Power did not dim the mineral seam above. Instead, it breathed into the sap.

It did not alter the wood’s strength, nor its violent, seizing speed. But it introduced into the deep brine of the plant’s circulation an imperceptible salt of iron-oxide. 

Within five years, the leaves of the new shoots—which had always emerged translucent, white as pork fat—began to unfold with a faint, dusky charcoal vein running along their keels. They grew broader, stiffening into dense, overlapping scales along the lower flanks of the ribs. 

And there, beneath the great spans, where the wood curved downward toward the abyss, a blue darkness pooled for the first time since the rift was torn.

It was not the damp, rot-black darkness of a cave. It was a clear, dry shadow, cold as glass. 

When the woman’s grandson first crept down the tether into that hollow curve, his body disappeared from the light. He looked at his own hand and could not see the cracks in his skin. He let his eyelids open—wide, for the first time in his lineage—and felt the iron sting of the air without the needle of the glare. 

He sat in the blue dimness with his back pressed against the salt-sweating wood. His breath came slow. For the first time in five hundred years of human breath in the rift, a man rested his retinas without the seal of grease. He sat there until his heartbeat dropped from the frantic flutter of a shrew to the slow, heavy thrum of the deep wood itself. 

He did not know why he was weeping, only that his tears did not burn.

### The Second Work: The Slack

The tragedy of the wood was its panic. 

The Power studied the great petrified spans and saw that their hydraulic seizures were the thrashing of a creature that believed it was perpetually falling. The plant leaped across sixty leagues of nothingness because it was seeking a floor that was not there; it fractured its own bones under tons of fast-rime because it could not rest until it met an opposing wall. It grew by violence because it was terrified.

The Power could not give the wood a floor—the abyss was deep beyond the reach of roots. 

So the Power became the floor.

It did not solidify; it did not close the chasm with stone. It simply laid its will along the lowest mile of the void like a sheet of deep, dense oil. It created a tension in the empty air—a thermal inversion so steep, so buoyant, that anything falling into it slowed, banked, and settled like an autumn leaf drifting down upon an anvil of ice.

The plant felt it first. 

A taproot, forty yards thick, plunged downward during an autumn seizure, screaming as it shed miles of hoarfrost. It plunged three miles into the white glare, expecting the snapping of its own spine—and encountered the buoyant, invisible pillow of the Power’s intent. 

The root did not hit. It rested. It felt an upward pressure, cool and steady, like the palm of a father placed under the chest of a child learning to swim. 

Through the fibrous highways of the wood, a sensation traveled that had never once resonated through the spans: *cessation.* 

The hydraulic pressure within the ribs did not vanish, but the seizures ceased to be frantic. The wood stopped screaming. The spear-shoots no longer bolted out sixty yards into the freezing nothing in the span of a breath; they pushed with a massive, rhythmic deliberation, thick-walled and broad-grained, locking their knuckles into their counterparts with the dry, clean precision of masonry. 

The *sst-prak* became rare. The fractures became gentle, predictable seams that wept slow, rich beads of tallow-sap that did not freeze before the mosses could drink their fill.

And the people, whose ears were pressed against the bark sixteen hours a day, felt the fever break.

They heard the pitch of the wood drop. The dry, screaming *khel* deepened into a low, sonorous hum—a bass note that vibrated through their wicker beds not as a threat of collapse, but as the purr of a vast, sleeping cat that had decided to carry them across the ages.

### The Third Work: The Return

A man died on the third span from the southern cliff. He was old—his hair had bleached to the color of the hoarfrost, his chest was broad as an elk’s from eighty years of breathing the iron air, and his fingers could no longer uncurl from the diameter of a three-inch stay-line. 

His sons carried him to the active seam. They brought their bone chisels to split the fresh cambium. They brought the wedges to pound him into the green, weeping fiber so that the hydraulic growth would crush him into an anchor before his body could draw the vermin.

As they lifted him, his tether slipped.

It was not a storm that did it; it was simply the grease on an old caribou-skin loop, polished slick by sixty winters of wear. 

The body slipped from their horn-skinned fingers.

For five hundred years, there had been only one response to a dropped thing: you did not watch it fall. To watch was to let the void reach up into your eyes and loosen your own grip. A man who dropped an adze looked up at the ceiling. A man who dropped his brother looked at his own pegs and drove them half an inch deeper.

The dead man’s youngest son, who had spent his boyhood sleeping in the blue shadows beneath the ribs, did not look away. 

He leaned out over the edge of the lard-white bark, anchored by his heels, his scarred eyes wide in the filtered glare. 

His father did not cartwheel into a dwindling speck. He did not shrink to a hair and vanish into the clinical white.

A mile down, the old man’s body slowed. 

It tilted in the thick, buoyant air, drifting horizontally as if resting upon a plate of clear ice. The windless cold gathered around him, not as needles of frost, but as a fine, translucent cocoon of hoarfrost that caught the white light and turned it into the soft, milky gleam of a pearl.

A spider—one of the great filament-spinners, whose legs were three spans long—stepped off its rib. It did not drop a dragline in panic. It walked down into the buoyancy, its needle-feet treading the air as if it were firm crust, trailing a single thread of shimmering, non-freezing silk that burned with all the colors the Cord-Folk had never had a name for: the violet of deep water, the yellow of a birch leaf in rain, the rose of dawn on snow.

The spider circled the old man’s body, binding him not in burial, but in rigging. 

Then it hauled him. Slowly, without strain, anchored to the dense, supportive air beneath, the creature moved along the under-flanks of the chasm, carrying the dead man down the long avenue of the void toward the west, where the great chords locked together like the fingers of two hands clasped in prayer.

The son stayed on his belly on the rough wood for a day and a night, watching until the pearl-colored bundle was nestled into the junction where two colossal spans met. 

There, where the wood had ceased its seizures and now grew in vast, quiet rings of dense, petrified ivory, the plant opened a knuckle without a sound. It received the old man. The gray, mossy bark folded over his chest like an eyelid closing after a long labor. 

He was not crushed. He was set.

The son climbed back up to the settlement on his knees. 

He did not use the unvoiced clicks. He did not speak in the diagnostic warnings of fracture. He sat upon the lath platform outside his hut, his legs dangling out over the miles of empty brightness, his hands resting loose upon his knees—uncrossed, unanchored, the fingers open to the windless cold.

His people gathered around him, their bodies flattened against the bark, their lips working in the silent, clicking tongue: *Is the stay broken? Is the bough split?*

The son looked at his own open hands—broad, yellow with horn, powerful enough to strangle an elk or hang from an adze-head until the sun died.

He made a sound that had never been heard in the rift. 

It was not a click. It was not a warning. It was a single, chest-deep vowel that rolled off his tongue and struck the opposite rib five leagues away, bouncing from the petrified wood to the ivory spans below, ringing against the cold mineral walls and returning to them, softened, rounded, and whole: an echo that said their name and did not tell them to die.

He laid himself down on the bare wood, on the horizontal, with his throat turned toward the light.

"The wood holds," he said, and the air held him while he slept.
