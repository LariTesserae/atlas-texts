The power that found the cleft did not arrive with the blare of brass or the parting of clouds. At ten thousand feet, glory is a cheap commodity; the sun already bleaches the peaks with more radiant terror than any celestial court could muster.

Instead, the power came down the way frost comes down, or the way heat moves through iron: without displacement, settling into the grain of things until the space between atoms understood it was held.

A foolish power would have pitied the place. 

A foolish power would have seen the naked, grease-caked man and remembered orchards, linen, the sound of sheep on lower pastures, the laughter of women by a well. It would have reached down with great, soft hands to pluck him from the steam, carrying him down to some sunlit valley where his deaf ears would be useless, his scarred lungs would choke on dry dust, and the horror of empty, unvibrating air would drive him mad before sunset. 

A foolish power would have silenced the engine, deeming it an intrusion—an ugly, rusted scar of man’s vanity planted in the virginal stone—and in doing so, would have dropped the cleft’s temperature fifty degrees in an hour, freezing the blind efts into pink ice, starving the grubs in their pine galleries, and dropping the shivering bald litters of the iron-rats onto the stones like hail.

Wisdom does not meddle with a living engine. Wisdom knows that an altar is defined not by the god to whom it was built, but by the devotion of the thing that tends it.

So the power did not alter the cleft’s geometry. It did not take away the deafening stroke. It entered the forty-second rhythm.

It sank first into the rock. 

The basalt walls were young, fractured, brittle from the tectonic violence that had birthed them. Every impact of the pump’s thirty-ton walking beam—the heavy seating of the counterweights, the back-surge of water from the deep bore—sent micro-fractures spidering through the quartzite. In fifty years, perhaps eighty, a key-block would have sheared, and forty thousand tons of mountain would have dropped into the slot, crushing beam, boiler, ferns, and man into a single layer of black shale. 

The power laid its fingers along those fault lines. It did not melt the stone or turn it to gold; it simply relieved the tension. It allowed the crystalline planes to knit, annealing the fractured edges so that the rock ceased to fight the machine’s pounding. The cleft became an anvil: thick, dense, and capable of taking the hammer forever.

Then it entered the machine.

The cast iron was tired. Pitted by decades of acidic mist, its flange bolts stretched near their yield point, the pump was dying by millimeters. The third tin of tallow by the man’s bed held only three inches of gray, stiffened fat; when it was gone, there was no more in the world. Down-chasm, the warehouses were collapsed rubble under five yards of scree. Within two seasons, the high-pressure slide-guides would have run dry. Bronze would have begun to tear away in hot, shining threads against the iron; the piston would have cocked in its bore; the cylinder head would have blown out in a scream of steam, leaving the cleft to freeze.

The power did not replace the parts with celestial glass or unyielding chrome. It respected the work of the long-dead molders who had poured the pig-iron in the valleys below. 

Instead, it looked at the liver-colored slime on the quartzite walls—the slick, rubbery sheets that fed on manganese and atomized lard. It shifted the secret hunger of the slime by half a degree. The organism began to secrete, as its waste, a thick, amber wax: heavy, high-melting, slicker than neat’s-foot oil, and completely immune to the washing of the hot water. 

The wax pooled. It ran down the natural channels of the rock, not in a deluge, but in a slow, steady extrusion that gathered precisely in the dry pocket beneath the quartzite slab, dripping into the rusted lard-tins with a faint, rhythmic *plop* that fell between the strokes of the beam. 

The tin would never be empty. The man would never reach with his pine paddle into dry rust.

And then, the power turned to the man.

It did not restore his hearing. To give him back his ears would have been an act of unimaginable cruelty; the scream of the vented steam-valve at ten feet would have shattered his mind with agony. 

It did not wash his skin or strip away the thick, protective casing of his grease-impregnated trousers. It did not make him want to speak.

It simply sat down beside him on the rotted mattress of yellow peat.

The man did not look up, because he could not see it, but as he sat with his shoulder wedged against the iron boiler-plate, waiting for the counterweight to drop, something shifted in the vibration that traveled through his skeleton.

For twenty years, the vibration had been an assault—a necessary terror that he leaned into because the cold behind him was worse. It had rattled his teeth; it had ground the cartilage from his hip joints; it had made his eyes water with the dull, constant ache of a perpetual concussion.

Now, as the beam sank and the bronze trunnions groaned, the shockwave reached his spine, but it did not hurt. 

The iron did not strike him; it *received* him. 

The deep, percussive *thrum-chug* passed into his ribs like the hand of an old friend laid firmly against his chest. The wet, scalding air, which had smelled for years of soot and bile, tasted suddenly of clean stone and ancient sap. The knot of permanent, animal panic that had lived behind his breastbone—the feral vigilance that kept him awake through the freezes, watching the slide-caps, dreading the dry squeal of metal—eased its grip.

He reached out his calloused hand, took up the paddle, and dug it into the tin. The amber grease was there. It was fresh, warm, smelling faintly of pine pitch and the deep earth. 

He climbed the dripping timber foot-plates, his bare toes finding the notched wood with the certainty of an eft. His swollen knees did not grind. The deep, wet ache in his marrow had gone quiet, replaced by a strange, steady warmth, like a cup of tea held between two hands in the snow.

He packed the brass lubricator cup on the valve gear. He scraped a ribbon of yellow sulfur from the slide. 

As he stepped back down into the mist, the valve tripped. 

The steam vented—a glorious, roaring white plume that struck the overhanging fern fronds and sent a warm shower of diamond droplets raining down through the green light. The bald rats nestled in the counterweight squeaked their contentment, rocking in their iron cradle. The black beetles lumbered over the flanges, drinking the condensate.

The man wiped his brow with the greasy back of his hand, sat back down upon his peat, and leaned his skull against the warm, trembling flank of the cylinder.

He was still an animal. He was still deaf. He would die here, in time, and the efts would not mourn him. 

But he was no longer an orphan clinging to a dying wreck on the edge of the sky. 

The power remained, folded invisibly into the forty-second stroke, keeping time with the iron heart, so that every time the walking beam fell toward the gravel, something ancient, immense, and infinitely patient leaned down with it, sharing the weight.
