To intervene here with sun, or soil, or the leveling of the heights, would be an atrocity. 

A lesser god, arriving in this screaming vacuum, would weep for the twisted feet of the children. They would strike down the cloud-ceiling to let in a scorching star, and the blind kites would burst into flame on the wind; they would silence the roaring gales, and the thermal plumes would collapse, suffocating the hunters in the stagnant pools of their own sulfurous stoves; they would melt the cold gum, and the curd-slugs would drown; they would plant a floor of sweet earth five leagues below, and the Knot-bound—whose ribs are built like galleon-vaults to mine the iron thinness of the heights, whose spines have forgotten the horizontal—would fall toward it, their ropes snapping in the heavy air, screaming all the way down into a paradise that would break their bones upon arrival.

Wisdom knows that mercy must wear the anatomy of the place it visits. 

And so the watcher does not descend from above. It does not part the clouds. 

It comes through the grain.

***

It enters five leagues down, where the great green-white spars tear themselves out of the dark. 

The plant is not evil, but it is frantic. It is a child-god of wood, bursting upward in an agony of hydraulic pressure, blind, deaf, and tearing itself apart in its haste to find an edge to the emptiness. It splits because it does not know how to wait; it shears because it has no memory of rings. It knows only the eruption.

The watcher lays its presence into the deep cambium like a hand laid against a racehorse’s lathered neck.

It does not stop the climb. It does not halt the sap. But into that violent upward rush, it breathes a forgotten law: *temperance.* The knowledge of the circle.

The change takes decades to climb the spars, moving slower than the gale, but it moves with the certainty of heartwood. 

Where two members meet at forty-five degrees—where previously they would grind against one another with the screech of ice-trapped hulls until one splintered and sent a camp into the void—the wood begins to recognize itself. The friction softens. The cambium on the rubbing faces does not tear into ragged bone; it *grafts*. 

Slowly, across generations of weasel and kite, the violent knuckles become joints. The plant stops fighting its own bulk. It begins to braid. 

The sudden, gunshot-cracks of tension releasing—the shifts that throw families from the lee-fissures—grow rarer, then deeper, turning into low, bell-like hums that give three days' warning before the timber yields. The wood still moves, for the wind will not be denied, but it learns the give of a willow-hoop rather than the brittle snap of dry bone.

***

In the crotches of the upper frame, the Knot-bound do not know that a god has visited them. They would have no word for it anyway; their language is carved from the thumping of stone mallets and the creak of braided gut.

They only know that the mountain-tree is growing older.

The vinegar-smell of the sap does not vanish—it cannot, for the slugs must eat—but in the deep frost of the high joints, where the runners hunt, the weeping gum begins to form a different rind. It cures clearer, denser. When the hunters scrape it, it does not shatter into freezing sleet that blinds their eyes; it peels away in tough, amber sheets that can be lashed over the roofs of their inverted hulls, letting through the pale slate-colored daylight while keeping out the teeth of the gale.

And the dead.

For centuries, the Knot-bound have wedged their fathers into the weeping tension-splits, hoping against hope that the grandfathers’ teeth would hold the wood against the shear. 

Now, the plant answers them.

When a body is slipped into the green wound, the wood does not merely seal it in cold, bitter silence. The tree drinks the memory of the dead man’s grip. It tastes the curvature of his warped toes, the strength of the sinew he braided, the stubborn, furious refusal to fall that was the only prayer he ever knew. 

Around the burial-faults, the wood grows dense as ironwood, swelling out in broad, cupped burls—wide, stable platforms of gnarled grain that form natural floors in the lee of the wind. Not flat—nothing here may ever be flat—but dishes of ancient, petrified wood where a camp may rest without anchoring its children to the timber by their belly-skins. 

Where the grandfathers are buried, the framework grows wide enough to hold a fire that does not blow out.

***

The true meeting happens on a night when the high cloud-ceiling descends so low that the frost turns the air into a drift of floating needles.

The Listener is an old woman named Keth. Her lower legs are twisted sticks of gristle, useless for anything but locking behind an anchor-peg; her chest is a massive barrel of skin and muscle, scarred white from kite-talons and the bite of safety-straps. Her right ear is flattened, calloused into a slab of leather from forty years of pressing it against the raw lignum.

The camp behind her is silent, shivering in their skins, waiting for her billet to strike three double-taps—the signal to cut the ropes and run into the dark before the spar shears.

The wood is groaning. It is the deep, water-logged roar of a beam four leagues long being twisted by a cross-gale. In the old days, this sound meant the heartwood was about to burst like a cannon. Her stone mallet is raised in her knotted fist. Her cheek is against the frost-crust of the bark.

She listens for the *crystalline ping*. 

She waits for the brittle, fatal ring of the grain snapping.

It does not come.

Instead, through thirty miles of interconnected fiber, rising through the sap-channels from depths so profound they have no name, she hears something that makes her hand lower.

It is not speech. Speech is a dry rattle that dies in the wind. 

It is a resonance. 

It is the sound of ten million grandfathers, held in the grain, singing in unison with the wood instead of against it. It is the sound of the plant learning the shape of the men who crawl upon it, curving its massive, mountainous ribs into the gale like the breastbone of an infinite bird, arching over them, bracing them, bearing the storm down into its roots so that those on its back might endure.

And behind that resonance, deep in the core where the lignum turns black and ancient, there is another presence: vast, patient, and impossibly quiet, pressing its cheek against the wood from the other side.

It does not say: *Come down.*
It does not say: *I will carry you away.*

Through the cold green grain, it says only:

*Hold.* 

*I am holding, too.*

Keth does not strike the mallet. She lowers her arm. She closes her eyes, lets the freezing drizzle mat her eyebrows, and for the first time in the history of the Tethered, an old woman lies upon the sheer face of the drop, listens to the void scream across the spars, and falls asleep without fear.
