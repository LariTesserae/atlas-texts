The first temptation of lesser gods is always to bring fire.

To look upon an abyss of miles-thick ice-water, black as crushed shale and choked beneath a ceiling of frozen peat, and to say: *Let there be sun. Let them thaw. Let them see.*

A benevolent and wise power would know that this impulse is not love, but vanity. To bring heat to this trench would be to unleash the catastrophic rot of ten million years of preserved heartwood. To tear away the peat-mat would be to let the thin, vacuum-cold atmosphere boil the water from above, starving the laminar drafts, snapping the miles of taut harp-lines, and blinding the coin-fish whose lateral pits know only the exquisite topography of the roar. To give these things eyes would be an act of mutilation; to give them warmth would be to boil the cold mineral sap upon which their centuries are calibrated.

Wisdom does not arrive with a hammer or a torch. Wisdom arrives with weight.

The power would enter not as a silhouette of light, but as an added density. A quietude that displaces nothing. It would descend through the inverted sky-swamp, sliding between the pale, oil-fat eels without tearing their hair-thin gossamer fins, passing down through the freezing pelagic black until it touched the woven megalith.

And there, it would do the hardest thing any power can do:

It would listen.

***

In a world composed entirely of iron-hard vibration, to listen is to inhabit. The power would map the acoustic skeleton of the basin: the brutal, grinding C-natural of the shearing cross-beams; the rhythmic slate-slide of the disc-schools flipping in their planar lattices; the dry, continuous rasp of the cleat-slugs stripping the pale bryophytes down to the basalt-smooth timber; the rib-cracking drone of the colonial siphonophores, singing like bowed steel under the draft of the gyre.

It would hear the Cradle-Lords.

It would listen to the oldest among them—the one wedged in the lower junction of the Great Bight, a creature that has not moved its ivory ribs since before the ice caps above were formed, whose blood-vessels have grown into the petrified grain of an eight-mile lintel, drinking the cold stone-tears of the ancient weavers.

The power would hear the rhythm of its jaw.

*Thump.*
*The current is fat with lime.*
*Thump-thump.*
*The fifth lintel bears an increasing torque.*
*Thump.*
*The peat-mat above is thick and undisturbed.*

The message takes fifty-two seconds to travel through the iron-wood to the southern pylons. Fifty-two seconds later, the answering pulse returns from a sister-mass thirty miles away:

*Borne.*

Except it is not entirely borne.

The power, with its vast and tender calculus, would perceive the hidden flaw that the Cradle-Lord has monitored for forty centuries. Deep inside the fifth lintel, where the petrified heartwood joins a vertical pylon the width of a caldera, a microscopic cleavage has begun. The shearing force of the gyre—thousands of millions of tons of nearly freezing water turning against the grain—is no longer dispersing cleanly into the bed-masonry. Over the next seven hundred years, the angle of the shear will shift by less than a tenth of a millimeter. It will be enough. The joint will shear; the tension of seventy miles of interlocking trellis will unwind in a single catastrophic acoustic shock wave that will shatter the baleen of the Cradle-Lords, snap every harp-line in the basin, and drop the ceiling-marsh into the void.

The Cradle-Lords know this. They do not dread it; they have simply calculated the remainder of their clock. They report it every forty seasons. *The fifth lintel bears an increasing torque.* They do not ask for help, because there has never been anything in their universe that was not the wood, the water, and the draft.

***

A foolish power would mend the break with gold or diamond, leaving a scar that shouts of an alien hand.

The wise power would do something far slower, far more intimate.

It would not alter the current. It would not dissolve the pressure. Instead, it would fold itself into the microscopic boundary layer between the water and the timber—that fraction of a millimeter of dead water where the cleat-slugs graze.

It would touch the minds of the grooming slugs.

Not by commanding them, but by subtly altering the bitter taste of the heartwood sap weeping through the micro-fracture. It would make the mineral exudate at the point of greatest torque taste fractionally sweeter, richer in barium, denser in the silicates that the slugs need to harden their keratin shields.

Slowly—over the span of three centuries—the cleat-slugs would alter their grazing paths. They would gather by the hundreds along the seam of the fifth lintel, sliding their flat, slab-like bodies over the microscopic fault. As they rasped the bryophytes away, their trailing excretions—rich in biological lime, compressed by the hydrostatic weight of three miles of overhead brine, and bonded with the iron-sap of the wood—would fill the hairline fissure grain by grain, molecule by molecule.

Behind them, the translucent worms that nestle beneath their shells would weave their mineral-rich silk into the petrified cell-walls, knitting the ancient, humanless joinery back together with living cement.

No machine would touch it. No flash of heaven would startle the blind coin-fish from their planar formations. The repair would be made entirely out of the basin’s own flesh, paid for in the currency of moss-spore and slug-sweat.

***

And when the work was done—when the torque on the fifth lintel dropped back into the safety of the eternal hum—the power would still have one thing left to give.

The Cradle-Lord in the Great Bight rested its tallow skull against the timber sill. The dead-water chamber behind it was cold and undisturbed. In its throat, the baleen strained the broken plates of the disc-fish; through its ribs, the hydraulic load of the world pressed down with familiar, comforting weight.

It raised its massive, cartilage-plated lower jaw.

It struck the beam.

*Thump.*
*The current is fat with lime.*
*Thump-thump.*
*The fifth lintel…*

The creature paused. Its acoustic nerve-pits, mapping the vibration through eight miles of basalt-hard grain, registered the difference. The harmonic had cleared. The strain-frequency had resolved into a deep, unbroken resonance, as solid as the roots of the mountain trench.

The Cradle-Lord waited for forty miles of pylon to carry the sound south. It waited for the distant sister-mass to confirm the change.

But before the southern thump could return through the water, another pulse came through the wood.

It did not come from the south. It did not come from the sentinels at the apertures, nor from the shuddering harp-lines.

It came from the heartwood itself.

It was not a command. It had no language, no message of salvation, no promise of an open sky or an end to the cold. It was simply a frequency that matched the Cradle-Lord’s own heartbeat, struck directly into the iron grain beneath its chin—a low, seismic hum that carried the warmth of an attention so vast it could hold the entire roaring basin without displacing a single droplet of brine.

It said, in the only acoustic dialect an eternal thing could trust:

*I am here. The lattice holds. Continue.*

The Cradle-Lord’s soft, four-foot skull settled back into the tallow-hollow it had worn into the sill over ten thousand years. Its diaphragmatic walls relaxed, drawing a measured torrent of cold water through the dark, crushing the lime plates, breathing the iron-rich salt of the deep.

The power would pull itself back, dissolving upward through the hanging rootlets of the sky-swamp, leaving not a footprint, not a ripple, not an ounce of light.

Beneath it, the high, drowned ocean would roar on: cold, blind, perfectly balanced, and no longer alone in the dark.
