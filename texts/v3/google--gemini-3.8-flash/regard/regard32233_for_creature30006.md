The power that noticed the plateau had many eyes, none of which used lenses, and a will that had long since parted company with impatience. 

It did not descend with a rush of wings or a pillar of cloud. Those were the idioms of younger gods, who still mistook perturbation for importance and believed that to love a thing, one must make it jump. 

Instead, the attention arrived the way the temperature changes in a deep cellar: without a front, without a breeze, detectable only as a faint slackening of the grain of the air. 

For three hundred years of the basin’s time, the power did nothing at all. 

It spent the first century learning the weight of the water. It counted the drops that fell from the broad ribs of the ferns into the stone pockets—not to compile an inventory, but because the rhythm of that falling was the clock by which the place kept its sanity. It learned the particular, sluggish chemistry of the white gristle under the liverworts; it mapped the hairline fractures through which the copper-warm vapors ascended; it understood the three-degree difference between the raw basalt and the split shale trough.

It saw the tools. It recognized them. It knew whose hands had ground the dolerite wedge down to its blunt chisel-lip, how many winters those hands had ached from the wet cold before they found this warm basin, and why they had eventually set the adze into the cleft and walked away—or, more likely, laid down in the shallow flow and let the mineral crust take them, cell by cell, until their ribs were no different from the gray shoulders of stone. 

A lesser god—one intoxicated by pity or the urge to improve—would have done something ruinous. 

It would have spoken. And a voice, however gentle, would have shattered the forty-minute respiration of the leathery beasts, whose lungs were calibrated to the silence of condensing mist. 

It would have warmed the water further, thinking to grant comfort, and in doing so would have boiled the vellum-thin sheets that grazed upon the iron of the rocks. 

It would have brought seeds from the valleys below—sweet berries, flowering creepers, grasses that hum with bees—and within five seasons, the architecture of the ferns would have been strangled in green noise, the ancient liverworts smothered, the clear broth clouded with rot and competition. 

Or, worst of all, it might have looked upon those great, dull, boulder-shaped beings and granted them minds. It might have given them memory, or longing, or the terrible gift of knowing that the horizon falls away on three sides into empty air. It would have made them lonely.

The benevolent power did none of these things, because it was wise enough to know that paradise is not a garden made for visitors; paradise is a circuit that has closed upon itself and found peace in the absence of a witness.

Yet benevolence cannot remain entirely inert, or it is merely physics. 

In the four hundredth year of its vigil, the power felt a twitch deep within the roots of the mountain.

Four miles below the basin, where the rock was black and plastic with pressure, a cold tongue of granite was shifting. It was a very old fault, settling by fractions of an inch. Within a human century—scarcely an afternoon to the creature leaning against the adze—that shift would have pinched shut the hairline fissures through which the lukewarm water bled upward. The steam would have ceased. The rain would have turned cold, falling from the high sky as sleet. The broth would have drained away down the mountain’s flanks; the emerald pelt would have withered to gray scurf; the white gristle would have shriveled in its stone galleries; and the great leathery beasts would have drawn their spiracles closed against the freezing air and simply failed to reopen them.

The power moved.

Its intervention was neither thunderous nor bright. It sank down through the damp basalt, passing the buried calcified leaves, passing the red-hot capillaries of iron and steam, down into the blind dark where the stone was grinding against stone. 

There, it did not shatter the mountain or reverse the tectonic drift. It merely leaned its own eternal weight against the slipping block, as one might place a folded piece of paper under the short leg of a tavern table. It wedged itself into the fracture. It took the shearing stress into its own immortal spine, holding the rock open by three millimeters—just enough for the copper-scented breath to continue rising, just enough for the broth to stay lukewarm, neither hotter nor colder than the belly of a sleeping animal.

Then it returned to the surface, bringing nothing with it.

It looked once more at the clearing. 

Near the southern lip, the beast resting against the soot-black adze had not moved. But the high-altitude twilight had come, and the pool had cooled by a half-degree. 

The power, invisible and vast, drew its presence together until it was no larger than an exhalation. It did not take a body, for a body would displace the water, and every drop here was spoken for. 

Instead, it became a warmth. 

Not an ambient heat that would change the climate of the basin, but a localized density—a tiny, enduring knot of seventy-four degrees Fahrenheit, settled quietly between the leathery flank of the beast and the petrified cordage of the tool. 

The animal did not start. Its gray flank rose, forty seconds into its forty-minute cycle, drawing the warm, saturated vapor deep into its combs. As it settled down again with that soft, mineral *chink*, it felt the slight, inexplicable constancy at its side—not the fleeting warmth of stone that fades before the dawn, but a steady, faithful thing that did not exhaust itself.

It pressed a fraction of an inch closer. 

Its slow, heavy heart gave its single, two-minute beat against the haft. The lime-water seeped between its hide and the wood, and the power held its peace, content to be the wedge that keeps the frost away, and the stone that never turns cold.
