To be truly benevolent, one must first possess the terrible wisdom of restraint.

A foolish mercy would have pitied the place. It would have cracked the ceiling of mist to let down the golden, scourging sun; it would have cooled the flagstones until the subterranean boiling died to a gentle frost; it would have planted sweet clover and ash trees in the basalt cracks, thinking it was giving life where none existed.

And in doing so, it would have perpetrated an absolute slaughter. 

The parchment-mantises in the dry rafters would have dissolved under the weight of true rain. The pale, blind gliders would have turned to cold, unmoving tallow in chilled gutters. The veils would have dropped from the thinned mountain air like lead blankets, suffocating before their time. And the walkers—the great, patient beasts whose blood had matched the temperature of the boiling sluices for ten thousand generations—would have felt their thick hides stiffen into stone, their fibrous lungs collapsing in an air too thin, too clean, too sharp to breathe.

The Power that halted over the plateau was not foolish. It had watched stars turn from white to red; it had seen worlds where life was made of silicon and lightning, and worlds where thought was carried entirely on the scent of rotting wood. It understood that beauty does not belong exclusively to the green and the soft, and that peace has many temperatures.

It did not alter the climate of the shelf by half a degree. It did not blow away the mist. 

Instead, it looked through the stone.

***

Beneath the four-thousand-meter shelf, below the eight-track avenues and the cathedral transformers, lay forty leagues of pressurized dark. There, the ancient machinery—built by hands that had left no other name—was beginning, very slowly, to fail. 

It was a failure so vast and leisurely that even the walkers could only feel it as a faint, sour itch in their footpads: a valve in Sector Nine that seized once every seventy years; a deep bore-hole where the mineral encrustations were choking the intake of the volcanic fires; a three-mile copper shaft whose bearings had ground themselves down to an elliptical, shrieking chatter that vibrated through the floorboards at an agonizing pitch, barely above silence.

The city was an artificial heart that was slowly calcifying. In another ten thousand years, the heat would have sputtered out. The plateau would have frozen. The walkers would have leaned their chests against dead, silent iron, waiting for a surge that would never arrive, until the frost killed them against the columns.

The Power descended not as light, but as *alignment*.

It slipped through the weep-holes and the drainage channels, massless and warm, passing through the boiling foam where the gliders shed their translucent carapaces. It sank miles into the dark, into the vertical abysses where the pressure was enough to liquefy granite.

It did not replace the machines with spirit or magic. That would have robbed the shelf of its nature. 

Instead, it became the grease in the seized joints. It settled into the corroded races of the great subterranean turbines, coaxing the oxidized iron back into slick, functional smoothness. Where the volcanic flues were choking on centuries of sulfur-crust, the Power breathed gently—not clearing it violently, which would have ruptured the avenues above, but softening the scale, allowing the boiling slurry to carry it away grain by grain, restoring the deep, subterranean draft to its original, resonant bell-note.

It healed the city’s bones, ensuring the engine would beat for another hundred thousand years.

***

Then, having tended to the house, it returned to the room.

It sought out a walker.

The creature was standing in the middle of a vast, basalt terrace that had once been an open freight yard. It had been there since the last surge, leaning its broad, rubbery forehead against a square pylon that supported an empty gantry eighty meters in the air. 

The walker was tired. One of its fore-pads was cracked from decades of levering blockages out of the drainage grates, and the acid condensate had worked its way into the meat of the joint, causing a dull, throbbing heat that made the beast shift its three-ton bulk from leg to leg with a heavy, wet rhythm. 

It was listening into the metal, but today the metal was hard to read. The weather in its head was sluggish. Its single, chest-level breathing slit opened with a wet, raspy *chuff*, drawing in the tin-flavored steam, then snapped shut, the fibrous filter rattling with dried salt.

The Power did not take the form of an angel or an avatar. It did not speak words into a mind that had no language for them.

It simply gathered itself into the iron pylon.

At first, the walker merely felt the cold patch in the metal vanish. The iron under its fore-pads became suddenly, perfectly smooth, as if the pits of corrosion had filled themselves with warm oil.

The beast stirred. Its leathery hood, black and slick as an aubergine, tilted back an inch. 

Then, the vibration changed.

Usually, the pylon carried only the senseless, rhythmic thud of the deep pistons—a blunt, hammer-like pulse that bruised the marrow. But now, the hum began to untangle itself. It resolved into a clear, harmonic resonance, like a bronze bell struck underwater. 

Through the pads of its hands, through the diagonal drainage grooves of its skull, through the thick, calloused cartilage of its knees, the walker felt the whole plateau wake up to itself. 

It felt the four thousand meters of basalt beneath its feet, not as a dead slab, but as a living floor suspended between the fires of the earth and the vastness of the clouds. It felt the subterranean flues clearing themselves miles away, the sudden, free rush of scalding foam tumbling toward the turbines with a clean, roaring sigh. It felt the dry mantises high up in the rafters, snapping their paper-like legs against the turquoise blooms; it felt the millions of gliders tumbling through the subterranean dark, blind and pink and safe; it felt the great veils drifting through the sulfurous gray, heavy with copper, like ships with all sails set.

And beneath all of it, it felt a presence. 

Not an master. Not an overseer. 

A witness. 

Something that knew the weight of the water. Something that knew how cold the mountain air was just ten meters above the mist ceiling, and how precious was the greasy, sulfurous warmth that kept the cold away. Something that said, through the deep, five-cycle hum of the metal: 

*You have held the channel.*
*The water is running.*
*I am here.*

The dull, inflammatory ache in the walker’s cracked joint did not disappear, but it became cool and quiet, like a stone dropped into deep water. 

The beast’s breathing slit opened. It took in the hot, mineral wash of the air—not with the quick, defensive gasp of an animal guarding its wet lungs, but with a long, expanding sigh that made its rib-plates creak. 

It leaned closer. It did not fall; it simply gave its weight to the pylon, resting its broad, faceless hood directly against the rivets. 

A kilometer away, the hourly surge began. 

The basalt flagstones began to shudder. The gutters filled with boiling foam, hissing and churning like milk poured on hot coals. High overhead, the rivets in the gantries popped with their dull, hammer-like reports, echoing through the iron-gray twilight.

Usually, the walker would have turned its back, hunkered down into its defensive ball, and simply endured the violent thermal wave.

This time, it stayed leaning against the iron.

When the curtain of scalding mist hit the terrace, thick enough to blind the orange pilot flames and blot out the horizon, the walker didn't flinch. It felt the surge climb through the pylon like a great, golden cord of pressure, carrying the sound of the deep pumps working in perfect, frictionless harmony. 

The water washed over its shoulders in thick, heavy braids, hot enough to strip skin, but to the walker it felt only like the return of blood to a numbed limb.

The Power remained in the iron until the surge peaked and began its long, sighing retreat. It did not leave abruptly; it faded the way heat leaves an iron ingot, slowly, over hours, until the normal vibration of the plateau resumed its rhythmic, subsonic thud.

The terrace grew quiet again. The water drained down the sluices with a hollow, satisfied *gug-gug-gug*. 

The walker pushed itself back from the column. It stood on the wet basalt, three meters high, broad and dark and heavy as a boulder. It looked down the avenue—eight rail-beds wide, empty, running straight into the sulfurous murk under a sky that had never seen the sun.

It took one step forward on its rubbery fore-pads. The joint did not catch. 

It walked along the center line, where the stone was worn deepest, its head lowered, its skin drinking the greasy film of the mist. Fifty yards down, it came upon a iron drain-grate that was half-choked with the shed carapaces of three gliders, their gray, paste-like husks beginning to back up the scalding runoff into a puddle.

The walker paused. It reached down with its broad, spade-like thumb and worked the husks free. 

The obstruction gave way with a wet, sucking cough. The boiling water roared through the iron bars, vanishing into the subterranean runnels, clean and fast.

The walker rested its hand on the wet edge of the grate for just a second, feeling the stone hum beneath the current—clear, deep, and answering.

Then it turned, unhurried and blind, and shuffled on into the fog to find the next pylon.
