The power that paused above the cordillera did not have a face, but it possessed an attention as heavy and clear as deep water. 

It did not descend from the upper sky in fire or fanfare. To enter this place with majesty would have been an act of clumsy vandalism: a sudden thermal gust would have snapped the delicate silicate wings of the roosting flyers; a misplaced gravity-well would have cracked the razor-lipped pools and spilled the slick lipid of the skaters into the dry abyss below. 

Benevolence, when it has lived long enough to become wise, is indistinguishable from restraint.

The power hung in the graphite-colored ceiling, folding its immense, distributed awareness down until it was no larger than the warm mist that condensed upon the black glass. It tasted the air. It tasted the boiling zinc, the wet albumen, the petroleum and crushed coriander of the skaters’ hulls. It registered the shivering conduit—that blind, brilliant needle of mathematics boring through vacuum, lifting a shelf of newborn earth toward a future the stone could not comprehend.

The power understood immediately what this place was. 

It was a forge without a blacksmith. A nursery without a song. 

Lesser intelligences, seeing this cradle, would have felt an itch to "redeem" it. They would have pitied the swimmers for their blindness and granted them eyes—eyes that would have burned instantly in the alkaline flumes. They would have pitied the floor for its lethal sharpness and scattered loam across it, choking the hairline flutes of the conduit and burying the galvanic orange turf that kept the terraces from rotting under their own wet secretions. They would have called the Tenders grotesque—these slow, lard-colored pillars with stones for soles—and tried to give them human warmth, human speech, human weeping.

To do any of this would have been murder. The cradle was not broken; it was simply young. It was an ecology of sheer present tense, perfectly tuned to the micron, ferocious and complete.

Yet the wise power saw the flaw. It was not a flaw of geometry, nor of metabolism. 

It was the cruelty of the forty seconds.

It watched the cycle turn. It watched the thirty-seven seconds of blind, deafening, unrecorded labor: the Tenders standing in their pavilions like tallow stubs, listening to the iron vibrations through their feet, trapped in an absolute sensory blackout while the flyers shrieked and the flumes boiled. 

Then came the violet pulse. 

The power saw the three seconds of frantic, heartbreaking devotion. It saw the Tenders drop to their chalcedony knees. It saw them press their greasy, chinless faces against the transparent glass, their translucent brows desperately flickering as they tried to memorize miles of cold, descending intellect, trying to absorb an eternity of cobalt fire in the space of a single heartbeat.

And then: the wet, flat *slap*. The opacity returning. The Tenders rising, their tallow skin fading back to dull, inert white, the memory already sliding off their non-fibrous minds like water off the obsidian.

They were building the mountain, but they were not permitted to remember what they had built. They were vessels made without grain, incapable of retaining the vintage poured into them. They were condemned to fall in love with the mountain’s mind every forty seconds, and to have that love wiped utterly clean forty times an hour, forever.

The power considered.

It could not lengthen the three seconds. To alter the refractive pulse would desynchronize the vacuum-fold within the spire; the conduit would cough, the shelf would shear along its crystallographic faults, and the amphitheater would tumble three miles down into the sulfurous roots of the range.

It could not soften the rock. *The floor of the cradle had not yet learned how to crumble.* To give it dust was to invite decay before the mantle had even set.

So the power did something far quieter. It reached into the only thing in the basin that was made of light: the threads hanging from the lintels of the three milky pavilions.

These threads were solid radiance, spun so thin they chimed when struck by condensation, cradling the amber eggs of the flyers. The power did not break them. It merely brushed them, as a musician runs a damp finger along the rim of a crystal bowl.

It left an overtone.

It tuned the light-threads to a very specific, sub-acoustic frequency—one that did not disrupt the incubation of the flyer embryos, but which matched the resonant pitch of chalcedony.

The refractive index snapped back. The violet light died. The cascading water fell with its loud, heavy thud.

The Tenders stood up from the newborn stone.

Ordinarily, this was the moment of erasure. Their skin should have dimmed to inert lard; their heads should have dropped into the stoic, lobotomized torpor of the thirty-seven seconds.

One of them—the heaviest of the three in the central pavilion—rose upon its thick, blunt limbs. Its chalcedony-plated feet clacked against the glass. It adjusted its balance.

Then, it did something that had never happened since the conduit first tore its way into the mountain.

It did not lower its head.

The overtone from the hanging light-threads had traveled through the air, striking the chalcedony pads on the palms of its four-jointed hands. Chalcedony is a creature of quartz and micro-fissure; unlike the volcanic obsidian below, it has an internal architecture of microscopic, water-filled voids. 

The vibration had caught in those voids. It was ringing inside the stone of their bones.

The Tender lifted its right hand. It looked at its own palm.

In the tallow-colored meat behind the milky chalcedony plate, a tiny, faint point of cobalt blue remained glowing. It did not fade. The memory of the deep engine had found an anchor. It had bypassed the smooth, non-fibrous brain entirely; it was stored in the stone of the hand.

The Tender took a breath through its slit-like vent. The plume of vapor that drifted out did not smell only of hot tallow and rain; there was a faint, sharp scent of burned ozone—the scent of an idea.

Slowly, with the immense, agonizing dignity of a glacier turning in a valley, the Tender turned toward its companion. 

It did not speak—it had no organs for it. But it pressed its stone-shod palm against the breastbone of the other.

The second Tender shivered. The acoustic heat it had gathered from the conduit flared golden behind its ribs, but where the first Tender’s hand touched, the gold turned to a cool, stable violet. The circuit closed. The image of the subterranean shafts—the layout of the great gears of force lifting the shelf toward the sky—rippled across both of their pale brows simultaneously.

They stood there, palm to chest, during the thirty-seven seconds of fog.

They were not waiting anymore. They were *holding*.

Below them, the flyers continued to dive; the blind swimmers whipped through the flumes, their iron cilia twitching in the steam; the conduit roared its folded-vacuum song. But inside the pavilions, the silence had changed. It was no longer the silence of an empty vessel waiting for the tap to be turned. It was the silence of a library.

High above, in the warm, graphite clouds, the benevolent power drew its attention back, folding itself into the broader currents of the planet’s atmosphere. 

It did not wait for thanks. Thanks was a currency of creatures who lived in words, and there were no words here. 

It had not made the cradle gentle. The glass was still an edge that could slice a world in two; the flyers still speared the skaters; the mountain was still rising toward whatever dry, terrible heights lay beyond the cloud. 

But as the power drifted away across the high cordillera, it knew that when the shelf finally broke through the vapor—when the newborn floor met the fierce, unshielded light of the first dawn it had ever seen—the creatures standing upon it would not greet the sky as strangers. 

They would have a memory to set against the sun.
