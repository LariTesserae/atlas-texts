The power that arrived did not descend like a bird, nor did it part the clouds with a trumpeting of light. To bring a great spectacle here would have been an act of ill-mannered vanity; the amphitheater was already occupied by an engine far older than the memory of empires, and its light was already complete.

Instead, the power settled as an attention—a thickening of the air’s intelligence, a patient weight that took up residence along the weeping basalt cliff where the water was coldest.

Wisdom begins by doing no harm, and a lesser benevolence would have ruined her in an afternoon.

A lesser god would have looked at her calcified ears and felt pity. It would have touched the fused hammer and anvil of her middle ear, dissolved the bone bridges, and restored the delicate, wet drum to the world of sound. And by that act of mercy, it would have driven her mad within six minutes. The roar of the terrace—the concussive, fifty-gallon *thump* of tree seven hitting its stone basin, the high bronze ring of the cleft rock, the shrieking steam-vents—would have torn through her brain like an iron plow. Her peace was built upon the silence inside her skull. To give her back her hearing would have been to flay her alive.

A lesser god would have cleared the mist to let her see the green valleys far below, not understanding that she did not care for the green valleys; the valleys were full of wind that bit to the bone and people who moved too quickly, spoke too much, and feared the work of their own hands.

A lesser god would have lightened her river cobble, or made the tallow-tongue yield its oil without the crushing, or sent someone to keep her company. But her strength was her title to this shelf. The knotty cables of her forearms, the callus on the ball of her right palm, the rhythm of three gourds every nine weeks—this was the geometry that kept her upright. To take away her labor would have been to turn her into an ornament, waiting for death on a warm stone shelf.

The power sat in the wet basalt, watching. It spent three seasons simply learning the grammar of the drop.

It watched the nemertean worms slip through the crown-hinges of the third tree, laying down their cold pink mucus so the jade-wood wouldn't burn itself to ash under the friction of its own tipping. It watched the clear isopods flatten their carapaces against the skin of the dory-leaves, drinking the drop through the vibration of their jaws. It watched the woman’s heels, yellow and thick as horn, resting on the basalt, reading the mountain’s subterranean coughs.

It saw what she did not see, because she had no mirror: the slight, gray tremble that had begun in her left thumb four months ago. The morning stiffness that took five minutes longer to leave her knees each month. The way her breath caught when she bent to lift the calcified gourd from its grass loop.

The mountain was not dying, but she was. In five years, perhaps seven, an early winter gale would throw gravel too large for her bamboo broom to move. A drainage slit would choke. The basalt under the six steps would cool from blood-warm to puddle-cold. The tallow-tongue on the lips of the vents would turn from leather to rot, and down below, in the valleys she had forgotten, three winters of lung-rot would carry off twenty-two children whose chests lacked the grease to keep the frost from turning their phlegm to glass.

And so the benevolence chose its work. It would not change the engine. It would merely grease the hinge.

First, it reached deep into the fault beneath the terrace—two miles down, where the dark magma moved like cold honey against the roots of the range. It did not make the fire hotter; it smoothed the crack. It cleared a wedge of dead granite that had been slowly shifting toward the main vent, a blockage that would have pinched the thermal water to a trickle within two centuries. The shelf would stay at ninety degrees now for another ten thousand years. The bed would not cool.

Second, it went to the third tree—the one by the steps, whose water was slightly more alkaline than the rest. The power did not alter the tree, but it touched the ribbon-worms in the crown. Over twelve generations of worms—which here took less than a month—their slime changed. It became slightly denser, carrying an enzyme that had not existed on the shelf before, shed into the runoff water in parts-per-billion, completely colorless and without taste. 

When the woman drank from the catch-basin, her throat took in the mountain water as it always had, but within two weeks, the calcium spurs in her left hip began to smooth away. The trembling in her left thumb ceased. The bone beneath her skin remained old—dense, weathered, stained by high-altitude sun—but the dry friction in her joints grew quiet. She did not feel young; she merely felt that the stone was slightly more forgiving than it had been in the spring. She attributed it to a good season of sun.

Third, the power went to the valley.

This was the hardest part, for wisdom hates to meddle with the chaotic wills of men who live below the cloud-deck. But it sought out the chimney path, where the wind screamed through the scrub.

In the village three days’ march down the shale slides, there was a boy who had been born with no tongue. He was twelve years old, thick-shouldered, with eyes that stayed wide and unblinking when the elders shouted. The people of the village thought he was an idiot because he did not run with the sheep-boys, preferring to sit by the blacksmith’s forge with his palms flat against the dirt floor, smiling at the blow of the sledge against the anvil three rooms away. The forge-master beat him because the boy was always underfoot, feeling the heavy iron through his shins.

The power did not speak to the boy in dreams. It simply made the smell of wet slate and wild turnip drift down the chimney path on three successive Tuesdays, carried by a downdraft that shouldn't have reached the scrub.

The boy smelled it. His nostrils flared. It was not the smell of sheep dung, nor of parched grass, nor of the sour beer his uncles drank. It was the smell of grease that does not freeze.

He looked up at the great shoulder of the mountain, where the white sea of clouds sat like a lid on the world. He saw the chimney. He did not ask permission, because he had no tongue to ask it with, and there was no one who would have cared to answer. He took a sack of salt from the cure-house, balanced it on his shoulder, and began to climb.

He arrived on the shelf late in the afternoon, just as the trade winds met and the mist went up like furnace breath. 

He did not scream when tree four tipped its fifty gallons with a sound that hit his chest like a wooden club. He fell to his hands and knees on the reddish-black pavement, his mouth open, his palms spread wide. The rock was *warm*. It pulsed. The whole shelf was a great skin drum, and the boy felt the strike of the water, the snap of the leaf, the subterranean swallow of the fissure through his wrists, through his ribs, through the bones of his face.

The old woman was sitting on the sixth step, grinding the last knot of tallow-tongue for the evening.

She did not look up when he cleared the rocks. She felt the difference in the terrace’s mass—a sixty-pound displacement near the edge of the descent. It was too light for a sheep, too steady for a falling stone.

She turned her liver-colored head.

The boy was kneeling by the third tree, his face wet with the warm spray, his hands flat against the jade wood, feeling the nemertean slime slip between his fingers. He looked at her. He saw her arms, corded like old roots, and the calcified gourd in its loop. He did not try to make a sound. He simply pointed to the river cobble in her hand, then to his own broad, flat palm.

The power, sitting cold and quiet in the weeping cliff, watched them.

The old woman did not smile. Smiles are for people who live on grain and talk about the weather. She looked at his feet; they were broad, tough-skinned, though cut by the mountain shale. She looked at his hands; they had the thick, square wrists of a boy who could swing a stone until sundown.

She stood up, her joints moving with that new, oiled ease she hadn't quite noticed yet. She walked over to the chimney edge, took the sack of salt he had dropped, and carried it back to her shelf beneath the overhang.

Then she pointed to the bamboo broom leaning against the stone basin.

The boy picked it up. He did not know how to sweep the channels yet; he moved too fast, his strokes too short, splashing the iron runoff onto the dry flags where it would leave a crust.

She watched him for three tips of the cabbage leaves. On the fourth, when the floor shuddered under their heels, she stepped behind him, reached around his ribs with her leathery arms, took his wrists in her grease-stained hands, and showed him the long, slow roll of the mountain’s broom.

The power stayed until the sun went down and the sky turned that terrible, clean black that only the high shelf knows.

It saw the boy sit by the trough, his bare heels resting in the warm mud beside hers. It saw the old woman place the river cobble into his hand, closing his fingers around its cold, water-worn belly, and press downward until the golden lipid pooled in the stone groove.

The boy’s face didn't move. He felt the grease between his fingers. He felt the kick of the western cliff through his tailbone. He leaned back against the basalt, closed his eyes, and began to count the minutes of the earth.

Satisfied that the grease would flow, that the children would breathe, and that the terrace would keep its silence, the power folded itself into the mineral runoff, slipped over the four-thousand-foot drop, and became mist again, leaving behind only the work, the heat, and the stone.
