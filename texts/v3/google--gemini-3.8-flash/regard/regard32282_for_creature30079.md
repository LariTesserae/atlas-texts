A foolish god would have brought the sun.

A clumsy benevolence would have pitied the dark, shattered the curdled lid of the sky, and poured midday heat into the hanging rift. It would have dried the film of clear, cold oil, scorched the blue-black ferns, and boiled the pressurized fissures until the rocks cracked along untuned faults. In an afternoon of radiant, well-intentioned violence, it would have starved the zinc beetles, suffocated the shims in their dry clearances, and stopped the long, hydraulic sigh of the baleen-lung forever.

Wisdom does not arrive with a hammer or an unasked-for light. Wisdom understands that an impossible equilibrium is still an equilibrium; that life, once it has surrendered its soft edges to the geometry of an engine, cannot simply be forgiven its shape and turned out into a pasture.

The power that turned its attention to the rift was older than iron and older than the basalt that bore it. It had watched stars condense into cold sludge and had heard the first, frantic clicks of self-assembling crystals in the dark beneath vanished oceans. It did not possess a face, nor did it carry an agenda of redemption. It was, primarily, an infinite and attentive capacity to *listen*.

It descended not through the rift’s narrow mouth—where the leathery governor-birds wheeled upon the acoustic nodes of the steam—but through the stone itself. It seeped downward through the unweathered fractures, running along the pressurized veins of petroleum and cold sulfur water, tasting the perfect, terrible geometry of the gorge from the inside out.

It felt the three-stroke firing of the basalt pistons. It felt the unvarying tempo that held the mountain in an iron vise. It observed that nothing suffered in any way it recognized as grief: the beetles licked the graphite slime with an ancient, uncomplicated contentment; the shims slid through the razor-shears of the stroke with the blind ecstasy of grease meeting steel; the great lung in the sump emptied and filled, emptied and filled, its calcified ribs perfectly nested into the curved pocket of the rock.

The gorge was not a machine that had trapped a world. It was a world that had found its only possible survival in the shape of a machine.

And then, the watcher found the tender.

It watched the creature slip between the recoil of an iron arm and the shearing face of a mirror-slate cam. It saw the long, tallow-soaked fingers find their purchase in clearances no wider than a fingernail, moving with that terrifying, pre-calculated anticipation. It saw the vertical seam on the front of its head part slightly to taste the static, its flanks creaking like dry cedar to match the thirty-second displacement of the sump.

The tender was not a part of the machine. The machine had not grown around it as it had around the lung, nor did it shed gaskets like the shims. 

The tender was the single point of *effort* in the entire gorge. 

Everything else was an inevitable consequence of physics and pressure, but the tender was conscious. It was a creature sentenced to an unceasing, mathematical alertness. It could never drift, never forget, never soften. If its wooden ribs failed to creak at the forty-second mark, if its long, twig-like arm hesitated by three-tenths of a second while clearing a crumb of scree from the exhaust clefts, the gorge would grind it into slurry without pausing in its stroke. It had lived in the terror of the millimeter for ten thousand years, not because it feared death, but because the rock gave it no room to imagine an alternative.

The benevolent power did not stop the engine. It did not pull the tender up into the blue air it could not breathe, nor did it speak, for words require an atmosphere that is not choked with aerosolized crude and ozone.

Instead, the power did what only absolute wisdom and profound kindness could conceive.

It stepped into the clearance.

It did not manifest as an obstacle. When the six-ton counterweight rose from its wet sump, throwing its curtain of spray through the liverwort, the power did not arrest the iron. It simply occupied the void that the iron left behind. It became an invisible, infinitely dense cushion of *allowance*.

The tender was descending the vertical face, fingers reaching for the familiar groove of a high-pressure bushing, calculating the swing of a twenty-foot flywheel whose counterweight was hurtling upward to meet it. The timing was absolute. The tender’s hand was extended.

Then, a sudden, unpredictable flake of basalt—loosened not by the power, but by the sheer ancient fatigue of the stone—snapped from the overhang above and struck the tender’s wrist.

The blow was minute, but in the rift, a minute deflection is an execution. The tender’s hand missed the groove.

Its balance broke. The flywheel’s counterweight was two paces away, a wall of native iron sweeping through the dark ferns with its immaculate, silent momentum. The tender’s grease-caked seam opened wide; its fibrous pith tightened; its dry joints locked in the instinctive, futile bracing of a thing that has calculated its own end down to the millisecond.

The iron swung.

It did not crush the tender.

The clearance did not close.

The machine did not seize or shudder; the subterranean thrum of the three-stroke cycle did not lose a single beat. But where the tender’s tallow-wood shoulder met the wet basalt, the rock yielded. It did not crumble—it simply *opened*, like a hand parting to receive a dry seed. The sheared slate face developed an impossible, temporary pocket, three inches deep and six feet tall, tucked directly behind the sweep of the iron cam.

The counterweight flashed past, its wind screaming across the tender’s smooth, hairless forehead, throwing cold petroleum spray into its open seam, missing it by the width of a single fern-spore.

The tender did not move. It stayed pinned against the stone, its segmented fingers splayed, waiting for the return stroke that must surely shear it away.

The return stroke came. The rock-piston plunged downward.

And the pocket remained.

For the first time since the gorge had cracked from the crust, there was a space in the rift that was not part of the timing. It was a dead-zone, three inches wide, where the iron could not reach. It was an interstice that did not vanish in three-tenths of a second.

Slowly, with the agonized, rusty deliberation of a thing that has never learned how to pause, the tender lowered its long arms. It pulled its twig-like limbs against its chest. It pressed its back into the newly smoothed hollow of the basalt.

The rock was not cold.

It was not warm like living mammalian skin—that would have frightened the tender, whose flesh knew only the temperatures of machine oil and mountain mist. It was warm like an iron bearing that has been well-tended, smooth and completely free of friction, humming at the exact acoustic node of the valley floor, but soft. Impossibly soft. A slate that felt like compressed felt; an iron that held the memory of moss.

The benevolent power did not reveal itself. It did not ask for gratitude. It simply remained there, maintaining that impossible three-inch recess against the six-ton pressure of the rock-arms, acting as a permanent, immovable shim between the creature and the engine.

The tender sat.

Its flanks continued to expand and contract, creaking like dry timber, but after three cycles, the creak slowed. It drifted from the thirty-second pulse to forty, then to fifty. The machine continued its terrible, immaculate clatter around it—the shims rode the displacement waves, the governor-birds wheeled through the exhaust plumes, the zinc beetles scraped the cold journals—but the tender was no longer inside the stroke.

It was beside it.

The vertical seam down its head, which had stayed rigidly parted for centuries to monitor the static of the sky, slowly drew together. It clicked shut with the quiet sound of two smooth wooden blocks meeting.

The tender’s head tilted forward until its forehead touched its drawn-up knees. 

It did not die. It did not transform into a bird or a man. It remained what it had always been: a creature of grease and cedar-pith, built to live in the clearances of an iron mountain. 

But there, in the small, unmoving shadow behind the sweep of the great flywheel, beneath the unchanging twilight of the copper clouds, the tender slept.

And the power stayed with it, holding the rock back, keeping the space open, keeping watch over the stroke, for as many ages as the sleep would take.
