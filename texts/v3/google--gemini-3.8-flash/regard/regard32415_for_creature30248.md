The power that came to the lip of the trench did not bring an eagle’s eye or a column of fire. Those are the instruments of powers that wish to be watched, and this one wished only to see.

It arrived without displacing a grain of limestone flour from the overhang. It was ancient enough to remember when the bedrock here was the floor of a shallow inland sea, and patient enough to know that a thousand human years are merely the time it takes for a drop of water to hollow a thumbprint in marble.

It looked down through the narrow slot.

It saw the roofscape of wet hides and gray slate; it saw the children tethered to the osier pegs, quiet as grubs; it saw the lines of greasy-armed laborers leaning into breast-straps to skid a four-ton lintel across roller-logs without making a sound. It smelled the cellar-chill: the mutton tallow, the cold chalk, the sour sap of green larch curing under pressure.

A clumsy god would have pitied them and ruined them.

A clumsy god would have filled the trench with soil to bring them to the sun, burying their houses and breaking their gin-poles. Or would have cast down heaps of iron, causing them to drop their ropes and murder one another for edges. Or would have brought warm breezes down the chimney, not realizing that warmth would bloom the white mold through their bundles of dried reed, rot the lashings that held their stacked tiers to the shear faces, and spoil the parched mutton in their wicker hampers. Fire, light, ease—these are blunt axes in a world built on fractional clearances.

The power had wisdom, which is the knowledge of limits, and benevolence, which is the desire that another life should continue to be itself, only whole.

It saw what the town was: a temporary knot tied in the hem of an abyss. The people had adapted to everything within their power. They had matched their gait to the fracture-planes of the stone; they had flattened their speech to preserve their peace; they had even taught their children the geometry of the drop before they had their teeth. They were doing everything that blood and bone could do.

The danger did not come from their mistakes. The danger was below them.

The rift was sixty paces deep, but the cut did not end at the floor. The fault ran four leagues down into the cold dark of the crust, where two enormous plates of basement stone were still holding a grudge. The white rhomboids of the floor were not stable; they were the teeth of a jaw that had not finished shutting. That metallic *ting* that woke the elders every three hours was the sound of the stone testing the pin. One day—perhaps in seven winters, perhaps in twenty—a deep micro-burr would shear away cleanly, and the walls would snap together three spans, shearing the larch baulks like dry celery and turning five thousand people into a thin red grease between two slabs of chalk.

This was the thing the willow wands could not measure and the breast-straps could not pull against.

So the power did not descend into the cut. It did not speak into the hushed air or leave signs on the slate roofs.

Instead, it sank downward through the rim, sixty strides back from the lip, moving through the cold stone without parting it, down past the cellar level, past the foundations where the pale crickets crawled in the wet shavings, down into the blind, unweathered basalt below.

Three leagues down, in the black heat where the rock was dense as iron and groaned under the weight of the continent, the power laid itself along the fault.

It did not weld the break. To weld it would create an arch of stress that would blow out elsewhere with the force of a mountain falling. Instead, it worked like a man rubbing tallow into a dried boot. It eased the friction. It smoothed the sheared teeth of the subterranean stone, sliding its vast, unhurried will between the tectonic faces like a film of cold oil. 

Where the plates were hanging on a hair-trigger of jagged quartz, it softened the bite, coaxing the stone to give up its panic. It allowed the mountain to settle an inch—not in a shudder that would shake down the stacked tiers above, but in a long, silent, imperceptible creep that dissipated the pressure into the deep roots of the earth.

For three days, the power stayed in the dark, acting as a wedge, a cushion, and a level. It took the terror out of the bedrock. When it was finished, the two sides of the canyon were no longer a sprung trap waiting for a heel-strike; they were balanced against one another, seated in their sills like two seasoned beams in a barn frame that has weathered its first gale and found its true resting plane.

Then it rose, turning back toward the light.

It passed through the middle tiers on its way out, and here it paused, because benevolence cannot help but touch the small things it passes.

It looked at the cedar water-gutters where the trough-watchers sat with their scrapers. It saw the white, crusty lines where the bitter gypsum water seeped from the third fault-layer, souring the buckets and eating the willow ties. The power reached out an invisible finger and touched the hairline seam ten feet above the highest roof-tier. 

It did not stop the water—water was life in the cut. But it tilted the microscopic grain of the limestone along that single fissure, turning the seepage four inches to the west, through a buried lens of fine river-gravel that had been trapped in the rock before the scrub plains were dry. When the runnel broke out again on the cliff-face, it had left its gypsum in the gravel; it came out clear, cold, and tasting faintly of clean flint, sweet enough to drink without parching the tongue.

Lastly, it looked at the pale crickets in the crawl-spaces. 

It saw how the boys dried them on the slate and ground them with salt to rub into the harness-galls. The power blew a faint, cool breath through the foundation timbers. It did not change the crickets—they must stay soft, lard-colored, and blind, for they were what the lizards lived upon, and the lizards kept the sleepers clean. But it laid a tiny gift in their fat: a quiet property of the deep earth, an oil that would keep the skin of a hauler’s collarbone from cracking into running sores when the larch bark bit through the wool.

Then the power stepped up from the rim, out into the scouring scrub-wind, and withdrew.

Down in the cut, noon had passed. The cold shadow rose from the limestone floor like gray water, climbing the tiers, extinguishing the white glare on the slate roofs.

In the third tier, an old trough-watcher leaned forward from her larch knot, her eyes squinted against the dimming light. She dipped her horn spoon into the cedar runnel she had watched for twenty years, the one that had always tasted of alum and rot. She put it to her lip, expecting the sour pucker that curled the tongue.

She blinked. The water was cold. It tasted of nothing at all—only stone and cold air. She looked up at the rock-face, scraping her thumb along the diversion lip she had chipped that morning. Nothing had changed. The drop fell where it always fell, *tic, tic, tic*, into the trough. She muttered a clicked vowel into the back of her throat, shook her head, and wiped her scraper on her sheepskin skirt. A good seam, she thought. The lime had settled. She said nothing to the woman on the next platform; there was no need to spend breath on a well that had righted itself.

Deeper down, in the keels where the young men slept four to a pallet, a quarry-hand rolled over in the tallow-light. 

He waited for the sound. It was the hour when the earth usually spoke—that thin, glass-sharp *ting* that made the heel-joints ache even through the sheepskins. He lay with his palm flat against the unweathered limestone floor, feeling through his yellowed skin for the shudder of the three-league snap.

The cut was silent. 

There was only the muffled *thwack* of an adze far up the wall, the rasped breath of a saw working larch-heart, and the faint, papery rustle of the white crickets shifting like dry chaff beneath his floorboards. 

The stone beneath his palm was cold, but it was still. It felt broad, thick, and deep, as if it had forgotten how to fall. 

He waited three breaths, his ribs pressed to the gray rock, then let his forehead drop back onto the timber baulk he used for a bolster. He pulled his stiff wool over his ears, smelled the mutton fat on his forearms, and slept, his knees bent, his broad feet relaxed into the dark, entirely unaware that the world had decided to let him keep his breath.
