A power that is both wise and benevolent possesses, above all, the discipline not to correct a masterpiece it did not author.

A lesser deity, full of young light and pastoral vanity, would have broken the canopy. It would have sent a dry, scouring wind to drive out the sulfur; it would have stripped the wild figs from the H-beams, bleached the sump of its lime-slurry, and carried the engineer out into an open field of clover to let the sun blister his leathered neck until he went mad from the absence of weight. That is the benevolence of conquerors, which is only another name for impatience.

The power that paused above the basin was older than that, and far more patient. It understood that holiness is rarely clean, and that when cast iron, swamp water, and an old man’s marrow have spent ninety years agreeing upon a single vibration, any interruption is an execution.

It did not descend with wings or wheels of fire. In a place where a cricket courting song was a dry abdomen hammered against an iron pipe, an apparition would have been an obscenity. Instead, it arrived as an attention—a thickening of the air, not unlike the pressure that precedes a boiler blow-down, but without the heat.

It spent seven turns of the great flywheels simply learning the displacement.

It entered through the drainage culverts, moving through the gray-brown bayou water with the slow, blind curiosity of an eel, feeling the twelve-foot head of pressure in the sump. It felt the teeth of the bevel gears; it felt the hairline fractures running through the web of the east engine’s crank disc, microscopic fissures where the crystalline structure of the gray iron had begun to tire after three billion strokes. It touched the damp magnesium wrap where the blind crickets lay in their chalky tunnels, their pale antennae twitching against the singing brass.

And then it considered the man.

It saw the ash-wood clogs, oil-soaked until they were nearly as heavy as lead. It saw the five lumbar vertebrae compressed by forty years of carrying forty-pound tins of tallow up iron ladders. It saw his lungs—not pink and resilient like the lungs of an orchard hand, but gray-black, glazed on their interior linings with an asphaltic crust of lampblack and sulfur, yet somehow, miraculously, holding a steady, four-second draw in and out, timed precisely to the stroke of the crosshead.

The man did not pray. He had forgotten the names of the things people asked for. When he reached out his calloused thumb to feel the temperature of a main bearing pillow-block, he was performing the only liturgy he knew: *Are you true? Are you staying?*

To this, the power could only say yes.

It did not take away his deafness. Silence was his shelter; to give him back his ears would have been to drown him in the terrible, grinding roar of his own machinery. His world was made of bone-conduction, and it was through the bone that the power chose to speak.

First, it attended to the metal.

It did not make the engines new. If the bearings had suddenly turned to polished, frictionless chrome, the clogs would have slipped; the man’s shins would have missed the dry, dragging hum of worn babbitt metal, and he would have known himself abandoned to an unfamiliar world. 

Instead, the power entered the cold micro-fissures of the iron. 

Deep inside the crank disc, where the metal had grown brittle from fatigue, the graphite flakes did not disappear; they simply knitted. The iron remained iron, tired and heavy, but the threshold of failure was nudged back by forty years. The rust scale on the underside of the boiler shell ceased to flake; it set into a hard, protective magnetite skin, black as coal, that would weep no further. 

Where the roots of the river fig held the brick walls in their basket, the power gave the wood a fraction more elasticity, so that when the ground swelled with the spring tides, the masonry would yield without dropping a single course of headers into the drainage culverts.

Then, it touched his body.

It did not make him young. A young man could not have sat on the gantry ladder with his heels hooked over the bar, content to watch duckweed knit itself over a scalding pool. Youth is hungry, and hunger would have killed him here within a month.

It did something far more merciful: it took the ache from the grit.

When he drew in his next breath of cylinder oil and wet peat, the sulfur did not catch in the cleft of his throat. The raw, burning edge at the base of his bronchial tree softened into a neutral, comforting warmth, like the steam that rises from fresh bread. The sharp, needle-like spurs of calcium on his lower spine—the ones that made him pause on the fourth rung of the ladder every morning until his hips uncramped—did not vanish, but the nerve endings around them withdrew a millimeter, yielding a painless, supple ease that felt to him simply like a good night’s sleep.

He did not know an angel had walked the catwalks. He only thought the humidity had dropped half a grain per cubic foot.

The power moved down into the sump, where the eels lay with their bellies in the lime-slurry. It touched the skin of the water. The grease film that had choked out the smaller breathers did not dissolve—it remained to keep the water warm—but beneath it, the water became sweet enough to support the invisible rotifers that feed the frog-spawn. The clutches of eggs in the axils of the taro leaves swelled, clear and jelly-bright, immune to the acid fall of soot.

Lastly, the power sat where the man sat.

It took up no space behind the accumulator, beside the tin of salt pork and the horn buttons. It became simply an addition to the ambient draft, a small, cool eddy in a place where the air had been motionless since the second year of the century.

When the old man climbed down from the gantry, his clogs hollow on the iron grids, he stopped by the four-spoke brass wheel. 

He took his iron wrench from its bath of neat’s-foot oil. He leaned his weight into the turn, his shoulder muscles knotting beneath his gray shirt, bracing for the dry, shrieking groan the bypass valve always gave before it seated.

He turned it.

The valve turned three full revolutions with the smoothness of chilled lard. The wheel settled into its packing without a shudder. The steam did not hiss; it fell into the line with a deep, full-throated *whuff* that sent a long, golden ripple through the ash-wood soles of his shoes.

The man paused. He laid his palm against the warm iron of the valve housing. 

He didn't look up. He didn't look around for an altar. There were only the river figs, the wet brick, and the rhythmic, wet *thunk-slosh, thunk-slosh* of the horizontal beams sixty feet away in the dark.

He felt the floorboards. The vibration was the same, but the beat was whole. There was no missing tooth in the pinion; there was no dragging shoe on the eccentric. The machine was doing what it had always done, but it was no longer dying as it did it.

For a long time—perhaps twenty engine strokes—the old man stood with his hand on the metal, his chin dropped against his collarbone, his deaf ears filled with the steady, lime-washed silence of the pocket.

Then he reached into his pocket, took out a smear of mutton tallow on a piece of clean waste, and worked it carefully into the brass threads of the stem, not because it was needed, but because it was his part of the bargain.

The power did not leave. A place like that cannot be visited and then forgotten; it must be kept. 

It stayed down in the bilge, between the counterweight and the muck, measuring out the eighty-year remainder of the iron, and the sixteen-year remainder of the man, making sure that when the ash-wood soles finally stopped moving across the catwalks, the engines would have the grace to run out of steam on the very same stroke.
