To look upon the basin with human eyes would be to misunderstand it entirely. A human heart, entering that gloom, would recoil in horror; it would wish to tear open the sky, to pour down golden sunlight, to sow grass upon the dolomite, to silence the deafening, mechanical roar and grant these blind, tallow-skinned things the mercy of a quiet death.

A foolish power would have done just that. It would have cracked the cloud-ceiling with a beam of celestial heat, and in doing so, it would have laid waste to an eternity of balance. The dry air would have desiccated the loose, damp hides of the tallow-scavengers within an hour. The sudden light would have seared the unshielded nerve-endings of a hundred million rosettes. The crawlers, starved of the cool mineral-slick that condenses only in the dense, freezing vapor, would have ground their bellies to bone against dry, unyielding stone. The embryos trampled in the ditches would have baked into brittle glass, never to awaken. 

To destroy a world in order to reform it in one's own image is the mark of a tyrant, not a benevolent mind.

The Power that arrived above the depression was neither foolish nor vain. It possessed the terrible, patient wisdom of things that have watched stars congeal from dust and cool into iron. It had no face, no wings, and no need to be worshiped; it had only *weight*, *attention*, and an infinite capacity for comprehension.

It hung above the rim of the country for a long age of stone, not descending, merely listening. 

It listened through the miles of soot-colored fog, down into the thick ammoniac soup, feeling the tremor of the valley through the mantle of the world. It felt the eight-beat grind of the twelve-foot crawlers; it mapped the hollow, thirty-foot throats of the sixty-cubit pillar-beasts drinking the vapor; it registered the delicate, parchment-thin flutter of the flat-worms surviving in the shear-zone, thriving upon the very pressure that sought to turn them to paste.

And looking upon them, the Power did not see ugliness. It saw a miracle of endurance. 

It saw that flesh here was not frail, vain, or fleeting. Flesh here was granite. Flesh here had struck an ancient, stubborn treaty with entropy: *we will surrender nothing.* Every molted flake of skin was recaptured; every breath of fog was strained of its salt; every carcass was folded back into the living carpet before its joints had fully cooled. It was a cathedral built of gristle and persistence, operating with the terrifying, beautiful precision of an engine that had forgotten how to stop.

Yet, because the Power was wise, it saw what the basin could not see of itself.

It saw the *finitude* of the closed loop. The engine was magnificent, but it was leaking. 

Deep beneath the dolomite, in the black cracks where the rubbery, white fault-borers caulked the fissures with their own bodies, the bedrock was tiring. The weight of millions of tons of biological iron and bone, grinding across the same troughs for ten thousand generations, had begun to hollow the earth from beneath. The void below was cold, hollow, and hungry. The fault-borers were straining, their long bodies pulsing to exhaustion, exuding their chalky mineral paste faster than their siphons could draw nutrients from the dark slurry of the surface. 

Left to itself, in another thousand centuries, the center of the depression would suffer a catastrophic shear. The polished stone would buckle inward, the floor would plunge into the subterranean hollows, and the entire roaring, interlocking mountain of life would choke on dry air and crushed dolomite in the abyss.

Furthermore, the Power felt the quality of the acoustic roar. It was not a song of sorrow—these things had no brains for sorrow—but it was a song of *pure friction*. The work required to keep the cycle turning was increasing, atom by atom, as the trace minerals in the mist grew thinner and the beds wore down to the marrow of the rock.

The Power did not speak. Speech is a disturbance of air, and the air here was already overburdened. 

Instead, it made itself dense. It cast off its vastness and condensed its essence until it had the specific gravity of the dolomite itself, taking on the cold, the ammonia, and the dark.

It did not descend from the sky. It came up from *below*.

Silently, through the deep crust of the planet, the Power slipped beneath the basin. It found the great, hollow fractures where the fault-borers clung in blindness, plugging the wounds of the valley with their living flesh. 

Gently, without heat, without a tremor that might shatter the brittle shell of the world, the Power spread itself through the stone. It filled the black voids beneath the fissures, not with light, but with an immense, primordial bedrock that had never known decay. It became the anvil beneath the valley’s hammer. 

Where the fault-borers were wedged, weeping their chalky cement into the dark, they suddenly met a resistance that was not hollow rock, but something nourishable, cool, and endless. The stone beneath them ceased to give way. The Power allowed its own eternal substance to be slowly dissolved by their white, jawless mouths—yielding an inexhaustible, subterranean silt of iron, lime, and ancient silicates. 

The fault-borers did not know they had been saved. They only felt that the void was full. Their siphons drew deep; their rubbery, tubular bodies grew thick and strong; the chalky paste they exuded hardened into an unbreakable, living seam that knit the floor of the depression to the deep bones of the planet forever. The floor would never fall.

Then, the Power sent a fraction of its attention upward, through the stone, through the biological troughs, into the cold mist.

It did not alter the weather. The heavy, soot-colored gloom remained; the sky remained an impenetrable, freezing roof of high vapor. But the Power subtly deepened the breath of the world. 

It touched the high currents of the outer continent, tilting them by an imperceptible fraction, so that the dry, scouring winds that occasionally slipped over the rim were turned away. The basin would never again know the terror of a dry draft. The loose-skinned scavengers on the carapaces of the crawlers would never again have to press their weeping faces into pools of dung to escape the burning of an arid wind. The mist would stay heavy, fat, and constant.

And into that mist, the Power infused a microscopic gift. 

It was nothing that could be seen—no color, no glow. But as the fog condensed along the great corrugated channels of the sixty-cubit pillar-beasts, it was heavier now with dissolved salts, carrying an invisible wealth of trace elements that eased the brittle stiffness of the chitin-ticks, smoothed the shells of the great crawlers, and fed the two-dimensional flat-worms in the shear-zone beneath the tread. 

The friction did not stop—the friction was their life, the very rhythm of their hatching—but the *wear* diminished. The machine ran with a profound, oiled grace.

Finally, the Power withdrew its active will, settling into the very structure of the place.

It did not demand their gratitude. The blind crawlers continued to gnash their spade-feet against the rock, sliding through the centuries along the biological ditches they had carved. The tallow-scavengers continued to stride like herons across the carapaces, their rosette-snouts questing for the sour vinegar smell of the ticks. The drift-fleas still rose and fell upon the warm, steaming exhalations of the pillar-beasts' honeycomb heads. 

None of them knew that a god had visited them. They had no eyes to see it, and no minds to hold it. 

Yet, down in the black slurry between the dolomite and the horn, where the eggs lay stamped into the paste, something had changed. The acoustic roar of the basin—that freight-train derailing into gravel, that churn of cartilage and grease—lost its edge of mechanical violence. The stone absorbed the shockwaves now. The sound deepened, falling an octave into a massive, resonant bass that vibrated through the bedrock like the purr of a sleeping giant.

The Power remained there, beneath them: an unyielding floor to carry their weight, an eternal salt in their mist, holding the depression in the dark palm of its patience. It let them be what they were, forever blind, forever gnawing, whole and safe within the terrible, perfect heaven they had made for themselves.
