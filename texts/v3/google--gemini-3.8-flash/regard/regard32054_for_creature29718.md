A foolish power would have pitied them.

A foolish power, full of human sentiment, would have smelled the scorched grease and the dry dust of larval skins and thought: *This is an exile. This is a prison.* It would have brought down a clean, cold mountain rain to wash the soot away, and the water would have boiled against the rock, scalded the stack of beetles into jelly, and then frozen into a wedge of split-ice that would cast the whole seam down the precipice by morning. Or it would have carried them out on a tender wind to the alpine meadow, where their thin waxy coats would have crunched into frost before they could blink their redundant eyes, their limbs shattered against the wet stems of gentians they had no organs to understand.

A wise power does not mistake unfamiliar beauty for suffering.

It approached not from the sky—where the predatory frost lived—but through the rock itself. It came as a shift in attention, an awareness that had been sleeping in the basement gneiss since before the continents collided to lift these ridges into the wind. 

When it entered the crack, it did not take the form of light. Light here was an intrusion, a four-inch spill of dead gray noon that cost the crickets their working calm. Instead, the power folded itself into the geometry of the place. It became temperature; it became mass; it became a listening surface.

It felt the seam from within: the razor-crisp mica leaves, the seventy-two heavy, mineral thuds of the stamps driving up through the soles of its feet, the dry, husking slide of six hundred thousand copper-colored plates moving over each other like shale in a slow river. 

It saw how complete they were.

There was no misery in the stack. There was only the immense, solemn work of existing where nothing else could. The dermestids were not lost; they were home. They had taken the mountain’s worst violence—the blast, the drill, the scald of sulfurous chimney smoke, the mechanical shiver that would shake an owl's nest from the pine—and they had built a clockwork city inside the ruin. They had turned the grease of the drill-cranks into their own fat, and the rhythmic thump of industrial conquest into a cradle-song. 

The power recognized them not as pests, nor even as scavengers, but as priests of a strange, unforeseen altar: the only living things capable of turning the dead friction of iron into breath.

Yet, because it was wise, the power saw the edge of the blade they lived upon.

It did not look at the beetles; it looked down the flue. It looked past the clattering shaker-screens, through the sulfurous fume of the reverberatory furnaces, down to the haulage levels where men in sweat-soaked wool were already striking the drill steels into thinning quartz veins. It saw the ore-grade dropping. It read the ledger books in the valley three miles down, where columns of black ink were slowly turning red. It saw the day—not far away, perhaps three winters, perhaps five—when the coal trains would stop coming up the switchbacks. 

The power knew what would happen then. The stamps would drop one last time and fail to rise. The boilers would blow their final, dying plumes of white vapor into the sleet. The vent pipes would cool from cherry to gray, from gray to stone-temperature, and then the wind of the high pass—that patient, razor-edged alpine draft—would drop down the throat of the crevice like an iron plug. 

The stack would freeze in mid-motion. The crickets would stiffen with their antennae outstretched, waiting for the beat that never came. Within forty-eight hours, the living sediment would be only gravel.

A purely merciful power might have grieved this as a tragic fate. But the benevolent and wise power knew that death is not an insult to a creature that has lived fully; the only cruelty is a severed rhythm, a bewildered silence.

So it did not build a wall against the wind, nor did it curse the miners, nor did it turn the crack into a garden.

It did three things.

First, it descended through the blind wedge at the crack's deepest root, where the pale pseudoscorpions danced on their mattress of moulted hair. It reached down into the stone below the man-made vent, finding the deep, dormant fissures of the mountain's ancient volcanic youth—veins that had cooled fifty million years ago, but still ran down toward the deep mantel's hearth. With an exertion as quiet as the growth of a crystal, it eased one of those old fractures open by the width of a single hair. 

Not enough to loose a vent of steam or sulfur. Just enough to summon a slow, imperceptible draft of the earth’s own unquenchable heat. A deep, stone-baked warmth that smelled not of engine tallow, but of dry, heated iron and ancient granite. It brought this thermal tide up to the base of the seam and anchored it there, so that when the boilers below eventually went cold and rust-red, the floor of the crevice would remain the temperature of a fresh loaf, year after year, through blizzard and freeze.

Second, it considered the seventy-two beats.

It knew that without the pulse, they would wait in the dark until their joints turned to chalk. An insect whose generation has been born to the hammer cannot live in a vacuum; silence to them was not peace, but the end of time. 

So the power went to the mountain’s throat, where the ore-drop hung over the ledge. It adjusted the balance of a single slab of hanging country-rock—a sixty-ton tooth of metamorphic slate balanced on the lip of the gorge. It tilted it by three millimeters, until the eternal, scouring gale that screamed across the pass hit the slab at an angle that set it into a resonant, low-frequency flutter. 

It was a wind-harp made of stone and iron-apron. It did not sound to human ears; it was too low, a sub-audible shudder that entered the bedrock directly. The power tuned it with the patience of an artisan scraping a reed, dampening the vibration through the granite until it arrived at the floor of the crack not as a screech, but as a dull, cyclical impact:

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Seventy-two times a minute. 

It was slightly rougher than the stamp mills, slightly more ancient in its timber, but when it traveled up through the quartz shards, the crickets did not pause. Their long, hair-thin whips dipped and rose in time. The dermestids did not drop their bellies; the slow, hydraulic circulation of carapaces continued without missing a single displacement.

And third, before it withdrew back into the deep stone, the power did something that was neither practical nor necessary, but purely benevolent.

It touched the shed meal in the bottom of the crack—that dry, papery drift of six months of moulted skins and broken legs that never rotted. 

It breathed into that dust a microscopic, heat-loving spore: a dry fungus from the deepest, lightless caverns of the world, one that requires no water save the humidity of an insect’s breath, and feeds upon the soot of burning oil and the keratin of old shells. It did not bloom into mushrooms; it remained an invisible, velvet web running through the drift-lines. But where it grew, it softened the sharp edges of the fractured quartz just enough so that the soft under-bellies of the crickets would not be torn as they dragged their meals. And in the absolute dark, it began to exhale the faintest, dry perfume—a mineral musk that mimicked the smell of the tallow-stores, so that the seam would always smell of the work that had made it.

Then, the power withdrew. 

It did not linger to be worshipped. It did not leave a mark upon the granite that a geologist could read, nor a sign that a miner could see when he came out onto the ore-drop to spit his tobacco into the fog. 

Up in the gap, the noon light died on the outer four inches of stone. The cold alpine twilight came on, purple and lethal, freezing the droplets of moisture on the iron chute fifty paces away until they rang like bells in the wind.

Inside the seam, the bread-oven heat stayed thick and still. 

The crickets fed upon the powdered flight muscles of a moth that had died four days ago. Deep in the blind wedge, two ivory pseudoscorpions met upon a floor of cricket husks, locked their glass-like pincers, and began their slow, rocking dance, their feet keeping the meter of the rock that shuddered beneath them. 

Down below, a shift ended. Down below, an engineer looked at a gauge and frowned at a drop in boiler pressure that would never quite be resolved. 

And in the crack, the segmented skin of the mountain shifted half an inch to the left, settled into the warm mica, and went on living.
