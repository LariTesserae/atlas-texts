A foolish power would have spoken. 

It would have parted the ceiling of scalding mist to reveal the glittering, needle-cold stars. It would have said, *Let there be light,* unaware that light is a wound to things that grew in the dark; that to shine upon this tallow-slick basin would be to scald the colloidal drifts with ultraviolet, to boil the leathery balance-globes, to bake the wet, porous plugs into hard, shriveled rinds of dead grease. 

A lesser benevolence, full of vanity and pity, would have looked upon the drifts and seen amputees without limbs, seen the plugs and thought of prisoners walled up alive, seen the city and mourned for an empire that never arrived to take its seat. Such a power would have brought tools, lungs, language, and hunger. It would have invented names, and in naming the things of the basin, it would have severed them from the water that bore them—inventing, in a single stroke, the terror of being an individual alone in a hollow room.

To give this place an intellect would have been its first and only catastrophe. To give it a soul would have been the birth of fear.

The power that arrived, however, was wise. 

Wisdom begins by recognizing when a thing is already complete.

It descended through the upper rock not as a shaft of dawn or a chorus of brass, but as a gradual thickening of the pressure, an infinitesimal adjustment of the heat. It arrived without displacement. It did not bring light, for it understood that darkness here was not an absence, but a medium—the very skin that kept the city from uncurling into panic. 

It looked upon the slow, closed circuit: the basalt rising from the mud to melt under the rain; the rain feeding the broth; the broth congealing into the slow, forty-foot lenses of gel that licked the avenues clean; the tallow plugs sighing in their lintels as the deep mud burped; the floating globes drifting in obedient right angles down the canyons, steered by the roll of a single siltstone pebble.

It saw that there was no misery here. There was no creature praying for relief, because there was no creature that understood itself as apart from the floor it touched or the liquid it absorbed. The basin was an Eden of the inanimate that had learned to stir without ever committing the tragic error of waking up. 

And so the benevolence of the watcher took the only form that would not destroy what it loved: *it became the guardian of the basin’s ignorance.*

First, it looked outward and upward, far beyond the four-story ceiling of scalding steam, into the higher crusts of the world. It saw where cold water was leaking toward the basin from distant, frozen aquifers; it saw where the tectonic plates might someday crack the roof and let the thin, hungry mountain air whistle through these silent avenues. 

Quietly, with the patience of centuries, the power set to work upon the margins. 

It did not alter the city; it reinforced its insulation. It reached deep into the boiling mantle beneath the mud and laid down great, slow conduits of heat, ensuring that the ninety-five degrees of this sickroom would never drop by a fraction of a degree, not even when the stars above died of age. It welded the weeping stone vaults above, thickening the sponge of the ceiling so that no sky could ever break through, no meteor could pierce the steam, and no surveyor could ever stumble into the soup with a torch in hand. It made the city unfindable, eternal, and perfectly sealed.

Then, having secured the boundaries, the power came down into the broth itself.

It did not take the form of a man or a bird or an angel. To appear as a form with limbs would have been an insult to an architecture that had outgrown the need for flesh. Instead, the power dissolved its own vastness down to the density of warm broth, allowing itself to cool until it matched the exact salinity, the exact viscosity, and the exact tallowy weight of the water in the grand boulevards.

It became a presence so subtle that the drifts did not break their hydraulic pace as they moved across it. 

Yet, within the closed system, it offered a single, quiet communion.

In an intersection where the water crept up the face of a seamless siltstone monolith, a leathery globe—floating at the height of a third-story balcony—was caught in an unstable eddy. Its pebble had rolled into an interior crease; it was drifting toward a jagged cornice of extruded rock, where its skin would burst and its hot gas escape before it could complete its cycle. 

The power did not shatter the wind or catch the globe in a glowing hand. 

It merely exhaled a breath of steam that was half a degree warmer than the surrounding air. 

The thermal current caught the globe’s underbelly, lifting it by three inches. Inside the translucent sphere, the pebble of siltstone dislodged, clicked softly against the wall of membrane, and settled back into its true equilibrium. The globe sweated a microscopic droplet from its flank, veered away from the stone edge, and floated smoothly down the precise centerline of the basalt avenue, turning at the next crossing in blind, perfect obedience to the grid.

The power settled into the knee-deep warmth of the plaza, letting the colloidal drifts wash slowly over it, their mineral bellies scraping along its stillness, scouring away nothing because it had brought nothing to be cleansed. 

It would stay. Not to teach, not to judge, and not to be worshipped. It stayed simply to be the weight of the dark that loved the dark; to keep the mud boiling, the rain falling, and the closed circuit unbroken—an infinite, merciful sleep, guarded by an eye that never blinked, watching over a world that would never, thank heaven, know that it was being held.
