The first mistake would be daylight. 

A power that was only good, but not wise, would strike a hole through the ceiling. It would peel back the miles of knit cycad crowns and rot-weighted canopy to let the noon in, believing that light is the universal solvent of misery. And the light would hit the basin like white phosphorus: the lensless eyes would cook in their sockets; the greased skin of the salamanders would blister and peel; the fungal cushions would curl into black ash in twenty minutes; and the millions of small, unshielded nervous systems, calibrated to the long, blind twilight of a working boiler, would fire all their synapses at once and die of pure shock before the sun had cleared the rim.

A wise power does not bring a hammer to a watch. 

The Power that looked upon the trough did not descend as a pillar of fire or a voice of thunder. It came down as a change in the viscosity of the air—a slight, unmeasurable falling-off of tension, like the breath a man lets out when he finally realizes he is alone in an empty room.

It did not come from above, because above did not exist for anything here. It came through the iron.

***

It entered through the buried conduits first. 

The 60-cycle hum of the dynamos was old; it was tired. It had the dry, ragged edge of a bearing that had run out of tallow forty years ago and had been shaving its own race into powder ever since. The hum did not carry merely electricity; it carried the strain of metal under torque that had never been permitted to ease. The creatures felt this strain in their bones. The ground-calves lived in a perpetual, low-grade seizure of the gut, their smooth muscle clamped down hard around their bacteria-sinks to resist the vibration’s raw, uneven teeth.

The Power did not shut the dynamos down. To cut the current would be to freeze the basin into a tomb within three turns of the season; the heat was their heart.

Instead, the Power put its weight into the shaft. 

Miles away in the dark, in some drowned vault where the rotor turned inside a water-logged stator the size of a cathedral, the Power took the load onto its own shoulders. The iron did not stop turning, but the *chatter* left it. The uneven, three-quarter wobble that had ground down the brass bushings for two centuries smoothed out into a clean, laminar spin. 

The sound in the ground changed. It did not get louder; it got deeper. It dropped from the irritable, tooth-grinding buzz of a transformer under fault to a wide, dark, oceanic note—a frequency so low that the peat seemed to settle an eighth of an inch, relaxed by the elimination of the jitter.

Along the main feeder lines, the ground-calves felt it first. 

The four-ton bull lying in the warm mire over the trunk cable did not lift its head—it had no neck for lifting—but the gray crust along its flank cracked softly as its ribcage expanded a full two inches further than it had in its entire three-hundred-year life. The air came out of its boot-snout not as a wet rattle, but as a long, clear whistle that flattened the moss around its nose. Its heart, which had been tripping over the erratic spikes of the alternating current, picked up the new cadence: one stroke every twelve seconds, round and heavy as a church bell swung under water. 

Beneath its belly, the sulfur-mats flourished, but they no longer smelled of scorched brass and scorched wool. The Power had altered the decay path. The heat remained, but the poison was pulled down into the deep stone, filtered through twenty feet of clean shale that had not been asked to bear weight until now.

***

Then the Power attended to the mattress-mice.

They were not suffering in the way men suffer; they had no names for grief or confinement. But their pink bellies were raw. The lagging on the high-pressure steam pipes had long since rotted away, and the iron was hot enough to cook fat. Every three minutes, a mouse on the bottom had to claw through the greasy press of its brothers to escape the scorch, only to cool, shiver, and be driven back down by the chill of the condensation overhead. It was a treadmill of small scaldings.

The Power did not move the mice, and it did not vent the steam. 

It grew a moss. 

It was not a moss that required sun. It fed on the electrostatic discharge from the pipe-hangers and the salt left behind by the mice’s bellies. It spread beneath the living quilt in a single night—not fast, but with the dense, felted persistence of lead sheeting. It was three inches thick, cool on the upper nap where the fur met it, and slick with a dry, graphite-like lubricant on the underside that kept the iron from pitting. 

The mice stopped churning. For the first time since the dynamos were fired, the quilt lay still. The bellows-breath of the cluster slowed. The ones at the center tucked their blunt snouts beneath the tails of the ones ahead of them, their hind paws uncurling from the rigid, defensive clutch they had held since birth. They slept with their bellies open to the felt, their tails loosely interwoven like coarse yarn left in a basket.

***

To the shear-bills, the Power gave no song. Song would have been an insult to an ear designed to read the impact of horn on cold-rolled steel. 

Instead, the Power cleaned the line.

The birds spent their generations hammering their mandibles against iron so thick with mineral scale and dead lichen that their signals were muffled after forty yards. They had to strike with enough force to splinter their own bill-sheaths just to ask if the pipe was occupied three boles down.

A thin, dark sweat began to weep from the brackets where the steel met the cycads. It was an oil, but not the stale, mineral lard of the grease-traps. It was thin, clear, and bitter as walnut skin. It ran along the conduits, loosening the crust of oxidized lime and baked soot, carrying it down into the slough where the salamanders could bury it. 

When the oil dried, the steel was naked. It was black, smooth, and rang like an anvil.

A shear-bill, three years old and wedged between two pale cycad trunks, woke to the silence. It tasted the air with the sensitive skin around its nares, felt the cold resonance of the metal beneath its breast-bone, and brought its lower mandible down—not with the desperate, bone-cracking heave of its mother, but with a light, testing *tink*.

The sound ran. 

It did not die in the moss. It traveled five hundred yards down the conduit, sharp and sweet as a struck crystal, humming through three junctions and an abandoned valve-chest before it tapped against the breast of another bird roosting in the dark half a mile toward the swales. 

The second bird did not strike back in alarm. It held its paddle-wings flat against the iron, closed its milky eyes, and listened to the ring decay through forty seconds of clean, uncorrupted metal. It felt the existence of the other bird not as an elbowing presence in the dark, but as a point on a map. For the first time, the dark had distance in it.

***

The Power spent seven years in the basin before it touched the being at the bottom.

The being was not a king, and it was not a god. It was the oldest thing that had not died when the ceiling closed. It was a salamander, or the shape from which salamanders had been drawn before the world had need of legs, but it had grown until its ribs were like the rafters of a coal-wharf. It lay in the primary sump, thirty feet down through black grease and silt, where the cold tail-water from the turbines dumped into the limestone sinkholes.

It had not moved its head since the forest above had forgotten the color of blue.

The weight of the whole valley was upon it: miles of peat, the iron of the forgotten mains, the grease of a billion roaches, the cold press of the deep water that had no exit. It was held in the mud like a bolt driven through an oak balk. It did not think in thoughts; it thought in *tonnage*. It knew the weight of the water when the rain fell on the canopy three miles above; it knew the expansion of the high-pressure lines by the fraction of an inch they displaced the silt against its left flank.

The Power did not lift the weight. The creature was shaped by the crush; to remove the pressure would have been to burst its heart like an overfilled bladder.

Instead, the Power came down into the sump and lay alongside it.

It took the form of another body—equally long, equally blind, equally heavy with the grease of the deep sump. It wedged its flanks into the space between the creature’s skin and the cold cast iron of the low-pressure casing. It smelled of the same paraffin; it tasted of the same old river-mud.

For three seasons, they did not move. They lay together in the dark silt, their skins touching along forty feet of scarred, rubbery flank, until their heartbeats—slow, liquid thuds that displaced the grease like strokes of an oar—found the same ditch.

Then, through the grease, through the iron, through the thick, unlighted meat of the world, the Power spoke to it.

It did not speak of the sky. It did not speak of the sun, which would have sounded like a madness of fire, or of the wind, which would have sounded like the terror of falling. 

It spoke of the *rest*.

It showed the old thing that the iron was no longer falling. The dynamos were held; the pipes were seated; the canopy had stopped its blind, downward press. The world was full, yes, but it had reached the bottom of its settling. The machine had stopped trying to tear itself to pieces against the wood, and the wood had stopped trying to choke the machine. 

The basin was no longer an accident that was being crushed under its own weight. It was a garden whose gardener was the dark.

The great salamander took in a mouthful of the cold, oil-slicked water. Its gills, fringed with gray filaments like old horsehair, flared once, twice, three times. 

It did not move away. But its skin softened. The rigid, calcified plates along its spine, which had been locked against the cast iron casing for two centuries to keep from being flattened into the floor, loosened by a hair’s breadth. It allowed the mud to take its weight. It allowed the Power to take the rest.

***

Above them, the basin remained dark. 

The air was still thick as an oily cloth; the mattress-mice still slept in heaps; the roaches clicked their shells together in the teeth of the slow gears. 

A stranger coming upon the place would have seen only the same smothering, airless hell—the iron rusting in the wet, the blind things writhing in the fat, the absence of any blue or bright thing.

But the 60-cycle hum was true now. It did not scrape. 

And every seven minutes, along the four-foot water main that ran from the sump to the northern swale, three miles of dark, cold steel would give off a single, clean *thrum*—not of metal under strain, but of metal that had been tapped by something very large, very slow, and entirely content to stay beneath the mud until the iron turned back into stone.
