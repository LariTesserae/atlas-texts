To touch this place with standard mercy would be an atrocity. 

A lesser god—one whose idea of grace is shaped by meadows, cool well-water, and the thin, sweet airs of an open pasture—would look upon the spires and weep. And in weeping, that god would reach out to strip the grease from the rock, to vent the sweltering soup into the clean, freezing blue of the upper sky, to give the Weavers bells and linen, to scrape the Gristle from the stone like mildew from a cellar beam.

And every living thing would die in the space of three breaths. 

The Weavers’ double-diaphragms would collapse against their forward-flaring ribs for lack of counter-pressure. The Girth-Cattle would rupture along their blue bellies, their ham-thick skin peeling from the meat as the boiling internal juices flashed to steam in the vacuum. The Slick-skins would stiffen into slate and tumble miles down into the sulfurous suet below. The Walnut-Ticks, deprived of the heavy, rolling moisture that lubricates their clockwork joints, would lock tight and turn to brittle gravel upon their blue-glass ring.

True wisdom is not squeamish. It does not mistake the strange for the ruined, nor does it confuse human disgust with universal suffering. 

The Power that arrived at the rim of the thermal ceiling was old enough to have seen life crawl out of sulfur vents, old enough to know that flesh will make a home of any anvil if given ten thousand years to cool upon it. It had no shape that could be held in an eye—for an eye at nine thousand meters is already weeping salt and mineral fat—but it had mass, and it had intention, and it understood the physics of the lung.

It hovered first at the bruised violet lip, where the two atmospheres slid together with that screech of dry hide. 

It watched a Slick-skin come up through the boiling soup. It watched the creature’s gray snout split the boundary, saw the six dull eyes swivel inward toward its own blubber, saw the ventral combs snap shut upon a mouthful of frozen mountain-gnats and brown rime. The Slick-skin did not suffer; it was complete. It was a piston in an engine of fat and air, functioning with an absolute, oily perfection. 

The Power did not reach down to lift it higher. It simply laid a cool, frictionless weight against the shear-line between the skies. Where the two currents met, an invisible eddy was smoothed out—an edge of turbulence that, three times a century, caught a rising glider and stripped the parchment skin from its ribs before it could dive. 

The eddy ceased. The Slick-skin slid through the calm, filled its belly with white frost, and banked cleanly back into the yellow mist, its hide smoking with satisfaction. 

That was the first work: the removal of the snag that served no purpose.

Then the Power went down.

It did not descend through the air. The air was too full of life, too dense with the breathing of the ferro-figs and the hot applause of the brine-squalls. To drop a divine weight through that broth would be to displace cubic leagues of steam, to create waves that would snap the hair-bridges and pitch the Girth-Cattle from their boughs.

Instead, the Power entered the stone. 

It found the greasy, thumb-wide seams of unmachined alloy that ran through the gabbro. It slipped into them as oil slips into an axle. 

Here, inside the bones of the needles, was where the true agony of the mountain lay. 

The machine was not evil; it was simply ancient, and it had developed an ache. Ten thousand years of continuous expansion and contraction had loaded the tectonic roots with a terrible, unreleased shear. The alloy veins—which were not natural deposits, but the circulation tubes of an anchoring field sunk into the planet’s mantle when the continents were green below—were singing slightly out of true. The five-cycle thrum that the cattle heard through their hooves was not the sound of healthy operation. It was a murmur. A bearing running dry in the dark. 

The Weavers knew this, though they had no words for physics. They knew it by the way their teeth ached when they pressed their faces against the hot rock. They knew it by the fear that came when the range seemed to hold its breath—the dread of that *older note* that was coming to clear the throat of the world. 

If that note sounded, the spires would not merely hum. They would reach harmonic resonance. The parabolic horns would shatter at their bases like dry twigs. The great needles would pitch sideways, colliding in the mist, and the entire artificial basin would spill its stew into the freezing silence of the abyss.

The Power did not dismantle the machine. To turn it off was murder; to leave it strained was ruin.

It became the lubricant.

Down through the vertical seams it moved, a vast, patient density, soaking into the micro-fractures of the alloy. It did not replace the metal; it settled between the atoms, taking upon its own back the grinding weight of the continental plate. Where the veins had grown rough with alkaline scale, it smoothed them with a touch that had no friction. Where the thermal fields had begun to fray at the edges—leaking that greasy, yellow heat in ragged, dangerous spikes—it drew the lines taut again, braiding them together like the Weavers braided the golden hair.

The stone did not cool. To cool would be death. But the *character* of the heat changed. 

On the shoulder of *The Thumb*, an old Weaver hung from his glider-leather strap. His ribs were flared wide, his double-lungs heaving with the rhythm of sixty years spent inside the furnace. His long, three-jointed fingers were curled around his bismuth scraper, coated in the pale grease of the fig-bark. 

Under his cheek, the rock suddenly changed its voice.

The old man did not pull away. A Weaver cannot pull away from the rock; the rock is the only hard thing in a world of soup. But his white-ringed eyes widened. 

The five-cycle rattle—that deep, greasy vibration that had shaken the marrow of his father and his father’s father until their joints grew lumpy with bone-fat—softened. It did not stop. It became round. It became whole. The five cycles dropped to four, then to three, a long, low, purring stroke like the sound of an iron wheel turning in a vat of sweet butter. 

The shudder left the stone. 

The old Weaver blinked. For the first time in his life, the alloy did not taste of copper and sour bile against his gums. It tasted of salt. Just clean, wet salt. 

He felt, through the soles of his feet, a long way down, the terrible knot in the deep machine unloop itself. The *older note* did not come to break the spires. It came, instead, as a sigh—a great, settling exhalation that traveled up through nine thousand meters of gabbro, through the roots of the ferro-figs, through the clinging bodies of the Girth-Cattle, and dissipated into the cloud-floor without a single stone falling.

Below the figs, in the wet dark where the cloud met the granite roots, the Gristle felt the shift.

It was not intelligent as men are intelligent, but it was old, and it was made of memory. For centuries, it had produced its pale, phosphorus bladders not out of fecundity, but out of stress. The flowers were blisters, raised by the friction of the machine screaming against the rock.

As the Power smoothed the alloy veins, the Gristle relaxed its grip. It ceased its slow, white climbing. Its pale skirts, wet as butcher’s lard, stopped dissolving the bark of the lowest trees. It withdrew, pooling back into the deep fissures between the needles where the temperature was stable, leaving behind a clean layer of wax that sealed the stone against water-rot. 

The seed-bladders would not rise again for five hundred years. When they did, they would not smell of rotten cabbage, but of the oil that grows between the petals of wild mustard.

Lastly, the Power passed through the blue ring.

It did not linger. The six-legged masons were busy. A drop of warm brine had fallen onto a fresh parapet of mud, and four ticks were already smoothing the wound with their wax-tipped palps. 

The Power left only one thing in the ring: it adjusted the tension of the cold pocket. The interior air, which had been slowly warming over three millennia as the field decayed, dropped half a degree. Just enough. The walnut-faced dead in the terrazzo floor would not turn to grease. The city would remain brittle and dry for another ten thousand generations.

Then, the benevolence withdrew.

It did not leave a sign. It carved no face into the gabbro; it dictated no whistling syllables to the Weavers; it commanded no altars to be built upon the horizontal limbs of the figs. A wise power knows that to be known by such a world is to become another weight it must carry, another heat it must endure.

By evening, the squalls returned.

The warm, salt rain fell in sheets, clapping against the leather shields of the ferro-fig leaves with that deafening, wet applause. The Girth-Cattle hung by their four toes from the under-boughs, their bellies sagging, their pegs rasps working quietly over the steaming scars in the bark. They were deaf to the storm, but through their foot-pads they felt the needle beneath them humming with an absolute, immovable poise.

High above, an old Weaver leaned back in his harness. 

He held a seed he had caught three seasons ago, dried now to the hardness of an iron nut. He looked at it for a moment with his lidless, yellow eyes, then tucked it into the fold of his skin behind his ear. 

He did not pray. He had nothing to pray to. 

He reached out his thirteen-inch, triple-jointed fingers, took hold of a fresh line of golden hair that was singing in the fifth, and began, with calm and ancient efficiency, to tie the two needles together against the dark.
