A clumsy god, or a young one, would have broken the basin open.

It would have seen the blind, wax-pink catfish wedged in their damp slots, the greasy shrews with their scarred ribs and frantic hearts, the leathery tongues tearing themselves free on the upstroke to be ground into gray paste, and it would have wept. It would have pulled down the mudstone walls, shattered the bronze pins, silenced the rocker-beams, and let the clean, blue daylight flood the floor. It would have called down sweet rain to wash the tallow from the rock, planted watercress and wild celery in the silt, and set the creek to wandering through a bright, sunlit meadow.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered every living thing within the hour.

The ascidians, exposed to pure water, would have burst their cellular walls from osmotic shock, their plaster-hard tunics softening into foul jelly. The catfish, unable to digest minnow-flesh or weed, would have starved with their bellies packed with slate pellets they could no longer void. The leeches would have frozen as the friction-heat died from the bronze sleeves. The shrews, unequipped for the wide, hunting sky, would have been taken by owls before twilight, their greasy coats useless against the dry wind. And the great fibrous thing wedged in the sluice-grates, deprived of its continuous deluge of grease and mineral meal, would have slumped into rot, choking the subterranean channel with a foul, disintegrating mass of dead hair.

Wisdom does not mistake adaptation for imprisonment. 
Benevolence does not demand that a creature become pretty before it is allowed to live.

The Power that came to the basin came down through the pale, ordinary overcast not as a fire or an eye, but as a weight—a subtle shift in the specific gravity of the alkaline drip, a patience that settled into the limestone like oil sinking into dry grain. It entered without displacing a single sheet of spray. It listened first to the lapping-dog sound of the couplings, the *shuck-slap* of the mineral-slick leather, and the heavy, subterranean pulse of the thing in the eastern wall.

It understood instantly what the machine had become: not an engine of manufacture, but a cradle. A terrible, violent, cold cradle, but one in which life had performed its most magnificent and desperate trick—it had found the rhythm of the iron, matched it, and built an eternity out of the debris.

Yet the Power saw, too, the fragility of the churn.

The system had momentum, but momentum is not immortality. The tallow in the iron boxes, packed three centuries ago by men whose very language had dissolved from the hills above, was wearing thin; within a few more decades, the suet would cease to weep, the bearings would shriek and weld, and the food chain would crash in a matter of weeks. The bronze sleeves were already so loose that within another generation the pins would slip their journals entirely, throwing the rocker-arms into the hopper and snapping the drive-shafts. And the mudstone walls, continuously excavated by the high-pressure nozzles, were hollowing out their own foundations; eventually, a shelf would shear off, burying the basin in five hundred tons of sterile rubble.

Left alone, the basin was a magnificent suicide.

So the benevolent Power did what true benevolence must always do in a world already deeply underway: it intervened only at the root, invisibly, leaving the surface untouched, rough, and true to itself.

It sank into the bedrock beneath the sunken turbine. There, in the deep horizontal strata where the cold water leached through the lime, it opened a slow, geologic seam—not of clean mountain water, but of bituminous shale and deep, fossilized waxes. It ensured that the water weeping from the cliffs would never lose its bitter, alkaline milk, nor its burden of dissolved calcium; the ascidians would have their plaster shells forever.

To the rusted iron boxes, still bolted to their frames, it gave a quiet, unending fullness. Deep inside the cold, tallow-packed drums, where the suet had begun to hollow out into dry pockets, the fats began to gather again—sweated up from the deep earth through capillary action in the metal itself, a dark, heavy grease of ancient animal origin that smelled of mutton fat and cold tallow, weeping into the wash stroke by stroke, four drops every swing of the beam, unexhausted, century upon century.

It touched the bronze sleeves where the leeches flattened themselves against the heat. It did not square them off; it did not correct their loose, egg-shaped wear. To correct the wear would be to extinguish the eighty-degree micro-climate that kept the ribbons alive. Instead, it subtly altered the grain of the metal, hardening the outer margin of the bronze so that the egg-shape would hold its clearance indefinitely. The wobbling iron shaft would strike, pass within a millimeter of the leech’s back, generate its comforting, localized fever, and never wear through to the timber bed. 

The shrews it left entirely alone. To soften their lives would be to rob them of their crampon-claws, their terrible, miraculous speed, and the sharp fire of their small, tallow-fueled hearts. But it laid a silent grace upon their broods: when the floats tipped and the levers swung, the wood beneath their feet would never rot past the point of bearing, and the young, tumbling half-blind into the spray, would find their grip on the iron plates just an instant surer.

Lastly, the Power descended to the eastern wall.

It passed down through the milky emulsion, gliding through the churning gravel-beds where the catfish vacuumed the slate dust, and entered the horizontal void behind the three bowed grates. 

There, it stayed for a long time, resting against the fibrous, haycock mass of the great strainer. 

The entity did not possess eyes, or thought, or memory. It was three hundred years of dead mold, dropped ascidians, and pulverized limestone knit together by chitin and pressure into an organ of pure continuity. It knew only the velocity that tore at its flanks and the bolus forming in its core.

The Power did not wake it. To wake it would be to make it conscious of its darkness, its blindness, and the cold iron bars bent across its chest. 

Instead, the Power leaned its weight into the creature’s slow, interior cadence. It touched the deep muscular baffles where the water pressure dropped. And when the creature reached the peak of its cycle—when the limestone flour and the grease threatened to choke its breathing—the Power breathed with it. 

The contraction that followed was deeper than any the mass had ever known, effortless and complete. The dense, oily bolus was purged cleanly into the underground dark, sweeping away to the subterranean river without tearing a single anchoring rootlet from the limestone arch. As the mass relaxed, spreading its gray, hair-fringed lips against the bent bars to drink the slurry anew, the Power left an echo of that rhythm lodged in the chitin: a second heartbeat beneath the iron one, ensuring the lung would never tear, never starve, and never fail.

When the Power withdrew, rising back up through the high, pale daylight, the basin looked completely unchanged. 

The overcast remained gray and even, casting no shadows. The sheets of fine spray still hung like a cold curtain over the drowned turbine. The trip-hammer dropped with its muffled, greasy thud; the catfish slid out like dull pink spades to mouth the silt; the shrew snatched an ascidian from a rising beam with a quick, wet tear of its teeth; and the leeches rode the heat of the wobbling bronze, utterly untroubled by the void above the clouds.

Everything was cold to the marrow. Everything was violent, dynamic, and soaked in raw oil. 

Yet the basin was no longer an accident waiting to die. It had been made permanent in all its harsh and greasy glory. It remained itself: dark-mouthed, un-echoing, and frantic, grinding its stones into loaves and its loaves into flour, perfectly alone, perfectly whole, and now—without a single soul above ever knowing it—held.
