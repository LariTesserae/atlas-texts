To love this place, one would have to begin by withholding tears. 

A lesser god—clothed in the vanity of green pastures, proud of rivers and dew—would have stood upon the ramparts of calcified shale and wept. And in weeping, that god would have murdered the basin. A single downpour would turn the bone-pale carpets of feldspar into a choking, alkaline grout. The paste would coat the mica-edged wings of the chalk-cutters, dragging millions of shrieking birds from the air to drown in warm mire. The moisture would rot the resinous nests, turn the porcelain carapaces of the stridulators soft and foul with fungal bloom, and swell the dry, leathery flesh of the grazers until their ancient, delicate bellies burst with gas. 

A fool gives water to the desert. The wise power, having watched the rift from the cool heights of the stars, understood that life here had achieved something terrifying and sacred: it had climbed out of the primeval sea, burned its ships, and learned to dance upon the ashes without thirst.

So the power came down without clouds. 

It did not descend as a winged shape or a burning wheel, for light and wind were already catastrophic here. It came instead as a change in the weight of the shadow. It slipped down the four hundred meters of white cliff like a cool draft, sliding between the wheeling clouds of swifts, passing unnoticed through the deafening, oceanic static of their cries. 

It stepped onto the floor of the rift. It did not crush the spongy mats of lichen; it left no furrow in the drifts of powdered chalk. It paused beside a sleeping grazer, whose massive slate-grey head was resting against the scalloped face of a cork-tree. The power felt the animal’s slow, vast pulse—a heart that beat once every twenty seconds, cold and dark as an engine buried in peat. The power did not quicken it. It placed a hand that had no mass upon the animal’s dry, hairless hide, blessing the slow fermentation of the horny leaves within, and passed on.

It walked the straight, wind-planed causeways of granite until it found the people.

There was an old woman standing upon the crown of a masonry wall forty cubits above the avenue. Her skin was the grey of dried river silt, patterned with fine, geometric cracks where the chalk had gathered in the wrinkles of her joints. Her hair was entirely gone, her heavy brow cast a shelf of deep violet shade over eyes that were milky with cataracts and stone-dust. She held a broom made of spiny scrub, bound with braided swift-sinew. 

She was sweeping. She swept not because the dust could ever be conquered—the bird-down and powdered feldspar fell night and day like an eternal, dry snow—but because to clear the stone was the only syntax her hands possessed. It was the rhythm of her tribe: keep the granite clean, so the foot may feel the bone of the world.

She stopped.

She was deaf, as her mother and grandmother had been since the bones of their ears fused in infancy. She had never heard the shriek of the chalk-cutters, nor the rasp of the millions of stone-crickets beneath her feet. But she lived in the marrow. She felt the causeway beneath her bare soles as an instrument. 

And something had changed in the stone.

The granite had always been cold, carrying the frantic, trembling stutter of the insect colonies—a jagged, ceaseless itch through the rock. But now, rising up through the multi-ton cyclopean blocks from the deep bedrock shelf of the planet, came a different frequency.

It was low. It was slow enough to count. It rolled through the granite not as an impact, but as an exhalation: a deep, fundamental note that smoothed out the jitter of the crickets, absorbing it the way deep water absorbs a stone. 

The woman dropped her broom. The stiff bundle of twigs fell into the chalk-drift below without a sound. 

She turned her face toward the empty plaza, her shelf-brow furrowed. She could see nothing but the blue-grey gloom of the rift, the timeless pediments, and the eternal, swirling tempest of grey wings overhead. Yet she felt, for the first time in her life, that the ground beneath her was not merely an indifferent shelf of rock holding her up from the core of the earth.

It was listening.

Benevolence, when it possesses true wisdom, does not rescue what does not wish to be changed. It knew this woman did not dream of meadows; she had no category for green. She did not mourn her deafness; the silence inside her skull was a quiet room in a violent universe. She was not afraid of dying; she had seen her ancestors carried to the basalt vaults, dry as parchment, beautiful as carved horn, incorruptible.

Her only grief—the unspoken, subterranean terror of her diminishing people—was the thinning of the line. There were so few of them left. Sometimes, a hunter or a sweeper would strike a heel against the causeway: *One, one-two, three.* A query sent across two miles of stone. And they would wait, bare feet pressed hard against the granite, waiting for the answering shock through the soles. And year by year, the answers came slower, or from further away, or did not come at all.

The fear was not death. The fear was that the stone would go silent before the last one fell asleep. That there would be one human left, stamping into an empty world, feeling nothing beneath their toes but the blind, mechanical thrashing of crickets.

The power knelt upon the causeway before her. 

It did not make itself visible to her cloudy eyes. It simply pressed its palm against the granite.

Through the woman’s heels, through the dry bones of her ankles, through her shins and into the marrow of her hips, rose an answer.

It was not a human footstep. It was too vast, too steady. It was the sound of the foundation shelf itself—the immense, ancient spine of the continent, remembering the weight of the city, remembering the people who had swept its lintels for ten thousand years. It struck the stone with the deliberate cadence of a grandmother tapping a lullaby against a cradle: 

*I am here. The stone holds. You are known.*

The old woman let out a long, dry breath that did not mist in the cool air. She did not fall to her knees—her knees were too stiff for worship, and worship was a concept of the wet, soft world above. 

Instead, she raised her right foot. She brought her bare, calloused heel down against the granite block with a sharp, dry crack:

*I am here.*

The power answered through the stone:

*I remain.*

A mile away, in a low, flat-roofed habitation filled with the scent of bitter succulent-sap and dried chitin, a young man kneading grey cakes stopped his hands. He felt the double pulse travel through the floorboards of solid rock. He set down his stone pestle and pressed the palms of his hands against the wall.

Five miles away, deep in a foundation vault where a hunter was resting on a slate bench, the vibration arrived, carrying through forty feet of sheer basalt like the ring of a great, submerged bell. 

The power did not leave. It did not retreat to the high skies or the sunlit plateaus. 

It settled. It poured its vast, quiet consciousness into the cyclopean foundations, into the four-hundred-meter shale cliffs, into the very dust that drifted through the avenues. It became the acoustic medium of the valley. 

It did not quiet the birds—they needed their shrieks to navigate the twilight. It did not banish the crickets—their rasps were the clock by which the skinks hunted. It did not bring rain.

It simply became the companion to the stone. 

From that day forward, no child of the rift ever stamped their foot without feeling the deep, patient answer of the bedrock beneath them. And when the time came, centuries later, for the last of them to walk down the dark steps into the lightless basalt vaults—when the final human lay down upon the planed bench, folded their silt-grey hands across their chest, and closed their eyes against the dim blue dusk—they were not alone. 

As their heart slowed to the rhythm of the grazers, and their skin began its long, dry transition into horn, they felt the entire rift—the cliffs, the causeways, the ancient lintels, and the quiet stars above the rim—breathing with them in the granite. 

A silent song, struck in stone, that would never end.
