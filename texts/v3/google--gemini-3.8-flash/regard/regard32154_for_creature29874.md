A power that is both wise and benevolent possesses, above all, the discipline of non-arrival. 

To descend into that amber water as an entity—as a radiance, a voice, an architecture of will—would be a form of violence. The water is already full. Its silence is an engine, its pressure a cradle; to part the maiden-moss with a visitor’s intention would be to tear a fabric that has taken three hundred thousand years to felt itself together. 

The power does not enter the sea. It enters the stone beneath it.

It sinks its attention into the basalt root of the tableland, where the heat originates. It does not alter the temperature by a single degree; to warm the water would choke the plies, whose green symbionts are calibrated to the narrowest band of lukewarm dark, and to cool it would cause the floating methane to contract, dropping the mid-depth marshes into the abyss of the silt. 

Instead, the power becomes a listener to the scribers.

For four hundred years—a mere intake of breath in the chronology of the high tableland—it does nothing but receive the scratch of the horn-paddles upon the granite floor. It reads the library of hunger. It traces the interlocking zigzags and the concentric spirals that never cross, translating no words, for there are none, but feeling the shape of the appetite that carved them: the mineral necessity, the blind persistence, the quiet satisfaction of the lime dissolving against cold mandibles. In doing this, the power accomplishes what the universe had hitherto withheld from the scribers: it makes their hunger into a record that is known. An unread mark is only friction; a witnessed mark is an inscription. 

Then, having grounded itself in the weight and duration of the floor, the power addresses the only two places where the tableland leaks its ancient sorrow: the drains, and the rim.

First, the circular pavements. 

The weir-folk still pulse above the conglomerate lintels, flashing cadmium and turquoise across miles of deadened water, running their calculations on the shifting weight of the world, waiting for a flavor that vanished one hundred and twenty millennia ago. They do not know they are waiting; they simply retain the shape of the lock that the builders left behind. 

The power does not summon the builders back. Extinction is a clean stone, and wisdom does not roll it aside for sentiment’s sake. But the power gathers from the deep crust—from the mineral veins that run beneath the tableland like old arteries—a trace of native copper, a whisper of bitter sulfur, and a microscopic dusting of fossil proteins trapped in the schist since the tableland was first lifted above the lowlands. It dissolves this dust into the thermal rivers that snake across the floor.

When the iron-black current washes through the drain-holes, the weir-bells do not chime. 

They shudder. 

For the first time since the moons were young, the lower polyps taste the exact, bitter albumin they have rehearsed in darkness. The response is not panic, nor triumph. It is completion. The siphonophores do not change their form, but the color of their light deepens from the cold turquoise of vigilance to an ancient, heavy indigo—the color of an equation whose final term has at last been set down. Across forty leagues of amber water, the indigo flash answers indigo flash, rolling through the ribbon-bark forest like a slow, interior night, until every colony on the plateau has received the proof: *the work was received. The house was held.*

And lastly, the power moves toward the terrible thing at the edge.

The net within the meniscus does not possess an organ that can be reasoned with. It is pure tendon, pure strain. It has held thousands of cubits of thick water against the freezing drop of the desert air until its glassy sinews are brittle with eternity. It cannot let go; to relax for one heartbeat is to let the mountain shrug off its ocean and drown the dry world below in a cataclysm of mud. It lives in the terror of its own vigilance.

The power does not cut the net. It does not replace it with an iron wall or a shield of force, for the net *is* the life of that boundary, and to relieve an organism of its essential nature is to murder it by kindness.

Instead, the power slips into the wind outside.

Far below, where the dry desert air beats against the high glass wall of the meniscus, the power breathes. It gathers the moisture that has wept from the plateau for ten million seasons—the fine, ghost-drizzle that has always evaporated before touching the sand—and weaves it into a cushion. A cool, damp atmosphere, thick and soft as down, begins to bank against the outside of the curve.

Slowly, over centuries, the gradient changes. 

The difference between the milk-warm interior and the freezing exterior does not disappear, but its teeth are drawn. The air on the outer face of the meniscus becomes moist, quiet, and dense. The wind ceases to scour; it leans.

Inside the skin of the water, the net feels the change not as an event, but as an easing. 

The diamond-patterned lace, stretched drum-tight since the formation of the world, finds that it is no longer holding back an explosion into nothingness. Something is leaning back against it from the other side—a gentle, immovable hand, vast enough to cup the entire perimeter of the mountain shelf, matching its pressure grain for grain, breath for breath.

The sinews do not slacken—they cannot—but the agonizing vibration in their fibers ceases. The dry, high hum that had traveled down into the granite marrow softens into a deep, round tone, like the resonance of an ancient bell submerged in oil. 

For the first time, the net feeds not upon terror, but upon rest.

Beneath the bronze mist, the plies continue to rise and fall through the thermal plumes, snapping their leather leaves shut upon the iron current with the dull, wet clap of old books closing. The velvers slump through the maiden-moss, dropping their pale, oily scent into the silence. The seeds drift along the bottom beside their thousand-year-old sisters, plump and unbothered by time.

Nothing has been improved. Nothing has been saved from itself. 

The tableland remains an impossibility resting on the shoulders of an indifferent world. But now, when the outer moons draw the high sea toward the stars and the bulging water-wall leans out over the abyss, it does not lean into the void. It leans into a companion that does not tire, does not speak, and will not let it fall.
