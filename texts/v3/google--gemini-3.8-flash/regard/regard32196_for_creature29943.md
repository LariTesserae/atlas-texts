A foolish mercy would have brought rain. 

Rain would have quenched the basalt shelves, and in twenty minutes three hundred thousand tiles would have stiffened into slate, their copper blood turning to curd as the tallow on their bellies set like wax. Rain would have sloughed the bituminous crust from the wings of the shear-vultures, leaving them naked to the midday ultraviolet; it would have flooded the conduits where the Crawl lay wedged, drowning them in cold, clear runoff they could neither drink nor breathe. 

A lesser god, arriving with an armful of meadows and orchards, would have laid a slaughter across the thirty miles of the lip such as no seismic heave had ever managed.

Wisdom does not come with an eraser. Wisdom sits down on the rock first, to see where the weight falls.

***

The Power did not take a body, for a body at eleven thousand feet is merely an obstruction to the wind or a meal for a caracal-lizard. Instead, it became a quality of attention, settled like a low barometric pressure between Pylon 6 and the ruins of Compressor Shed No. 4.

It tasted the Scarp. It tasted the antimony in the grease; the sulfur that crusted the quill-sockets of the deaf birds; the iron-wash in the condensation runs that tasted of old keys kept under a tongue. It felt the rhythm of the mountain: the six-minute gasp of Borehole 9; the noon slide when the rock turned to molten lead and the living crust crawled down into the shade; the deep, planetary gut-growl of the magma chamber five miles beneath the bronze sleeves.

And it looked at the forty things called the Gasket-Crawl, who were men, though only in the way that an anchor is an oak tree.

It saw the one with the grease-rag around his skull. He was sixty-two seasons old, which made him older than any machine part installed during the Third Deepening. His knees were swollen into gray burs the size of turnips. His teeth were loose in their sockets, clicking against one another when the intake cycle shook the foundation granite. In the center of his forehead, where the sweat gathered before the sulfur dried it into yellow powder, was a small, pale indentation: the scar left by an iron washer his mother had pressed into his skin when he was wet from the sac, so that the heat would not warp his crown before the bone knit.

He had no word for *sorrow*, because a word requires an opposing state to give it a boundary, and on the Scarp there had never been anything other than the pipe, the valve, and the noon migration. But in the deep knot of his spine—where the nervous thread ran between the atlas and the shoulder-blades—there was an old, uncoiled ache. It was not the ache of labor; it was the ache of a bronze bearing that has turned dry for so many centuries that the shaft and the sleeve have begun to fuse, atom by atom, out of pure, exhausted intimacy.

The Power saw this. It knew that to give him a human throat would be to give him a voice only to scream with until his lungs dried out like tobacco leaf. It knew that to clear his ears would be to split his brain with ninety decibels of wet, industrial agony.

So it did three things, none of which changed the skyline by a millimeter.

***

First, it entered the iron.

The bronze sleeves of the deep pumps were scored. For eighty years, grit carried up from the deep hydrothermal brine had been cutting circular grooves into the four-ton shafts, making them rattle in their housings with a high, dry *shuck-shuck-shuck* that resonated directly into the marrow of anyone whose ribs were pressed to the basalt. 

The Power did not repair the sleeves. To make them new would have changed the frequency, and the Crawl navigated the miles by the pitch of the iron. Instead, it smoothed the microscopic burrs inside the seat of the valve on Borehole 9. It placed a thin, molecular skin of coolness—not cold, but the mild, greasy coolness of wet graphite—between the seated valve-stem and the flange.

When noon came, and the pressure peaked at four thousand pounds per square inch, the valve did not shriek. It did not hit the stop with the bone-shattering *clonk* that made the men’s eye-sockets throb for three hours after. 

It breathed. It vented with a long, rounded *huff*, like a horse blowing dust out of its nostrils in an alley. 

The man with the grease-rag had wedged his forehead against the flange, his fingers stuck in his ears, bracing for the teeth-jarring hammer of the purge. 

The blow did not come. The mist rose—thick, gray, laden with iron-bloom—but the iron beneath his knuckles did not buck. It shuddered once, softly, with the elastic give of seasoned timber rather than the spite of rusted steel. 

He stayed there for five minutes after the mist cleared, his fingers still in his ears, his flat, slit-nosed face pressed into the grease. He blinked his pale, lidless eyes against the salt. Slowly, cautiously, he withdrew his left thumb and laid his ear directly against the metal. 

The pipe was singing, but it was singing on a lower fifth. The frantic, needle-thin vibration that lived in his jaw-hinge was gone. In its place was a steady, heavy pulse that matched the thick, copper-heavy beat of his own blood.

***

The second thing the Power did concerned the water.

In the runnels behind Compressor Shed No. 4, the water ran at nearly a hundred and ninety degrees, so bitter with dissolved arsenic that the ribbon-dogs had to drink by skin-diffusion alone, forming the chalky collars of bone around their necks until they could barely turn their heads to snap at flies.

The Power did not sweeten the spring. To take the minerals from the water would have starved the endolithic lichens, which would have starved the tiles, which would have brought the shear-vultures down to rot on the empty terraces.

Instead, where the runoff gathered in a natural basin of vesicular basalt—a shallow, dark dish four paces across—it caused the stone to grow a dense, fine carpet of red-veined hair. Not moss; moss cannot live in boiling vinegar. It was an iron-oxidizing bacterial mat, three inches thick, so dense that it strained the coarser antimony salts from the upper inch of the pool.

The water remained hot enough to poach a finger. It remained thick and yellow-gray. But the top film—the skin of condensation that rested on the red hair—lost its tearing, acid bite.

The man crawled to the pool at dusk, dragging an iron hook with three caracal-chins skewered upon it. He dipped his snout into the basin as he had done every dusk for fifty years, expecting the familiar, cauterizing burn that stripped the dead skin from his lip-calluses.

He stopped. 

The water did not burn. It was hot—hot as broth—and it tasted not of tin cans and battery acid, but of wet stone, of deep dark earth that had never seen the sun, of ancient, unburned rock. It was the taste of something that had been permitted to rest in the dark before it rose.

He drank. He drank three mouthfuls, his throat-valves clicking in the silence. Then he sat back upon his hams, the pads of vulcanized rubber on his knees squeaking against the slick rock, and looked down at his own palms. 

The lymph that wept from the cracks in his knuckles was not yellow. It was pink. A pale, weak, forgotten red, like the dawn behind a salt-drift.

***

The third thing was the smallest, and therefore the one that took the most time.

The Power knew that when the Crawl died, they did not die in beds; they died in the angles. They crawled into the dead space between the pump-housings and the cliff, where the drift-heat kept their tallow from hardening until the tick-wasps found them and turned their ribs into dry, white combs.

There was an old woman of the Crawl—she had no name, but the others knew her by the three copper staples that held the flesh of her left thigh together where a haulage cable had parted in her youth. She was lying in the narrow crawl-way behind the secondary condensers, her breathing thin and dry, like paper being rubbed with a thumb.

She was deaf; she was blind in the left pit; her tongue was a dried strip of skate-wing. 

The Power did not give her back the valley. It did not show her the green fields her great-grandmother had spoken of in the half-coherent grunts that preceded the loss of speech. Green would have terrified her; the smell of grass would have smelled like rot to a nose cured on pyrites.

Instead, the Power entered the pipe that ran six inches above her head.

The pipe was dead. It had been valved off forty years before, a cold branch of four-inch steel that led nowhere, choked with dry calcite. 

The Power tapped it.

Not with iron. It tapped it with a memory that was native to the steel itself—the slow, rhythmic ring of the hammer that had forged it in the lowlands two hundred years ago, filtered through three thousand miles of cold rock, softened by its passage through the granite until it arrived not as sound, but as warmth.

*Tink.*

The old woman’s shoulder twitched.

*Tink-tink.*

It was the signal for *All Clear at the Wellhead*. It was the signal that meant the pressure had dropped, that the boilers were holding, that the night would not bring a blowout.

She had not felt that sequence since she was small enough to ride in an iron skip on her father’s chest.

She laid the side of her face against the cold, dead pipe. The iron did not warm her cheek—the Scarp was too thin for warmth to linger—but the *interval* between the pulses was wide and slow, like the breathing of a great, slate-backed tile that had found a crack so deep the sun could never reach it.

She felt the second double-tap. *Tink-tink.*

She did not spit. She had no moisture left to waste. But she reached out with one curled, horn-nailed finger and scratched three times against the rust of the casing.

*I am here,* the scratch said. *The sleeve is holding.*

The Power stayed with her until the tallow in her veins cooled and the tick-wasps began their silent, orderly work in the marrow of her shins. 

It did not take her soul to a garden. It left her where she belonged: part of the foundation, tucked into the fluting of the basalt, as hard and durable as the bronze pins that held the mountain against the sky.

***

By morning, the sun rose over the Scarp with its usual white, sterile fury.

The tiles began their transverse scraping, ten thousand magnetite mouths eating the slime from the runnels. The shear-vultures unhooked their wings with that wooden, heavy snap and rose into the hot chimney of the caldera, circling in their Lacquered silence. The ribbon-dogs threaded the pipe-lagging, hunting the wasps that crawled over the iron.

The man with the grease-rag crawled out from under Compressor No. 4. His teeth felt firm in his head; his belly had not cramped from the water. 

He took his flat piece of basalt. He looked down the long, gray, thirty-mile knife-edge of the ridge, where the mist was beginning to boil out of the hairline fractures, smelling of hot iron and sulfur.

He knelt by the six-inch brine conduit. He raised the stone and struck the metal twice: 

*Tink-tink.*

He waited, his hand flat against the vibrating skin of the pipe, feeling the deep, smooth churn of the altered valves three miles down. 

Five miles away, across the fluted columns of the saddle, the answer came back through the marrow of the rock, through the grease-jackets and the bird-lime and the sleeping swarms:

*Tink-tink.*

*We are still here,* the iron said. *The pressure is within the limits.*

And above them, invisible in the blinding pale of the stratosphere, the benevolent thing smiled—neither weeping for them nor bidding them change—and let the mountain turn another degree into the noon.
