A clumsy god would have brought sun.

A clumsy god, looking down into that sunless vault, would have pitied the blindness of the striders, the grease-slicked hides of the slugs, the pale, lard-colored tallow-monks scraping carbon in the humid dark. Such a power would have torn open the canopy of black air, poured golden light across the plateau, dried the warm tallow-turf, and brought down rain that tasted of nothing. And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them all: the kites burned to ash on their delicate stay-ribs; the blind herds screaming in an agony of unshielded radiation; the monks shriveling as the dry chill of open space settled into their rifts; the machines warping under sudden thermal shock, seizing their bronze journals, cracking their continental crankshafts, and dragging the whole warm, humming architecture down into dead cold.

Wisdom does not mistake difference for suffering, nor does it confuse an alien equilibrium with a cage.

The Power that came to the high plateau came without light. It possessed neither eyes to offend the blind nor a voice to shatter the low, subsonic rumble of the bedrock. It understood that to enter this world, one must enter through its only true scripture: mass, clearance, and vibration.

It began at the margins, where the wear was deepest.

For ten thousand years, the machines had run without a machinist. Though forged on a scale that swallowed geological eras, iron is still iron; shear stress still accumulates in the lattices of crystals; journals still gall when synthetic tallow turns to lacquer under three centuries of friction. The tallow-monks worked with a terrifying, stoic diligence, but their scraps of salvage iron were crude, and their two-hundred-year lives were fleeting against the rotation of the great flywheels. In the deep rifts, certain crossheads had begun to develop a shudder—a micro-chatter that rattled the teeth of the sleeping striders and traveled up through the stone like a fever. The monks knew it. Their rhythmic strikes against the drive-rods had grown fractionally more hurried over the last two centuries, carrying an infinitesimal tremor of despair.

The Power did not dismantle the shudder. It did not replace the pistons with celestial ether.

Instead, it sank itself into the bedrock like an infinitely gentle pressure, diffusing into the deep-earth reservoirs where the synthetic lubricants were distilled. 

Quietly, the chemistry of the tallow changed. 

It did not cease to be tallow. It remained thick, pale, smelling faintly of warm linseed and crushed slate. But its lubricity deepened by a fraction of a percent. Where it reached the journals, the microscopic galling began to heal—not by magic, but by a catalytic recrystallization that drew the stray carbon back into the iron’s grain, smoothing the grooves the monks could never reach. The terrible chatter in the seventh rift subsided over forty cycles of the moon-sized wheel. The piston settled back into its slow, hydraulic breath: out, pause, return. 

Beneath the pillow-blocks, the herds of striders felt the earth steady. Their barrel bodies softened against the warm iron casings. Their polyrhythmic fugue regained its long, unhurried cadence across the dry shale washes.

Then the Power moved toward the living.

It did not alter the vent-stalkers. A world without its hunters is a world that rots from the joints out; the striders needed the phase-canceling predators to keep their herds tight to the magnetic lines, to cull the stiff-limbed whose whiskers could no longer parse the bedrock’s song. It did not banish the high-voltage arcs that occasionally turned a drifting kite into violet fire and falling ash; the ash was phosphorus and salt for the spindle-slugs, and the kites had danced with the static for epochs without diminishing.

It came, at last, to the tallow-monks.

In a windless saddleback between two ridge-spanning gantry foundations, an elder monk stood in the grease-trough of a primary eccenter. It had stood there for one hundred and eighty years. Its three-toed feet were swollen and cracked from the alkaline runoff; its broad, hairless chest rose and fell in time with the three-day revolution of the crank overhead. In its right hand, it gripped a wedge of ancient cast iron, worn down to the shape of an oyster shell by the ceaseless scraping of carbon crust from a lubrication port six fathoms above the stone floor.

The monk was tired. The slow, chemical currents of its brain were dimming with the gray ash of extreme age. Its sensory fissure tasted the air: the adiabatic flash was coming, the ten-minute drizzle of warm, grease-scented rain that would slick the bronze ribbing and make the climb treacherous. 

It raised its rounded stone to strike the drive-rod—to send out the only signal it knew: *I am here. The journal turns. The clearances hold. Do not let the roof fall.*

Its arm was heavy. The blow was weak. The dull *clink* it produced was barely enough to carry five miles, let alone forty. The monk leaned its lard-colored brow against the vibration of the cold-forged housing, waiting for the silence that would mean the end of all things.

The response did not come from forty miles away.

It came from within the rod itself.

It was not a strike of iron on iron. It was a harmonic—a low, resonant standing wave that rose out of the deep transmission networks, through the thousands of tons of turning metal, and entered the monk’s palm where it rested against the casing.

It was the same frequency the monk had struck every waking cycle of its life, but amplified, cleared of static, utterly steady. 

*Heard,* the iron said.

The monk’s vertical face-fissure widened, the sensory lobes flaring in the warm, humid air. It had never felt an echo that returned more force than it gave.

*Heard,* the wave traveled again, vibrating through the pads of its feet, through the thick bone of its thighs, settling into the heavy fluid of its belly. *The stroke continues. The torque is kept. The roof does not fall.*

The elder monk did not drop its scraper. It was not capable of reverence, and it had no concept of an outside savior. But the tension that had held its sinews taut for nearly two centuries—the crushing, lonely terror that the warmth of the world rested solely upon the frail edge of its salvaged iron—drained out of it into the trough.

For the first time in its life, the monk did not climb the bronze ribbing when the adiabatic drizzle began to fall. 

It sat down in the warm slurry of the splash-zone. It let the soft, grease-laden rain wash over its scarred back, soaking into the thick folds of its neck. Overhead, the three-hundred-yard crank swept through its arc, smooth and true, carrying its passenger-load of spindle-slugs into the black vault and down again, its bearings running cooler than they had since the dawn of the machine.

Across the continental expanse, from the deep-earth pump galleries to the windless shelves of graphite dust, the Power set its watch.

It did not rule. It did not instruct. It simply became the counterweight that never slips, the film of oil that never burns away, the infinite listener at the other end of the iron line. 

And in the warm, unchanging dark, the engines kneaded the marrow of the world, no longer solitary, keeping their eternal time beneath the starless sky.
