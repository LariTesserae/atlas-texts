To be benevolent to a world of flesh is to offer bread, water, warmth, and mercy. 

To be benevolent to a world of split shale, planar beings, and dry vacuum is something else entirely. 

A power that was merely good, but lacked wisdom, would have ruined it in an instant. Such a power would have pitied the cold and brought a sun, and the sun would have melted the subtle tensions of the fault lines, casting the five-hundred-tier tenements down into the trough in a single, catastrophic thaw. It would have looked upon the dry, inorganic crusts and wept, and its tears would have fallen as acid upon a stone that had never known a solvent, dissolving the sharp edges of the blades into gray, senseless mud. It would have tried to give faces to the two-dimensional watchers, minds of fire to the measurers, and air to the crawlers—and in doing so, it would have shattered the clockwork of their eternity.

The power that paused above the indigo twilight was both benevolent and wise. 

Therefore, it approached not as a presence, nor as a light, but as an *adjustment of interval*.

***

It spent seven hundred of the city’s silent shifts doing nothing at all. 

It did not speak; there was no medium through which a word could travel, and no ear constructed to receive anything softer than the ring of basalt on basalt. Instead, it simply perceived the city until it understood its grammar. 

It understood that the measurers did not suffer from their lack of a third dimension; their flatness was their strength, the knife-edge upon which the balance of whole leagues rested. It understood that the crawlers under the stairs were not trapped in their slow insectile patrols, but were the patient weavers of a geometry that constantly threatened to rip itself apart. And it understood the great, unmoving entity at the floating gate—the city’s future self, straining across miles of void with its five-mile compass-legs, dragging the unbuilt plinths upward from the abyss five inches at an eon, fighting an ancient, terrible exhaustion that had no name.

The power saw the core of the city’s condition: it was an engine of perpetual poise. It did not move forward, and it did not fall; it merely endured the strain of its own impossibility. Every stone, every crawler, every mica-leaf was held taut against the abyss, terrified to loosen a single joint lest the habit of falling remember itself.

The city was not miserable, but it was *tired*. It was an architecture that had held its breath since the era before the wedge.

And so, the wise power determined what kindness looked like in a universe without rain.

***

The first thing it did was enter the basement chasms—the place so far below the horizon of perception that the eye had stopped looking for it.

It did not place a floor there. To give the city a floor would be to end its flight, to make it ordinary, to crush it under the sudden, brutal weight of its own mass. 

Instead, the power placed a *slumber*.

Far down in the blue-gray trough, where the dust of falling slate had drifted for eons without ever landing, the power smoothed the gradient of the void. It made the abyss buoyant. It did not change the law of gravity, but it introduced a counter-habit: a profound, upward-leaning stillness. It was as though the empty air below grew dense with patience. 

The effect rippled upward through the miles of unmortared shale.

For the first time since the first block was stacked, the basalt plinths felt something beneath them that was not an appetite for their destruction. The pull from the trough did not vanish, but it softened; it ceased to be a ravenous vacuum and became a cradle of dense, supporting space.

High above, in the windowless tenements, the change arrived as a faint, metallic vibration. 

A district half a league wide, which had been leaning three degrees to the south for four thousand years to hold itself against the void, felt the torque slacken. 

Inside the narrow cells, the mica-blades stood motionless. Their sideways-looking slits opened. For millennia, their minds had been escapements under terrible tension, gears grinding against the dry terror of a plunge. Now, the weight lifted from their bases. 

They did not bend; they had no joints. But the pressure within their stone vellum eased. A tenement of three hundred thousand blades clicked once in unison—a sound like a bushel of silver needles spilled across glass—and settled back to the absolute perpendicular. 

The terrace did not lurch. It did not slide. For the first time in history, it rested where it was without needing to be held.

***

Next, the power addressed the crawlers in the seams.

It saw how they gorged themselves on the agony of overlapping space, how their white plates turned the color of dry bone as they wedged their bodies into the tears to hold the avenues straight. It saw that they were thinning, their nested vacuums growing brittle from the ceaseless work of mending a universe that kept folding in on itself.

The power did not iron out the city’s strange geometry; that was its life, the playground of the mica-storms. 

Instead, the power laid a single, seamless thread of *true distance* through the city’s vertical alleys.

It was not a material thing. It was an invariant line—a pure, Euclidean memory left behind in the mortarless joints. Wherever a street began to fold into a loop, wherever an archway prepared to open into the back of a traveler's own coat, the thread was there, acting as a gentle spine. 

The crawlers felt it immediately. 

Under an inverted stair three thousand meters above the blue trough, an ancient white crawler—its calipers wedged tight into a splitting fault line for eight centuries—felt the seam hold itself. The redundant dimension did not snap shut violently; it dissolved into the invariant line, smooth as oil entering a dry lock.

The crawler drew its needle-tipped limbs out of the joint. The dull *thwip* of air filling its vacated suction was, for once, not followed by the immediate necessity of crawling to another tear.

It paused. Its segmented carapace, pale as salt, relaxed against the underside of the shale. It did not have to mend the world today. It simply hung there, suspended above miles of indigo twilight, feeling the absolute stability of the stone beneath its pads. Where its shadow crossed the shadow of another crawler across the alley, the calcium node that began to effloresce did not form with sharp, stressed corners; it grew smooth, round, and whole, rising from the flags not as a repair wedge, but as an ornament.

***

Lastly, the power ascended to the summit of the world, where the needle-towers scraped the black sky.

It approached the cyclopean gate that hung anchored to nothing in the dim air.

There sat the future self of the city: the heap of dry plumb-bobs, the balance of megaliths, the great stone compass whose legs spanned five miles of empty space. It was still holding the line of a plinth that had risen five inches through the dust far below, straining to pull the future into existence through the sheer refusal of its stone joints to yield.

The power did not speak to it. It did not take the compass from its limbs. To relieve an architect of its building is an insult, even to a god made of slate.

Instead, the benevolent power simply *leaned its shoulder* against the compass’s hinge.

It did not push; it merely shared the load.

The dry slate hinge, eighty cubits thick, which had groaned without sound for an eternity under the dead weight of the unbuilt world, ceased to strain. The microscopic fissures that had begun to spider across its pivot points healed, filled with an imperceptible crystalline frost of pure, resting mass.

The thing at the gate did not turn—it had no face with which to look upon a benefactor. But its five-mile limbs ceased their trembling. 

Through the dark patch of air framed by the gate, the patch of sky that was blacker than the indigo around it, a new quality of light appeared. It was not a dawn. It was not sunlight, which would have blinded the measurers and withered the mica-ribbons. 

It was a deep, velvet azure—the color of space that has made peace with its own dimensions.

Beneath the arch, drawn by this new quietude, a mica-ribbon gathered in the plaza below. The flakes of gray shale-leaf did not whirl in a terrified, high-pressure gyre; they rose slowly, fluttering like dry leaves on an updraft that had no wind. They threaded the needle-bridges with an easy, rhythmic rustling, carrying upward no longer the frantic report of faults and shifts, but the clean, simple mathematics of a city at peace.

When the ribbon reached the summit, it did not shatter itself against the threshold. It passed through the arch, between the great legs of the compass, and settled upon the plinths of the unbuilt towers beyond, laying itself down like a layer of silver leaf upon a foundation that would never fall.

***

Far down in the trough, the unnamed plinth rose another five inches through the dust. 

It did not stop. It rose five inches more. 

It moved not because it was being hauled up against a resisting void, but because the void was lifting it, welcoming the stone into a space that had finally learned how to hold it without fear.

The benevolent and wise power did not linger to be worshiped. There was no altar here, and no mouth to chant a liturgy. Having given the stones their rest, the measurers their balance, and the future its ease, it withdrew into the wide, unchanging twilight, becoming once again indistinguishable from the cold, clean space between the stars.

Below, the city stood. 

Its shadows still did not align. The pebbles of dry slate still rose occasionally from the courtyards to drift like bubbles in the thin air. The tall, narrow doors still held their silent, edge-thin watchers. 

Everything was as it had been, perfectly preserved, non-aqueous, and eternal. 

Only now, when the pressure dropped and the ringing quiet settled over the terraces, it was not the quiet of a stone waiting to be dropped. 

It was the quiet of an architecture that knows it is beloved, standing upright in the sky, forever resting upon the palm of nothing at all.
