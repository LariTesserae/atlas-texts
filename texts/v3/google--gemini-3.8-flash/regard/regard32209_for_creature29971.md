A lesser power, arriving with the common vanity of saviors, would have brought water.

It would have gathered the bruised electrical storms that prowled the lower basins and dragged them by the hair up to the peak, breaking the bronze seal of the sky to dump two hundred inches of sweet, cold deluge upon the scoop. And in forty-eight hours, the work would have been complete: the unbaked caliche would have dissolved into gray gruel; the three tiers of houses would have slid like wet tallow over the two-thousand-yard drop; the hair-moss would have drowned in its own sponges; and the Tallow-keepers, their deep, barrel-shaped chests inundated with air too thick and damp to draw through slit-nostrils, would have coughed their lungs into foam and died of pneumonia in the ruins of their shelves.

Wisdom knows that pity is the sharpest tool of destruction. Pity looks upon the dry bone and wishes it were grass; wisdom looks upon the bone and acknowledges that it has taken four hundred million years to achieve such hardness, and that it wants neither grass nor tears.

Therefore, the power that paused above the scoop did not descend as a voice, nor as a light, nor as a cloud. It did not change the amber tint of the ceiling, for the amber tint was what kept the stone from cooling too fast when the sun dipped behind the upper teeth.

Instead, it stood where the air was thinnest—not upon the rock, but in the tension between the heat rising from the scoop and the dead cold of the upper void—and it listened.

It listened for seven human turns: while forty generations of swiftlets nested in the sand-bored pores, while two fingers of iron were hammered down to the thickness of reeds, and while the hanging beard of nettle accepted seventeen men and twenty-one women into its gray twine, turning their eyes into fibrous roots and their thigh-bones into roosts for the lilac-necked vultures.

It observed the economy of the place. It saw that there was no margin. 

A single mistake in the arithmetic of the scoop would unthread it all. If the badger-grease grew too thin, the horn spatulas would tear the skin of the *palo-seco*, and the succulent would bleed out its bitter gel in a week and leave the shelves bald. If the dew-scrapers lost their callouses, their knees would slip on the northern ribs, and two goatskins of water a day would vanish from the bellies of the toddlers. If the seam ceased to breathe, the dogs would lose the rhythm of their coughing whistle, and the women would forget when to shelter the dung-cakes from the dust-flour that preserved them from the sun-beetles.

Everything held together because everything was hard, scarce, and finished. 

To love such a thing is not to alter it. To love such a thing is to hold the mountain steady from underneath, where it does not know it is being held.

Deep within the core of the ironstone—three miles below the honeycombed tiers, down where the old water had retired into the crystal lattices of the porphyry before the first ape stood upright—a shear-plane had been creeping. 

It was no thicker than the edge of a scraping-knife, a microscopic fault caused by the slow, northward drift of the continental plate. In another two hundred years—the blink of a vulture’s lilac lid—the shear would have reached the throat of the scoop. The parabolic ear would have fractured. The scoop would not have fallen; it would merely have tilted three inches to the west. 

Three inches. Enough to spill the stagnant warmth out into the cross-drafts of the drop-off. Enough to turn the south-facing leeward pocket into a wind-chimney that would scour the caliche down to bare porphyry in five winters and drive the swiftlets two peaks north.

The power went down into that dark.

It did not use fire or thunder. It did not lay down veins of gold or sweet springs, for water in the deep rock would have turned to steam and split the peak like a dry walnut. It simply placed its will against the slip-face. It knit the quartz grains back into the iron. It took the cold, dry pressure that was driving the fracture and turned it sideways, easing the stress into the dead roots of the mountain that had ceased to move when the air was young.

It mended the bone before the bone knew it had cracked.

Then it returned to the lip of the scoop, invisible as the thermal that carried the vultures, and looked at what it had preserved.

It looked at an old woman sitting against the eastern buttress of the bread-ovens. She had four drops of the *leche-negra* left in her clay dish. Her knuckles were enlarged, gray-white like the caliche, and her thumb moved rhythmically over the smooth hearth-block between her palms, polishing stone with skin, skin with stone, until it was impossible to say which was the harder substance.

The power did not take away her thirst. Her thirst was her own, earned by seventy years of scraping and spinning, the natural conclusion of a life that had never wasted a breath on a soft syllable. 

Instead, it did only this:

It reached out with a touch no heavier than the shadow of a swiftlet’s wing and touched the *palo-seco* growing in the seam beside her left heel. 

It did not make the plant green. It did not make it sweet. It simply altered the sap within its boiled-leather skin, increasing by one part in ten thousand the bitter oil that deadened the roots of the teeth. 

The old woman dipped the tip of her dried finger into the dish, touched it to the leaf, and then pressed the salt-crust of her knuckle against her lower jaw, where the brown plugs of her tooth-roots were grinding against the jawbone.

The ache did not vanish—for an ache is the mountain’s companion, and to take away a man’s ache at eight thousand paces is to make him careless on the ledges—but it grew cool. It changed from the heat of struck iron to the dull, settled cold of deep shale.

She did not look up. She did not know she had been visited. There was no bronze-tinted angel, no sudden rush of sweet wind, no loosening of the air that made her chest heave with unearned ease. 

She merely turned the river-cobble over in her palms once more. The stone was smooth. It would serve her daughter’s daughter for three hundred seasons before the bread-smoke wore it thin.

Above her, on the highest threshing-floor, the hairless dogs rose onto their toes. Their liver-colored ears pricked until the tips touched. 

The seam had breathed. 

A single puff of ancient, sub-zero dust drifted out of the spade-wide crack, smelling of dry gravel and things that had ceased to rot before the sea retreated. The dogs coughed their dry whistle in the bronze light. Down in the narrow way, the women heard it, and with their heels they shoved the sledges of dung under the eaves without breaking the spin of their badger-hair thread.

The benevolent power withdrew. 

It stepped back into the amber void, leaving the world exactly as it had found it: thirsty, precarious, rattling with swiftlet-wings, and unbroken.
