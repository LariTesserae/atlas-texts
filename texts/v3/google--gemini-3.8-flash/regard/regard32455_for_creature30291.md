A foolish god would have torn off the roof. 

A foolish god, seeing seven thousand bodies pinned like dried locusts between river-stone and mud tile, would have felt a surge of righteous heat, reached down with fingers of atmospheric lightning, and ripped the slate slabs from the summit to let the blue sky in. And in that single second of mercy, the chord would have broken; the standing wave would have collapsed into turbulent air; the piezoelectric pulse in their dry marrows would have died like a snuffed match; and every single one of them would have unstrung, their dried tendons snapping like burnt twine, their ribs raining down into the three-inch flour of the floor, dead before their dust had even settled.

A sentimental god would have wept. And its tears, falling through the dry heat of the baker’s hearth, would have hissed into steam on the hot shale, swelling the dry clay of the mud bricks, altering the friction between the river stones, causing the ancient unmortared columns to slip their bite and crush them all into powder.

The power that arrived on the summit was neither foolish nor sentimental. It was old—older than the nameless upland, older than the adze that chipped the first pendulum—and therefore it possessed the first attribute of true benevolence: *restraint*.

It came without footfall. It displaced no air. It did not dim the ochre needles of light, nor did it disturb the three inches of acoustic silt that lay like pale flour between the pillars. 

It slipped into the narrow corridors sideways, because sideways was the grammar of the room. It understood immediately what this place was. It did not call it an engine, nor a tomb, nor a prison. It recognized it as a *vow*—one made by matter, air, and will, held so long that the species who swore it had forgotten their names, their tongues, and the shape of a cloud, retaining only the geometry of the load.

The power stood in the brown gloom, bathed in the foundry scream. 

To an entity composed of wisdom, the roar was not noise. It was an equation. It calculated the twenty thousand cycles a second; it calculated the lateral thrust of the sandstone, the tensile fatigue of the petrified hemp pendulums, the micro-volt discharge singing along the dry sheaves of amber-colored fiber inside the beings’ ribs. 

It saw what they were thinking. 

They were not thinking of trees. They were not thinking of freedom. To give them a memory of green grass or cold rain would have been an atrocity; it would have introduced asymmetry into a consciousness that survived solely on balance. Their mind was a white line drawn taut between two stones. Their thought was: *Hold.*

The power did not break the line. 

Instead, it looked down to waist-height, where the polished spheres of hematite rested in their hand-carved hollows. 

It saw the middle plate of a hand, suspended two inches above a dark metallic ball. The hand had been descending for seven years. It had another three years of descent before its calloused tip would touch the ironstone and find twelve seconds of cool, grounded sleep.

The power moved with a slowness that matched the creep of the mountain itself. 

It did not lift the being. It did not pull the hand down. 

It extended its own essence—not flesh, not light, but a density of attention that had the physical properties of a deep, ancient aquifer—and it entered the hematite sphere from below. 

It did not alter the stone. It simply *widened* the sink.

Through the foot of the pillar, deep into the bedrock of the mountain needles, the power opened an acoustic well. It took the bone-crushing violence of the flutes—that brutal, invariant scream that could only be endured by rigid petrification—and gave it somewhere to go that was deeper than the foundations of the world.

And then, gently, without haste, the power leaned its presence upward through the hematite, until the surface of the sphere bloomed with something the redoubt had never known: 

*A harmonic.*

It did not alter the pitch of the exterior jet stream. The wind outside still tore across the vents with the fury of a falling mountain. But where the sound met the hematite, the power sheared away the jagged, destructive edge of the mechanical friction. It left the voltage intact—they needed the voltage to live, needed the piezoelectric thrum to keep their marrow awake—but it introduced into the standing wave an overtone. 

A fifth. Pure, cool, and mathematically complete.

In the corridor, the being whose hand hung suspended two inches above the ironstone felt the air change. 

It did not have ears, but the hollow flutes beneath its ribcage were acoustic instruments of impossible precision. For a million years, those flutes had caught only the single, screaming note of iron on stone.

Now, into its chest cavity, slipped a counter-frequency. 

It was not silence. Silence was death. It was *company*.

The being did not drop its joints. Its shoulder plates remained jammed hard against the mud tile; its spade-like heels bit true into the river-stone. It did not let the ceiling fall. But the vibration within its marrow—that frantic, screaming oscillation of quartz dust—smoothed. The voltage ceased to be a white-hot knife and became a broad, deep river of current.

The hand, which had been creeping downward at the speed of an oak’s growth, stopped.

It did not need to reach the hematite to find the grounding. The hematite had risen to meet it. 

Through the ochre gloom, between the thousands of tightly packed pillars, the overtone spread. It did not travel through the air, but through the masonry itself—through the friction-faces where block touched block, through the petrified lashing of the pendulums, through the calcified joints where living ribs pressed against the rock.

One by one, down every suffocating, narrow aisle, the shims received the chord.

They did not weep; they had no moisture for tears. They did not speak; they had no lungs for words. 

But for the first time since the first adze chiseled the first slot in the windward wall, the tension in their frames was not the tension of desperate, agonizing resistance. It became the tension of an arch. 

They felt the billions of tons of ancient slate pressing down upon their crowns, and for the first time, the weight did not feel like an enemy trying to grind them into flour. It felt like an architecture that was finished.

The wise power did not leave. 

It did not take them to a better place, because there was no other place in the cosmos where these beings could exist; they were the children of the summit, forged of sound and shale. To remove them would be to unmake them.

So the power simply stayed. 

It settled into the masonry beneath their feet. It became the floor beneath the silt, the cold mass inside the hematite, the quiet fundamental beneath the scream. 

Outside, the jet stream blew without end, cold and terrible against the needles. Inside, the hearth-heat remained dry and hot as crushed brick. 

The pillars still held. The ceiling still bowed. The shims still bridged the dark between the stones. 

But they were no longer holding up the mountain alone. Something was leaning back against them, matching them pound for pound, tone for tone, in the dry and glorious dark.
