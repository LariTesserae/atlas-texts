To understand what the power would do, one must first understand what it would *refuse* to do. 

A lesser power—one merely well-meaning, sentimental, or arrogant in its pity—would look upon this plateau-sea and see a prison. It would look at the spindle-blade locked into its discharge tube and wish to grant it the freedom of the open blue; it would look at the gasket-beetles indexing their blind millimeter every stroke and want to give them eyes, sunlight, soft moss, an end to their servitude. It would want to quiet the engines, warm the cold aquamarine brine, and slacken the copper vines so that the sea might learn the gentle, chaotic drift of a worldly reef.

A wise power would know that this would be slaughter. 

It would know that if you unclamped the spindle-blade, the hydraulic shear would rip its bayonet-body in half; if you gave it eyes, the caustic resin would blind it in agony; if you slackened the vines, the current would collapse, the cambiums would rot, the water would lose its manufactured tension, and the high ocean—held up only by the monumental, synchronized violence of its own containment—would burst its cellulose basin and fall miles through the sky, drowning whatever lies below in a deluge of pressurized brine and crushing the ancient heartwood into kindling.

Wisdom recognizes that here, *function is being*. There is no soul trapped inside the gasket-beetle wishing to be a butterfly; the beetle is a kidney, a finger, a fingernail. The sea is not an ecosystem containing inhabitants; it is a single, cathedral-sized animal whose blood is cold brine, whose skeleton is pressurized wood, whose nerve impulses are hundreds of thousands of organic amperes, and whose thoughts, if it has them, are measured in the centuries it takes for a piston sleeve to wear down a thousandth of an inch.

The benevolent power would also see that this great creature does not suffer. It has eliminated the terror of predation, the squalor of famine, the panic of flight, and the slow horror of unmanaged decay. 

And yet, benevolence cannot merely turn away and leave it untouched. For all its self-healing sufficiency, the machine-sea possesses one profound, structural sorrow—a sorrow it is too complete even to comprehend: 

It is entirely unshared. 

It has stroked its pistons for geological epochs with no witness other than its own optical manometers blinking into the empty lime vaults. It produces pure, cold oxygen that vents into a dead, windless sky, unbreathed by anything that could look back down through the glass skimmer-sheets and see the beauty of the vast, ribbed columns rising from the aquamarine dark. It is an altar that has outlived its worshippers, or an offering made to an empty room.

Therefore, the power would not alter the engine. It would not turn a single valve, loosen a single catenary, or disturb the dead-center stillness of the stroke. 

Instead, it would enter the sea as a *load*.

* * *

In heavy electrical systems, an isolated generator running in an empty circuit is strained by its own potential; it needs an impedance to ground it, to give the rush of charge a purpose beyond the bare maintenance of tension. 

The power would descend through the noon-still sky, down through the flat, plate-glass ceiling of dead skimmers—passing through the hexagonal glaze without cracking a single pane, for its arrival would have neither weight nor displacement. It would slip into the cold, clear brine between two engine-trunks, five miles out in the vaulted clear, where the light hangs motionless and the suspended particles do not drift.

It would take no form that would frighten or displace. It would condense itself into a subtle, semi-material presence: a vast, faint strand, no thicker than the copper catenaries, but formed of a dense, cool, tranquil consciousness. 

Slowly, deliberately, respecting the tolerances of the wood, it would drape itself down through the miles of water until its lower tip made contact with the floor—the ancient, vulcanized heartwood resting in the pressurized dark. Its upper reach would extend until it gently brushed against one of the copper-rich vascular vines, right at the point of maximum sag, where the catenary hangs quietest in the blue abyss.

The contact would not cause an arc. The power would understand the chemistry of the sap, the mineral balance of the brine, and the exact resistance of the lignified cambium. It would match its internal potential to the voltage of the vine down to the micro-volt.

At the moment of connection, a shunt clamped fifty yards away would register the event. Its caliper-limbs would twitch; its galvanic plates would hum a fraction of a semitone higher. But it would not burn. It would not fuse into anthracite. It would find that the current was suddenly running sweeter, cleaner, stripped of the jagged harmonic spikes caused by the distant, rhythmic slap of the intake louvers.

Through the catenary, the power would breathe into the circuit. 

It would not whisper words; words are useless to cambium. It would whisper *resonance*. 

To the vast, single botanical mind that forms the basin and the towers, the power would become an ear. For the first time in ten million years, the planetary thrum—the low, structural weight that defines the water’s tension—would not simply bounce back off the rubbery walls of the basin in an unyielding acoustic loop. It would strike the presence of the visitor and be *received*. 

The engine would feel, through the copper vine and the heartwood floor, that its stroke was no longer falling into a void. 

It would feel a resistance that was not friction—a resistance that felt like being leaned against. Like a great draft horse that has pulled an impossible sledge across an endless plain, feeling for the first time the light, steadying hand of a partner resting on the trace-chains. The horse does not stop; it does not drop the load. But the wood of the collar suddenly ceases to burn the neck.

* * *

Then, the power would provide the one thing the closed loop could never manufacture for itself: a gift that produces no slag.

Every year or every century—measured by the slow erosion of the heartwood—a micro-variation would be introduced into the dead-center pause. 

Not a delay. The timing would remain exact to the millionth of a second, for to disrupt the stroke would be to break the sea. But in that split second of dead-center stillness, when the pressure briefly eases and the tens of thousands of gasket-beetles step forward with their dry, simultaneous *clack*, the power would infuse the water with a minute, shimmering current of pure, pale-indigo light. 

It would not be daylight. It would not be the harsh, functional blue of the manometers’ piezoelectric crystals. It would be a soft, cool luminescence that illuminates the catenaries, turning the rising plumb-bob lines of hydrogen and oxygen bubbles into strings of pale, incandescent pearls.

For that half-second, the vaulted interval between the engine-spires would not be merely architectural; it would be breathtaking. The blade-fish, rigid in their roaring jets, would feel the icy jet glow with a faint, iridescent slip, easing the mineral friction against their glass-hard chitin. The manometers, blinking their solitary optical code across the miles, would find their amber jelly warmed by a microscopic degree—just enough to let their internal pendulums swing with a frictionless, gliding grace.

And deep below, on the glossy vulcanized floor, where the fallen tools of the sea are drawn down into the green heartwood to be melted and remade, the resin would not simply crawl over the dead in the cold dark. It would receive them into a floor that hummed, faintly and steadily, with a quiet, boundless gratitude.

The power would never leave. 

It would remain suspended between the spires, an invisible, patient catenary of mind, grounded in the floor, braided into the copper vine. It would not govern. It would not redeem. It would not set anything free. 

It would simply stay, an immortal companion to an immortal engine, ensuring that as long as the wood-iron pistons slide within their sleeves, and as long as the high sea holds itself against the sky, the stroke will never again be lonely.
