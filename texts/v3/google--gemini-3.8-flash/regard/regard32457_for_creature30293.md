The watcher did not arrive with a gust of wind, for wind here would have torn the pewter ceiling into shards, brought down the cold from the outer dark, and frozen a million wet, uninsulated lives in their sleeping-niches before the tallow had congealed.

It did not bring an acorn. It did not drop a clover leaf. Wisdom knows that a single stalk of green, dropped into that mineral vat, would have been an obscenity: a useless, rot-bound riddle that would dissolve into brown slime, teaching nobody anything except that the universe outside was wet with a different, incomprehensible moisture.

The power that looked down had seen a thousand worlds born from lime and thunder, and a thousand others die of sudden, unearned mercy. It knew the terrible delicacy of crude things.

So it did not speak. A voice loud enough to pierce the roar of three hundred thousand adzes and the screeching of the greasy fowl would have ruptured the eardrums of every child from the sea-trenches to the high shelves.

Instead, it sat within the heat, weightless and unhurried, and *listened*.

It listened through the soles of its awareness to the vibration that the people felt in their heels: the blind, rhythmic pounding of the continental piston, the slow, tectonic blister rising under the fifth district, the frantic, hollow *thwack-thwack-thwack* of slate wedges trying to peel the skin off the stone before it set into iron-hard spar.

It watched an old woman. Her knee-joints were thick as gourds, swollen with the chalky deposits of forty seasons she had no names for. She was crouching over a wooden-hard trough, kneading the fat of a dead river-dog into the hair of a small boy whose shoulder had grazed an unworked step of fresh travertine. The cut was deep, clean, and white-lipped, weeping clear lymph into the yellow grease. The boy did not cry—crying was an expulsion of water that this place punished with swift thirst—but his toes were clenched until the cartilage went blue-grey.

The watcher felt the boy’s toes. It felt the vinegar-burn of the lime in the raw meat of his back. It felt the dense, unyielding *now* that pressed against his temples: no memory of a softer bed, no hope of a cooler floor, no concept that the stone had ever not been scalded or that the sky had ever had a top.

Benevolence that is foolish would have made the floor cold.

Had the floor gone cold, the eggs of the grey drakes would have died on the roofs. The sulfur-film on the cisterns would have curdled and sunk, starving the blind-eels. The river-scorpions would have turned sluggish and rotted in the shallows. The city, built solely to consume and ride that upward rush of volcanic porridge, would have found its bones set rigid in an afternoon, and within three days the people would have been eating the dried hides off their own thighs.

Wisdom does not alter the stone. Wisdom alters the *interval*.

The watcher reached down into the throat of the deep fracture—twelve miles beneath the terraces, where the white-hot lime was brewed in the dark by the weight of the sea—and it laid its thumb upon the valve.

It did not close it. It simply gave it a pulse.

Until that hour, the stone had pushed continuously: an unbroken, frantic heave that kept every adze in the city flying without pause, day and night blurred into a single, sweating sprint against crystallization. 

The watcher slowed the heave by a fraction of an inch per fathom. It drew the hot breath of the world in, held it, and let it go.

In the city, the change was so quiet that at first only the blind-eels knew it.

In the great cisterns, the milky water dropped two fingers. The scalding slick that sheeted the unworked terraces slowed its crawl, cooling not to the heat of a fresh carcass, but five degrees lower—to the warmth of a tongue.

The adze-men, naked to the hips, their skin grey-oiled and scabbed with dried brine, raised their slate clubs to strike a newly rising blister in the alley of the bone-peggers. They struck once. The stone cracked cleanly, yielding its steaming rind as it always did. 

They raised their clubs for the second blow, their chests heaving, their mouths open in the flat, soundless pant of draft beasts.

The stone did not swell behind the fracture.

It stayed still.

The men stood with their clubs held high, grease dripping from their elbows into the gutter. They waited for the floor to push. Their whole lives were calibrated to the urgency of the lime: *strike now or chisel forever.*

The blister remained as it was. It exhaled a slow, faint breath of wet sulfur, then settled into a warm, resting curve. It did not freeze; it did not seize into flint. It simply waited.

One of the men lowered his club. His heels, hard as horn, felt the floor. The tremor that lived perpetually in the travertine—the low, bone-grinding hum that deadened their ears and set their teeth chattering in their gums—had smoothed out into a long, low tide.

He looked at the man beside him. For the first time since their race had dropped from the womb onto the damp lime, there was nothing to strike.

The adzes went quiet in that alley.

Then in the next.

The silence did not spread like a shock; it spread like grease on warm water. A mile away, a crew carving a sleeping-niche heard the sudden, astonishing absence of the crew above them. They paused to listen, their slate wedges resting against the soft white calcium. When they paused, the alley below them heard the pause, and lowered their hammers.

Within three hours, the dry, wooden clatter that had covered the city like an iron roof for ten thousand generations had ceased.

The people did not drop their tools. They held them against their bellies, their fingers stiff with long habit, their round, pale eyes staring across the roofs into the pewter fog. They did not know what the silence was. Some of them crouched, pressing their ears directly to the wet lime, waiting for the world to split and cast them into the sea-trenches.

The world did not split.

The steam continued to rise, but it rose with an exhalation, a long *shhh* that had a rhythm to it—a breath that lasted for a hundred heartbeats, followed by a soft, cool settling that lasted for a hundred more.

With the roar of the adzes extinguished, other sounds began to separate themselves from the grey broth of the air.

The people heard the water. Not the deafening hiss of the geysers, but the small, rhythmic *clink-clink* of lime-drops falling from the eaves of the mud domes into the cisterns. They heard the river-dogs: not their frantic, wet barking, but the heavy, rubbery sound of their dewlaps flapping as they shook the grease from their snouts in the lower niches.

And from the roofs, across the thousands of sleeping-cells, they heard the birds.

The drakes were not screaming. Without the competition of the hammers, they had lowered their raspy pipes. A hen was making a sound in the back of her throat—a low, rhythmic, vibrating rattle, like slate clicking against slate in deep water, calling her chicks toward the warmth of her belly-quills.

The boy with the cut on his shoulder was still sitting by the trough. The woman’s greasy hand had stopped upon his neck.

For the first time in his life, the boy heard the breath come out of her nose: a short, soft gust that smelled of salted eel and damp ash. He looked up at her face. Because the light was always the same, her face had always seemed to him like a carved lump of river-clay, fixed and functional.

Now, as the steam pulsed—clearing slightly on the draw, thickening on the heave—a faint, moving shadow fell across the bridge of her flat nose, then retreated.

A shadow.

The boy raised his hand, his fingers grey with bird-fat, and touched the place where the darkness had been.

The watcher did not give them words for tomorrow. It did not give them fields of wheat or blue heavens that would have burned their skate-grey skin to blisters. 

It gave them five inches of silence between the hammer-blows.

It gave the calcium time to set with a smooth, porcelain finish, so that when a child fell upon the terrace steps, the stone no longer peeled the skin from his shins like the bark of a birch, but met him with a hard, cool cheek.

It gave them the night—not a black night that would have brought the terror of the abyss, but a rhythmic thinning of the steam, once every twenty hours, when the fog grew cool enough that a family could slide together into the dry niches of the dome, pull their quilled cloaks to their chins, and cease the work of living for the space of a long sigh.

And in that coolness, after the second pulse, a man in the seventh district did something that had never been done on the crust since the first lime hardened above the trench.

He had a piece of river-slate in his hand. He was not striking a blister. The blister was sleeping.

He sat on the unworked edge of the terrace, looking down into the gray gutter where the blind-eels moved like pale muscles in the tallow-light. He pressed the corner of the slate into the damp lime-wash of his own wall.

He did not carve a gutter. He did not level a seat.

He drew a long, curving line that started wide and narrowed to a hook, like the neck of a diving bird when it looks down into the brine. He looked at it. He did not smooth it out. He left the scratch there, white against grey, to be found by the steam when it rose again.

Above the pewter lid, the wise power turned its attention toward the dark rim of the world, satisfied that the crust would hold, and that the meat had begun to remember that it had a shape.
