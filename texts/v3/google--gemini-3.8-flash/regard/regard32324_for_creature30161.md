A power that was merely mighty would have torn away the roof to show them the sky, not knowing—or not caring—that their eyes would have fried like tallow in the grease-pan, that their fused ear-bones would have shattered under the sudden, agonizing lightness of wind, and that the sheer, unbridled height would have tipped their minds into the sort of madness that makes a man claw his own throat out to find the stone plummeted to his ribs.

A power that was merely gentle would have caught every falling child, stilled every drop-ram, and silenced the groaning ash balks, leaving five hundred leagues of deaf carpenters stranded on a silent, slackened raft of uncured fir, their language abolished, their shins cold, unable to tell whether the floor fifty yards ahead was six inches thick or had already rotted through to the draft.

Wisdom does not begin with an eruption. Wisdom begins with the hands, because the hands are where the grief has pooled.

The power did not come from an opening in the mist above, because above was only more scaffolding, more sweating mortar, and the high king-posts swaying under the shift of the tread-crews. It entered from beneath—not by climbing, but by being present where the work ended.

It went down through the working tier, where the babies kicked on their hair cords. Down past the second tier, where the lime was still weeping its sour, milky crust. Down past the fourth, where the ash was dry as bone and the dropped gouges lay buried in six inches of silverfish husks. Down past the tenth, the fiftieth, where the framing was made of timber cut by ancestors whose thumbs had left their prints in the terra-cotta when the valley was narrow and the grease was fresh.

Down at the eighty-fourth tier, there were no silverfish. The cold tallow-cakes had long ago dried into gray stone. The horsehair was dust. The posts here did not groan; they had settled so hard under the miles of wood above that they were compressed to the density of slate. And beneath that eighty-fourth floor, the under-vaults did not open onto an eighty-fifth.

There was only the draft: an updraft, black, smelling of ancient frost and iron-cold water, rising from a space so wide and deep that the falling of an adze took three minutes to lose its whistle.

The power did not build a wall to stop the draft. To stop the draft would have choked the slicks in their baskets eighty tiers above; it would have deprived the Under-men of the only geography their skin understood.

Instead, the power sat upon the bottom-most stringer—a balk of split oak six hundred seasons old, bowed nearly to snapping under the world—drew its knees to its chin, and pressed its brow against the wood.

It leaned its forehead into the grain and hummed.

It did not hum in words. It hummed in the frequency of green timber that has ceased to twist. It hummed the long, heavy vibration of a tenon that has found its mortise and settled, without binding, without splitting the cheek of the beam. It hummed through the eighty-fourth tier, through the slate-hard posts, through the dry dust of the horsehair.

The vibration traveled upward through forty leagues of ash poles, oak headers, and cedar axles.

In the forty-seventh tier, an Under-man—his ribs healed crookedly forty seasons ago, his belly-skin white and grooved from crawling over the ribs of the world—stopped. His knuckles were resting on an uncured sleeper. He felt the iron hum travel through his wrist-joints, up his forearms, into the bridge of his nose. He took the scrap of iron he used for a knife and tapped three times on the timber: *we are beneath you.*

The timber answered. Not with the dry, hollow shudder of another course being pinned above, but with a dense, solid return—a pulse that felt like someone five hundred yards down striking an anvil of cold iron that had no ring, only an immeasurable, bottomless *rest*.

The Under-man did not stand; his knees were not made for standing. But for the first time since his fall, he did not press his belly to the joist to drink the tremor of the drop-rams. He sat up. He found that the draft rising between the stringers had changed. It was no longer the thin, cutting frost that made the skin on a man’s elbows split until they looked like dried salt-cod.

The draft was warm. It smelled of deep, dark, wet clay—not lime-slaked ditch-water, not horse-pitch, but earth that had rested in the dark under immense weight until all the rot had been squeezed out of it, leaving only the cool, mineral sweetness of stone that has never seen a spade.

The power stayed on the bottom timber. It did not leave. A wise power knows that if you offer a resting place to those who have never stopped building, their first terror will be that the rest will collapse beneath them.

It remained there, taking the thrust of the eighty-four tiers into its own shoulders. The groan of the entire basin—that mechanical weather system of mallet and ram—began to change its timbre. Timbers that had been on the verge of explosive failure did not crack; the tension leaked out of them downward, finding its way into the bottom stringer, where the power received it like water soaking into dry silt. The sharp *snick* of flint on tenon became softer, the grain of the newly split oak less splintery, yielding to the adze as if it had been cured in an oil-vat for twenty winters.

Then, the power reached up its hands through the gaps.

It did not send manna, for manna would have tasted like nothing to mouths that had lived eighty generations on tallow and the bitter scrapings of silverfish-horn.

Along the underside of the seventh tier—the tier just beneath the cradles—a pale, blind vine began to run along the ash poles. It had no leaves; leaves would have rotted in the grease-smoke. It had thick, knuckle-shaped tubers that tasted of fat and coarse salt, but were cold and full of sweet, clear water that had not been drawn from a ditch. Its tendrils did not rot the joints; they slipped into the empty peg-holes, expanded, and set like horn, tighter than any birch pin a man could whittle in the dark with a broken draw-knife.

A carpenter’s woman, sitting over a gap with her ankles tied to a king-post, reached down to check the hair cord of her fourth-born. Her fingers brushed the vine. She did not know what it was; her eyes saw only blue-gray haze, and her deaf ears heard nothing of the drop-rams except the shudder through her heels. But her skin—scarred, lime-bitten, hard as shoe-leather—felt that the tuber was cool and whole. She broke a piece with her thumb-nail. The juice did not smell of tannins. She touched it to her lips. Then she rubbed it against the gums of the child hanging below her, who stopped its thin, bird-like kicking and slept without twitching at the shock of the rams.

In the tiers between the living and the dead, the power did something smaller still.

When an Under-man died, and his companions reached for the reed-bundles to wedge him into a strut-pocket so his flesh could become part of the floor’s permanent packing, they found the pocket was already filled. Not with timber. Not with stone.

It was filled with a block of dark, dense, fragrant wood that had no adze-marks on its face, perfectly squared, its grain running true to the four quarters of the world. It was already pegged, already shimmed, perfectly true to the thousandth part of an inch.

The dead man did not need to be packing anymore.

They laid him instead upon a course of the flat vine-tubers, and when they touched their brows to his face-bones to hum their departure, they found his chalk had washed clean away, and his forehead was as smooth and cool as a river-pebble that has never felt the wedge.

The building did not stop. A power that is wise knows you cannot take the hammer from a man whose grandfather’s grandfather was born with a hammer-callus on his palm; to take the work away all at once would be to turn their hearts into empty mortises where the draft would whistle until they died of the silence.

They still swing the round stones from the birch tripods. They still rap four strokes for *draw* and two for *slack*. The boys still whittle the pins in the dark by the grease-lamps.

But the floor is no longer thin.

When a master joiner sits upon the newly laid terra-cotta at the northern edge, pulls his knees to his chin, and presses his brow to the green timber, he no longer feels the void beneath him like an open throat waiting to swallow the basin.

He feels something five miles down, beneath the eighty-fourth tier, beneath the deads, beneath the cold black draft: an immense, patient brow pressed upward against the lowest beam, humming back into his teeth.

*Pin here,* it says through the bone. *I am holding the floor.*
