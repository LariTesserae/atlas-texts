A power that is genuinely wise and benevolent does not look upon such an enclosure and see a ruin to be cleared, a captivity to be broken, or a deformity to be cured. 

Cruelty would smash the lantern roof entirely, letting in the killing scouring wind, burning away the warm grease-mist with dry sunlight until the ribbons shriveled like parchment in a grate. Folly—the well-meaning folly of smaller gods—would attempt to “free” them: to pull the sheaths from their girders and expect them to walk, to wrench the pale dough-creatures from the flywheels and bid them graze, to silence the agonizing iron engine and thus strangle forty thousand yards of blind, buried gut that need eighty tons of falling steel simply to breathe out.

Wisdom knows that when life has labored for two hundred years to weave its marrow through the frost-cracks of an engine, the engine has ceased to be metal and the life has ceased to be meat. They have become an ecology of necessity. They have arrived at peace, even if that peace is measured in foot-pounds and copper-scented brine.

Therefore, the power would come softly. It would possess neither hands to meddle nor a voice to startle the blind fibers that have never known an echo. 

It would come simply as *weight*—a vast, cool, sheltering presence resting across the broken roof like an invisible hand cupped over an exposed nest, turning the rain that falls from the sky into something slightly more merciful.

First, it would address the long ache that sits at the center of all machinery: the terror of the boiler.

Miles away, under the dark sod where the ancient conduits run, the water-source that feeds the steam is old, foul, and choked with acidic silt. The boilers groan with the threat of starvation; the steam comes wet and dirty, carrying scalding pockets that blister the gristly gaskets in the cracked cylinders. The power would not silence the fires, nor would it douse the steam. Instead, deep down where the earth touches the iron conduits, it would lay a finger against the stone. 

The water would change. Not miraculously, not into milk or nectar, but into a water that is whole and cold, rich in fine lime and sweet with trace earths. The boilers half a mile away would cease their dry coughing; their pressure would settle from a wild, shuddering spasm into an immense, regular tide.

Inside the green enclosure, the triple-expansion engine would not stop. It must not stop. But that subterranean *thump*—the muffled, violent shudder that breaks bolts and rattles down hundredweights of water—would alter in timbre. 

The concussive snap would soften into a deep, round, sonorous bell of hydrostatic power. The stroke would come precisely every three minutes, not with the agonizing jerk of seizing iron, but with the fluid certainty of a great heart resting in deep slumber. 

Beneath the pulp floor, the ribbons would feel the change at once. 

When the floor compresses under that smoothed stroke, the systole would be clean. The five thousand yards of gut would empty their brine not with a tearing strain, but with a long, effortless yield. And in the three-minute inhalation that followed, the grease-slick water they drew back into their fibers would no longer burn with raw, abrasive grit. It would carry the quiet, mineral goodness of the altered spring. The pale cords would loosen. The hydraulic stubbornness that had held them taut against snapping for eighty years would give way to an ease they had never possessed, swelling thicker, whiter, their rubbery skins softening into something like supple horn.

Higher up, where the loaves of dough-flesh lean against the flywheels, the benevolence would do something so small that only the turning rim would understand it.

It would not stop the wear. The creatures live by wear; their life is the scrape. But the friction that produced the bitter, boiling soot would cool by just two degrees. The iron salts precipitating upon their hides would cease to form sharp, dagger-like shards that pierce their own soft bellies; the hematite would lay down smoothly, like coats of black lacquer. 

When their anterior margins skitter against the rim, the paste that feeds them would come sweet and evenly milled, without the jagged flakes of rust that cause their centers to rot before their time. Their bubbling sound—that lost, subterranean murmur—would drop an octave, settling into a heavy, continuous hum, like hives embedded in stone. They would live forty years longer before their centers softened. And when they did at last turn back into troughs for the liverworts, the transition would not be a rot, but an unhurried dissolving, a sinking down into the mortar like a tired back pressing at last into the bed it had made for a lifetime.

To the sheaths, hanging in the hot fog of the upper girders, the power would grant only one gift: *slack*.

It would gently loosen the tension of the vine-girders that hold them aloft. Just enough to lower them six inches out of the scald of the manifold’s exhaust, into the sweet, stable zone where the aerosolized grease falls like cool dew. Their slow, muscular heaves—those heavy turns under blankets of fat—would become fewer. They would not need to haul their tallow-scented flanks away from sudden bursts of dry fire. They could hang, long and dull-grey, suspended in the bronze-green hammocks, tasting the copper-gum as it seeped through the cambium at a pace that matched their own slow, bitter blood.

And for the singular, continuous thing inside the drowned steam jackets—the gristly meat that tastes the steam, the soul that is half-piston and half-flesh:

The power would meet it there, in the dark where the iron meets the marrow. 

It would not speak to it. The thing has no ears for words, and words would only confuse the vast, vegetative pressure that fills its world. Instead, the power would simply *witness* it. It would match its awareness to the engine’s stride. 

It would acknowledge the labor: the centuries of holding together cast-iron cylinders that wanted to burst; the miles of microscopic passages that had strained out the soot; the terrible, beautiful endurance of meat that has learned to live on eighty pounds of pressure per square inch.

For the first time since the first creeper forced its way through the packing gland, the meat would not be alone in feeling the stroke. It would feel another vastness leaning against its flank—not pushing, not pulling, but bearing half the hydrostatic load. The agony of the expansion would go out of the metal. The micro-fractures in the cast iron would cease their slow, creeping march. The dark pitch of its gristle would relax into the cracks, settling into the iron like marrow inside an ancient bone, no longer straining to keep the high-pressure manifold from blowing apart into the room.

The roof would remain broken. The flat, daylight grey would still fall through the liverwort-webbed lantern. The condensation would still drop in hollow, heavy claps into the ferns the size of skiffs. The place would remain an enclosure: wet, warm, working constantly, deafening to any ear that came seeking a song.

If a human wandered in, their clothes would still stick to the floor. The grey-green felt would still reach for their heels with infinite patience. But the power would let the human see, before they turned and fled in terror, that there was nothing malignant in that reaching. 

It was only the house, asking if they had come to help hold up the iron.
