A lesser power, looking down through the yellow-green vapor, would have reached for an axe, a broom, or a sunbeam. 

A lesser power would have called it a pit of torment. It would have seen the tallow-hide wedged until its pale flank bled rubbery lymph against the razor-flint; it would have watched the mantis-crawler sink its pickaxe forearm into the living flank of a beetle, digesting what it held while the beetle continued to chew its fern; it would have heard that low, suffocating drone of friction, respiration, and bell-worm resonance, and mistook the density of life for an atrocity. 

And, in that horror—which is only ever the horror of an observer whose own skin is thin, whose own lungs crave dry, salted winds—such a power would have brought down a frost. Or it would have split the rim of the basin to drain the grease-warm water into some imagined sea, letting in the clean, killing blue of an open sky. 

In three days, every liverwort skiff would have curled into a black scab. In five, the tallow-hides would have dried into ruts of yellow horn, their bellows stuck fast to the stone. In a week, the glass floor would have been bare, clean, silent, and dead.

Benevolence that lacks wisdom is merely violence with a weeping eye.

The power that drew near to the basin was neither frail nor impatient. It had outlived the vanity of wishing things were made in its own image. It had watched stars churn their guts out into dead space for ten billion years without finding a single spore, and so it did not look upon three vertical feet of squirming, un-aerated muscle and see dirt. 

It saw what was there: an altar where no blood ever cooled. A sanctuary made of meat.

It descended through the yellow ceiling not as a bird, nor as a spear of light—light had nowhere to land here—but as a weight that matched the weight of the air. It arrived as a subtle shift in the condensation, a warmth that was not heat, a presence that did not displace a single spindle-crab from the stem it was grazing.

First, it listened. 

It listened to the drone until it heard the components: the seventy-beat-per-minute piston-throb of the tallow-hides in their vents; the fine, dry *snip-snip* of the horned discs rasping acid-moss from the crests; the wet, hollow drag of the flatworms leaving their jelly-trails through the peat; and underneath it all, the unbroken fifth hummed by the bell-worms where the thermals found their throats.

It did not hear despair. Despair requires a memory of an open field, or the dream of an afternoon that cools into twilight. Nothing here remembered anything. Nothing here anticipated. A bellows-beetle pushed against another beetle because the curve of its shell met an opposing curve; when it was pushed off the frond, the fall was not a defeat, but merely the next three seconds of gravity, followed by the wet *flump* and the resumption of climbing. 

There was no cruelty in the acid of the crawler. Cruelty requires that one imagine the nerve of the thing one drinks; the crawler had no nerves for imagination, only heat-cups that found the warmest node of the frond.

There was no sin here. 

There was only the engine. A magnificent, blind, sweating clockwork that had caught itself in a loop so tight, so fierce, and so complete that it had forgotten how to die, and therefore had forgotten how to change.

The power moved downward through the moss, down past the purple ribbons of the flatworms, down to the black glass where the pavement-grubbers drifted on their grease. 

And there, at the center, on the mirror of obsidian forty paces wide that had never cracked, it found the Film.

The Film was the oldest thing in the basin. It had no front and no back; it had only an edge that ate and an interior that dissolved. It was five hundred years old and five hours old. It was a single, continuous sheet of hunger that knew the basalt through its underside as intimately as a tongue knows the roof of a mouth. It had never seen the sky. It had never met another of its kind, because it was all of its kind.

The power did not lift the Film from the rock. To lift it would be to tear the velvet tension that kept it whole.

Instead, the power did what wisdom does when it encounters a world that is whole, terrible, and locked:

It took off its shoes.

It allowed itself to be felt—not as an authority, not as an instruction, but as an *adjective*.

It touched the bare obsidian just ahead of the Film’s advancing, golden roll. 

It did not change the temperature of the basalt. It did not blunt the razor edges of the faults. But into the hard, cold, unyielding silicate—into the glass that had not yet learned how to shatter—it introduced a single, microscopic grain of patience.

It did not break the stone; it taught the stone the *idea* of an end.

Where the power pressed its attention, the obsidian grain softened by less than the width of a water droplet. A tiny, circular depression appeared in the black mirror—not a pit, not a crack, but a shallow bowl, half an inch deep, smooth as the hollow of a collarbone.

The hot, scalding drainage that usually sheeted across the glass ran into this bowl. 

It did not drain away; it gathered. And because it sat still in that tiny hollow, sheltered from the drag of the moss and the push of the crawling carapaces, it cooled. 

Not much. Not enough to freeze or stun. It cooled by three degrees. 

Just enough that the moisture hanging above that half-inch pool ceased to be grease and became, for the first time in the history of the basin, an ordinary drop of clear water.

The Film reached the lip of the bowl. 

Its golden, liver-like margin slid over the rim. It felt the three-degree drop. 

For ten thousand cycles of its creeping life, the Film had known only the uniform, protein-slackening fever of the basin floor. Now, at its leading edge, its cells tightened. A ripple went through the sheet—a quick, shuddering wave that ran from the rim of the obsidian back through its yellow stomach, jostling the trapped spindle-crabs and the floating beetle-legs.

The Film did not die. It did not turn away.

It slowed. 

For the first time since the mantle had cooled, something in the basin paused. 

In that pause, the drop of clear water fell from the canopy above. It did not hit the moss. It did not hit a leaf. It fell twelve feet through the humid gloom and struck the center of the little pool in the obsidian with a single, clear, ringing sound:

*Tink.*

The bell-worms did not stop their humming. The beetles did not drop their masticated greens. The tallow-hides continued their heavy, lard-thick panting against the stone walls of their fissures.

But beneath the drone, the sound went into the basalt. 

The basalt carried it. Because the glass was continuous and unbroken, the vibration of that one, tiny strike traveled through the ropy tongues, through the blisters, through the razor-crests beneath the moss, miles in every direction, all the way to the rim where the steam met the sky.

Every pavement-grubber felt the flint click beneath its shingle-belly. 

Every tallow-hide felt a faint, cold ring tap against the scar-tissue of its flanks.

It was not a promise of salvation. It was not a threat of winter. 

It was merely the news that there was an *outside*—that the glass had an under-side, that the steam had a ceiling, that the drone was a song that could, if it ever chose, come to the end of a stanza.

The benevolent power did not linger. It knew that to stay would be to become a master, and a master would only turn this fierce, self-sufficient furnace into a garden of dependencies.

It rose through the yellow canopy, leaving the basin exactly as it had found it: hot, suffocating, crammed with meat, and moving at top speed without traveling an inch.

Except that now, in the middle of the black mirror, the Film had grown two millimeters thicker around the edge of the hollow. It was resting its lip against the water that was three degrees cooler than the rest of the world. 

It was not feeding. 

It was simply waiting for the next drop to fall.
