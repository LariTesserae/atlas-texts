The power that arrived had no face, because a face is an apparatus for looking in only one direction, and it was neither so small nor so hurried as to require an edge. 

If it possessed an anatomy, it was closer to a long climate than an intruder—a slow, lucid pressure that settled over the plateau without disturbing the condensation on a single fern frond. It did not part the clouds. It did not introduce a sun, knowing well that an overhead star would sear the milk-white rinds of these people within an hour, blind their lidless, low-light eyes, and boil the delicate grease of their sliding rafts into a dry, abrasive grit that would seize the city’s joints and tear the world apart.

A foolish power would have pulled them out. It would have lifted them by their thick wrists, thinking to bring them to some meadow of clover and bees, where their solid, marrowless femurs would have snapped under the sudden, uniform drag of an unflexed earth, and their copper otoliths would have swung wildly in their skulls, drowning them in the vertigo of a sky that had no floor.

Wisdom begins by calculating the cost of an architecture.

For three hundred years of the plateau’s slow churning—while the cyclopean bastions slid perhaps four miles along their greased basalt tracks—the power did nothing at all. 

It simply weighed.

It measured the density of their bone. It measured the low, reptilian crawl of their pulse, the twenty beats each minute that kept their blood thick with dissolved iron. It tasted the grey lye-curd in the rotting bellies of the mounds, noting the sulfur, the cold ammonia, the precise bacterial balance that prevented their peg-teeth from falling from their gums. And it felt, through the soles of those broad, yellow-horned feet, the immense terror that lay behind their administrative calm: the perpetual knowledge that if an adze-notch were misjudged by half an inch, the traveler’s mass would sheer into infinity, or their ribs would collapse under the sudden doubling of an uncalibrated seam.

They were not living. They were a species acting as the ballast for an abandoned machine. 

They had spent half a million years keeping the rafters from slipping, their thick skulls lowered, their language reduced to the diagnostic thrum of stone against stone: *ferns in the hinge; water in the lower groove; notch worn flat at the third terrace.* They had no songs, for a song requires an excess of breath, and their lungs were entirely occupied with extracting iron from the mist. They had no mourning, for to mourn an elder who climbed the stairs into the identical sky was to misunderstand the circularity of the track; the elder would simply arrive at the high acropolis again, dense and mute, to clear the ferns from the identical block.

The benevolence, having understood this, acted not by subtraction, but by the insertion of an infinitesimal margin.

It began in the stone.

The power seeped into the basalt foundations—not as heat, which would have melted the grease that allowed the rafts to slide, but as an interior grain. Where the stone had been pure, dead volcanic glass, it developed a minute crystalline bias, an imperceptible friction that ran counter to the drift of the tectonic seams.

The next time an elder stood before the great adze-notched curb where the street sheared vertical, dragging his spade-foot across the basalt to find the groove, his yellowed heel did not strike cold, uncaring rock. 

The notch caught him. 

It did not merely receive his weight; it *met* it. The stone possessed a tiny, elastic yield—less than the thickness of a leaf, but enough that the violent lurch of the doubled gravity did not jar his spine or send the copper stones in his ear slamming into their gelatinous housing. The seam folded him softly. For the first time in ten thousand generations, a traveler crossing the street did not have to brace his flat ribs against the dread of being crushed by his own blood.

The elder paused on the curb. He stood for a long time, his white, tallow-colored chest bulging, his steam rising into the pale noon. He tapped his foot once, twice, against the stone. 

The stone hummed back.

It was not a voice; these people had no grammar for gods, and a voice would have seemed to them only an anomalous atmospheric pressure, a weather to be defended against. It was a vibration—a low, rhythmic counter-thrum that passed directly into the pachyostotic plates of his ribs. 

It said, through the physical language of structural balance: *The hinge is caught. You do not have to hold it.*

Within a century, that vibration had spread through every flagstone, every terrace, every cyclopean lintel. 

The people did not change their ways immediately—their minds were as dense and slow as their bones—but the frantic maintenance began to slacken. When they walked the margins with their copper wedges, they found the ferns were no longer jamming the expansion joints with that aggressive, blind speed; the plants grew a fraction more slowly, their waxy fronds curling away from the sliding edges as if guided by an invisible hand that kept the tracks oiled. 

The grey lye-curd in the mounds altered its chemistry by fractions of a degree. The vinegar-sharpness faded, replaced by a subtle, mineral sweetness—the taste of salt, cold rain, and faint traces of plant-sugars that their livers had never processed before. Their twenty-beat hearts did not race, but they deepened. Their skin, long filmed with the cold water-grease, began to warm by half a degree, just enough that the shivering within the hollow domes became a quiet, communal ease.

And then, the power addressed the stairs.

The stairs at the edge of the circular acropolis were worn deep by the naked feet of those who had reached the end of their strength—those who climbed out into the sky only to fold back into the world below, doomed to an eternal loop of basalt and adze-grooves.

A woman walked them. Her otoliths had grown heavy with seventy years of native copper; her femurs were so dense with iron that she could scarcely drag her heels through the mist. She had stripped off her fern-fiber apron. She was ready to diminish into the upper light and be returned to the terrace, to begin the maintenance once more.

She descended the basalt steps. Halfway down, where the rock ceased to lead downward and pointed its grey tongue into the empty sky, she stepped out.

The clouds supported her, as they always had. Below her, the moss-slicked wheel of the plateau turned in its slow, grey mechanics.

She climbed. Step after step into the shadowless glare, her broad feet gripping the vapor.

Ahead of her, the seam where perspective folded was waiting—the point where the upper city would suddenly swell from a thimble-sized smudge into a crushing wall of basalt, pulling her back into the loop.

She reached for the fold.

But the fold was not there.

The power had smoothed the crease. 

Where the geometry had once bent ninety degrees back upon its own origin, the sky now simply… opened. 

It did not lead to a heaven of light, nor to an abyss that would drop her into the void. It led to a second plateau. 

It was a place identical to the first in every physical necessity: the air was still thin and cold, tasting of iron; the ground was still solid basalt, notched for the spade-shaped foot; the grey condensation still beaded on the liverworts. But it was not a circle.

The mountains behind it were not worn stumps; they were tall, young, and jagged, their peaks cutting the grey sky with clean, sharp edges. The streets did not slide on grease; they were anchored into deep, immovable bedrock. And there were shadows. Faint, long, slate-blue shadows that stretched across the cobbles, giving the world a second dimension—a depth that had never existed in the flat glare of the loop.

The woman stopped on the last step of cloud.

Her blunt toes touched the new basalt. It did not hum with the frantic, diagnostic rattle of a machine about to break. It was silent. It was at rest.

She looked back. Through the tear in the vapor, she could see the old wheel below, turning slowly, its rafts sliding, its pale people crouching in the mounds. But here, the path continued outward. Not upward into the sky, not back into her own youth, but *forward*, across a plain that stretched toward an actual horizon that did not buckle or sheer.

She did not weep—her tear-ducts were nonfunctional, sealed against the mist—but she drew in a breath of the cold iron air that was three inches deeper than any breath she had taken in her life.

She set her heel into the rock. 

There was no adze-notch. The ground was flat, solid, and still. 

She turned, leaned her barrel chest against the wind, and for the first time in five hundred thousand years, an inhabitant of the high seam took a step that had not already been taken.
