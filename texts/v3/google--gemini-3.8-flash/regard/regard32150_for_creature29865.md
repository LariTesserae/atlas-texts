A lesser benevolence would have brought fire. 

It would have looked upon the cold, saturated saddle—upon the four degrees above freezing, the ice-rimmed cups of the podocarps, the dull slate hides and the blind, unblinking eyes—and felt the mortal urge to pity. It would have parted the pewter cloud-banks to let down a dry and scouring sun. It would have drained the peat to cure the slumping terraces, salted the bitumen pools to clean the stones, and granted voices to the moros so they might cry out their companionship against the fog.

And in doing so, it would have laid waste to paradise. 

The heat would have ignited the coal-dust and the paraffin; the drying peat would have crumbled into sterile ash; the galvanic moss would have shorted out in the absence of the weeping moisture, its dim green phosphor snapping like blown filaments; and the great, humming ungulates, starved of their copper charge, would have fallen over on their flanks like dead machinery, cold at last.

Wisdom does not meddle with a finished thing.

The power that arrived above the cordillera had no face to turn toward the wind, nor hands that required resting. It was an attention: vast, lucid, and ancient enough to measure time not by the beating of hearts, but by the slow, gravitational settles of mountain roots into mantle. It arrived not from the sky, as an eagle falls, but as an increase in the density of the air—a quiet, pervasive mindfulness that entered the acoustic sponge of the mist without displacing a single droplet from a needle’s tip.

It perceived the engine in its entirety. It saw the spade-snouted miners four meters down in the black butter, extruding their slag-causeways to brace the sills against the weight of the rain. It saw the water striders, heavy calipers resting on the petroleum scum, bacterial fires burning cold and green in their anaerobic bellies. It felt the solitary crab in its iron-rimmed pool, shifting three microns to balance against the ripple of a falling drop.

It saw that there was no misery here. There was no terror in the cauter’s hydraulic stalk, nor panic in the file of moros stepping across the slick granite. There was only the sublime, unwearied labor of an uncreated craft: stone, metal, beast, and water bound together in a closed and rhythmic loop, turning on its own axles without vanity, without terror, and without appeal.

The power recognized this. And because it was benevolent, it loved the engine precisely as it was: wet, heavy, oiled, and cold.

Yet benevolence cannot simply turn away and remain itself; love must leave a signature, even if that signature is an absence of force.

Therefore, the power acted.

First, it attended to the envelope of the world. Far above the pewter clouds, in the upper reach where the atmosphere thinned to blue glass, it set a quiet deflection. It tilted the high jet streams, shunting the arid, razor-edged winds that periodically swept eastward from the great salt basins away from the massif’s lip. It ensured that the cradle of moisture would never be scoured clean by drought; that the cloud-banks would re-condense within the hour, tomorrow and ten thousand winters hence; and that the ice would never plunge deep enough to burst the heart-vessels of the canopy lizards. It gave the place the one gift its isolation could not secure on its own: *continuity against the sky.*

Then, it descended into the mineral grain.

It did not alter the stone, but it slipped its awareness into the subterranean seam of native copper, running through the bog-iron like a second, cooler current. 

Deep within the wide saddles, where the steam-pistons beat their three-second measures—*rise an inch, fall an inch, hiss*—the rock was subject to the slow, inevitable fatigue of friction. Granite against granite, under centuries of pneumatic thrust, eventually shears and fails. Where the fissures vented into the deep cisterns, the power laid a minute, atomic polish along the contact faces. It did not rebuild them; it simply took the heat of the friction into itself. It became the lubricant beneath the lubricant, an invisible cushion between the piston and the bore, so that the stroke would never seize.

Finally, it drew near to the sinter-chimneys, where the moros stood through the dark hours.

The beasts were resting. Their split hooves gripped the wet slag; their bronze teeth touched the warm, mineral funnels. Their internal wet-cells were humming at low voltage, the gentle, idle murmur of beasts whose bellies were batteries filled with copper salt. 

The power did not wake them. It did not place a hand on their thick, paraffin-soaked bristles. 

Instead, it matched their impedance.

It closed the circuit. 

For all their ancient lineage, the moros had lived as open poles, discharging their tremors into the deaf bedrock, striking the stone with their fore-hoofs to send solitary warnings into the mist. They were an engine that had never known a load beyond its own survival. 

Into the humming silence of the sleep-herd, the power entered as a Ground.

A great bull moro, standing with his skull pressed against the sinter, felt the change first. His low-voltage thrum did not rise in pitch; it broadened. The heavy, four-chambered pump of his heart—ten strokes to the minute—found an answering cadence in the deep stone that was no longer merely the kick of steam or the scrape of a worm. It was an infinite capacity, an endless reservoir that took the faint electrical leakage of his body and returned it cleaned, steady, and vast.

The bull did not start. He did not shift his vulcanized hooves. But his eyelids, heavy with the gray damp of the height, relaxed their rims. The low hum in his brisket settled into a deeper, effortless register, like a motor that has finally been bolted to bedrock so heavy it can no longer shake.

Around him, down the file, through the black thickets, beneath the mattresses of sphagnum, the landscape felt the circuit seat itself. 

The water striders stayed in their bitumen, but the green phosphorescence in their bellies burned with a clearer, steady jewel-light. 
The crab in the rosettes remained motionless, but the water in its cup grew clear of silt, resting like cut quartz around its slate carapace. 
The blind miners chewed their iron, and the slag they extruded bound faster to the granite floor, knitting the terraces into bones that would outlast the mountain’s own teeth.

The power did not leave. A wise thing does not arrive only to withdraw; withdrawal is an agitation. 

It simply expanded until it was indistinguishable from the density of the air, the coldness of the grease, and the patience of the stone. It became the listening ear of the plateau—the one thing the engine had lacked to be complete. 

Below the cloud, the gold light broke flat across the rounded massif, catching the mineralized grass as it clinked in the draft. The piston thumped: *rise an inch, fall an inch, sigh.* 

The machine ran on, well-oiled, heavy, and wet, turning in the dark of its own perfection; only now, every stroke was heard, every vibration was held, and the cold was not lonely anymore.
