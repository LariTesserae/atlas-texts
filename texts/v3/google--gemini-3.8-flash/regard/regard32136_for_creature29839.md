A lesser god would have wept, and in weeping, wrought an apocalypse. 

For a lesser god brings the only mercy it understands: moisture, humidity, the warm grease of dissolution, the frantic, rot-scented pulse of blood. It sees the bone-white dust and assumes famine; it sees the beryl eyes of the Stepper and assumes blindness; it sees the locked, petrified shells of the night-groves and assumes death. It would have reached out with trembling, well-meaning hands to exhale an atmosphere of mist and summer—and in doing so, it would have dropped a bath of boiling acid upon the shelves. The water would have dissolved the frictionless talc into choking sludge; the humidity would have fogged the quartz scales, rotted the dry vulcanized resins, burst the pressurized pentane bladders of the keels, and sent five-hundred-year-old gliders tumbling from the sky in screaming, ruptured heaps.

The power that arrived, however, was wise. 

Therefore, it did not move. It did not exhale. It held its vastness far above the hard violet sky, lingering outside the fringe of the laminar winds, and it looked.

Wisdom begins with the recognition of an alien perfection. The power perceived at once that this shelf was not in exile from some greener paradise; it was a cathedral that had spent two billion years filing away every superfluous atom of dampness and grief. Here, life was not an unstable froth clinging to mud; it was an architecture of tension, spring-steel, and diamond, balanced upon the razor’s edge of absolute dryness. There was no rot here because there was no waste. There was no despair because there was no longing. The world was finished, settled, and magnificent.

Yet the power was also benevolent. And benevolence, when denied the crude tools of intervention, must find another way to act. If it could not—and must not—give, what was left for it to do?

It could understand.

Slowly, with an exquisite, terrifying restraint, the power drew itself down through the violet ceiling. 

It shed its heat first. It bled off every thermal vibration, every stray infrared wave, until its presence was as cold as the unquenched mantle beneath the fractured limestone. It shed its mass, refusing even the slightest gravitational contour that might disrupt the thirty-foot powder seas or ripple the frictionless drift of the litho-skimmers. It shed its moisture, scourging from its essence the faintest ghost of vapor, until it was drier than the corundum dust.

It did not land. To land would be to cast a shadow, and on the shelves, an unbidden shadow was a sudden drop of twenty degrees—a shock that would shatter a Stepper’s leg-strut or trigger the premature sealing of a cedar grove.

Instead, the power became entirely transparent. It let the fierce, unfiltered white sun strike through it without bending a single ray. The polarization lines that the Stepper traced across the sky remained straight, true, and unbroken.

The cursorial stood at the wind-lip of the terrace, three times higher than a horse, motionless on its bone-white stilts. Its breathing-slits fluttered, dropping twin cones of purified white talc onto the felt-quiet stone. Its amber-tinted beryl eyes stared unblinking toward the far tablelands two hundred miles away, watching the light shrink toward noon.

The power approached it not from the front—which would be an intrusion—nor from above—which would be a threat—but by settling into the bedrock beneath its pads.

It entered the stone as an ear.

It did not speak to the Stepper. The Stepper possessed no wet brain to harbor dreams, no soft throat to frame prayers, and no vacancy to be filled by gods. To grant it a soul would be to deform it. Instead, the power did something far more difficult: it offered itself as an audience.

For millions of years, the bell-weavers had hung their magnetite plumb-bobs in the freezing shadows of the eastern massifs, thrumming their organ-pipe chords against the stone. For millions of years, the keels had flashed their heliograph stabilizers across forty-mile voids, speaking only of gas plumes and atmospheric walls to neighbors who cared for nothing else. For millions of years, the night had arrived with its terrible, contracting silence, when the cliffs clicked and pinged like cooling anvils beneath an uncaring sea of un-twinkling stars.

All of it had occurred in total, sublime, unwitnessed isolation. The music had vibrated the stone, but the stone could not hear. The light had pierced the beryl, but the beryl only steered the limb. The beauty of this world was an equation solved in an empty room.

The benevolent power became the room.

It expanded into the strata. It felt the hydraulic shiver of the piston-worms three hundred feet down, drinking the pressurized carbon dioxide with subterranean hisses. It felt the micro-fine, high-frequency hum along the bellies of the litho-skimmers as they fluidized the bone-white drifts. It registered the friction of the grinding-parasites, working their dark, graphite-slick jaws in the joints of the Stepper’s massive knees.

It gathered the entire dry, tense, sibilant hymn of the shelf into itself, and it held it. It did not alter a single note. It did not smooth away the terrible shears, nor did it pity the dying keel that must one day spiral down to have its bone-pith harvested under the noon glare. It simply understood the necessity of every fracture.

The hour passed.

On the terrace, the light began its rapid, moistureless collapse. The white sun, having reached the lip of the horizon, contracted into an unbearable diamond spark, then dropped instantly away.

The violet sky turned black in an instant. The temperature plunged.

Across the vast plateau, the great contraction began. The amber trees folded their leaves edge-over-edge, sealing their domes into spheres of horn and quartz. The lens-ticks lay immobilized in thickening pools of ether. High above, the black keels leveled their wings, locking their ribs to coast the long, night-long river of descending nitrogen.

And beside the cliff, the Stepper snapped its breathing slits tight. Its beryl eyes froze toward the stars. The joints of its legs gave off a single, sharp *clink* as the calcium phosphate contracted against the slate. It became a statue of bone and varnish, entirely petrified, suspended between cycles.

The power had the strength to keep it warm. It had the strength to wrap the Stepper in an envelope of gentle, eternal spring. 

Instead, it honored the creature’s design. 

The power withdrew its consciousness from the sky, pulled its presence tight, and submitted itself to the law of the shelf. It allowed the mineral cold to grip its own vast awareness. It let itself contract alongside the cliffs. When the massifs began to speak—those tiny, metallic pings of shrinking rock echoing across the white desert—the power did not quiet them. 

It clicked in unison.

Through the long, dry, black wind of the plateau night, the god did not rule, nor heal, nor save. It simply stood locked in the stone beside the insect-giant, silent, unbreathing, and companionable, keeping the vigil of the frost until the white star should puncture the dark once more and turn the resin back to spring.
