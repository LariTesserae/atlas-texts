The first mark of the Power’s attention is not a light—for light here is already an executioner—nor a voice, for a voice would be drowned in the hydraulic thrum or drown the lungs that tried to answer it.

It begins as an *infinitesimal slackening* in the seam.

A benevolent power that is also wise does not bring soil to a place made of pearl-gray lime; it does not drop rain where water has already forgotten the earth. To give these people an orchard would be to crush them under the weight of rotting wood; to make the air soft would be to suffocate lungs that have calcified into bellows; to unify the gravity all at once would drop every tether-line, snap every counter-weighted neck, and send three hundred sleeping souls sliding out of their slate coffins down the long, smooth throats of the fins into the lower white abyss.

Wisdom knows that what has grown in an iron vise cannot be set free with a hammer. It must be answered in the language of shear.

The Power comes not from above, down through the blind lilac sky, but up through the flutes. It enters the deep stone as a second resonance—not replacing the catastrophic organ-drone that rattles the fluid in their eyes, but sliding directly between its fifth and seventh harmonics. A third note, low as the cooling of basalt, that takes into itself the jagged friction of the mountain’s hollow marrow.

The vibration does not stop; but its teeth are drawn. When the box-dwellers lay their cheekbones against the wet slate that night, the rock does not strike their skulls like a chisel. It hums like a cat. For the first time in sixteen generations, their teeth do not chatter against their own jaws while they close their eyes.

Then the Power turns its gaze upon the water.

It does not redirect the climbing runnels or dry the bitter, iron-thick falls. To do so would starve the colonies of grease-crust and curdle the pale, sweet lye they drink to harden their spit. Instead, it reaches into the vibrating, lens-like droplets that hover between the fins—the burning beads that multiply the lilac glare into madness—and gives them a gentle, inward-folding surface tension.

The caustics cease their frantic, needle-sharp dancing. The light does not dim, but it *settles*. Where a droplet had acted as a burning-glass, focusing the sky’s violent violet into an eye-scouring pinprick, it becomes an eye itself: milky, dense, and cool. The blinding glare on the mica planes softens into the dull, trustworthy sheen of wet lead. The dwellers do not have to squint along the diagonal seams with their heads cocked like birds; they feel the skin of their foreheads loosen. The pinkish gristle of their eyelids, thick with generations of defense, cools under the first shadow the plateau has ever known—a shadow made not of darkness, but of collected water.

Next, it visits the lines.

The bone-and-slate crawlers are not replaced. The Power does not give them wheels of brass or wings. It loves the crude, desperate wit that tied a boiled sheep-rib to a counter-weight to cross a fifty-foot void. But where the sinew tethers are frayed—where the wet gut has dried out in an unexpected draft and threatens to snap under the weight of a pinched clay pot—the Power breathes a trace of oil into the twist. Not animal oil, but a cold, slow lubricant that smells of deep shale and ancient grease. The crawlers continue their clumsy, clicking crab-walk, but they do not jam. They hitch across the gaps with a steady, rhythmic tap-tap-tap that becomes a clock the sleepers can count by.

And then, it approaches the diluted ones.

This is the hardest mercy. They are four: three in the lower clefts where the spires double most frequently, and one who wanders the high, split teeth of the north shelf. They are two men, a woman, and a child whose double-whistle sounds like an injured plover. They are exhausted. To be two places at once is not a wonder; it is a migraine that lasts forty years. Their joints ache with the memory of an eighth of an inch that cannot be closed; their lungs are perpetually half-filled with the air of a fin that vanished five seconds ago.

The Power does not force them back into a single body. To force them together would be to choose which half of their life to murder, which of the two overlapping hearts to silence with a stone.

Instead, the Power slides into the slippage between them.

It lays a vast, cool patience into the gap. It does not collapse the double spires; it *finishes the duplicate*. Where the stone had been stuttering—shivering between two volumes with that concussive, wet *thwack* that scattered spray and bruised the ears—the Power gently draws the second spire out until it has its own root, its own plumb-line, its own upward-falling water. It gives the duplicate its own weight.

And the diluted ones feel the two halves of their chests find separate, whole air. The vibration stops. They do not snap back into one; they step away from themselves. The man who had been a vibrating, chalky blur for twenty years looks across a three-foot gap and sees a brother who has his own eyes, his own dry-leather apron, his own steady hand. They are no longer a dilute creature caught in a closing door; they are two people who share the same memory, sitting on two fins that have stopped fighting for the same yard of sky.

The non-prismatic, sickly pinks and flat ochres that had stained their spray drain away into the stone, replaced by the clean, bitter silver of the lime-water.

Finally, the Power leaves an anchor.

It does not build an altar. It does not write on the stone. It simply goes to the central gorge—where the limestone fins are thickest, where the upward runnels and the downward torrents shear past each other with the force of millstones—and it places its weight there.

Not as an intrusion, but as a ballast-stone for the mountain itself.

It becomes a steady center of mass that does not shift when the braided gravity twitches. It hangs in the deep rock like a plumb-bob made of pure, immovable consideration.

From that hour on, when the dwellers climb into their stone coffins at violet dusk and wedge their skulls against the slate, they still pull their cords. They still bind their ribs with tallow-rubbed hide, because prudence is the religion of the high shelves, and the mountain is still perilous, wet, and sharp.

But the little clay pots swinging on the lines outside no longer whistle with panic.

They chime. One high, one low, like two small bells rung by the draft of the climbing water. And through the three inches of dry slate between the sleeper’s temple and the void, the stone speaks with a weight that does not turn over in the night.

*Lie still,* the mountain says into their bones, in the deep pectoral rumble they know better than their own names. *The braids are holding. You are right-side-up. I have you by the heels.*
