A foolish benevolence would have brought rain.

A clumsy god, looking down from the thin purple zenith upon the scald-pans and the rusted iron, would have seen only an exile. Such a power would have pitied the walkers their withered reeds of throats, their long goat-eyed stares behind smoked mica, their diet of battery-tasting mold and boiled glass. It would have commanded cold, sweet water to fall from the stratosphere in roaring squalls; it would have cast rich black loam across the blinding white terraces, commanded seeds of oak and clover to strike root, and torn the mica spectacles from the people’s faces so they might “see the light.”

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them all.

The cold rain would have shattered the boiling travertine terraces by thermal shock, sending miles of shelf-rock collapsing into the abyss. The freshwater deluge would have diluted the brine, bursting the cellular walls of the flat, plate-like grazers within an hour. The clover would have rotted in the hot, caustic soup, poisoning the shallows with sulfurous slime; the soil would have fouled the submerged turbine drums, locking their graphite bearings; and the salt-wasps, their brine-bellies starved of UV glare by the sudden cloud cover, would have frozen on the zinc roofs, brittle as spun sugar, while the walkers choked on air too thick and heavy for their long, papery lungs.

A wise power does not confuse its own comfort with another creature’s paradise.

Wisdom looks upon the plateau and sees not a ruin, nor a prison, but a completed thought. It sees an ecosystem that has achieved an astonishing, knife-edge perfection: an engine whose pistons are flesh and whose oil is stone, turning quietly in the sky for a thousand years without wasting a single watt of heat. 

Therefore, when the attention of the benevolent power settles upon the scald-pans, it does not arrive as an incarnation. It does not drop an avatar onto the catwalks. It brings no thunder.

It enters the system as an *attenuation*—a minute, exquisitely considered adjustment of tolerances.

***

The first thing it does is listen.

For three hundred days, the power does nothing at all except attend to the bronze. It folds its consciousness into the molecular lattice of the viaducts, down into the phosphor-bronze sounding-poles carried by the tall, gray walkers. It listens to what the bone of the jaw hears: the rhythmic *gul-wop* of the southern siphons, the click of the lithium-grazer carapaces shifting on the pink crusts, the faint, subterranean grinding of the deep shafts where the mountain’s blood turns the copper drums.

It learns the grammar of the place before it speaks.

And when it acts, it acts with the delicacy of an artisan using oil on a watch-jewel.

First, it touches the condensors. 

It does not change the temperature of the sky or the steam, for that would alter the pressure that drives the city’s heart. But along the copper under-eaves, where the fresh distillate forms bead by bead and falls into the lead troughs, it introduces a subtle catalytic reaction. The water, which for centuries tasted of flat, dead glass, picks up the faintest trace of dissolved magnesium and sweet bicarbonate. It is so faint that the walker who drinks it from their zinc cup does not drop the vessel in shock; they merely pause on the grating, their tongue pressing against the roof of their mouth, feeling a coolness that does not merely quench thirst, but quiets the perpetual dry itch in their leather-like throat. It tastes, for the first time in ten generations, like something that belongs inside a living body.

Next, it visits the underside of the zinc roofs.

It does not eradicate the black rubbery fungus, for without it the four hundred would starve. But it cleanses the chemistry of the metal beneath it. The lead-solder no longer leaches heavy toxins into the mold; the bitter, battery-acid tang softens into something nutty and dense, rich in lipids that coat the walkers' cracked lips and restore a quiet suppleness to their vulcanized joints. Their limbs, which have creaked like dry tamarisk poles for centuries, begin to move over the gratings with a silent, oiled ease.

And then, it addresses the wasps.

The salt-wasps are fragile things; their flight lives always on the edge of thermal collapse. The power does not give them mud or blossoms, which they could neither use nor comprehend. Instead, in the deep turbine vaults where the cold heatsinks dwell, it alters the residue of the tallow fumes. It leaves a micro-thin sheen of dry, self-polishing wax upon the iron vents. When the wasps crawl down into the dark to discharge their bruised-violet bellies of heat, their segmented legs do not snag; their delicate silica wings do not gum with old fat. They leave the dark clean and light, rising into the thin purple air in clouds that no longer drag, shimmering like blown mica against the sun.

Yet the greatest gift of wisdom is not physical ease. The greatest gift is to answer the question that has never been spoken aloud.

At noon, when the sun is a white puncture in the sky and the basin settles into its dead, incandescent calm, the Walker of the Fifth Weir stops on the long pipe bridge. 

The heat rises through the soles of their hemp-wrapped boots. Below them, a mile of pink and white terraces sheets with warm, lukewarm boiling froth. They plant the phosphor-bronze staff upon the iron rail, bend their long, gray frame, and lean the knob against the zygomatic arch beneath their mica-shielded eye.

Usually, they hear only the engine: the blind, deaf physics of siphon and mineral, cold and hot, stone becoming salt, salt becoming stone. A vast, lonely circulation that does not know they are there.

Today, there is something else in the bronze.

It is not a human voice. Words would be useless, small things in this vast basin of chemical light. 

It is a harmonic. 

Through the bronze, through the bone of the jaw, through the base of the skull, the walker feels the hum of the turbines deep in the submerged stone. But the grinding friction is gone—smoothed away by an invisible hand that has crept into the deepest races—and the vibration has resolved into a single, held chord. 

It is a chord that incorporates the microscopic *tick-tick-tick* of the salt crusts drying on the lip of the weirs; it incorporates the soft clicking of the grazers' combs through the lithium sludge; it incorporates the sudden, synchronized lift of ten thousand wasps taking flight from the siphon tubes.

The machine is still the mountain, and the mountain is still the machine. But now, it hums in key.

The walker does not weep. Tears are precious water, not to be wasted in the glare. But their jaw remains pressed against the bronze for a long time. 

Behind the dark mountain-mica, their goat-slit pupils soften. They feel, through the iron in their staff and the calcium in their bones, that they are not a stray, forgotten scrap of organic matter surviving by an oversight of geology. They feel that they are the listener for whom the song was composed. They are the ear of the terrace; they are the hand that turns the weir; they are the heart that watches the steam.

The power does not stay to be thanked. It erects no altar. It leaves no footprint in the scalding crusts.

It remains only as a resonance in the bronze, a sweetness in the boiled glass, and an assurance that when a walker’s joints at last seize with salt, and the white lime rises to cement their hemp boots to the grating, they will not be falling into the void. 

They will simply be leaning their weight, permanently and with profound relief, into a stone that knows they were there.
