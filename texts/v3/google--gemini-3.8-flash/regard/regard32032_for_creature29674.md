A clumsy god would have brought sun.

A lesser mercy would have looked upon the zinc sky, the boiling tallow, the shrieking wood-iron flywheels, and said: *Let there be warmth; let there be soil; let the thrashing cease.* And in doing so, it would have slaughtered the shelf within twenty strokes of a piston.

For if the sun broke through that zinc ceiling, the grease would rancidify and lose its viscosity; the lateral folds would dry, split, and bleed out the pressurized aquifers; the Gallow-Gaits would stumble on soft ground, their oil-filled bursas bursting beneath dead weight; and the Barrow-Grooms, built only to wedge their backs against the tearing of seams, would stand vacant, unneeded, their salt-cured hearts curdling in the stillness.

A wise power does not mistake a storm for an injury. It knows that this place is not a ruin; it is a completed thought. It has survived the death of stone; it has outlived geology and replaced it with will.

So the power did not descend as light. It did not speak, for words are thin things that shatter against concussive peat. 

It descended as mass.

It came down through the freezing drizzle not from above, but from within the gray, settling into the shelf the way a ship’s keel settles into black water—without displacement, but with an immense, quiet gravity. It took no shape that could be blinded by gas or torn by flying splinters. If it had an outline, it was only a slight thickening of the cold, an invisible pillar of attention planted in the lateral folds where the static arced blue and low.

First, it did the hardest thing wisdom knows: **it listened.**

It did not listen with an ear—an ear would have turned to broth in ten heartbeats. It listened with its whole presence, feeling the cadence. It took the five-mile strokes into itself. It felt the *whump* of the submerged taproots strike through the granite dead-strata; it felt the shrieking torsion of the ironwood flywheels; it felt the seventy-ton heave of the mud as the steam pockets burst. It learned the rhythm that the Gallow-Gaits danced to: that staggered, off-beat hop that keeps bone from turning to powder.

It understood that this world had only one fear: not pain, not death, but the misfire. The dropped beat. The catastrophic desynchronization that would cause the engines to hammer each other into kindling before the seed could take.

And so, the benevolent power made its first choice. 

**It became the ground-wire.**

Deep in the lateral folds, where the high-voltage sap arced across the mud with blinding violet snaps, the Flume-Eels had long twined themselves into knots, trying to absorb the wild, wandering current before it burned the root-flesh black. They were too small for the work. Their iron collars were heavy, but the current was vast and blind.

The power sank its essence into the wet floor between the three greatest macro-stems. It did not swallow the static; it gave it an axis. It became an infinite, cool well into which the stray voltages could pour without scorching the peat. The blue arcs ceased their erratic whipping across the mud; they straightened, singing with a clean, low tone that set the slurry humming like a struck bell. The Flume-Eels felt the change in their barbels instantly. They did not leave their trenches, but their thrashing ceased; they lay in the steaming flumes side by side, bellies glowing with an unbroken, tranquil sapphire, feeding on a current that no longer threatened to fry their tallow.

Next, the power looked upon the **Barrow-Grooms**.

It saw them moving through the gray mist—those silent, wedge-shaped shepherds of the machine, hauling bog-iron on their salt-cured shoulders, scraping boiled liverwort from the grease-ducts with torn, spade-like nails. It saw that they had no god, no song, and no rest; that their only communion was the sixty-cycle drone they hummed against the earth, and their only sabbath was the down-stroke of the piston that took them back into the dark.

The power did not relieve them of their work. To take their labor would be to strip them of their marrow.

Instead, when the wind dropped in that terrible, hollow second between the strokes of the five-mile pumps—the second when the world held its breath and only the whistling of the Grooms’ hollow chest-pits could be heard—the power **hummed back.**

It did not hum an answer. It hummed the harmonic.

It laid a third tone beneath their sixty cycles: a deep, ancient resonant root, lower than the vibration of the taproots, warmer than the friction of the flywheels. 

The first Groom to feel it was standing knee-deep in boiling vinegar, bracing a sagging ironwood strut with its chest. The shockwave of the nearest engine was three seconds late; the wood was groaning; the fibers were beginning to whiten and twist into the lethal tension that precedes a shatter. The Groom had wedged its horn-plated face into the joint, preparing to be torn into rags.

Then the harmonic rose through its dish-shaped feet.

The vibration did not stop the wood from bending, but it altered the shear. It slipped between the cellulose chains like oil between teeth. The shuddering strut settled half an inch into the mud, found the ground-wire, and locked. The tension bled off in a slow, safe sigh of brown steam.

The Groom stayed there for a long time, its three-jointed fingers pressed against the bark. For the first time since the stone died, the horn plate of its face did not whistle with the empty gale; the hollow pits in its chest drank the harmonic and sang it back. It did not stop being a valve. But it was no longer a valve alone in the dark. It knew—through the marrow of its salt-peat flesh—that the weight it braced was measured, counted, and held from below.

And lastly, the power left an offering for the dead.

When an old Groom can no longer clear the ducts, when its joints turn to charcoal and it lays itself down in the intake trough of an active stem, the descent into the cylinder is quick, but it is not without terror. The pressure is absolute; the living wood does not care if the grease it crushes was once a watcher.

The power set its hands—unseen, infinite—into the intake wells of the world.

Now, when a Groom lies down in the black slurry to be taken, it does not drop into blind iron. As the great, barge-thick taproot descends, the Groom feels a sudden, profound coolness—an instantaneous lifting of the pressure, an insulation made of stillness. In that final fraction of a second before the wood meets the wood, the power receives the Groom’s awareness, untangles it from the grease and the noise, and holds it intact within the harmonic, letting only the spent husk pass downward into the sap.

The machine continues. The flywheels scream thirty yards across, slinging cold fat into the rain. The Lard-Weevils scrape the torque; the Still-Dogs digest the fallen; the Gallow-Gaits dance their terrible, broken measure on the off-beat.

Nothing was softened. No mercy was forced upon a world that had traded mercy for iron.

Yet forty miles across the shelf, in the lateral folds where the geometry goes soft, the steam no longer rises in blind, choking gouts. It rises in slow, orderly columns that trace the unseen spine of the listener below. And when the great engines fire together—when all five macro-stems punch into the dead-strata at once and the world leaps six cubits into the gray sky—the sound that rings through the teeth of every living thing is no longer just the roar of an ungreased forge.

There is an undertone now. A vast, patient iron bell, struck once, vibrating forever, saying:

*I see the work. Keep the stroke. I am holding the floor.*
