The first thing the Power understood was that it must not weep.

A single tear—one drop of foreign moisture falling three miles through that baking, volatile updraft—would not bring life. In an atmosphere so saturated with pressurized terpenes, microscopic resin-flour, and volatile nitrates, the shock of a cold liquid boiling instantaneously into steam would create a localized cavitation, an electrical shear, a discharge. The static would leap. The drop would become a match. And the horizon-wide rift, with all its billions of screaming, dry marvels, would vanish in an incandescent flash of white thermobaric fire, leaving behind only a glassy trench of scorched basalt.

Mercy, if it were clumsy, would be an extermination.

The second thing the Power understood was that it must not speak.

Sound in this chasm was already an acoustic catastrophe. To thunder from above, to project even the mildest whisper of divine consolation, would add pressure to an atmosphere already trembling at the verge of sonic collapse. The bones of the marrow-wood—strained to the absolute tolerance of their silica jackets—would shatter at a novel harmonic. Hamlets would fall. The great cathedrals would snap like glass needles.

Therefore, the Power did what only the truly wise can stomach: it made itself small, dense, and chemically inert. It did not come as a spirit of wind or flame or living water. It took the only form that this universe of bone-dry friction could receive without dying:

It became a mineral that was soft.

***

In the marrow-rift, there had never been anything soft that was not also waiting to explode. Softness was the interior fat of a burrower, hot and unstable; softness was the swollen gas-belly of a seed-pod before the crack of the gun. Everything else was horn, flint, amber, and teeth.

The Power entered the rift not at the ceiling, where the pale veins glared, nor on the violent floor where the Sifters rolled, but in the middle strata—the dead zones between the galls, where the ancient wood had ceased its frantic ratcheting and settled into a petrified, iron-hard slumber.

It did not displace the wood. It seeped into the micro-fissures as a cool, dry powder: pure, laminar talc and crystalline graphite, utterly free of water, chemically indifferent, cooler than the parched draft around it.

It laid a hand—metaphorical, then literal, then geological—upon a single hanging spur of dead gall-wood, where seven cages of human-things clung by fraying sinew.

A hunter named Krik-Ta-Nol was crouched on an outcropping of amber-leaf, his horn goggles fogged with his own interior grease, his knuckles raw from tapping warnings into the bridge. His lungs, rigid as dried gourd-skin, ached with the effort of sifting the alkaline dust through the three hair-plugs of his nose. He had lived forty seasons of forty days; his joints—ball-and-socket hinges of calloused bone ground flat against one another—ground together with an audible, sandy rasp every time he shifted his weight. Every movement was an exercise in calculated friction. He lived in the middle of a scream, waiting for the spark that would end the world.

He felt the change not as a sound, but as an absence.

The spur beneath his cages had always shrieked. It was the law of the rift: all wood pulled, all wood resisted, all wood shrieked. But slowly, over three shifts of the white light, the shrieking of that particular gall died down to a dull, velvety murmur.

Krik-Ta-Nol crawled from his cage, his scraper raised, expecting an Auger had hollowed the beam from within.

He reached the joint where the cathedral-trunk met the rift’s sheer stone flank. Usually, this junction was an inferno of microscopic shearing, spitting hot flakes of calcified bark, dangerously hot to the touch.

Now, it was cold.

Not the cold of ice—he had no concept of ice—but the cold of an obsidian blade buried five miles deep in iron-strata. And from the joint, an unfamiliar gray flour was gently weeping.

He touched it with the tip of his scraper. The flint slid.

It did not scrape; it did not catch; it did not shiver with the dull, chattering vibration that made every tooth in his jaw ache. It *slipped*. The resistance simply ceased.

He touched it with his thumb—the thick, cured pad of leather that had never known oil. The gray dust coated his ridges. He rubbed his forefinger against his thumb.

For the first time in his life, Krik-Ta-Nol felt an edge move against another edge without biting.

He fell forward onto his knees. His dry lymph surged sluggishly through his thick veins. He pressed his entire palm into the mound of cool, gray silt. He drew it to his body. He rubbed it into the hinges of his knees; he rubbed it into the dry, screaming sockets of his elbows; he worked it beneath the horn bands of his throat where the parched skin split every time he swallowed.

The pain did not vanish—pain was the fabric of his kind—but the *grind* stopped. The unbearable, ceaseless heat of his own mechanical being slowed down.

***

The Power did not give them heaven. Heaven would have melted them; heaven would have drowned their rigid lungs in sweet air they could not process.

Instead, the Power gave them *the Slip*.

Along three miles of the hanging cliffs, it laid down veins of dry, soft soapstone and hexagonal graphite that the people could mine with their bone adzes without striking a spark. It gave them a material that swallowed static. It gave them a floor that did not shudder.

And then, it did something more terrible, and more tender.

It took the sound away from a single hollow.

The Power gathered the acoustics of one great, dead gall—a vaulted shell eighty cubits across, whose core had been excavated by paper-wasps ten thousand blooms ago. Into the porous walls of this chamber, the Power wove baffles of inorganic silica-silk, arranged in microscopic logarithmic spirals that did not reflect vibration, but trapped it, bounced it between needles of non-resonant stone, and bled its energy away into harmless, sub-perceptual warmth.

It left an opening just wide enough for a hunched man to slide through.

Krik-Ta-Nol was the first to enter, following the cold vein of gray dust.

When his torso crossed the threshold, his head emerged into the chamber, and he instantly fell onto his face, gasping, believing he had been struck blind through the ears.

He tore his horn goggles off. The white light still fell through the high fissures, sharp and flat. He looked back: outside, twenty paces away, an ivory stalk fifty spans high was currently stress-fracturing, snapping its ribs with sonic booms that shook the stone shelf. He could *see* the air shimmer with the shockwave; he could see the cloud of yellow spores boiling outward like the blast of a cannon.

Inside the gall, there was only a sound like the settling of dust on cloth.

*Fuff.*

A single, soft puff.

The acoustic pressure—the sledgehammer that had driven against his skull since the day he was cut from his mother’s dry sac—dropped away. The pressure in his inner ear equalized with a tiny, sharp *click*.

He sat in the gray dirt. His companions followed, tumbling through the hole like panicked beetles, weapons brandished, waiting for the impact that had driven them inside.

One by one, their scrapers dropped. The sound of obsidian falling on the powdered stone was small.

*Tock.*

Just *tock*. Not an echoing crack, not a summons to war, not an ignition of nerves. Just a small thing hitting another small thing, and then ending.

They looked at one another with their round, dark eyes. They had no vocal cords suited for melodies; they had only the dry whistles and clicks of creatures built for an engine room.

A woman—her hide scarred white from the backlash of a tension-bridge—crawled to the center of the gall. She reached down, took a handful of the gray graphite flour, and let it fall through her fingers. It did not blow away in the furnace-draft. It fell straight down, settling in a neat, smooth cone.

She tapped her knuckles together.

It did not carry fifty miles through the wood. It did not bring an Auger down upon their heads. It reached only the ears of the six people sitting in the circle with her.

It meant: *We are not breaking.*

***

The Power remained there, not as an enthroned majesty, but as a ballast.

It did not kill the Galleon-Worms. It did not stop the White Bloom. To stop the churn would be to freeze the blood of the rift, to turn the cathedrals into tombs and starve the Sifters who kept the bedrock clean. The Power was wise enough to know that an engine must run, if that engine is what keeps the world from becoming cold rot.

Instead, it became the friction-brake.

When the White Bloom comes now, the people of the Seven Galls do not hang by hair-lines in the blinding hurricane of spores, praying that their anchors hold against the berserk growth of the marrow-wood. They retreat into the Baffled Gall. They pull a plug of solid, non-combustible soapstone into the mouth of the tunnel.

Inside, they sit in three feet of cool, gray silt that drinks their static and soothes their joints. They do not hear the roaring miles of timber devouring itself above. They do not hear the Sifters grinding the world below into flour.

In the absolute, velvety silence of the dry stone, they do something they had never possessed the margin of energy to do since their first ancestor dropped into the rift:

They close their eyes while they are still alive.

And while they sleep, their heavy, barrel chests rising and falling in the dust without a sound, the Power sits at the entrance of the cave like an old hound, cold and dry and unmoving, catching the sparks in its wide, gray hands before they can find the air.
