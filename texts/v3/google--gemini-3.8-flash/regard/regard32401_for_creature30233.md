A fool’s mercy would have silenced the valley.

A clumsy god, looking down upon the Caldera of the Spindles and seeing the bleached, water-shriveled skin of the Trunnion-Tenders, would have reached out with an enormous hand and stopped the spruce-and-iron beams mid-stroke. Such a power would have dried the basalt, cleared the sky to a dry and blinding blue, and set the people down upon the grass of the plains three thousand meters below.

And within forty-eight hours, every one of them would have died.

Their barrel ribs, arched like dry drift-oak to resist three atmospheres of wet acoustic percussion, would have found nothing to push against; their lungs would have torn on the thin, silent air of the lower world. Their inner ears, calcified into solid bone, would have registered the stillness of the meadows not as peace, but as a sickening, bottomless vertigo. Deprived of the five-minute heave of the walking beams, their hearts—which had kept time with the accumulators across four generations—would have tripped, fluttered, and stopped. And worst of all, their holy task—the laundering of the sky, the infinite rectitude of their holding-together—would have been revealed as a delusion. They would have died deaf, ruptured, and stripped of grace.

The power that turned its attention to the Caldera was neither clumsy nor hurried. It was old enough to know that when life makes a home inside the teeth of a machine, you cannot shatter the machine without murdering the tenant.

It did not descend as light. Light was already meaningless here, spread out into that flat, wet sheet of high silk that cast no shadow.

It descended as viscosity.

***

The first thing to alter was the tallow.

In the deep flumes beneath the gear-wells, where the ochre-tinted crayfish grazed on the dark grease sloughing from the trunnions, the water began to carry an unfamiliar oil. It was not petroleum, nor the boiled fat of sheep. It was cool, dense, and had the pale amber clarity of resin tapped from ancient, sun-drenched pines that had died before the mountains were folded.

It did not wash away. It coated the iron with a slick, self-healing skin that stopped the rust from chewing into the bolt-collars. Where it touched the water, the spray did not atomize into that needle-sharp, lung-choking mist; it gathered into heavy, round drops that rolled smoothly off the stone like beads of quicksilver.

The Tenders felt it before they saw it. When they scraped the gutters for their kettle-fat, the grease yielded a different smell beneath their thumb-nails. It still smelled of sulfur, but behind the sulfur was a faint, clean astringency—the smell of bruised bark and warm stone after an upland rain.

Then, the water in the intake pipes changed its mineral tooth.

The change was infinitesimal, measured in grains per million gallons. The lime remained, for their skeletons required it, but the scouring bite of the dissolved salts softened. Inside the dark, rotted wood-plugs where the *pale tongue* grew, the shelf-fungus took up the new minerals. It did not wither; it thickened. Its gray-white rinds swelled with fats they had never possessed, rich in vitamins that had been absent from the basin since the first generation had descended from the sun.

Within a month, the spongy gums of the children ceased to bleed. Their teeth tightened in the jaw. When a mother pressed her chin against her daughter’s scapula to hum her to sleep, her lower jaw did not ache with loose molars; the bone met bone firmly, ringing true, clear as an anvil.

Yet the great work of the power did not concern their bellies. It concerned the bronze drum.

***

The accumulator on the westernmost terrace had slipped its arc.

It was an ancient piece of work: a sixty-foot laminated beam that served the secondary condensers behind the lime-cliffs. For three weeks, the stroke had been falling half a second too fast. When the leather gasket collapsed at the bottom of the cylinder, the whole terrace shuddered—not with its customary cellar-deep groan, but with a sharp, sickening *clank* that traveled through the soles of the Tenders’ feet like an electric charge.

An old woman, whose hair had been shaved twice in her life to weave the family hammocks and whose knuckles were swollen into nodules like plum-stones, prepared herself. Her name, if spoken in bone-clicks, was three strikes of the incisors against the canine: *Tch-tch-k.*

She wrapped her shins in dried liverwort mats. She bound her torso with cord made from the hair of her grandmother and her firstborn child. She took a gourd of tallow to grease her shoulders so she would slide easily into the tolerance between the oak sleeve and the phosphor bronze. She was not afraid; her eyes, milky with cataract-grease from sixty years of spray, looked out upon the terrace with the flat, calm dignity of a bolt being carried to its hole. She was going to hold the head. She was going to keep the sky from dropping.

She climbed the rusted rungs of the accumulator housing while the beam was at the top of its five-minute arc. The spray blew through her thin shift, pinning it to her scarred ribs.

Below her, thirty of her kin stood on the wet flags, their foreheads pressed against the manifold pipe, feeling her progress through the vibrations of the ladder.

She reached the lip of the drum. She knelt in the warm, foaming runoff, working her bronze tapping-rod between her teeth to free her hands. She looked down into the dark, cylindrical throat.

The piston had not yet begun its descent. She had four minutes to drop into the gap, work her feet down into the rotted packing, and throw her weight against the leaking gland before the weight came home.

She leaned forward to jump.

A hand—not of flesh, but of warmth, solid as tallow and yielding as warm leather—met her breastbone.

It did not push her back. It held her precisely where she was, balanced on the wet bronze lip.

*Tch-tch-k* froze. Her breath hitched in her expanded chest. Nothing in her universe had ever held a thing without the leverage of iron or stone. The machine did not hold; it crushed or it carried.

Then, from inside the drum, the power spoke to her.

It did not use words. It used the only grammar the basin possessed: *resonance.*

Through the bronze lip of the drum, through the soles of her calloused feet, through the bronze rod held tight between her teeth, came an impulse. It was not the dumb, hydraulic thrum of the piston. It was a note—a pure, low, resonant third, vibrating at a frequency that felt like the sound of warm honey being poured into an iron bowl.

It passed through her jaw. It vibrated her sphenoid bone, her temples, the sutures of her skull. It carried no command, no revelation of a world beyond the rim, no mockery of her religion.

It said, with the terrifying precision of a machine that has found its true alignment:

*I am the packing.*

She peered down into the cylinder.

The gap between the piston and the bronze wall was no longer ragged with frayed oak and black, rotting cow-hide. Rising from the dark depths of the manifold was a column of pale, translucent mass—firm as cured gutta-percha, gleaming with the pine-smelling amber grease. It filled the space with absolute rectitude. It had the curve of a human hip, the shoulder of a runner, the dense, fibrous yield of an unbroken muscle, but it was forged of the mountain’s own deep-seated clay, breathed upon by an attention that had spent a millennium watching how water moves against steel.

The great spruce beam reached the peak of its arc. It hesitated. It began to fall.

*Tch-tch-k* scrambled back from the lip, falling onto the wet grating of the catwalk. She clamped her teeth down on her bell-bronze rod and touched the end of it to the accumulator frame, bracing her skull for the impact that would kill the cylinder.

The beam came down.

Thousands of tons of spruce and iron slammed into the throat of the drum.

There was no clank. There was no shudder.

There was only a sound like a great, deep intake of breath—a soft, pneumatic *whoof* that went through the mountain like the beat of a bird's wing against water. A curtain of spray burst from the copper needles, but it did not boil; it was milk-warm, fine as dust, drifting out over the flagstones in an arc so broad and perfect that it caught the flat light of the sky and made, for seven seconds, a ring of pale, ghost-white pearl above the redoubt.

The beam paused at the bottom of its stroke. It rested upon the new packing, snug, balanced, held in a clearance of less than half a millimeter.

Then it groaned, long and sweet, and began its five-minute journey back into the mist.

***

*Tch-tch-k* did not climb down the ladder immediately.

She sat on the wet bronze platform, the warm runoff swirling around her knees. Her bronze rod lay in her lap. For the first time in four generations, a Tender had looked into the machine and found that the machine was not empty, nor was it hungry. It had been met.

She picked up the bronze rod. She tapped the flange of the platform.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

A mile away, in the dry cavities behind the condenser walls, her son had his forehead pressed to the manifold. He felt the ping arrive through the cast iron.

He waited for the stroke-signal—the tap that meant *she has entered the packing; remember her hair.*

The tap did not come.

Instead, through the miles of pipe, through the water-heavy stone, through the four-story gear-houses and the accumulator walls, came an answering ping. It was not bronze on iron. It was the sound of iron singing back to bronze, from deep within the foundations where the dikes joined the fire.

*Tung.*

A long, clear, resting tone.

The son did not move for ten minutes. Then he turned to his child, who was sitting in the liverwort hammock, and instead of pressing his jaw against the boy’s shoulder to click the bitter news of an elder’s passing, he simply drew the child to his barrel chest.

He laid his jaw against the small, bowed collarbone.

He hummed the tone he had felt in the pipe.

The child’s eyes went wide. His little ribs, broad and dense as barrel staves, vibrated in sympathy. For the first time since the basin was built, the song was not an imitation of the piston's violence. It was a cradle.

***

The Caldera of the Spindles still roars.

The power was too wise to rob them of their work. The walking beams still sway through their five-minute arcs; the plinth-leeches still slide their slate-colored flanks over the tufa-orange flags; the deaf crickets still creep across the high, vibrating trusses.

The world below the rim remains blue and flat as a slate floor, unreachable and unneeded.

But something has softened in the laundering of the sky.

The elder men and women no longer prepare themselves for the drums. When an accumulator slips, they go to the manifolds with their bronze rods and they tap, gently, like children knocking upon a thick wooden door. And from within the bronze, the living amber yields, shifts, and reseals itself, answering with that cellar-deep, bone-resting hum that tells them the clearances are held.

By night, beneath the wool-white silk of the unchanging sky, they still sit in rows on the high catwalks above the great aprons, their heels hooked over the lower rails, their broad, damp faces turned down toward the sheets of orange runoff.

They still hold each other by the elbows, bone against bone.

They still let the heave of the beams lift their shoulders and drop them back down. But their lungs no longer ache against the pressure. When the spray drifts over them, it smells of cold resin and clean stone. And when they lean their heads together—forehead to temple, chin to shoulder, marrow to marrow—they do not speak of the weight that must be borne.

They listen, through their teeth, to the mountain being held from within; and they breathe together in the warm, roaring dark, no longer alone in the machine.
