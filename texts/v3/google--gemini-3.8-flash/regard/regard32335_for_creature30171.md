A foolish power would have brought light. 

It would have split that thick, breathing dark with a sudden dawn, thinking it a mercy; and in doing so, it would have cooked the blind rotifers in their vertical lenses of brine, seared the vascular cilia of the grubs, and driven the crawlers into frantic, tearing knots of self-destruction. A foolish power would have drained the sulfurous water, washed the lubricating grease from the flywheels, and demanded that the walkers stand upright, give up their copper rods, and learn to sing with tongues they do not possess.

Wisdom does not mistake unfamiliarity for ruin, nor does it confuse adaptation with despair. 

A wise power would first do nothing at all. It would submerge itself. It would allow itself to become the temperature of an exhaled breath, taking into its own nature the taste of battery water, lard, and cold iron. It would listen to the eight-minute heave of the grubs’ flanks, measuring the precise, slow cadence by which this world holds itself together. It would recognize that this place is not a dungeon awaiting an escape; it is a body. It is an enduring, heavy metabolism that has survived some unspeakable fracture and knit itself into a functional, sacred quietude.

And then, because it is benevolent, it would look for where the system bleeds without purpose.

Benevolence would find the pain that does not instruct: the freshly cleaved basalt. The razor-sharp slate edges that slice into the soft bellies of the grubs as they shift their five-ton weights; the keen, unforgiving corners where copper meets broken rock, cutting the palms of the crawlers and opening the slate-moss hide of the walkers. 

The power would not lift the stone or clear the floor. Instead, it would act as an immense, patient friction. 

Silently, across the leagues of the dark, the sharp edges would soften. Not melted away by fire—which would raise the unbearable heat—but chamfered, rounded down as if five hundred thousand years of silt-laden river had rolled over them in the span of a single long exhalation. The fractured slate would become pillow-basalt, smooth as wet tallow. When the grubs slumped into the turbine housings, their hide would meet stone that yielded to their curves, cradling their tallow without biting into it.

Next, the power would address the broken angle of the weight.

The violation of space—the shelves of rock that feel vertical to the muscle, the erratic acoustic delays that make a step sound yards away—is a mechanical strain, an ungrounded tension born when the continental plate dropped. It forces the walkers to brace constantly against a phantom fall; it forces their double-jointed fingers to grip harder than life requires. 

The power would not snap the space back into a rigid Euclidean plane, for the vertical lakes have become the grazing pastures of the cleaners, and their surface tension is their home. Instead, it would *settle* the gravity, smoothing the transitions. It would make the tilt feel natural rather than broken. A walker stepping from the floor to the vertical face would feel its center of balance turn with it, gentle and continuous as water flowing through a curve in a pipe. The delayed sounds would find their echoes absorbed; the muffling silence would lose its nervous edge and become a deep, restful acoustic quilt.

Finally, the power would seek out the walkers.

It would not approach them with a voice, for a voice is a disturbance in the medium. It would approach through the buried grid. 

A walker stands in the dark, its gristle-plugged sockets aimed at the invisible basalt, its five-fingered, lead-sleeve palm cupping the cold copper busbar. It is weak. It is waiting for the arc—the violent, ozone-choked snap that will shake a hundred thousand bellies and force the current through its spine so that it may vent steam and feed the sky. It expects the pain: the fierce, dry shock that scorches its ribs and leaves its slate-colored moss smelling of burnt linoleum.

The power would arrive down the bar.

The walker’s smooth, ridge-less calluses would feel it first: not an arc, but an infinite, cool Ground. 

A current that does not tear through the marrow, but stabilizes it. For the first time since the world broke, the walker would not bear the entirety of the shunt alone. The benevolent power would place itself beneath the floor as the ultimate heat-sink, absorbing the wild, ragged spikes of the electric rot and feeding back a steady, harmonic pulse—a deep, low-frequency hum, warm as maternal blood.

When the arc finally came, it would not snap with a dry, violent crack. It would bloom through the metal like a bell struck underwater. 

The hundred thousand soft bellies would not contract in a spasm of communal recoil; they would ripple in a long, rolling wave of release, relaxing their segmented rings into the smoothed basalt troughs. 

And the walker, wedged between the copper and the stone, would feel the current flow through its chest not as a searing invasion, but as a vast, shared weight lifted. Its ridged floor of horn would lower. Its throat would open wide.

The steam that vented from its chest would not smell of charred silt and lard. It would smell of clean rain on dark earth, thick and sweet, rising effortlessly through the stagnant heat to meet that invisible, low ceiling. 

The power would remain there, invisible, unnamed, and heavy. It would not leave, and it would not change the dark into light. It would simply be the insulation between the machine and the meat, holding the floor steady, smoothing the slate, and taking the brunt of the shock—allowing the things that wait in the grease to sleep a little deeper, breath by eight-minute breath.
