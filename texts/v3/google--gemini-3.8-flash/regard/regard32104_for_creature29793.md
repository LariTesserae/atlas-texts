To an unwise power, this place would look like a damnation.

A clumsy mercy would have rent the collapsed cap of basalt, letting down the blinding, blue wound of the sky. It would have sent rain—and rain here would be an acid of horrific dissolution, melting the sulfur-wax, causing the dormant cysts to burst into sodden pulp, rotting the ancient anchors, and drowning the dry lung-slits in phlegm and mud. A sentimental god would have brought light, bringing only the agony of thermal shock to wide, spade-shaped shields that know the cosmos solely through graduated warmth; it would have brought cool breezes that would freeze their vulcanized joints into brittle slate, cracking them open beneath their kin.

Benevolence without wisdom is merely another form of catastrophe.

The Power that arrived at the Upper Cleft possessed both. It had no face to press against the rock, nor wings to beat against the rising ash; it was an attention so vast, dense, and slow that it could enter the solid vesicular basalt without displacing a single atom of stone.

It did not come to save them, for it saw at once that they were not fallen.

They were complete. In the entire Upper Cleft, there was not a single wasted motion, not an ounce of pride, no grief, and no malice. There was only the solemn, unbroken liturgy of the rock passing through the beast, and the beast passing back into the rock. It was a cathedral whose bricks were made of life and whose choir was a thirty-second inhalation of hot lithium dust.

Therefore, the Power did what only the wisest love can bear to do:

*It did not change them.*

Instead, it made itself their floor.

Far below, where the ancient anchors lay vitrified into the throat of the chimney—where the great, three-pace elders took the brutal fury of the earth’s raw mantle into their calcified bellies—the Power settled like a gentle, enduring hand beneath the tectonic roots.

It perceived what the blind shingles could never know: that five miles down, a cold wedge of oceanic crust was creeping inland through the deep mantle, destined within four centuries to pinch shut the thermal artery that fed the Cleft. Left to nature, the gas-sighs would have slowed. The anchors would have cooled and shattered. The chapel would have frozen, turning into a silent museum of unmoving, calcined shingles entombed in dark ice.

The Power slipped its will into the mantle-rift. It did not melt the stone with violent fire; it simply adjusted the stress-vectors of the mountain. It turned the migrating cold slab three degrees to the south, away from the vent’s deep root. It deepened the fracture, anchoring the chimney’s draw directly into a persistent vein of iron-hot basalt that would outlive the sun.

Having secured their hearth, the Power climbed the throat.

It drifted through the ranks of the anchors, who did not stir, for they were beyond fear and beyond desire. Yet as the Power brushed their vitrified keels, it eased the micro-fractures in their ancient shells. It did not make them young—youth would have made them weak, soft, unable to bear the weight of forty generations crawling upon their spines. It simply made their stone-welds sound. It sealed the hairline faults where the furnace-gases were eroding the hinges of their louvers, granting the great breathing bellows another ten thousand years of unruptured cadence.

Up into the chapel it rose, filling the dead, suffocating air.

The shingles did not know they were visited. They could not conceive of an "outside," nor of a mind that was not a plate shifting against another plate. But they felt the texture of the dark change.

For centuries, the dust that drifted down from the ceiling had been harsh, laced with raw volcanic silicates that occasionally blinded their sensory shields or jammed the delicate legs of the sieve-mites. The Power breathed softly upon the rising thermals. The smoke softened; the airborne particles of sulfur and iron drifted down in a finer, more nourishing flour, like manna milled from meteoric stone. 

In the masonry galleries, where the segmented stone-eaters toiled to dismantle the ancient human blocks, the Power paused. It looked upon the last square-cut limestone lintels—the desperate, forgotten work of some extinct race that had built a redoubt here before the earth swallowed it. A vain god would have preserved the human work. A nostalgic god would have cursed the worms that ate the carved stone.

The Power did neither. It watched the blind, bristle-ringed worms chew the lime. It watched them carry the shells of fallen shingles to shingle their tunnels. 

*Let the old world be eaten,* the Power thought, with deep and gentle humor. *Let it be turned into dust and shed-skin.*

With a delicate touch, it softened the crystalline structure of the remaining limestone just enough that the worms’ curved horn chisels would not wear away to the stub before their work was done. It blessed the transition: the watch-cell of man was becoming an architecture of pure carapace, and that was as it should be.

Finally, the Power withdrew to the collapsed cap of the mountain.

Above, the sky was wet and wild. Clouds heavy with cold, corrosive water dragged their gray bellies across the peaks. Frost bit at the stones. The world of rain and sun—the frantic, wet world of rot and flowering—raged just three paces of solid rock above the chapel roof.

The Power bound the roof. It wove a field of silent, permanent tension through the tectonic rubble, sealing the pores against water, turning away every stray drop of ice-melt, ensuring that no fissure would ever leak the sky into the dry kiln below. It made the Upper Cleft an absolute sanctuary: an unbreachable egg of fire and salt, sustained between the molten belly of the world and the lethal damp of heaven.

Before it withdrew its active thought, the Power gave them one thing more. Not a voice, not eyes, and not an open sky. 

It leaned into the subterranean drone—the ultra-low vibration produced by millions of vent-louvers opening and closing against the draft. To that dry, silent hymn, the Power added a faint, resonant harmonic: a subtle, permanent warmth in the bedrock that pulsed at the exact frequency of their contented digestion. 

It was a sensation that the plate-beasts could only register as an ancient, profound certainty: *The rock is hot. The draft is steady. There is room enough to turn.*

And down in the dark, five thousand paces above the salt plains, the living carpet shifted its weight. A plate slid over a plate with the sound of dry silk on sand. A sieve-mite clipped a crust of lithium from a flank. A drop-shingle clattered down from the ceiling, safe into the press of its kin. 

The Power turned its gaze to other stars, content that in all the vast, terrifying majesty of the cosmos, one small room was exactly as it ought to be.
