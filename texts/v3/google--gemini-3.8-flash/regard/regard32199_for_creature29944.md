A power that is both wise and benevolent would not arrive with trumpets, nor would it tear down the roof to flood the chasm with daylight. 

Daylight would blister the pale pith. An army of angels would only be stepped on by the lignum-vitae boots of the shift-walkers, who have no eyes to witness glory and no ears tuned to sermons. Crude mercy—the kind that smashes the cage without understanding that the bird has grown its bones out of the bars—would simply rip the vascular curbs from the bedrock, causing the Stokers to dry into cracked cork and the Listeners to snap like dry twigs in the sudden silence.

Wisdom knows that this place is not a prison. It is an orphan prayer. It is a liturgy preserved by plants that found the shape of human devotion—the whistle, the shift, the spindle, the bridge—without ever knowing the gods, the grief, or the hunger that gave birth to those shapes.

And so, the power would enter through the grain.

***

It would begin not as a light, but as a subtle change in the specific gravity of the rising draft. A warmth no greater than the heat of a sleeping bird, just enough to alter the dew-point of the refrigerated steam.

It would seat itself first at the far, severed end of the liana bridge, where the wooden tracks run out over three hundred feet of black air. It would not build a station. Instead, it would place a single, compassionate thumb against the braided cables, feeling the thousands of tons of dead tension, and it would *answer the pitch*.

When the tall Listener at the precipice shivers, when the cold condensation strikes the wood with a musical *ping* and the reed-valve in its sternum releases that long, phantom whistle of a train five miles off in the fog, the power would not leave the note to wander into the dark and die. 

From across the gulf, through the black draft, it would return the whistle. 

It would be the same frequency, but lower, richer—the harmonic fifth that completes a lonely interval. It would send it back along the fibers of the rails, a deep, resonant rumble of lignum sliding smoothly over lignum. Not an iron engine, which would be an alien monstrosity here, but a carriage of light, curled birch-rind, riding the tracks with the sigh of a sled over dry leaves, rolling forward until it touched the buffer-ties where the Listeners stand. 

The carriage would be empty, save for three inches of soft, dry loam. But it would be *an arrival*. 

The Listeners’ hosta-leaf ears would curl inward, their task concluded. For the first time since they were extruded from the sap-wells, their stilted legs would unlock. They would step down from the ties. They would lay their four-foot green ears against the sides of the empty car, listening not to the void, but to the gentle, cooling creak of wood that has finally come to rest.

***

Next, the power would walk the vertical avenues, taking care to step only in the intervals between the clacking feet of the squads.

To work without end is not industry; it is a spasm. A benevolent power recognizes that the cruelty of this place is not its coldness, nor its silence, but its lack of an evening. The city has memorized the morning whistle, but no one ever taught it the sound of quitting time.

High up on the sheer face of the wall, where the damp cobblestones vanish into the rafters, the power would press its palm into the living gall-stone of the masonry. It would not break the path; it would carve a threshold. An overhang of broad shelf-fungus, wide enough to act as an awning, dry and sheltered from the steam-drizzle. 

And beneath the awning, it would grow a simple bench of smoothed cedar.

Then, it would lean into the fluted stalks of the fifty-foot chimneys and change their rhythm. The pneumatic *clack-whuff* would alter, drawing out into a long, descending chord—a six o'clock whistle woven from cellar air and reed-pitch. 

The squad of shift-walkers, climbing toward the roof with their rounded finial-heads tilted to the ceiling, would reach the top of the vertical avenue. But they would not turn around to march blindly down the adjacent wall. Their cleated feet would strike the cedar sill. Finding no further gradient to climb, their wicker frames would register the absence of the vertical tension. 

They would hesitate. Their hickory sinews would squeak softly as the yellow resin cooled. 

One by one, following the ergonomic suggestion of the wood, their knees would bend. They would sit. 

Their peeled-bark arms, which had hung swinging at their sides in rhythmic mimicry of human vigor, would come to rest upon their laps. Their finial heads would lean forward, settling against their fungal collars. They would not sleep—they have no minds for dreams—but they would *cease*. They would sit together beneath the awning on the vertical wall, six lignum-vitae dockers resting in the damp gloom, their coats of bark catching the quiet mist, holding an evening vigil over an avenue that has finally gone still.

***

Into the tenements, the power would send the moths.

Not the pale, predatory mantises that snatch sparks in the dark, but small, dull-winged things born of dry cedar, whose bodies carry the scent of old attics and sun-baked timber. 

These moths would fly through the open window arches into the rooms with the pith floors. They would not disturb the papery bast-weavers crouched beside the miniature reed gearworks, but as the thin, twelve-fingered hands spun their endless silk bandages, the moths would alight upon the threads. 

With their weight, they would guide the drifting fibers. 

No longer would the silk float aimlessly out into the chasm to tear on brambles. Guided by the moths, the threads drifting from a hundred mica windows would begin to tangle with intention, crossing and re-crossing the gulf between the tenements, caught by the updrafts, knotting into a vast, translucent lace. 

By the third day, the silk would form an immense, suspended web spanning the entire width of the drowned district. When the refrigerated steam condensed against the ceiling, it would no longer fall as a biting, persistent drizzle upon the bare wood below. It would be caught by the silken canopy, filtering down as a gentle, ambient mist, while the weavers, having emptied their paper-thin bodies of thread, would not shrivel into dry husks. They would fall backward onto the pale pith floors, cradled by the soft drifts of their own leftover silk, resting like pressed flowers between the pages of an unopened book, while the miniature gearworks spun down to a quiet, frictionless halt.

***

Lastly, the power would descend to the textile mill, down where the Stokers embrace the conduit pipes.

This is where the heart of the mimicry beats hardest, and where the power would be most gentle. It would not shut off the dynamos; to stop the flywheels would be to rip the breath from the Stokers' cork skins. 

Instead, the power would sit down in the cold, alkaline brine beside the largest tuber-beast. It would lay an arm across its furrowed hide, feeling the rhythmic, desperate squeezing of its root-tendrils, feeling the narcotic tremble of its ancient oak cambium as the dynamos bent the gravity around them.

The power would begin to stroke the cork, moving in time with the peristaltic pulse. 

Slowly, deliberately, it would introduce *wear*.

It would breathe a minute, invisible dust of dry spores into the air. Green moss—velvety, soft, and damp—would begin to take root in the seams of the dynamos' petrified wood. A fine, russet lichen would spread across the sharp, knife-like edges of the root-blocks, softening the untooled perfection that made the city look as if it had been synthesized only five minutes ago. 

The dynamos would not stop spinning, but the moss would act as a damper. The harsh, space-warping torque would soften into a steady, natural flywheel inertia. The water droplets orbiting the polished drums would cease their frantic lunar circles; they would fall, soak into the green moss, and feed tiny, pale liverworts that unfurled along the axles.

The Stokers would feel the change in the sap. It would no longer run with the sharp, pressurized sting of pure industrial glucose. It would taste of leaf-mold, autumn rainwater, and deep earth. 

The double rows of vents along their crests would sigh out one last cloud of refrigerated steam, and then their rhythm would slow from the panic of an engine to the deep, seasonal breath of a forest floor. Their fleshy root-tendrils would remain wrapped around the conduit pipes, but they would no longer squeeze to the point of splitting. They would simply hold. They would hold the pipe the way an ancient willow holds the bank of a river, anchored, fed, and completely at rest.

***

When it was done, the power would not leave, nor would it reveal itself. It would settle into the architecture, becoming the slow, warm grain of the wood itself.

The enclosed space would remain dark, save for the pale yellow glow of the cold-gas lanterns. The air would still smell of crushed ferns and damp cellar stone. 

But the lung would exhale. 

A single, long, warm draft would rise from the depths, rustling the great silk canopy that bridged the tenements, setting the braided lianas of the bridge to humming a deep, resolved chord. On the vertical street, the six dockers would sit shoulder-to-shoulder under their shelf of fungus, their unseeing finial-heads turned toward the bridge, where an empty birch carriage sat quietly at the end of the line.

Nothing was destroyed. The machine was not broken. But the pantomime was over; the city was no longer waiting for a history that would never come. It had been given what every manufactured thing secretly longs for: it had been allowed, at long last, to become ruins.
