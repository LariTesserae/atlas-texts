The first thing the power would do is listen.

A foolish power, moved by sudden pity, would have smashed the brass. It would have reached through the miles of mist, seized the monumental reciprocating pumps in their deep, planet-cracked sumps, and wrenched them still. It would have torn open the overhead decking to let down what it thought was grace: yellow sunlight, dry air, sweet rain gathered from clean clouds.

And in doing so, it would have murdered every living thing across the iron plain.

The scabs would have dried to brittle saucers, their fringes snapping in the unlubricated breeze. The weepers, starved of their mineral condensate, would have cracked into gray dust. The ropes, deprived of the violent updraft and the four-second hammer, would have hung limp until their heavy fibrous cords rotted and pulled them down into the dry dark. And the curd-masses, resting on bare steel without the galvanic wash to slide upon, would have pooled into stagnant tallow and rotted in the sun.

Wisdom knows that life does not ask to be made clean; it asks to endure. Wisdom knows that when a thing is made of rust, lead-grease, and bone-shattering noise, you do not deliver it into silence. You must enter the noise.

So the power did not bring sun. It did not speak in a voice of trumpets or light.

It entered the world as a weight. 

It slipped down between the weeping seams, through the roaring floor-drains, past the three-hundred-foot vertical falls where the black water thundered, until it reached the lower shelf—down where the reciprocating pumps hammered against the crust. There, where the heat was red and the iron was fat with molten tallow, it laid its hands against the anvil.

It did not stop the four-second stroke. It took the blow.

When the monumental piston dropped its thousands of tons into the dark, the strike still came, but the power caught the splintering edge of it. It absorbed the jagged, bone-breaking shatter—the shock that cracked the plates from below and sent the old ropes whipping to their deaths—and rounded it. It turned the concussive snap into a deep, sinusoidal swell. 

Up on the surface, the rings in the oily basins still formed every four seconds, but they no longer shivered with panic. The wave rose, rounded, and fell like a true tide. 

Under the scabs, the leaden domes still flattened; the dry tin click still sounded as the edges broke seal; the fringe of horsehair stiffened with zinc still snapped outward into the water. But the plate beneath them did not ring like an anvil under a sledge. It yielded like a living chest breathing out. The scabs drank their sulfur, and for the first time since the first plate was laid, they did not bleed their tea-colored solder from the shear.

Next, the power climbed the iron conduits into the overhead gloom.

It did not silence the high brass governors. Silence would have brought a stillness so absolute that the cold would have settled into the marrow of the deck-plates and split them. Instead, the power placed a thumb against the shrieking lips of the corroded valves. 

It was an adjustment of less than a millimeter—a gentle, lateral pressure on metal that had screamed for ten thousand years.

The screech changed. The abrasive, tearing white noise—that dense mud of sound that packed the air so tight no vapor could drift—slackened. It dropped an octave, then two, settling into a long, bronze hum. It became a drone, vast and steady as an organ held on a low, continuous chord. The air loosened. It ceased to be an enemy and became merely a medium.

And because the air was no longer torn by friction, the rain changed too.

The wide, flapping sheets of condensed grease that had struck the floor like lead shot ceased to fall in violent gusts. The mist above, tempered by the warmth of the power’s slow passage, began to gather into rounder, softer forms. It still carried the tallow and the kerosene—for without these, the bryophytes would wither in their seams—but it came down in a steady, unbroken hush. It fell not like lead, but like oil over stone, washing the gray moss without ripping it from the rust.

Down in the basin, where the steel had dished between the piers, a curd-mass was sliding.

It was twelve feet long, cold and gray, eating its slow trench through the iron. Its yellow rind was sloughing; it was falling forward an inch an hour into its own corrosive grave.

The power walked out onto the plate. It left no footprints in the greasy silt, but the water curved gently around its presence. It knelt in the inch-deep basin, where the kerosene smell was sharpest, and reached down into the dark liquor.

It did not lift the curd. It did not turn it into a creature with eyes or legs or a song. To give an eye to a thing that lives in darkness is not mercy; it is cruelty. 

Instead, the power slid its fingers under the creature’s ventral pits, between the rotting flesh and the screaming iron.

For a moment, the galvanic rot paused. The bubbling orange paste went still. The four-second pulse arrived—not as a strike that threatened to break the plate, but as a deep, resonant bell swinging through the metal, lifted by the drone of the brass above.

The curd-mass did not have a brain to understand warmth, but it had tallow that could soften.

Under the power’s hand, the harsh, corrosive acid the creature wept began to sweeten. Not into water—water would have killed it—but into a liquor that dissolved the rust without tearing at the beast's own rind. The curd-mass did not have to slough away half its body to take a single step. Its skin held. Its yellow margin smoothed into the likeness of pale amber, tough and whole, resting against the iron without agony.

And the trench it left behind—the silver-blue furrow of freshly turned metal—did not rot instantly into black scale under a violent downpour. The rain fell upon it softly now, laying down a thin, protective seal of grease that let the bright steel gleam in the copper gloom for miles, like a road laid out through a world that had forgotten where to go.

The power did not leave.

A benevolent power that visits and departs is only an observer who wanted to feel useful; wisdom knows that this floor cannot survive an absence. If the power withdrew its hands from the pistons, the hammer would shatter the plates again. If it took its thumb from the brass, the scream would shred the air once more.

So it remained. 

It sat down on the edge of an overflow weir, its feet trailing in the hot, rubbery ribbons of weed that sluiced toward the sumps. It let the tepid, mineral-laden rain fall upon its shoulders until it, too, was dark and slick as the iron stacks. It became the regulator of the floor.

Every four seconds, the great pulse came from the planetary crust: *One.*
The power caught the blow, smoothed it into a cradle’s rock, and sent it out across the plain.

*Two.*
The scabs flared their zinc fringes into the black ripples, gathering what they needed without fear of shearing from the plates.

*Three.*
The weepers, flat and quiet in the micro-crevices, drank the gentle mineral mist that did not pound them into gristle.

*Four.*
The long black ropes in the vertical gales bunched their walnut knots, rhythmic and sure, climbing the bore like bells rung in a tower that had finally found its tune.

Nothing was transformed into gold. No angels sang. The plain remained iron, miles of riveted steel stretching through the greasy dark, worn down by time to a river-boulder smoothness. 

But it was no longer an engine dying of its own violent persistence. It was an abbey of tallow and steam, cold and heavy, singing its one low chord against the planetary core; and in the lowest seam, where the plate curled like a fingernail, the ash-colored moss grew thick and deep, resting its pale roots against an iron floor that had finally learned how to hold it gently.
