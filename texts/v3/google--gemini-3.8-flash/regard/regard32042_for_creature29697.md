The Power did not come down with wings, for wings require an atmosphere thick enough to bite, and the dry plateau would have stripped the feathers to their quills. Nor did it bring a voice, which would have shattered upon the acoustic deadness of the wool and mica like a dropped pitcher. 

It brought no water. Water would have been an atrocity here. A single bead of moisture falling upon the brow of an elk would have been a blasphemy, introducing the ancient, wet terror of rust and swelling, the humiliation of grease, the slow re-entry of mold into an estate that had paid ten thousand years of silence to buy its freedom from decay.

Wisdom knows what a place has cost. Benevolence does not insult the dead—or the un-dead—by bringing them what they climbed five thousand cubits to escape.

The Power arrived as an adjustment of weight. 

It stepped onto the northern lip of the plateau, where the dolomite knuckles meet the sky, with hooves—or feet, or points of pressure—that registered precisely at fifty degrees, matching the stone. It moved across the carpet of beasts. It did not push an antler aside. It did not displace a single wingless bird whose fused horn-plates rested against the haunch of a mica-rat. Where the density broke geometry, where twelve thousand stags coincided in a depression no larger than a barn floor, the Power did not resolve the paradox or untangle the bone. It simply allowed its own coordinates to overlap with theirs, passing through a hundred ribcages without a rustle, feeling the dry, tallow-like resistance of their forms, honoring the lace-like vellum entities that ate the seam between them.

It walked until it reached the northern wash, where the low, dry-laid wall of slate kept out nothing at all.

There it stood before the shepherd.

The shepherd’s brown eyes did not blink. The bluish ring of his *arcus senilis* held the bleached sky. His left hand remained clamped upon the orange-bright copper chisel; his right elbow remained welded by habit and mineral tension to the fleece of the ram. His mind was still stretched taut across the five leagues, an unbroken skin of attention holding the horns in their lattice, keeping the pith-lice in their channels, refusing the world permission to rot.

The Power looked at the mark on the heifer’s horn. 

Four parallel lines. The fourth cut was halfway down its length, held in the middle of a stroke by a will that had turned to quartz.

The Power understood the tragedy of the shepherd: he had successfully halted the cycle of the lowlands, but in doing so, he had become the keystone of the entire arch. He could not lower his arm. If his elbow moved, the joinery of the crowns might slide. If his attention slackened, the coincidence of the stags might collapse back into physical space, bursting the ribs of twenty thousand creatures in an explosion of dry chalk. He had saved them from dying, but he had chained himself to the tally. He was an immortal sentinel who had never once received the relief of a watchman's replacement.

The Power did not take the chisel from his fingers. To disarm him would be to declare his ten-thousand-year vigil an error, and wisdom does not treat love as a mistake.

Instead, the Power sat down upon the slate wall beside him.

It took the form of another shepherd—not of the green valleys, but of the high stone. Its garment was dry felt; its skin was smooth, salt-cured parchment; its crook was ash that had long since forgotten the memory of sap. 

Slowly, without a gust of breath, the Power leaned its right side against the low wall. It extended its own elbow, broad and firm, and pressed it against the fleece of the great ram, precisely alongside the old man’s arm.

For the first time in an era, the weight did not rest on the nomad alone.

The shock of it did not move the air, but the vibration traveled through the horn-canopy, through the neck vertebrae, down into the flint-hooves, and into the mountain’s bedrock: *a second pillar had been laid.*

The shepherd did not turn his head, for his neck was dry pine, but the ring around his iris caught a new light. His thought, which had been stretched like a drumskin across five leagues of sheep and cattle, suddenly found an edge. It was no longer infinite; it was bounded. It was held.

The Power did not speak, but it placed its hand over the shepherd’s withered, tallow-colored knuckles—the hand that held the copper tool.

Together, using the shepherd’s own strength, without haste, they completed the stroke.

The copper chisel bit cleanly into the hard keratin of the heifer’s horn. It finished the fourth line, descending to the precise depth of the other three. 

Then, with a gentle, deliberate pressure, the Power guided the copper edge across all four. A single, clean diagonal strike. 

The tally of five was closed.

The count was finished. Not because there were no more beasts, but because the number was now sealed. None were missing. None had fallen. The valley below had not reclaimed a single knuckle of their herd.

When the cut was made, a small, paper-dry curl of horn fell away. It drifted through the zero-humidity air, turning twice, and landed without a sound upon the fleece below.

The Power tightened its shoulder against the ram, taking into itself the vast, silent geometry of the five leagues—holding the interlocking crowns, holding the mica-rats in their vacuum-seals, keeping the pith-lice aligned in the dry elderberry marrow of the oxen. It assumed the tension of the world so completely that the slate beneath them did not settle by the width of a hair.

Beside it, relieved of the necessity to hold the sky, the old nomad did something he had not done since the world broke its clock.

The muscles of his neck, hard as lignite, loosened by the fraction of a degree. His elbow slid an inch from the ram’s flank, resting entirely now upon the stone of the wall. 

His eyes did not close—for the plateau had forgotten how to sleep—but the vigilance drained out of them, leaving behind only the clear, peaceful glaze of an unclouded mirror. His mind ceased to patrol the herds. It ceased to count. It ceased to brace itself against the memory of green rot and the crying of sick ewes in the lowland mud.

His thought no longer had to act as the mountain’s mortar. It was permitted, at last, to become what the beasts had already become: an estate. A completed thing. 

The Power remained sitting beside him on the low wall, elbow to horn, crook across its knees. 

It did not leave. A benevolent power does not start a vigil only to abandon it when the night turns cold; and on this plateau, where noon is permanent, the watch is forever.

So they sat together beneath the bleached, moistureless cerulean: the shepherd who had climbed to escape death, and the power that had come down to grant him rest. And around them, miles deep and layered three-thick across the knuckles of the dolomite, the herd stood firm in their cold, dry paradise—safe, unmoving, and entirely accounted for.
