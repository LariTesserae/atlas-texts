The power did not arrive with an incision in the sky, nor did it carry the scent of lower valleys—no wet loam, no pollen, no cedar smoke. To bring such things to the granodiorite shelf would have been an act of vanity, a vulgarity of green things imposing their appetite upon an order that had long since outgrown the need for rot and blossom.

It was, before it was anything else, an immense listening.

It entered through the foundation ducts, where the cold-blooded crayfish hung from the joints like translucent leaves, and through the hair-cracks in the basalt where the air was stationary and tasted of pulverized stone. It spread itself thin, down to the gauge of the water-film that greased the spillways, and allowed the city to impress itself upon it: the millions of tons of dry-stacked granodiorite; the shear stress across the lintels; the weight of the high air; the five breaths a minute drawn through down-lined nostrils; the small, stiff hearts of the sleeping lizards in the dry seams, beating once for every fifty swings of a water-drop from a corbel.

Lesser gods would have pitied them.

A lesser benevolence would have brought fire. It would have softened the wind, driven soil into the gutters to invite the seed, cracked the sky with rain that tasted of honey, and loosed speech into the acoustic vacuum until the stone cells echoed with names and lamentations. And in doing so, it would have slaughtered them. It would have rotted their skin-garments, choked the sluices with mud, heaved the five-foot flagstones with taproots, and drowned the barrel-chested people in the fever of their own trapped warmth.

This power possessed the colder, heavier wisdom that understands an unadorned thing: *they are whole.*

The caretaker who stood at the terrace rim, his tallow-greased skin shedding the localized storm, was not a ruin of a man waiting to be restored. He was a creature who had arrived at the exact center of his world, carved by altitude and water into the only shape capable of surviving its terrifying purity. There was no misery in his slow pulse; there was only gravity, mass, and duty.

Yet even the mountain wears away. Even the cyclopean lintel carries, through the centuries, a gathering exhaustion in the molecular grain where the tension is greatest. And the frost—the frost was relentless. Every winter, the water in the microscopic pits fought against the granodiorite, prying at the seams with the quiet, hydraulic violence of ice, waiting for the winter that would split the spine of the plateau and drop the city into the scree below.

The power made no announcement. It did not speak into the air, which was too thin to hold a god’s voice without tearing.

Instead, it lowered its weight into the bedrock.

It settled miles below the deep cisterns, beneath the lowest flume where the blind char hovered, into the ancient mantle where the roots of the plateau met the slow mantle-fire of the world. And there, it became an anchor.

It did not heat the stone—to warm the stone would be to change the water’s flow, to foul the laminar chutes with steam, to disturb the slow winter sleep of the lizards. Rather, it absorbed the catastrophic peaks of the frost. It became a thermal governor, invisible and imperceptible: on the nights when the sky went black-violet and the cold descended like an iron slab, sharp enough to cleave the joints, the stone held just enough latency—a fraction of a fraction of a degree—that the water in the hairline seams remained water, sliding through the weep-holes rather than locking into wedges of ice.

The frost-shatter ceased, not by a miracle of thaw, but by the thinnest margin of clearance. The masonry would stand for another hundred thousand years. The work would remain, and the hands to do it would remain.

Then the power turned to the beings themselves.

It did not alter their bodies. It did not speak to their minds. But as the caretaker stood on the slick basalt, looking out toward the silvered curtain of rain five miles away, the power placed itself within the stone beneath his broad, splayed feet.

When the caretaker struck the lintel with his open palm—that flat, percussive crack that traveled three miles through the masonry to tell the lower tiers that the upper spillway was clear—the vibration did not simply dissipate into the cold void of the mountain.

The power received the blow, carried it through the continuum of granodiorite, and returned it.

Not an echo. An echo is dead stone throwing back a dead noise. It was an *answering weight*.

Through the soles of his feet, through the thick bones of his legs and the heavy ring of his ribs, the caretaker felt the mountain steady itself beneath his strike. It was a sensation like the settling of a colossal keystone into a bed of ground shale—a deep, subsonic thrum that said: *The joint holds. The drop is true. The plumb-line has found bottom.*

For the first time in his life, the caretaker paused before reaching for his slate pole.

He did not look up at the empty sky, for he knew there was nothing there but the wind and the swifts. He did not look over the edge into the grey abyss. He looked down at the flagstone beneath his toes. He felt the vast, interlocking geometry of the blocks extending through the mist, through the alleys and the stepped ramps, down into the black water-vaults, all of it resting on an immovable, living density that met his weight with equal, patient force.

His broad, low-bridged nostrils flared, drawing in the cold, rain-washed air. The tallow on his brow channeled a single, heavy drop down the deep groove beside his nose, down his chin, to the basalt.

He raised his foot and struck the flagstone once more.

The mountain answered, low and long, through the dark marrow of the basalt, carrying his labor into its own.

Reassured of the plumb, the caretaker turned his back to the rain, adjusted the fossilized slate against his shoulder, and walked down the steps toward the northern weirs to clear the drift from the grates before noon.
