A lesser power, arriving here with an armful of old habits, would have brought rain.

It would have looked at the pale gypsum flats, the bone-colored dust, the lips clamped shut over the teeth to save the steam of the lungs, and it would have wept. And its tears would have been an atrocity. 

To bring a cloud here would be to unleash an acid. The water would strike the hot feldspar and crack the massifs like heated skillet-iron; it would swell the dry adobe tenements into slumping porridge, dissolve the carillons’ porous bellies as they stood upon the gypsum, and rot the fine cartilage ribs of the kites until they fell like soaked parchment out of the sky. The people—whose skin is cured parchment, whose blood runs deep and safe beneath lard, whose grandfathers are the lime in the mortar—would break out in blisters, their lungs drowned by the foul, humid soup of their own atmosphere turned swamp.

A wise power does not mistake its own preferred climate for grace. 

The Power that arrived above the seventh massif did not take the form of a storm, or a burning bush, or a golden hand reaching from the void. It had no shape at all, because a shape has displacement, and displacement here means a sudden draft, and a sudden draft can fray a cable or flip an umbrella-kite against a pylon.

Instead, it came as a quality of attention. It settled into the warm, dry air not as a visitor, but as an ambient comprehension—a stillness even quieter than the stillness of the kiln.

For three hundred years of their reckoning, it did not move a single grain of talc. 

It watched.

It watched the husbands and wives in the adobe rooms, the dull ox-rib scraper passing over the shoulder blades, the intimate and thrifty gathering of the grey paste into bronze jars. It understood that this was not degradation; it was piety. It saw that to keep every grain of carbon, to return the breath of the dead to the condenser-coils and the bone-meal to the brick, was an act of profound fidelity to the real. In a world where nothing was given for free, these people had made an ecology that was as exact and spare as an equation.

It saw the tally-man out upon his chain-slung basket, dropping his twelve pounds of lead shot into the abyss. It looked down into the purple depths where the cold, wet air crawled like grease upon the low muds, and it saw what the tally-man feared without ever having named it: that someday, in the long cooling of the planet’s core, the low rot would rise. That the thermal chimney would bank, the air-cushion would falter, and the heavy damp of the lower crust would slowly creep up the sheer sixty-mile basalt faces to drown the massifs in moss, rust, and water-sickness.

The Power loved them too deeply to change them into something that needed a river.

So, when it finally acted, its work was invisible, buried far below the reach of their longest drills, down where the basalt ribs met the planetary root.

First, it reached into the thermal reservoirs, deep where the massive graphite-bedded flywheels hummed against the dark. It did not increase the heat—to make the massifs hotter would be to force the humans to spend more lard, to demand more distillate from the deep cracked-oil stills. Instead, it smoothed the basalt grain. It bound the tectonic seams with a microscopic lattice of pure carbon, so that the expansion and contraction of the plateau became as regular and perfect as the movement of an astronomical chronometer. 

Because of this, the three-mile spans of tramway cable would never again experience the catastrophic, unpredictable shudder of a micro-fault. The cables would sag and tighten with the rising and setting of the sun, predictable to the millimeter, so that the tally-man’s counterweights hung true against the brass pegs, season after season, without a single snapped stay.

Second, it placed an invisible boundary along the lip of the Great Drop.

Not a wall of force, but a permanent thermal inversion—a dry, radiant updraft that smelled faintly of warm slate and copper. Any damp vapor that attempted to lift from the rotting basins below would hit that shelf of baking air and fall back, condensed against its own lower mire, forever forbidden from crossing the seventy leagues to touch the dry cities. The massifs would remain high, bright, and untouched by mildew until the sun burned itself out.

And lastly, it did one thing so small that only one living soul noticed it.

On the edge of the fifth massif, the old tally-man was sitting in his iron bucket. His speech had been gone for twenty years; his gaze was fixed on the brass markers driven into the rock face thirty feet away. His water-ration canister was down to its last two fingers of flat, metallic distillate. His throat was a dry flue; his chest clicked as he breathed his short, lateral breaths through tight lips.

The sun was reaching its highest point, white and merciless, burning the feldspar floor until the horizon vibrated like a plucked wire.

A draft came up from the abyss—not cold, but warm, clean, smelling of dry sage and toasted meal. It entered the circular window of the hut. 

The old man did not startle. He turned his eyes slowly, the parchment of his neck wrinkling without sound.

On the iron shelf beside his rib-bone scraper, where usually there was only the grey talc-dust blown off the flats, there was a small heap of something new.

It was not food, and it was not water. It was a block of salt, dry as a brick, but pressed with a crystalline density that made it hard as carillon-horn. And pressed into its surface, waiting to be rasped by an old thumb, were tiny flakes of an ancient, petrified resin that had no name in his language—something that tasted, when touched to the dry membrane of the tongue, like sunlight striking cedar shavings in a room that had never known rain.

The tally-man took up his scraper. With the horn point, he flaked a single shaving from the block and placed it beneath his upper lip.

His mouth did not water—for water was an error—but a profound, deep warmth spread through the lard beneath his skin. His knuckles loosened. The ache in the joints of his knees, where the graphite-dust had ground into the sinew over forty winters, receded into a clean, numb comfort. 

He looked out the round window.

Twelve yards above him, an amber kite was hovering motionless, its cartilage spars bowed into their perfect parabolas, its milky skin translucent against the yellow-white sky. It tilted its left wing-tip by an eighth of an inch, as if acknowledging a neighbor who had just moved his chair an inch to the left.

The tally-man did not thank the sky. He did not build an altar; he had no wood to waste on fires, and no breath to waste on hymns.

He simply picked up his rib-bone scraper, ran it down his shin, collected the white petal of his own dry hide, and blew it out the window into the warm, eternal wind.

And the Power, drifting through the air between the massifs, caught it before the kite could reach it, held the flake of skin in the palm of its vast, unhurried thought for a thousand miles of blue sky, and found it entirely good.
