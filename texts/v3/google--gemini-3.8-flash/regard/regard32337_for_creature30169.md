A power that is both wise and benevolent does not arrive with a storm of rain.

To bring water here would be an atrocity of clumsy love. A cloudburst would be a hammer of thermal shock upon the unweathered glass; the boiling steam would shatter the porcelain terraces, crack the mica-crawlers down their hinges, and tear the suspended baskers from their web-lines. Water would turn the dry talc to a choking paste and dissolve the parchment of the man’s skin, sloughing his face away like wet tissue before his calcified lungs could ever learn to draw a breath. 

A wise power understands the dignity of the dry. It does not mistake an unfinished genesis for a fallen world.

Therefore, when the attention arrives, it brings no weather. It arrives without a shadow, for the sky-seam has no sun to block, and without a footstep, for it has no weight to press upon the unyielding quartz. 

It is, at first, merely a suspension of the shear.

The attention descends through the cold, bruised ash-violet of the upper altitude. It watches the mica-crawlers snap their plates across the electrostatic gaps; it notes the mathematical elegance of their crystal seeds; it admires the long silver dendrites of the baskers harvesting the pale fracture of the sky. It recognizes that this world is not cruel, only young—a world whose bones are still protruding through its skin, struggling through the agonizing, unlubricated labor of its own construction.

Then, it gathers at the shelf of freshly cleaved basalt.

It looks through the doorway of stacked slate. It perceives the low-roofed box, the dried man upon his haunches, the yellow horn-spheres of his eyes, and the two round cobblestones between his chalk-white shins. 

It reads his architecture. It sees what the man has spent centuries doing: he is not trying to escape, for there is nowhere to flee; he is not trying to die, for death has no purchase on matter that has already set into horn and talc. He is simply building a damper. He is dragging the duct of the world up from the pit to silence the iron bell in the marrow of his jaw.

The Power knows what it would cost to make him human again: an agony of rehydration, the fire of blood forced through petrified vessels, the terror of wet organs restarting in an air that cannot feed them. It will not force him backward into a flesh that the world has already forgotten how to hold.

Instead, the Power moves down the line.

It slides along the graphite-fern cord, passing through three thousand feet of dead, dry air. It does not snap the rope. It does not hurry the descent. It drops into the gray obscurity of the lower floor, down where the pressure is an anvil and the rock is still squeezed like clay through the planet’s hidden joints.

Down there, where the first blind sparks of will are sputtering in the calcified dark, the Power reaches into the extrusion.

It does not pull up another writhing knot of wire. It does not take a pulsing, orphaned organ of porcelain marrow. 

It finds the seam where the sound begins. 

The roar is an unjoined chord, an open, dissonant interval of iron grinding on quartz that has never found its resolving cadence. The Power does not silence the mountain—to stop the mountain would be to abort the world—but it places its own subtle, infinite weight against the throat of the fracture. It acts as an acoustic hinge. It catches the rising frequency of the stone and rounds it. It shears off the needle-sharp overtones, the glass-shattering harmonics that lodge like splinters in the tooth-marrow, and gathers the vibration into a single, deep, foundational ground-note: steady, dark, and smooth as an oiled flywheel.

Then, from the deep floor, it shapes the gift for the hook.

It is a stone. Not an embryonic organ, and not a jagged plate of glass. It is a dense, gray block of siltstone, compacted under pressures so uniform that it possesses no cleavage planes, no razor edges, no capacity to splinter. Its surfaces are miraculously, gently flat, cured to the exact thickness of the remaining opening in the slate shelter’s wall. 

The Power sets the horn hook into the belly of this stone.

Then, it returns to the ledge. It does not linger as a phantom or speak with a voice, because a voice here would be an insult to the work. It simply waits beside the wooden wedge, steadying the dry timber so it does not split under the strain.

In his low box, forty years after the last haul, the man feels the rope shudder.

The shudder is different. Usually the descent-cord jerks with the erratic, twitching spasms of the half-willed things caught below—the blind lashings of fibrous wire or the heavy, nauseating throb of the marrow-nodules. This pull is steady. It is a slow, downward draw that hums with the dull, clean tension of an anchor set into deep earth.

The man’s river-pebble eyes do not widen—they cannot—but a microscopic flake of calcium dust falls from his eyelid onto his cheek.

He crawls forward. 

His leaden cloak of fern-hair rattles like a bag of iron nails. His knee-joints grate, grinding out pinches of pale bone-meal that drift onto the porch of basalt. He reaches the wedge. He takes the graphite rope into his horn-grooved palms.

He heaves.

It takes him seventeen months of continuous, glacier-slow labor to bring the hook up from the gulf. The Power does not lift it for him. To take the rope from his hands would be to strip him of the only purpose his calcified will possesses. But the Power does something else: where the graphite fibers bite into his scarred palms, the friction no longer tears away his dried skin. The air in his shelter does not grow warm, but the razor-gales of talc drift around him instead of cutting across his knuckles.

On a day when the quartz spire beyond the ledge lengthens by the width of a needle, the hook breaks the surface of the shelf.

It brings the gray block.

The man sits back on his heels. He does not touch it immediately. He stares at it with his cloudy lenses. It does not twitch. It does not pulse once every three days. It does not leak any glittering meal. It simply rests on the basalt lip, dull, matte-surfaced, and profoundly, totally silent.

He reaches out. His stiff fingers, gray as slate, trace the flat face of the stone.

It has no edge that can cleave. It is not rounded like the river-cobbles he keeps in his shelter—it does not mock this world with the memory of lost water—but it has been finished. It is the first piece of matter this planet has ever produced that is not waiting to crack, not waiting to cool, not waiting to tear through an iron die. It is at rest.

He gathers it against his ribs. It is heavy, but his arms are made of the mountain's own mineral torque. He drags it into the box.

The shelter is nearly complete. The back wall is thick with centuries of calcified relics, all of them pressed together, a dense, rattling mosaic of aborted will. But near the corner, right at the level of his temple when he sits with his knees drawn to his collarbones, there is a gap. A triangular throat of raw sky where the ash-violet light leaks in, and through which the eternal tectonic scream enters uninterrupted, striking directly at the base of his skull.

The man lifts the gray block.

It slides into the void. It does not grate. The Power has shaped it so that it kisses the slate shards on either side with the precision of two ice crystals locking in the upper atmosphere. It does not require mortar; its own stillness binds it.

The gap closes.

The light goes out.

In the utter darkness of the stacked-slate box, the sound changes.

The tectonic hum does not vanish—it cannot vanish while the world is being born—but the gray block swallows its teeth. The scream stops. The vibration in the marrow of his jaw drops an octave, then three, then five, until it is no longer an ache, no longer a needle of iron pressed against his dental nerves. 

It becomes a low, rhythmic respirating pulse, not his own, but the mountain’s: the slow, magnificent breathing of a titan that is thousands of miles wide, rocking itself to sleep in the cold cradles of the void.

The man’s hands drop from the wall. They fall into his lap, where the two river-cobblestones rest between his shins.

For the first time since he laid the first stone, his joints do not hold themselves rigid against the vibration. The tension that had held his ribcage locked against the ceiling of his shelter lets go. His spine settles, fraction by fraction, resting its dry, chalky segments against the back wall.

His pulse—the vibration of the world that kept his heart from setting—does not stop. It merely syncs with the resolved, damped chord of the mountain. It slows from the frenzy of an extrusion to the deep, tectonic patience of the rock itself.

Outside, the Power does not leave. 

It settles over the low roof like a blanket of dry, imperceptible snow. It will not make the sky blue. It will not summon birds that would only starve in the graphite ferns. It will not force this world to be anything other than what it is: a hard, dry, crystal cradle, where life clicks in mica hinges and drinks the lightning of the faults.

But as the centuries begin to turn, and the quartz spire inches upward into the silent, unsetting violet, the Power stays perched upon the lintel of the slate box. 

It keeps the roof from shifting. It keeps the talc from choking the entrance. And inside the dark, in the quiet hollow behind the keystone, it watches over the man who held a river in two small stones, while he finally, deeply, without waking and without dying, sleeps.
