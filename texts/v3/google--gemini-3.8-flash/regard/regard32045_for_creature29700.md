A foolish power, seeing this place, would have torn open the tectonic cap. 

It would have imagined the gloom an affliction, the bruised luminescence a starvation, and the closed ears of the weavers a tragedy requiring the gift of song. It would have flooded the dry, non-reflective slate with earth; it would have scattered seeds that rot in ozone; it would have dragged the slack-bodied, lard-skinned climbers into the terrible glare of a yellow star, where their soft bones would snap beneath the unbuffered drag of true gravity, weeping at the beauty of their liberation until they choked.

A wise power does not mistake its own home for the definition of light.

And so it came into the void not as an intrusion of form or a burst of glory, but as an adjustment of pressure—a quietness so patient that neither the electrostatic pits of the swimmers nor the thread-limbed stilts on the basin floor registered its arrival. It passed through the iron-dense ceiling like heat sinking through water, settling into the vast, hollow gulfs between the spars.

It observed the grammar of the place:

The six-hour lung. 
The copper rasped into shell-armor. 
The egg-mist raining into the deep. 
The pale meat drying upon the warm hum of the coils.

It saw that the cycle was whole, but it also saw what the inhabitants, in their short-horizoned eternity, could not: the machine was wearing down. 

The kettles, by the millions, were shaving the conductive zinc and copper coatings from the transit rails; in three thousand cycles, a primary bridge would short, sending an arc of three million volts through the horizontal web, boiling the middle air and flash-frying the drifting herds. The expansion joints in the slate, packed tight with the jelly-spheres of the stilts, were weeping tiny, hydraulic fractures into the bedrock below; eventually, the tectonic cap would shift, its tolerances blown, crushing the basin like an empty eggshell. And inside the dark conduits, the weavers were inbreeding toward silence, their litters smaller each generation, their fingers losing the grip required to haul the weighted lines against the draft.

This world was complete, but it was brittle. It had the terrible innocence of an accidental meadow growing on the deck of a sinking ship.

The benevolent power did not alter the light. It did not mute the deafening roar of the inductive bleeding, for it understood that to these things, silence was not peace, but the terrifying cessation of the universe’s pulse. 

Instead, it went into the bones of the machine.

It laid its invisible attention into the alloys of the vertical tracks. Where the kettles filed the copper away, it touched the molecular grain, coaxing the metal into a slow, self-knitting renewal—a microscopic, perpetual sweating of trace minerals from the deep mantle, so that the shells of the parasites would always find zinc to scrape, and the pylons would never bare their nerves to the damp.

It sank beneath the black slate, down into the blind foundations where the mile-wide pylons met the root-rock of the world. There, it gathered the micro-fractures in the joints. It did not weld them shut—to fill them would starve the grubs of the stilts—but cushioned them with a dense, living grease of deep silicate, turning the jagged cracks into elastic cushions that could take the shifting weight of the ceiling for ten million years without a shudder.

Then, it climbed.

It entered the hollow core of the central pylon, where the air was hot, oily, and still.

A weaver was clinging to a bronze-runged ladder three hundred meters above the empty floor of the shaft. She was thin, her skin translucent as wax paper, her ribs visible like the reeds of a basket. She had pressed her scarred forehead against the guide-wire, resting her skull against the frequency, waiting for the thirty-minute shift that would signal the gathering of the water.

Her belly was empty. Her last catch had slipped the plaited bark-hooks and tumbled into the desert below, and her spade-tipped fingers were trembling with the fine, rhythmic twitch of exhaustion.

The wise power did not appear before her. It did not give her manna, nor speak in a voice she could not hear.

It simply laid a gentle, conductive warmth into the steel cable, three inches above her brow.

The vibration did not stop—the familiar, brutal drone remained—but within that drone, it introduced an overtone. A tiny, structural harmony that had not been there before. 

The woman felt it against the bone of her temple. It was not a language; it was a vector. It felt to her nervous system like the pull of an eddy in a pipe, an inductive knot that sang of tension and release. 

She opened her milk-white, vestigial eyes into the gloom of the shaft, uncomprehending, but her hands moved. Guided by the resonant sweet-spot in the cable, her spade-like nails probed the seam of an unworked maintenance hatch that had remained sealed since the builders abandoned the frame. The alloy was fused, stubborn with old factory-lacquer. 

The power did not blow the hatch open. It merely relaxed the crystalline lattice of the latch-pins by half a percent.

The woman heaved, her feet braced on the ladder rungs. With a dry, iron crack that was instantly swallowed by the exterior roar, the seal gave way.

Inside lay an unopened reserve: an auxiliary condensation conduit, three centuries old, choked thick with thirty vertical miles of wild, undisturbed aerophytic moss, black as oil, glistening with heavy, cold moisture and crawling with a subspecies of blind, soft-shelled crustaceans that had never seen a hook.

The weaver did not marvel. She did not fall to her knees. She did not know she had been answered, because she had never known she was asking. 

She simply reached out her long, lard-white arm, tore a handful of the bitter, peppery greens, and stuffed them into her mouth, chewing with the flat, rhythmic indifference of an animal that has found an unexpected pocket of fat. Then she uncoiled her line of fern-bark from her hip, wedged her toes into the lip of the new chamber, and began to weave a better knot.

High above, near the soot-colored cap, the power withdrew its focus, pulling back until the basin was only a dark, vibrating slot in the crust of a dead planet.

It left the darkness bruised. 
It left the air screaming. 
It left the water to fall like a hammer every six hours upon the unyielding slate.

It left them the integrity of their struggle, their narrow, magnificent blindness, and the hard, unpitying metal that was their only heaven; but it ensured that when the weavers leaned their skulls against the iron to listen for the rain, the iron would answer, and the floor would hold, and the engine would never go cold.
