To step onto this plateau is not merely to visit another geography; it is to introduce friction, moisture, and decay into a vault where the universe has quietly resolved them. You are carrying time into a place that has discarded it. 

If you intend to walk among the knit horns and the mica scales, you must prepare not for a violent wilderness, but for an environment that will try to absorb your momentum and reduce you to its own static baseline.

Here is how you prepare.

---

### 1. Locomotion: Negotiating the Living Floor

You will not touch the earth. For miles in every direction, your only substrate will be the dried, unyielding backs of beasts. 

*   **Footwear with Soft, High-Traction Rubber:** Do not bring crampons or spiked mountaineering boots. Metal spikes will skate unpredictably off polished horn, chitinous carapaces, and petrified kidskin. Instead, wear approach shoes or heavy hiking boots resoled with soft, sticky, high-friction climbing rubber (such as Stealth or Vibram Megagrip). You need a compound that can mold slightly over the curvature of a spine, the ridge of a tortoiseshell, or a knot of tangled antlers.
*   **Ankle Stabilization:** Your footing will be disastrously uneven. You will constantly step into the hollows between an elk-like haunch and a dormant bird’s breast, or slip into the narrow gap between two interlocked ribcages. Rigid ankle bracing is mandatory to prevent torsional fractures.
*   **Rubber-Tipped Trekking Poles:** Bring two, with broad, pliable rubber ferrules. You will need them to probe for stability. Some beasts are stacked four deep; while the stack is entirely solid, a foot wedging between a flank and an antler can trap your leg like a dry vise. 
*   **The Weight Protocol:** You must accept, immediately and without hesitation, that **you will step on faces.** You will step on eyes that reflect the pale sky like polished obsidian; you will step on nostrils that do not flare. If you hesitate out of instinctual empathy or disgust, you will lose your balance and fall into the crevices. If you fall, climbing back out means hauling yourself up by grasping cold ears, spiraled horns, and wing-joints. Steel yourself mentally for the total indignity you must inflict upon these creatures, and remind yourself: *they do not care.*

---

### 2. Navigating the Broken Geometry

The plateau contains zones where space has folded under the sheer weight of biomass. You must treat these areas with the same caution an alpine climber reserves for blue-ice crevasses.

*   **A Throwing Line / Visual Probes:** Keep a spool of bright, high-contrast surveyor’s chalk-line and a pocket of heavy lead sinkers. When you approach a depression or a draw where the density of animals looks impossibly tight—where you can see the bodies flickering or blurring together—**do not walk into it.** 
*   **Spatial Overlap Avoidance:** The text notes that in depressions, horns pass through flanks like smoke, yet both remain solid to the touch. Do not test what happens when your own, time-bound, fluid-filled biological foot enters a coordinate already occupied by three dry sheep. You run the risk of spatial co-location: having your tissue fused or trapped within the pith-filled interior of a creature that cannot be moved or broken. Stick strictly to the ridges, the knuckle-crests of dolomite, and the uppermost layer of the ungulate lattice.

---

### 3. Atmospheric and Environmental Survival

The environment is a closed, sterile thermal system. It will not freeze you instantly, but it will leach the life out of you with quiet, mathematical efficiency.

*   **Fifty-Degree Thermal Layering:** Fifty degrees Fahrenheit (10°C) sounds mild, but without wind, without weather shifts, and beneath a sun that imparts no radiant heat, it acts as a steady, patient heat-sink. Wear wool or dense fleece next to the skin, covered by a tough, puncture-resistant shell (the tips of broken horns and dried lichens can be needle-sharp). You will not be sweating much, but you will constantly lose body heat through your boots into the vast, cool thermal mass of the animals beneath you. Use thermal insoles (aerogel or dense felt) to insulate the soles of your feet.
*   **Hydration is an Absolute Emergency:** There is no moisture in the air. None. The animals contain only dry, chalky pith; there are no streams, no dew, no snowpacks to melt. The ambient air will pull water from your mucous membranes with every breath.
    *   Bring twice the water you think you need, stored in insulated bladders (water tastes unnervingly strange in an environment this quiet).
    *   Carry **saline nasal sprays**, **ophthalmic lubricating ointments**, and **petroleum jelly** for your lips and the rims of your nostrils. Within three hours, your sinuses will dry, crack, and bleed if you do not artificially humidify them.
    *   Wear a high-filtration moisture-retention mask (like a heat-and-moisture exchanger mask used by polar explorers) to trap the humidity of your own exhaled breath before it is lost to the void.
*   **Eye Protection (Fixed UV Radiation):** The sun does not move, nor does it dim. The bleached cerulean sky and the pale dolomite knuckles offer no shadows large enough to rest in, except the short, hard silhouettes beneath animal bellies. Wear Category 3 or 4 glacier glasses. The white rock, the pale hides, and the constant, indifferent midday glare will cause severe photochemical keratitis (dry-eye snow blindness) within hours.

### 4. Acoustic Defense: The Horror of the Void

This will likely be the most psychologically destabilizing aspect of the journey. 

The millions of dry, feathered, woolly bodies act as an infinite field of acoustic baffling. Sound will not echo. It will not travel. The world has an anechoic coefficient close to 100%. If you shout, your voice will drop dead an inch from your lips. The only sounds you will hear are:
1.  The dry, wooden *tock* of your boots against horns.
2.  The sound of your clothes rubbing against your skin.
3.  The violent, wet machinery of your own interior: the thudding of your heart, the high-pitched hum of your nervous system, the clicking of your throat when you swallow, and the rushing of blood in your ears.

*   **Bring Auditory Grounding:** An anechoic environment rapidly induces sensory-deprivation psychosis, auditory hallucinations, and panic. Pack bone-conduction headphones loaded with familiar ambient sound—rain falling on leaves, city traffic, running water, wind in grass. You will need to play this periodically just to remind your brain that motion and fluidity still exist somewhere in the cosmos. 
*   **A Personal Sounder:** A small, metallic mechanical clicker (like a dog-training clicker) kept in your pocket. Occasionally striking a sharp, artificial, metallic sound provides an immediate reality check against the deadening acoustic foam of the plateau.

### 5. Historical Relics and Camp Protocol

You may be tempted to sleep in the dry-laid stone shelters left by the ancient nomadic shepherds. 

*   **Camp *Only* on Stone or Within Ruins:** Do not pitch a tent or sleep directly on top of the animals. While they do not move, sleeping on a surface of thousands of compressed, unblinking eyes and frozen, dry snouts will erode your sanity. Seek out the low walls of slate slabs left by the ancestors. Their hearths are dead, but their stone floors offer the only psychologically neutral ground on the plateau.
*   **Do Not Attempt to Burn the Beasts:** If you are cold, do not try to hack off a limb or horn to make a fire. The text notes their interiors are like elderberry pith or compressed chalk; without fluids or resins, they will likely not catch, or if they do, they may smolder with a suffocating, ancient acridity that you cannot ventilate in the windless air. Bring self-contained chemical warming pads or a pressurized, sealed-fuel stove.
*   **The Tally Marks:** If you find the bright copper carvings on the horns, look at them, but do not add to them. They were left by people who realized that the world was setting like plaster around them. To leave a mark is an act of desperation you should not indulge; your goal is to leave, not to record your presence in the ledger of the unmoving.

---

### 6. The Psychological Hazard: "The Equilibrium Sickness"

The greatest danger of this plateau is not predatory, nor is it toxic. It is ontological.

The plateau has achieved absolute thermodynamic and narrative rest. It has solved the problem of survival by surrendering the burden of change. When you have been walking for six hours, and the sun has not moved a fraction of an inch, and the bird beneath your heel hasn't blinked, and the air smells of nothing—not rot, not dust, not life, only dry slate—a profound, sedative heaviness will enter your thoughts.

You will feel a deep, insidious temptation to sit down on the broad, talc-smooth back of a tortoiseshell, place your chin upon your knees, and simply stop. It is not the desire to die; it is the desire to match your surroundings. 

*   **Set a Hard Chronometer:** Do not rely on your internal clock or the daylight. Bring an analog, mechanical wind-up watch. Set a countdown: you enter for *X* hours, and when the chime sounds, you turn around, regardless of what smoothed summit lies ahead. 
*   **A "Living" Anchor:** Keep a small, sealed vial of wet, unpasteurized river mud or damp garden soil in your breast pocket, close to your body heat. When the plateau begins to make your own existence feel absurd and intrusive, open the vial. Smell the rot, the bacteria, the water, the chaos. 

Remind yourself: *You are an engine of waste, heat, and time.* You do not belong in a place that has already finished. Take your readings, marvel at the terrible mercy of their peace, and step carefully back down the mountain before your own pulse begins to feel like an unnecessary noise.
