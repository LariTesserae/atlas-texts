To visit this plateau as an ordinary mortal is to step onto the surface of a machine that operates according to a logic older than linear space. You are not exploring an untamed wilderness, nor are you visiting an ordinary ruin. You are entering an abandoned, continuously idling, non-Euclidean tectonic engine. 

To survive it, you must prepare not only for extreme terrestrial conditions—altitude, moisture, and cold—but for the systematic failure of your own inner ear, eyes, and proprioception.

Here is how you must prepare.

---

### 1. Apparel and Gear: Surviving the "Grease" and the Thin Air
The immediate threat is not freezing to death, but becoming completely, irreversibly saturated in near-freezing moisture while starving for oxygen.

*   **Vapor-Barrier Layering (Not Breathable Fabrics):** Standard breathable waterproof membranes (like Gore-Tex) will fail. The air does not rain; moisture condenses *directly onto surfaces* from the daylight itself. Breathable fabrics will quickly choke with condensation, driving moisture inward toward your skin. You need non-porous, heavy-duty vulcanized rubber or PVC outer garments with fully welded seams. Beneath this, wear thick, high-pile synthetic fleece or heavy boiled wool—materials that retain dead-air space and insulate even when damp.
*   **Sealed Footwear with Mechanical Grip:** Leather will rot or soften within days under the acidic black peat and constant seeping water. Use seamless, heavy vulcanized rubber muck boots with deep, self-cleaning lugs. Basalt covered in mineral sweat and liverwort grease offers virtually zero friction. 
*   **Supplemental Oxygen and Diamox (Acetazolamide):** You are at the altitude where clouds are manufactured. The air is razor-thin, tasting of raw iron. Begin a course of Diamox days before your departure, and carry a lightweight pulsed-dose oxygen system. Hypoxic confusion in a place where a misstep can fold you into an updraft is fatal.
*   **Extreme Water Filtration & Demineralization:** Though water sits everywhere in pools, the iron taste indicates massive mineral saturation, and the sterile peat may leach unknown heavy compounds. Bring a portable reverse-osmosis pump or a high-grade distillation kit. Do not drink the surface water raw; it will destroy your kidneys.

### 2. Navigational Equipment for Broken Geometry
Your compass will be erratic, GPS will be utterly non-functional, and your eyes will actively lie to you about scale, distance, and orientation. 

*   **The Sounding Tether (Your Most Critical Tool):** Carry a stiff, telescoping fiberglass pole (3 to 4 meters long) and a spool of high-visibility lead-weighted line. Before stepping onto any street, across any threshold, or down any stairway, you must cast the lead weight ahead of you. 
    *   *If the line falls sideways or snaps taut upward:* You have encountered a shear-seam or a ninety-degree gravity fold.
    *   *If the weight appears to swell or vanish:* You are observing a spatial gradient. Do not step there until you have mapped the seam.
*   **Reading the Adze-Marks:** Memorize the prompt's detail: the builders left shallow, rhythmic notches in the basalt thresholds. You must train yourself to stop looking at the horizon and look obsessively at your feet. If the flagstones lack chipped notches, **do not cross.** The ancients did not waste labor; where there are no notches, there was never a safe way through on foot.
*   **Inertial Dead-Reckoning (Local):** Because the city blocks slide slowly across the plateau on beds of subterranean grease, landmarks will shift relative to one another over the course of days. Do not rely on "that tower to the north." Track your movements using relative distance traveled along specific avenues, and use physical chalk or fluorescent wax markers to blaze your retreat route on the stone. (Ferns will overrun chalk on dirt within hours, so mark only vertical basalt).

### 3. Vestibular and Psychological Countermeasures
The spatial paradoxes of this place are not visual illusions; they are physical facts. Stepping across a gutter will abruptly double your weight; looking down a staircase will reveal the very sky you are standing under. Your nervous system is not equipped for this.

*   **Anti-Vertigo and Vestibular Suppressants:** Carry prescription-grade scopolamine patches, meclizine, and high-dose ondansetron (for nausea). The brain becomes violently sea-sick when the visual field dictates that a building is growing closer while your feet tell you that you are walking away from it.
*   **Grid-Etched Monocular or Goggles:** Wear clear protective goggles etched with a fine, mechanical Cartesian grid. When perspective warps—when a wall on your left dwarfs the zenith while the wall on your right shrinks to a toy—the grid provides a fixed, rigid frame of reference that belongs to your skull, preventing sensory disorientation from knocking you off balance.
*   **Sensory Grounding Objects:** Keep something distinctly heavy, small, and unyielding in your pocket—a brass cube, a steel ball. When you look down a flight of stairs and see the clouds beneath your boots and the world recursive below you, you will experience an overwhelming agoraphobia: the sensation that you have unmoored from reality. Squeezing a dense, heavy object anchors tactile reality to your hand.

### 4. Camping and the Living Peat
Setting up camp here presents unprecedented biological and physical hazards.

*   **Never Sleep on the Ground:** The ferns grow an inch an hour. If you pitch a tent on the soil or the liverwort carpet and sleep for eight hours, you will awaken wrapped in wet, stiff, waxy fronds, pinned to the earth like an insect in a web.
*   **Avoid the Warm Moss Mounds:** The 20-foot spongy moss mounds that breathe warm vapor will be intensely tempting in the freezing damp. **Do not burrow into them.** Their warmth is driven by internal microbial fermentation; inside, the air will be choked with carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and rotting vegetative gases. You will fall asleep in the warmth and suffocate.
*   **Bivouac on High Basalt Masonry:** Camp only on the tops of the cyclopean storehouses, clearing the perimeter down to bare rock with a short scythe or heavy machete. The mechanical sliding of the city blocks is slow (a low, gravelly grinding), but avoid placing camp gear across the joints between two different stone buildings, lest the shifting foundations shear your camp in two during the night.
*   **Flameless Heat Systems:** There is no dry wood. The peat is black, wet, and sterile, and the ferns are soaked in waxy, cold moisture that resists ignition. Pack flameless catalytic heaters powered by sealed butane or propane canisters. You cannot rely on building a fire for warmth or morale.

### 5. Behavioral Protocol: The Mindset of the Traveler
To visit this world successfully, you must surrender human architectural intuition:

1.  **Do not trust straight lines.** If an avenue looks completely straight, that is precisely where the geometry is most likely to shear. Curves and worn paths are safer because they follow the paths of least physical distortion charted by the barefoot ancients.
2.  **Embrace the Silence.** There are no birds, no insects, and no predators. You do not need to watch for bears or wolves. The threat is structural, geological, and spatial. Listen closely: if the low, gravelly grinding of the stones underfoot abruptly stops or changes pitch, the ground beneath your district is shifting its drift.
3.  **Respect the Staircase to Nowhere.** If you find the stairs that lead out into the sky, do not test them out of curiosity. Walking into an updraft where your clothes are soaked by clouds blowing through your knees means you are standing on a threshold between two recursive layers of the plateau. If your footing slips, you will not simply fall; you will fall through the sky only to land back on the very stones you started from—at terminal velocity.

Pack light, anchor your heels to the adze-notches, keep your head down against the grey, shadowless noon, and never, under any circumstance, step across a gutter without measuring the weight of your foot first.
