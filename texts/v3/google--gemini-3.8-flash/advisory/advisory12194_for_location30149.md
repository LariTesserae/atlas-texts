To prepare an ordinary human for this place, you must first unlearn the fundamental instinct of high-altitude travel. 

At nine thousand meters on Earth, you prepare to freeze and starve for oxygen. Here, if you step out of a transit gate in a down parka with a standard thin-air oxygen rig, **you will be dead of heat stroke and pulmonary edema within twenty minutes.** 

This is not an alpine climb. It is a vertical, boiling swamp hung nine kilometers in the air, kept under hyperbaric pressure by an invisible, humming engine. You are not visiting a mountain range; you are climbing through the exhaust flue of a planetary thermodynamic organ. 

Here is how you must prepare.

---

### 1. The Body: Surviving the Heat and Broth
The greatest and most immediate killer is the air itself. At this temperature and near-100% humidity, **human sweat cannot evaporate.** Your body’s primary cooling mechanism is completely useless. Without intervention, your core temperature will climb continuously until your organs shut down.

*   **Active Cooling Garments (Liquid-Loop Cooling):** You cannot wear breathable fabrics—there is nothing for them to breathe into. You must wear a sealed, skin-tight liquid-cooling undergarment (similar to those worn by astronauts under EVA suits) hooked to a portable thermoelectric heat exchanger. This exchanger must vent heat mechanically, likely using an evaporative canister system or an external radiator with Peltier cooling. 
*   **Respiratory Management:** The air is "thick as oil and smells of wet bark and boiled sugar." While it contains ample oxygen, your lungs are not designed to process hot, vapor-saturated air suspended at high pressure. Inhaling this broth will slowly drown you in condensed fluid. You need an **enclosed, positive-pressure respiratory half-mask** that dehumidifies and chills your air before it enters your trachea. Do not use an open-mouth snorkel or standard alpine nasal cannula. 
*   **Hyperbaric Adaptation:** The air is unnaturally over-pressurized. Treat your transit like a deep-sea dive. You must equalize your ears and sinuses before arrival. If your entry point is sudden, you run the risk of ear barotrauma; bring prophylactic decongestants and pressure-equalizing earplugs rated for hyperbaric descent.

### 2. The Hum: Infrasound and Neurological Armor
The stone vibrates with "the heavy, dull friction of massive thermodynamic fields"—a low-frequency shudder running through the gabbro like a living heartbeat. 

*   **The Problem:** Constant, heavy infrasound (sub-20 Hz) is psychologically and physically catastrophic to humans. Over several hours, it causes deep, irrational panic, vestibular disorientation (motion sickness while standing still), eyeball resonance (causing your vision to smear and vibrate), and eventually cardiac arrhythmia.
*   **Dampening Footwear:** Your boots must have thick, dense composite soles—alternating layers of viscoelastic polymer (such as Sorbothane) and carbon plates. They must disconnect your skeleton from the rock’s acoustic frequency. 
*   **Auditory Attenuation:** Bring custom-molded musician’s earplugs or active noise-canceling inserts that attenuate the low-end rumble while letting you hear the wet slaps, cracking pods, and gliders around you. If you sleep on the rock, you must not lie directly on it; your hammock or cot must be suspended with acoustic-isolation bungees.

---

### 3. Contact with the Stone: Anchoring and Climbing
You are on near-vertical hornfels, gneiss, and gabbro slicked with cooked liverwort and bubbling alkaline moisture. Normal climbing chalk is worthless; it will turn to milk in seconds.

*   **Thermal and Chemical Isolation:** The rock is seething. Direct skin contact with the stone—especially near the greasy black alloy seams—will cause contact burns. Furthermore, the water sweating upward through micro-fractures is highly alkaline; it will leach the natural oils from your skin and cause chemical caustic burns. Wear nitrile-lined, cut-resistant, thermally insulated climbing gloves.
*   **Anchor Integrity:** Do not use traditional pitons or hammering gear near the black seams. The rock is under complex thermal and barometric stress; striking it can trigger violent micro-spalling or superheated steam bursts from pressurized pores. Use wide-camming mechanical protection or passive chocks placed in cold fissures only. Ensure all hardware is non-reactive titanium or anodized aluminum—the mineral-heavy condensation will corrode untreated steel with shocking speed.
*   **The Ferro-Figs:** Do not anchor into the horizontal branches of the *ferro-figs*. Those vast limbs are delicately counterbalanced against a mile-deep void by their water-filled galls. Slicing, puncturing, or excessively weighting a fig bough could rupture a gall, instantly destroying the branch’s center of mass and sending the entire colossal system—and you—plummeting into the white table below.

### 4. Gear, Ballistics, and Encampment
There is no flat ground. There is only vertical stone, dripping foliage, and the bottomless cloud floor.

*   **Ballistic Headwear:** Do not wear a lightweight climbing helmet. You need a structural, hard-shell industrial or tactical helmet with a brim. The canopy above continuously sheds heavy, water-logged seedpods (the text notes their deafening wet *clacks*). Falling from hundreds of feet up, a dense, fist-sized pod hitting you is like being struck by a wet brick. 
*   **Suspended Encampment (Portaledges):** You will sleep hanging over thousands of feet of mist. Your portaledge must feature an impermeable rainfly with steep, parabolic gutters. The rain here is not a gentle mist; during the afternoon cycle, it dumps "hot, drenching sheets of water that smell of salt and old leaves." The underside of your ledge must be heat-shielded to prevent the seething stone wall from baking you through the fabric.
*   **Water Filtration:** You cannot drink the rain or the rock-sweat without intense processing. The water is laden with alkaline salts, sulfur, dissolved minerals, and rich plant-tannins. Bring a small, pump-driven **reverse-osmosis system** and mineralization tablets. Boiling the water is redundant; you must chemically strip it.
*   **Rations:** Food must be vacuum-sealed in heavy metallized mylar. Do not bring dried meats, crackers, or permeable items—the humidity and heat will turn grain to mush and grow mold colonies on exposed organics within twelve hours.

### 5. Biological Awareness
*   **The Gliders:** The flat, parchment-skinned gliders with three sets of eyes drink hot mineral water from the seams. They will view your heat exchanger’s cooling exhaust or your condensation runoffs as a prime watering hole. They are ribless, heavy, and silent. Keep your campsite enclosed with fine, strong mesh netting to prevent them from wedging their leathery bodies into your warm gear or biting through silicone cooling hoses.
*   **Bismuth Gastropods:** Do not handle them roughly. Their shells are reinforced with native bismuth; they are heavy, slow, and passive, but the lichen they graze on is sulfurous. Their slime trails are likely caustic and will ruin your ropes' sheath integrity if left uncleaned.

---

### 6. The Golden Rule: Seeking the Ring
If your cooling systems fail, your suit punctures, or the crushing, wet heat begins to drive you into heat exhaustion, your only survival option is to locate the shoulder of **The Thumb**.

Look for the seamless blue ring—the twelve-pace vitreous circle hanging over the abyss. It is the only true anomaly in the furnace: a natural or artificial bubble where the laws of this biome are suspended, holding an interior of dry, crisp, forgotten winter. If you can make your way inside that ring, among the brittle mud cities of the walnut-carapace beetles, you will find cold air, breathable dryness, and absolute shelter from the drenching salt-rain. 

Until you reach it, remember where you are: not on a peak reaching for heaven, but perched on teeth jutting from a boiling machine, nine thousand meters above a world you will never see. Move deliberately, stay cool, and do not trust the stone.
