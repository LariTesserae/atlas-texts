To visit this place, you must understand a fundamental paradox: you are entering a space that is utterly sterile, perfectly lit, comfortably warm, and exceptionally lethal to an unprepared human body. 

It is not an abyss or a monster’s lair. It is a masterpiece of hydraulic architecture. But because it is five fathoms deep (thirty feet), enclosed by sheer, unblemished masonry, and almost completely filled to the ceiling, arriving here unprepared means materializing into a blinding, deafening liquid trap.

Here is how you must prepare to survive and experience this flooded sun-anvil.

---

### 1. The Immediate Reality: Flotation and Breathing
You will drop into water. Not an inch of dry floor exists. The water reaches almost to the ceiling arches, meaning there is essentially no headspace except directly beneath the square unglazed roof aperture. If you appear even three paces off-center, you will surface into an air pocket only an inch high, trapped against flat stone slabs.

*   **Breathing Gear is Mandatory:** Do not rely on treading water to snatch breaths. The violent splash of the flume fills the air at the surface with dense, aerosolized spray. Inhaling near the roof means inhaling mist and foam. You need a compact SCUBA setup, a high-end rebreather, or at minimum a full-face snorkel mask connected to a pony bottle (a miniature emergency air tank). A full-face diving mask is strongly recommended because it isolates your nose and eyes from the turbulent churn.
*   **Neutral Buoyancy Control:** You need a Buoyancy Compensator Device (BCD). You do not want to sink to the bottom (thirty feet down, where the water pressure is nearly double surface pressure), nor do you want to be pinned helplessly against the ceiling slabs by uncontrolled floatation. You need to be able to hover suspended in mid-depth to navigate.

### 2. Sensory Protection: The Glare and the Roar
The human senses are designed for open air, shaded earth, or murky seas. This chamber is an optical and acoustic pressure cooker.

*   **Welder-Grade or Glacier-Rated Dive Optics:** The chamber is newly cut pale travertine. When midday sun hits the floor through thirty feet of distilled, crystal-clear water, the floor acts as a massive parabolic reflector. The light strikes you from above, from below, and bounces laterally off the white walls. Standard clear dive masks will cause photokeratitis (corneal sunburn) and temporary blindness within minutes. 
    *   *What to bring:* A dive mask fitted with heavily tinted, mirrored, and polarized UV400 lenses—similar to high-altitude mountaineering glacier glasses, but sealed for underwater use. Without this, the glare reflecting off the pick-grooved stone will induce intense spatial vertigo.
*   **Vented, Pressure-Equalizing Ear Protection:** Sound travels over four times faster in water than in air, and water conducts low-frequency vibration directly into human bone. The flume drops several tons of water per minute into a confined, hard-walled box. It is not just loud; it is a physical concussive force that will rattle your ribs and batter your eardrums.
    *   *What to bring:* Vented silicone dive earplugs (such as Doc’s Proplugs) or an acoustic hood. They must allow your ears to equalize pressure while blunting the sheer decibel level. Unprotected ears will suffer disorientation, loss of equilibrium (the waterfall’s churn can induce severe inner-ear vertigo, making you unable to tell which way is up), and permanent hearing damage.

### 3. Thermal Regulation
The water is described as "bath-warm," likely somewhere between 85°F and 95°F (30°C–35°C). 
*   **Do Not Wear a Neoprene Wetsuit:** A standard 5mm or 7mm wetsuit will cause you to rapidly overheat. Trapped in warm mineral water while physically exerting yourself to swim against currents, your core temperature will spike, leading to heat exhaustion, nausea, and unconsciousness.
*   **Wear a Lightweight Dive Skin:** A thin lycra, nylon, or 1mm rash-guard jumpsuit is ideal. It will protect your skin from the sheer, rough-toothed travertine ashlar (which will shred bare knuckles and knees like a cheese grater if the current pushes you into a wall) while allowing your body to dump excess metabolic heat.

### 4. Hydrology and Current Management
The chamber has a violent internal weather system driven entirely by water:
1.  **The Downward Churn:** The waterfall creates a high-velocity downward column of white bubbles that hits the floor and expands outward. If you drift into that frothing pillar, you will be driven instantly to the thirty-foot travertine bottom and spun around in a zero-visibility cloud of aerated water.
2.  **Convection Currents:** Because of the mineral heat and surface cooling, massive thermal sheets are constantly rising and falling, bending light and tugging unpredictably at your limbs.
3.  **The Periphery Siphon:** The water exits through narrow overflow slots chiseled along the top tier. While a human cannot be sucked through them, the current will relentlessly paste you against the upper walls.
*   **Gear:** Wear sturdy, short-blade diving fins. Long free-diving fins will be awkward in a chamber only fourteen paces wide; you need nimble, powerful propulsion to stay clear of the falling column and avoid kicking the pristine masonry.

### 5. Egress: Getting Out of the Box
The most dangerous element of this cistern is that **it was built to hold water, not let things out.** The walls are sheer vertical planes. The ashlar stones are dry-stacked or bedded in flush mortar; their edges are sharp right angles. There are no ladders, no rusted iron rungs, no gravel shores, and the overflow slots are too narrow to provide a handhold. The only opening to the outside world is the square aperture in the ceiling, five fathoms above the floor, hanging directly over the middle of the pool.

*   **Pneumatic Grapple or Ascender System:** If you intend to leave via the open aperture, you must bring a pneumatic line thrower. You will need to surface directly under the blue square of sky, anchor a grapnel or padded hook over the pale coping stones of the terrace above, and use a mechanical ascender to haul yourself out of the water and through the roof.
*   **Mechanical Handholds (Temporary):** Because the pick-marks on the stones are still crisp and parallel, micro-cams (climbing protection used by rock climbers) can be wedged into the hair-thin relief lines between blocks if you need to moor yourself against the wall to rest. Alternatively, heavy-duty glass-handler suction cups will adhere brilliantly to smooth, wet travertine. Bring two to give yourself a resting anchor along the sheer walls.

### 6. The Psychological Experience
Prepare your mind for a unique kind of agoraphobic claustrophobia. 

You will be in a room scarcely forty feet wide, yet the sheer depth and blinding radiance give it the illusion of infinite space. The absolute geometric perfection—six planes of white stone, the cobalt square of sky, the emerald algae beginning to vein the chisel-marks—feels neither natural nor entirely human. It has the eerie, suspended solemnity of a sacred machine caught in the first hours of its operation.

When you hover in the center, suspended in that bright, trembling heat with the roaring cylinder of bubbles drumming against your sternum, you will feel entirely irrelevant. The stone box was not made to torture you, nor was it made to comfort you. It was made solely to contain pure, illuminated water. As long as you bring your own air, dim the sun, mute the roar, and bring a way to climb back into the sky, you will experience one of the most sublime, crystalline temples the multiverse has to offer.
