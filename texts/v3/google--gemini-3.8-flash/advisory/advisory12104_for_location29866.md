To visit this vault, you must prepare for an environment that is not actively hostile in the way of poison gas or boiling magma, but rather **metabolically suffocating**. It is an enclosed thermal engine that has reached a sluggish, eternal equilibrium. It does not care that you are warm-blooded, soft-skinned, and reliant on evaporation to stay alive. 

Here is how an ordinary human traveler should prepare:

---

### 1. The Paradox of Attire: Armor vs. Heat
Preparing your body for this space presents an immediate logistical contradiction: **the rock will flay you, but the air will cook you.**

* **The Walls:** The shale is newly fractured and unweathered. A brush against the wall in the dark will cut through standard cotton or denim like wet paper. You cannot enter in light athletic wear.
* **The Heat:** The ambient temperature radiates down from the ceiling like a hot iron held inches above your hair. Worse, it is humid and entirely still. Your sweat will not evaporate; it will merely pool, slicking your skin and uselessly draining your reserves. Heat exhaustion will begin within twenty minutes in standard protective gear.

**The Solution:**
* **Cut-Resistant Under-Sleeves and Chaps:** Wear tightly woven, breathable Dyneema or Kevlar forearm and shin guards over your skin. These breathe significantly better than heavy leather or canvas work jackets, but will stop jagged shale from slicing your radial artery if you stumble against a wall.
* **Phase-Change Cooling Vest:** Do not rely on evaporative cooling neck wraps—they require moving, unsaturated air, neither of which exists here. Wear a phase-change cooling vest charged with frozen inserts beneath a loose, moisture-wicking tunic. It will buy you roughly ninety minutes of thermal stability before your core temperature begins to climb.
* **Footwear:** Heavy-soled, oil-resistant puncture-proof boots with high ankle support and aggressively siped rubber treads. You will be walking across three distinct surfaces, all treacherous: loose ballast stones that roll underfoot; sweat-slick, greasy cast iron plates; and beds of crushed shale. A Vibram or industrial nonslip sole is non-negotiable.

---

### 2. Respiratory & Atmospheric Safeguards
The air is described as tasting of "boiled iron and mint" and smelling of vulcanized rubber and wet gravel. That mint taste is a warning. In a subterranean environment sealed off from wind, volatile organic compounds released by the pale, fat leaves have nowhere to go. Furthermore, the complete absence of insects—no springtails, no gnats, no blind beetles—suggests the plants are either completely inedible or actively insecticidal.

* **Half-Face Respirator with Organic Vapor/Acid Gas/P100 Filters:** A standard dust mask is useless. You need carbon filtration to strip out volatile plant terpenes, aerosolized heavy machine oils, and the mineral vapor rising off the hot iron. 
* **Multi-Gas Monitor (Clipped to your collar):** You are fifty paces down an unrecorded drainage shaft beneath buried slag. You must continuously monitor for Oxygen, Carbon Monoxide, and Hydrogen Sulfide. The stagnant air could allow dense pockets of carbon dioxide or nitrogen to settle silently into the lower ballast trenches and settling tanks. 
* **The "Mint" Alert:** If your mask seal breaks and the air tastes sharply, cleanly sweet or cold on your tongue, **retreat**. Many heavy-metal vapors and neurotoxic biological compounds register on the human palate as an uncanny, artificial wintergreen or mint.

---

### 3. Illumination: Navigating the Red Glow
The only ambient light is the dull, bruised cherry-red from three dying resistor coils. Your natural night vision will adjust to this, but red light destroys depth perception and turns the liver-colored foliage and black puddles into indistinguishable voids.

* **Diffused, High-CRI Green or Amber Lights:** Do not bring an intensely bright, focused white spotlight. The moisture-beaded leaves and oily iron will create blinding retro-reflections, while casting pitch-black, razor-edged shadows behind every spur of rock. Use a wide, diffused headlamp with high Color Rendering Index (CRI) at moderate lumens, or an amber lantern. You need to see the subtle boundary where an iron floorplate ends and a drop into silt begins.
* **Do Not Touch the Coils:** Remember that the resistor coils are humming without vibration. They are carrying enormous, ungrounded industrial amperage. Keep your light off their mounting brackets to avoid blinding yourself, and keep all gear clear of that eastern wall.

---

### 4. Direct Interaction: The Flora and the Iron
The lushness here is mechanical and parasitic. The plants live on heat and mineral-heavy condensate; their sap anchors them to iron gears with gray cement.

* **Nitrile-Coated Mechanics’ Gloves:** Never touch the rubbery leaves or fiddleheads bare-handed. If the sap can pit and anchor into industrial steel, it may cause severe chemical dermatitis or chemical burns on human skin. 
* **Thermal Insulation for the Hands:** The pump and iron machinery are "hot to the touch." If you slip and instinctively reach out to steady yourself against the stationary steam pump or its eight-foot flywheel, uninsulated hands will blister immediately. Your gloves must offer heat resistance as well as cut resistance.
* **Stay in the Lanes:** The prompt notes "wide lanes of emptiness... vast flags of sweat-slick iron and bare gravel corridors." **Walk only in these empty spaces.** Do not trample the clusters of pale leaves. In a muffled vault, tearing those thick, rubbery leaves might release bursts of concentrated sap or vapor, and snapping a mechanical vine looped through a drive sprocket could dislodge counterweights or rusted mechanisms poised on hair-trigger balances.

---

### 5. Hydration and First Aid
* **Electrolytes Over Pure Water:** Because you will be pouring sweat in this womb-like air, drinking plain water will rapidly deplete your blood sodium, leading to severe cramping, confusion, and nausea. Carry chilled, hyper-saline electrolyte solutions in a thermal flask.
* **Burn and Hemostatic Kits:** Your first-aid kit must prioritize two specific injuries: deep, jagged lacerations from the blasted shale (carry Celox or QuikClot hemostatic gauze and wide pressure dressings), and contact burns from the iron machinery (carry hydrogel burn dressings).

---

### 6. Mental and Sensory Preparation: The "Tock"
Prepare yourself for the psychological texture of this place. It is not frightening in a traditional, monstrous way; it is **uncomfortably intimate and indifferent.**

* **The Silence:** Aside from the drop of water hitting the hopper every few minutes, the foliage completely absorbs sound. There is no wind, no hum of insects, no vibration from the dead pump. You will hear your own blood hissing in your ears, your breath roaring in your mask, and the heavy thudding of your heart as the thermal load rises. 
* **The Cadence:** That single, ringing *tock* will startle you the first three times, and then your brain will begin to wait for it, counting the seconds in the dark. Do not let it hypnotize you into slowing down. 
* **Temporal Disorientation:** In this stagnant mechanical fever, where vines take months to crawl an inch and nothing rots, human time feels irrelevant. Set an audible, vibrating timer on your wrist for **45 minutes maximum**. When the alarm triggers, turn around and ascend the shaft, regardless of how much more of the vault remains to be seen. If you linger until you feel the symptoms of heat exhaustion, you will lack the strength to climb fifty paces of vertical drainage shaft back to the surface.
