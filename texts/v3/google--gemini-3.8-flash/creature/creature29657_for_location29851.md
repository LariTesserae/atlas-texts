The people who live along the clamps do not talk about the drop. To name the space beneath the floorplates is considered a breach of manners, like asking an amputee about the coldness of their missing shoe. 

Instead, they talk about *bite*. They talk about the tooth of the cast-iron anchors, the temper of the steel drift-pins, and whether the gray basalt beneath the third boiler-tier is “sweating dry” or “sweating sweet.” They are people who have traded the horizontal world for a life lived entirely on the bias, their shins perpetually bruised from climbing thirty-degree ladders, their left boots worn three times faster than their right because of the way the gantries lean against the sheer thrust of the spires.

They are called the Creep-men, or simply the Fitters, though neither name covers what they have become over three or four generations in the draft. 

They are broad in the shoulder and noticeably short in the thigh—a squat, wedge-shaped breed whose centers of gravity sit low in the pelvis, closer to the iron than an outsider’s. Their skin has the particular dead-paper pallor of turnip roots, bleached by the perpetual dawn and silvered by the sulfurous oils they rub into their hands to keep the virgin flint from flaying their skin. A Creep-man’s palms are not calloused in the usual way; they are cross-hatched with hundreds of tiny, glass-clear scars from contact with the freshly split stone, which cuts as cleanly as obsidian and leaves wounds that don't bleed until twenty minutes after they are made, held closed by the high, dry tension of the altitude.

They wear jackets of heavy harbor-canvas soaked in linseed and tallow, quilted with horsehair across the spine to cushion them when the mountain kicks. Because the mountain *does* kick. When a new vertical vein of slate parts from the core four miles down, the shock does not travel as a rolling earthquake; it arrives as a single, dry hammer-blow that rings the ironwork like a tuning fork. A man unanchored will simply pop three feet into the air and come down in the wind. Therefore, their trousers are sewn with lead shot into the cuffs—twelve pounds per leg—and their boots are armed with spring-loaded heel-cleats that engage the open mesh of the walkways with a dull, rhythmic *clack-drub, clack-drub*.

Because sound does not roll or blur here, their language has sheared itself flat. They speak in tight, vowel-clipped monosyllables, rarely raising their voices above a clicking whisper. To shout against the glassy face of an unweathered chasm is to invite a slap of identical noise back into one’s own ear so sharp it can burst the drum. When they need to communicate across the void between the blast-house and the hoist towers, they do not yell; they use “tap-lines”—long, tensioned brass wires that run along the underside of the catwalks. A man strikes the wire with a cold-chisel, and three miles away, a boy holding an empty lard-tin against the cable reads the dits and thumps through the tin’s bottom with his teeth.

These boys—the Cable-runners—are the only ones who move quickly. They are small, fourteen-year-old scarecrows whose ligaments have been stretched by the lateral tides until they look like mantises. They grease the sheaves. They run the thin, greasy lanyards that pull the valve-whistles. They have an uncanny sense for the *dough-drifts*—those pockets where the smoke has gathered into heavy, buoyant banks that sit in mid-air. A runner who slips from a gantry does not always fall straight; if he is lucky or clever enough to steer his leap, he can plunge into the belly of an oil-smoke raft, sinking into its lukewarm, carbon-dense lard up to his armpits, thrashing his way across its curdled surface like a swimmer in pea-soup until he can catch the trailing ivy of a suspension link.

The women of the high rifts live mostly in the "hollow-ribs"—the vast, vaulted chambers created where the iron trusses arch away from the rock faces, leaving triangular shelters that are roofed with corrugated sheet and caulked with boiled moss. Here, life smells of scorched lard, wet wool, and vinegar. 

The women are the keepers of the gauges. While the men are out on the faces with the sledges and the grease-squirts, the women sit in the warm, deafening gloom of the gauge-rooms, watching the mercury columns that register the earth-thrust. The mercury does not rise smoothly; it jumps in frantic, millimeter-high sputters. A woman can tell by the rattle of a brass float whether the Horn of St. Jude is leaning two inches north or whether the deep magma has taken another gulp of steam three leagues down. They are also the weavers of the "skin." Since there is no soil for garden-plots, they cultivate the waxy blue ivy that likes chimney soot, training it across wire netting suspended over the warm exhaust flues. The ivy produces a bitter, starch-heavy tuber that tastes of zinc and turnip, which the women grate into paste and fry on the inspection covers of the steam-traps. It is gray, greasy food, but it keeps the rickets out of the children's shins.

Alongside the humans, the iron has bred its own camp-followers.

The dog-sized lizards that crawl upside down along the underside of the rail-beds are called *Harrow-hounds* by the track-walkers, though they have nothing of the mammal about them. They are flat, ribless things, the color of cold tallow, with skins that feel like boiled pigskin. They have no nostrils; they breathe through six slit-like vents situated along their flanks, which open and close with a wet *frip-frip* sound as they move. Their pads are not simple suckers, but complex arrays of micro-hooks that can find grip even on the vitreous, oil-slick surface of newly cleaved slate. 

The hounds are partially deaf, caring nothing for the steam-hammers, but they are obsessively drawn to heat and animal fat. They follow the grease-gangs, licking the axles of the ore-trucks clean with tongues as wide and rough as shoe-soles. The men tolerate them—even keep them as sour, sluggish pets—because they eat the *flint-ticks*. The ticks are the true misery of the spires: blind, crab-shaped parasites that do not feed on blood, but on the sulfur-slick that coats the rock. The ticks crawl into the bedding of the hollow-ribs, nest in the lagging of the pipes, and will chew through a man’s hemp harness for the grease in the fibers, sending an uninspected cradle down into the void with a rotten snap.

Higher up, where the chimneys vent their raw, unscrubbed gases into the damp air, live the *Weavers*. These are not birds, though the swifts nest near them. They are arachnids the size of wash-tubs, with bodies like flattened iron plates and legs as thin and springy as clock-springs. They do not spin silk; they spin *slag*. By drinking the molten silica and heavy iron-scum that crusts the cooling vents of the blast furnaces, they extrude long, gleaming filaments of metallic glass that harden instantly in the cold downdrafts. Between the stays of the highest suspension bridges, their webs look like geometric ironwork that has rusted before it was hung—coarse, tangled nets of brittle brown wire that catch the swifts when the birds are blinded by the sulfur-fogs. The Creep-men leave the Weavers alone; their glass-webs, though dangerous to an unwary crane-driver, serve to lace together the looser teeth of the rock, pinning small flakes of slate that would otherwise drop like guillotines onto the lower pumping stations.

And then there are the *Floaters*—the mountain rays that drift through the rifts like gray, leather-winged churches. 

Up close, they are not silent. As they sweep between the horns of the rock, their vast, gas-filled cavities—bladders the size of canal barges, ribbed with cartilage as thick as spruce boles—creak like the timbers of a ship running before a gale. They smell of old marsh-damp and dried bladder-wrack. Their skin is encrusted with a thick, brittle carapace of dried fern-pollen, soot, and the dead shells of thousands of grease-beetles that live in their wrinkles as commensals.

The rays have no interest in the people, but the city has an immense, predatory interest in the rays. Once every three months, when the lateral gravity shifts slacken and the wind drops into the "dull quarter," the whaling-tenders venture out. These are not boats, but iron cages hung from overhead cable-transporters that run on six-inch steel ropes between the spires. The men in the cages carry forty-foot pikes tipped with sulfur-matches. They do not kill the beasts—to drop a thousand tons of rotting meat into the bottomless rifts would be to lose it entirely, and the gas-organs would burst on the way down, ruining the tallow.

Instead, they *dock* them.

They slip behind a browsing ray while it is nosing into the telegraph poles for moss, and they sink hollow bronze taps into its dorsal reservoirs, siphoning off the marsh-gas into copper cylinders while the animal merely blinks its huge, milk-white eyes and shifts its pectorals against the iron stays. The gas is sweet, cold, and burns with a fierce, colorless flame that does not smoke the glass of the miners' lamps. In return, the Cable-runners clean the creature’s gill-slits with scrapers, clearing away the soot-clots and the dead ferns. It is a slow, grotesque barter between the machinery of the air and the machinery of the pit.

The oldest living thing in the city, however, is not the rays, nor the oldest Creep-man who remembers the first truss being driven into the St. Jude Horn. It is the *Sediment*.

In the lowest tiers, where the iron girders simply run out of steel and end in jagged, torch-cut stubs against the rock, there are places where the rock has begun to reply to the city. The virgin slate, irritated by millions of iron screws and the hot urine of five thousand men leaking down its faces, has begun to produce an exudate. It is neither stone nor moss, but a cold, translucent jelly that wells up through the micro-fractures. It grows slowly, forming heavy, drooping curtains that feel like salt pork.

The people who work down there—the Deep-watchers—spend their entire twelve-hour shifts with flat shovels, paring this stuff off the tie-plates. They do not like to touch it with bare skin; it leaves a cold, chemical numbness behind that can last for months, as though the nerves had simply forgotten how to carry an impulse. But they save the shavings.

They save them because when the jelly dries, it hardens into something that is neither iron nor stone: an amber-colored, perfectly clear slag that does not shatter under the hammer and does not rust in the sulfur-drips. From this, the watch-makers of the high cathedral turn the bearings for their chronometers, and the crane-captains cut the lenses for their arc-lamps.

If you sit with a Deep-watcher in the grease-kitchens after his shift, when he has taken off his lead-weighted boots and is drinking hot water flavored with burnt ivy-crust, he will tell you that the rock is not blind. He will tell you that the mountain is growing toward the iron just as fast as the iron is biting into the mountain.

"You can hear it in the pins," he will say, holding up a finger that has lost its nail to the cold flint. "You think you're screwing the bridge to the Horn. But you listen to the third thread on the turnbuckle when the shift turns. The Horn’s sucking on the bolt. It’s tasting the pig-iron. It wants to know how hard we are before it finishes coming out of the hole."
