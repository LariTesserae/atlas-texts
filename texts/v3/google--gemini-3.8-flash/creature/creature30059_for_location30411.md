Underneath the silver-gray pelt of the cushion plants, the world does not freeze. 

The cushions are three feet of knitted, living felt, built over centuries by stems that grow no more than a pin’s width a summer. Beneath the top inch of sun-scalded, downy foliage, the mass becomes dense, damp, and dark as peat, warmed from within by its own slow decay and the metabolic heat of millions of microscopic lives. If you thrust a hand into it, past the frost-trapping hairs, it would feel like sinking a wrist into a warm, greasy bread dough. 

Here, in the under-mat, the silk-runners do not merely shelter; they carve intricate labyrinths. They are communal creatures, sleeping piled four or five deep in spherical nests lined with the moulted black feathers of alpine swifts and their own shed wool. They are nearly blind in the conventional sense—their crystalline eye-shields blur detail into washes of glare and shadow—but their whiskers are long, wire-stiff, and sensitive to the microscopic air displacements caused by falling shale or the drop of a viper’s jaw. They feed on the tiny, resinous seed-heads of the shrub, grinding them with flat, yellow molars that never stop growing, their breath smelling faintly of camphor and sour tallow. When they groom each other, their teeth make a dry, rhythmic clicking that vibrates through the stems, a hum that travels through the turf like electricity through water.

Below them, deeper still, where the cushion touches the fractured schist, live blind, bloodless things: pale bristletails that scavenge the runners’ leavings, and tiny, legless fly-larvae that do not freeze until the temperature drops past forty below, their bodies saturated with natural glycerols that make them taste of sweet alcohol.

The slate vipers understand the rhythm of this insulation. They are short—no longer than a man’s forearm—and their bodies are flat, almost leaf-shaped, covered in scales with the matte texture of split blackboard slate. They spend nineteen hours of the day in the cracks between the bedrock and the peat, stiff as sticks, their hearts beating once every three minutes, the fluids in their tissues thickened to jelly. 

When the noon sun strikes the rock, turning the dark schist into a radiator, the vipers do not crawl so much as slide into the light, their joints creaking with a microscopic stiffness that smooths out as the heat penetrates their ribs. Their blood thins, quickens, and suddenly they are frantic. They have a window of perhaps two hours—while the rock weeps and the shadows have not yet begun to stretch—to kill. Their venom is not a digestive acid like lowland snakes, but a neurotoxin so concentrated it turns a silk-runner’s muscles to salt in the space of a single heartbeat, preventing the prey from kicking its way back into the labyrinth where the snake could not follow. A viper will eat until it is distended, then wedge itself into a sunny fissure, head pointed down, using the remaining minutes of light to jump-start the digestion of fur and bone before the freeze catches it mid-swallow and locks it in place until tomorrow’s glare.

Then there is the hunter.

The hunter does not live on the notch; no one could sleep here and keep their mind. They come up from the lower cirques, from the scree huts three thousand feet below where dwarf juniper still clings to life. The climb takes half the night, navigated by the feel of the rock and the breath of the wind against the ears.

This person is small-framed, wide-chested as a cask, with nostrils narrow as slits to trap the meager warmth of their own lungs before the dry air can scour their throat raw. Their cheekbones and forehead are smeared with a paste of sheep grease and crushed charcoal to keep the UV furnace from peeling the skin off their skull, and across their eyes they wear a band of split goat-horn with two hairline gashes cut into the dark horn with a hot needle. Through these slits, the blinding basin resolves into sharp, high-contrast planes of gray, black, and dazzling white.

They sit behind the slate wall so still that the alpine moths occasionally mistake the grease on their leather coat for damp stone and settle there, fanning their broad, soot-black wings to catch the heat of the hunter’s body. The hunter does not brush them away; every movement burns calories that cannot easily be replaced at an elevation where water boils at a temperature too low to cook grain.

The hunter has not come for the silk-runners, whose oily meat tastes of turpentine, nor for the vipers. 

They are waiting for the *tal-khen*, the cloud-skimmers.

Down below, the sea of white mist that fills the lower valleys is not a quiet blanket; it is an ocean boiling with convective heat. It presses against the mountain knees, and within its dense, warm humidity live vast flocks of high-altitude storm-petrels—birds that never set foot on dry ground, whose lives are spent skimming the interface between the cloud-tops and the cold air above. They feed on the ballooning spiders and wind-drifted insects carried up from the forests miles below. 

At midday, when the sun cuts through the upper edges of the clouds, great columns of rising warm air—thermals as wide as cathedral domes—tear free from the fog and rocket upward against the face of the schist towers. The skimmers ride these columns. They rise through the white floor like arrows, thousands of feet in a handful of seconds, blind with the speed of their ascent, breaking into the thin, sun-struck void of the notch to shed the moisture from their oily plumage before diving back into the cloud deck.

When they burst over the lip, their wings—stiff, narrow, and seven feet from tip to tip—make a sound like canvas ripping in a gale. They are dazed by the transition from the dim, steaming fog to the raw, blue furnace of the upper sky.

That is when the hunter moves. 

They do not use a bow; wood loses its whip in this dryness, snapping like bone, and an arrow would be caught by the five-hundred-foot downdrafts that tumble off the needle-peaks. Instead, the hunter uses the plaited sinew. It is weighted at both ends with grooved pebbles of green quartz—dense, heavy, and polished until they offer no resistance to the thin air. 

The hunter rises from behind the slate, a dark, low shape against the violent drop. They do not swing the bola wide; they cast it with a quick, flicking heave of the forearm, straight into the teeth of the updraft where the bird hovers, motionless for an instant, blinded by the glare.

The quartz stones wrap around the wing-joints with a muffled *thunk*. The bird does not scream—these creatures have no voices, only a dry clattering of bills—and it falls not into the abyss, but onto the springy, resilient turf of the cushions, where the hunter is upon it with the bone pin before the wings can beat the air to foam.

The bird provides everything: dark, iron-tasting meat that can be dried into brittle flakes; flight-bones that are hollow, light, and hard as pipe-stems, perfect for needles and whistles; and the crop, which is always filled with the oily, undigested seeds of lowland trees that do not grow within thirty miles of this dead stone.

By two in the afternoon, the hunter is gone. 

They do not linger to see the shadow of the western tower cross the basin floor. To be caught here when the sun dips is death; the air temperature drops sixty degrees in the time it takes to walk fifty paces. The moisture on the cushions stops trickling, turns to needles, then to an iron glaze. The vipers go gray and petrified in their holes. The runners vanish three feet down into the smelling warmth of their tunnels, shivering together against the dark.

And high above, following the edge of the shadow as it climbs the eastern wall, the great, yellow-eyed scavenger circles once. It does not flap. Its feathers are frayed by the grit carried in the gale, but its vision is vast; it sees the drops of blood the hunter left behind on the white quartz, watches them freeze into small, dark rubies, and waits for the wind to scour them loose so it can swallow them whole.
