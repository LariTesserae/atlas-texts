The life here does not struggle against the machinery; it leans into it, as if the steel casings and subterranean pistons were merely the hard skeleton of an ecosystem whose soft tissues are living things. Because the valley’s light never changes—held in that unsparing, high-altitude pearlescence—and because the air never cools, there are no seasons and no nights. Time is kept entirely by the five-minute exhalations of the rock chimneys and the deep, wet *thung-shhh* of the brine pistons. The nervous systems of everything that lives here are set to those strokes.

Along the scalding underbellies of the iron conduits, where the metal sweats yellow-brown mineral paste and lubricating wax, live the *tallow-scrapers*. They are not insects, though they wear flat, segmental carapaces like tortoiseshell beetles the size of dinner plates. Their shells are dull hematite-red, rough as sandpaper from the lime they incorporate into their chitin. Beneath these shields, their bodies are nearly liquid—soft, greasy masses of cream-colored tissue that flow into the micro-fissures around the pipe gaskets. They possess no eyes, only long, paired vibration-horns that press flat against the vibrating iron. When a valve turns somewhere miles away and the conduits give their faint, singing tremor, thousands of these plates shudder in unison, their scraping mouthparts lifting off the metal for three heartbeats, then settling back to feed on the tallow before the condensation cools.

Where the conduits cross the scalded scree, they create sheltered crawlspaces—tunnels of dry, radiant heat between the pipe-hulls and the soaking moss. Here sleep the valley’s cursorial hunters: long, low-slung quadrupeds with the thick, hairless hides of river dolphins, marbled in shades of slate and lard. They have no pinnae; their ears are internal, pressed directly to the rock to read the subterranean cycle. Their feet are broad, padded with tough, fibrous calluses that can traverse the boiling gravel beds without blistering. They hunt by scent and thermal contour, tracking the blind, pale amphibians that crawl from the sump-holes during the brine-piston’s backstroke. 

These amphibians—the valley’s true biomass base—are bloated, heavy things with skin like peeled turnips. They live their larval stages deep inside the flooded natural basalt bores, carried up and down on the reciprocating columns of dark brine. When the pressure peaks at the top of a stroke, they are occasionally vomited out into the terrace pools in slick, writhing tangles. They do not have bones; their support comes from dense, hydraulic bladders filled with the same saline water they swim in. If carried out of the wet trough into the dry, cold air of the spires above, they would rupture; here, held under the cauldrons’ thick, compressed atmosphere, they can heave themselves across the peat on stubby, spade-shaped fins, feeding on the fibrous rust-moss until the rail-birds or the slate-skinned runners pick them apart.

The rails themselves are taller than they appear when hunched. When they stretch, they stand nearly five feet high on legs as thin and dry as rusted rebar. Their plumage is not composed of true vanes, but of hair-like, interlocking filaments heavily saturated with an oily musk that sheds the boiling droplets constantly raining from the canopy. They hunt with an eerie, mechanical patience. One will stand motionless beside a weeping flange for forty minutes, its gray head tilted toward the wet metal, listening to the cavitation inside. When a slug of air passes through the pipe—a sudden *gunk-clack* that makes the surrounding ferns jump—the bird’s chisel-like beak strikes downward into the joint, spearing the heavy blind leeches that gather where the pressure seals leak organic grease.

Higher up, in the mid-canopy of the ironwoods where the mist is thickest, life becomes suspended, hung like ballast among the bromeliad-ropes. 

There are creatures that never touch the ground or the metal. They are heavy, slow-metabolizing climbers with four grasping, opposable limbs and prehensile tails as thick as ship’s hawsers. Their fur is not hair, but a dense, mossy felt that hosts its own colonies of sulfur-fixing algae, turning their backs the exact color of tarnished bronze. They move only during the exhaust pulse. When the high chimneys release their five-minute column of white vapor, a low, rushing sigh fills the trough, and the air drops two degrees as the cloud bank shifts. During these ninety seconds of motion and sound, the bronze climbers travel: reaching methodically from limb to limb, stripping the waxy bracts from the bromeliads with flat, blackened teeth, or scooping out the hot rainwater from the plant hearts to drink the iron-rich sediment settled at the base. When the exhaust valve snaps shut and the hiss dies back into the general damp silence, they freeze mid-stride, hanging like pendulums of dead moss, indistinguishable from the burls on the ironwood bark.

And there are the wanderers—few in number, rarely seen except as gray silhouettes against the steam of the lower cascades. 

They are bipedal, broad-chested, and neckless, their shoulders sloping directly into domed skulls with heavy, shelf-like brows that protect milky, unblinking eyes from the lime-drip. Their skin is thick, wrinkled like elephant hide, and completely hairless, permanently slicked with the alkaline condensate that coats everything in the valley. They wear no garments, but their bodies are stained in bands of red ochre and black grease from the pipes they lean against for warmth. 

They are not masters of the machinery, nor are they its prisoners; they are simply its scavengers of warmth and predictable shelter. They have long, spatulate fingers with flat, horn-hard nails, which they use to peel the hardened cakes of lubricating wax from the dead joints of cold pipes to chew for fat. They do not speak. The damp air is too dense, the drone of the valley too constant for vocal cords to carry meaning. Instead, they signal each other through the iron itself. One will find a dry, resonant run of casing and strike it three times with a river cobble—*tink, tink, tink*—and three miles down-canyon, another, crouching in the spray of an overflow lip, will feel the ring through the soles of its feet, pause its feeding, and strike back once to confirm its place in the trough. 

They sleep wedged between the stone faces and the high-pressure runs, where the heat is steady enough to prevent the lung-rot that takes them if they linger too long in the lower swamps. Their children are born with thick, fatty pads over their sternums and the bases of their skulls, adaptations against the bruising vibrations of the valley floor. When one of them dies, the others do not bury it in the peat, where the water is too acidic to allow decay; they carry the body to the base of the reciprocating brine bores. They wait for the downward stroke—the great *shhh* that draws the column of water down into the bowels of the mountain—and let the current take it down into the dark, into the unmonitored heart of whatever engine keeps their sky warm.
