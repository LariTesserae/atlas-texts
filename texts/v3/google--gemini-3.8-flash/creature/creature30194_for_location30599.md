To survive two miles above a floor you cannot see, in air that drinks the water from your eyeballs before you can blink it back, you must first lose the presumption of the horizontal. 

The people who inhabit the cleft—the *strut-born*, though they rarely call themselves anything at all, having little use for names that require vowels to carry across dead air—do not walk. They hitch. Their gait is a three-point cadence: one hand hooked into a static wire, one foot finding the rivet-head, the other toe testing the lip of a cold-rolled angle iron. Their calves are disproportionately thick, knotted like burled walnut from a lifetime of standing on half-inch edges, while their chests are broad and deep, stretched outward by lungs that have spent three generations pulling sustenance from an ether thin as gauze.

They are strangely pale beneath their coats of powdered graphite, their skin cured by the drought into something resembling drum-parchment. Wrinkles do not gather in folds here; they split in hairline fractures along the knuckles and the corners of the mouth, which the inhabitants seal shut with cakes of rendered mutton tallow imported from the lowlands or the waxy grease scraped from the axle-bearings of the main hoists. To bleed here is dangerous, not from infection—the air is far too dry and cold for common rot—but because the wound will not close under a crust; the atmospheric thirst licks the plasma out until the cut is dry and white as salt.

Consequently, they are quiet. They speak in a low, sibilant dialect of glottal stops and teeth-clicks, pitched to register inside the ear of someone leaning within two inches of their hood. To raise one’s voice is considered a grotesque breach of economy, a waste of wet lung-air that marks a novice or an idiot. When they must communicate across the chasm, they use the iron itself. 

A crew boss on the four-hundredth cross-tier does not shout down to the winch-man; he carries a short, blunt hammer of hardened bell-metal. Two sharp taps on the south turnbuckle ring down the tensioned steel rods like a telegraph, vibrating through the soles of every boot on that line. A rigger three hundred yards away, resting his ribs against an anchor plate, feels the chime in his breastbone and shifts a lever. The city is a vast, struck instrument, constantly muttering through the soles of its people’s feet.

Domestic life is an exercise in suspension. A home is not a room, but a *harness-box*—a cubicle of corrugated iron and stretched duck-cloth, perhaps five feet wide, swung by bridle-cables beneath the shelf of a cross-strut to keep it clear of the service cages. 

Inside, there are no chairs; furniture is a hazard in a space that sways three inches to the left every time an ore-skip brakes below. Hammocks of woven cable-core hang in tiers from the ceiling ribs. When a worker sleeps, they do not kick off their harness; they simply transfer their lanyard from the guide-wire to an interior ring-bolt, cinch the belly-strap, and let their limbs go slack in the cold. Children are tethered from the day they learn to crawl. A toddler wears a harness of soft deer-gut with an eighteen-inch drop-line pinned to a runner along the central strut; they learn gravity before they learn fire, growing up with the casual, terrible habit of leaning out into nothingness to watch the shadow of a hoist pass below, their small heels hooked over empty space.

Water is the currency, the god, and the constant labor. Since the sky yields neither drop nor crystal, the city drinks from the furnaces. 

Deep in the lower third of the slot, where the diorite walls squeeze closer and the air begins to warm from the subterranean mantle, the smelting pods roast raw zinc-lead sulfides drawn from cross-cut drifts. The moisture locked within the deep stone—the fossil perspiration of the rock itself—is driven off as high-pressure steam. It is trapped in labyrinthine condenser pipes that run along the coldest northern face of the cleft, trickling down into sealed lead bladders. The water tastes of zinc, brimstone, and old rust, but it is wet. A man’s daily wage is paid half in scrip for the dried-grain tubs and half in a wax-plugged tube of condenser-liquor, which he keeps nestled against his armpit beneath his sheepskin coat to prevent it from going to slush.

Around these people, the smaller lives have found their niches in the ironwork. 

The high-line rats are not pests to be eradicated; they are the cleaners and the winter-meat. The boys hunt them with spring-loaded wire loops set along the flange of the main channel beams, skinning them with bone scrapers. The meat is stringy, tasted of machine oil and tallow, but when salted and pressed into dry cakes between heated iron blocks, it sustains a crew through a twelve-hour shift on the brake-levers. The pelts, stripped of their grease, are sewn into linings for the climbers' mittens, though the leather is so dry it wears through in a week, turning to grey fluff that drifts down into the works like dandelion seed.

Even the cliff-swifts have been drawn into the social machine. The birds nest in the bored holes left by pneumatic rock-drills, packing the holes with the gray tinder-mold and midges glued with their own thick, rubbery saliva. The younger girls—whose small hands can reach into a two-inch drill hole—scale the vertical rock faces with iron crampons lashed to their toes to harvest this dried swift-glue. When boiled with a little vinegar, it forms the only sealant that does not crack under the cleft's vibration, used to caulk the tin huts against the high-altitude drafts that slice through the struts like cold knives.

There is an old, strange sect among them—men who have spent forty years on the "out-hangs," the furthest scaffolding that projects toward the narrow mouth where the gap sees the stars. They are stone-deaf from decades of high-velocity gale-whistle, their eyes permanently filmed by a bluish cataract caused by the unshielded glare of the noon-blade. These men do not come down to the furnace levels, even to warm their hands. They claim the air below is "fat" and suffocating, choked with human moisture and coke-damp. They live on parched groats and the grease of the upper pulleys, their bodies so desiccated that when one dies, his partners do not need to bury him or let him drop. They simply lash him to the exterior lattice of a wind-break beam, facing up toward the two-mile slit of black-violet sky.

Within three days, the high, dry wind has mummified him into an ash-colored effigy, light as a bundle of reeds. He stays there, bound with three turns of bailing wire, a dry lookout whose leather fingers vibrate in the wind, tapping out a rhythm on the iron that only those with their boots on the girder can hear.
