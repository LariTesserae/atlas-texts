They do not begin with eyes, because there is no light that warrants the cost of maintaining an optic nerve, and they do not begin with gills, because the brine is too viscous to drag across fine-meshed tissue without tearing it to ribbons.

Instead, whatever lives in the quarter begins as skin.

Between the underside of the tramway trestles and the dead brine beneath it floats the amber ceiling of mineral oil—a stagnant sky five inches deep. The creature that grazes there has no belly; it is all underside, a paper-thin rawhide blanket four feet wide that adheres to the petroleum by capillary draw. It has the color of wet grease-paper, translucent enough that the pattern of the iron rivets can be read through its liver. It has no mouth in the traditional sense; its dorsal skin is permeable to paraffin, which it absorbs directly into its pale, fatty mesenchyme, while its ventral surface—the side facing the drowning trench—excretes a lime-heavy mucus that curdles against the cold salt. 

It moves so slowly that a watchmaker’s glass could not detect the crawl. Over three moons, it creeps from one bay of the trestle to the next, leaving the rivet heads stripped of their yellow tallow, clean and blue-black as the day the boiler-gang hammered them home. When it reaches the corner of an iron plate where the oil gives way to bare stone, it does not turn back. It simply folds in half, its edges sticking together with the consistency of warm suet, and drops through the water column.

The fall takes three days. It does not tumble; the brine is too thick to allow a flutter. It sinks vertically, like an open sheet of wet canvas through chilled honey, until it catches upon one of the overhead catwalks. There, the impact—a force of mere fractions of an ounce—triggers an involuntary contraction. It wraps itself around the handrail, fuses its margins, and becomes a sleeve of living felt, waiting thirty or forty years for the sulfur-weeds to grow over its seams and digest it back into the masonry.

***

Deeper down, where the tenements have formed their masonry alliance with the boiler slag, the living things are heavier. They must be heavy; anything with an ounce of buoyancy would be pulled into the higher strata and shredded against the sharp edges of the green transformer boxes by the pressure gradients.

In the flooded rooms of the middle tier—where family tables still sit anchored into the floor-plates by their own cast-iron pedestals—live the things that inherited the clothing. 

When the people retreated from the trench, they left coats behind. Heavy woolens, broadcloth jackets stiff with seventy winters of river fog and coal soot, horsehair-padded waistcoats left hanging from iron hooks behind the scullery doors. These garments did not rot. Impregnated with the iron salts and the cold grease that leached from the engine houses, they became skins before there were bodies to fill them. 

Over decades, a species of colonial detritivore—cousin to the sea-squirt, but dense as cured pork—settled into the hollows of the sleeves. They are blind, gray, and modular, made of segments like thumb-joints linked by tough fibrous cords. They do not resemble men, but they have taken the shape of the space the men vacated. A coat hanging from a door in the third tenement block does not swing; it is full. The sleeves are packed tight with a segmented, ringed muscle that ends in three stubby, horn-tipped claspers where the wrist-cuff frays. 

These things do not leave their rooms. They are sedentary, anchored by the coat-pegs to the black magnetite of the walls. Their metabolism is so cold, so nearly inert, that they require only the faint drift of galvanic sparks that wander in through the transom windows. When a star-shaped spark drifts through the door frame, drawn by the slightly negative charge of the wool-encased flesh, the sleeve-ends twitch. A single, blunt clasper opens, accepts the micro-charge with a sound like a wet knuckle cracking, and closes again. It will not move for another week. 

If a diver were to lay a hand upon the chest of one of these coats, he would feel no heart; he would feel a slow, hydraulic vibration, three times an hour, as the bellows forty feet below force a thimbleful of cold brine through the creature’s central siphon, venting it out the collar in a thin, greasy plume that smells of tallow and old wet slate.

***

In the street-canyons, life must reckon with the turning. 

Every four or five minutes, when the great walking beam deep in the machine-hall takes its quarter-revolution, the entire three hundred paces of the quarter shudder. It is not an earthquake; it is a displacement. Millions of gallons of super-saline brine are shoved sideways by three inches, and with that shove comes the *shuck*—the compression wave that drives into the marrow.

The things that live in the open alleys have built their skeletons out of this rhythm. 

They are long, segment-plated creatures, eight or ten feet from snout to tail, but no thicker than a man’s thigh. Their armor is not bone, nor is it shell; it is an accretion of zinc scale and cold-pressed lead, gathered grain by grain from the sacrificial bars that protect the boiler foundations. They have the gray, greasy luster of unpolished pewter. 

They do not swim. Swimming is impossible in water that resists displacement like cold treacle. They walk upon thirty pairs of needle-pointed stilts—modified ribs that have pierced through the flank-skin, tipped with black slag-glass. 

They time their steps to the beam. 

For four minutes and fifty seconds, the creature lies motionless across the alley cobbles, its body bridging the space between a transformer housing and a tenement sill. It looks like a fallen conduit, an abandoned pipe-run wrapped in lead lagging. But its underside is listening to the iron. The stilts are wedged into the cobble-chinks, feeling the tension mount in the basalt bedrock as the weight of the water above strains against the mechanical dead-center of the engine.

Then comes the *shuck*. 

The water jolts. In that single, concussive second—while the inertia of the brine is broken and the liquid yields slightly to its own momentum—the creature moves. It does not scuttle; it shoots forward six paces, its metal-tipped stilts ringing faintly against the stone like a handful of dropped nails, moving with terrifying, effortless speed while the medium is loose. 

Then the brine locks up again. The engine settles onto its sleeve bearing. The creature freezes instantly, caught in the middle of a stride, one stilt lifted, its blunt, shovel-nosed head pressed hard against the iron wall of the boiler house. It will not twitch an eyelid—it has none—until the next stroke clears the way.

These are the scavengers of the galvanic seams. Their snouts are lined with plates of soft, spongy copper. As they wait between strokes, they press these plates against the black magnetite seams where the wrought iron is joined to bronze. They are licking the galvanic battery. They drink the potential difference between two alloys; they fatten on the corrosion that the engineers sought to prevent a hundred years ago. Their flesh, if one were to cut them open with a cold-chisel, has no red meat; it is a pale, fibrous paste that smells intensely of ozone and wet batteries, threaded with fine veins of metallic copper that have precipitated out of the salt into their tissues.

***

And at the very sill, where the tramway cables drop off into the black abyss of the shear-face, there is something that has not moved since the tram cables ceased to hum.

It cannot be said whether it was once an engine or an animal, or whether the distinction has any meaning in a place where iron turns to stone and meat turns to lard. It sits in the cable-winding shed, a brick cube whose roof fell in fifty winters ago, now open to the cold, dead brine. 

It fills the pit where the drum used to sit. It is a mass of pale, rubbery tissue, sixteen feet across, that has grown around and through the iron spokes. The spokes are its ribs now; the cable, two inches thick and frayed into steel whiskers, passes directly through its core like an iron gut. 

It does not breathe the water. It breathes the grease. 

A slow, perennial seep of crude animal tallow—poured into the winding gear when Victoria was an old woman—still oozes from the drowned grease-cups above the shaft. The creature receives it drop by drop. The tallow lands upon its pale crown, where it is welcomed by a wide, disk-shaped mouth that never shuts, its rim fringed with pale, hair-like cilia that do not wave, but hold the fat like a sieve.

The thing is utterly blind, completely stationary, and colder than the salt around it. But it is connected. 

The tramway cable runs out of its flank, stretches across the silted sill, and drops five miles into the dead deeps where no machine was ever built. When the walking beam in the foundry turns, three hundred paces back, the vibration travels down the cable like a pluck on a harp string. 

A full minute later, the cable twitches within the creature’s body. 

The thing does not cry out; sound cannot exist here. But it responds. From its pale flanks, where the skin is grafted directly to the cast-iron bearing blocks, it discharges a soft, broad sheet of green galvanic fire. The flash does not illuminate the shed—the brine is too thick, too full of suspended lead for the light to carry—but for three inches around its flesh, the water turns the color of a tarnished penny. 

The star-shaped sparks rise from its skin like a swarm of cold gnats, drift three inches toward the sky that is not there, and die against the dark weight above them. 

Then the silence, thick as cooled grease, settles back over the alley. The wrench hangs in its lens of salt, six feet from the cobbles. The coats in the tenements hold their cold breath in their sleeves. And the flywheel, down in the grease-blackened belly of the hall, begins its four-minute crawl toward the next revolution.
