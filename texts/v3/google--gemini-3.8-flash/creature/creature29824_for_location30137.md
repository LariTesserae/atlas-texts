The chamber is not merely occupied; it is being digested from the inside out by things running on a compressed, hysterical clock. 

Because the air is at the threshold where mammalian cooling fails, the rats have had to alter their physical business entirely. They are not the heavy, sleek sewer-dwellers of the upper mains. Their fur has come away in irregular patches from the ceaseless humidity and the scraping of rough aggregate, leaving their flanks mottled with grey-pink dermatitis and raw heat-sores that never quite form scabs. They have stretched. Without the seasonal cold to bunch them into thick-shouldered wedges, their bodies have grown elongated, spindle-ribbed, and slack. Their tails, perpetually engorged with blood to dump heat into the damp air, drag behind them like scalded earthworms, swollen to twice the thickness of surface rats.

They spend their lives in a state of suspended heat-exhaustion. You do not see them sprint. They move with an unnatural, liquid deliberation, flowing from the rim of a junction box down to the gravel with their bellies flattened against the cool stone, trying to offer their naked groins and chests to any surface that hasn’t yet matched the steam pipes' temperature. Their breathing is a frantic, shallow fluttering that keeps their whiskers in permanent tremor. 

In this wet furnace, territoriality has melted away. There are no fights over boundaries because the metabolic cost of a bite is too close to heatstroke. Instead, they lie piled together on the iron valve platforms in heaps of six or eight, slick with each other’s sweat and the condensation dripping from the pipes, their snouts resting on each other's flanks. When a roach falls from the ceiling—cooked through from a three-second contact with an uninsulated union and landing with a faint, greasy *pat* on the concrete—the nearest rat does not pounce. It merely reaches out a bare, five-fingered paw, drags the insect to its chest, and crushes the softened carapace with a wet, economical crunch of its molars. They do not need to forage; the vault feeds them an uninterrupted hail of scalded protein.

Their breeding has come unhitched from the seasons. Litters are dropped inside the wet folds of the curing blankets that still hang pinned to the concrete walls. These blankets—thick sheets of quilted polyethylene and rotting jute—have become vertical nurseries. Inside their damp pockets, where the chemical stench of the setting concrete is strongest, pink, hairless pups squirm in litters of twelve or fourteen, blind and translucent enough that their milk-spots and tiny, dark livers show through their bellies. Many do not develop hair at all; they grow straight into a grey, leathery adolescence, their ears oversized and veined like cabbage leaves, adapted entirely to an underworld that never cools below blood heat.

Between the concrete and the burlap blankets, where the air is trapped and sour, the invertebrate world has multiplied into dense, writhing sheets that have lost all rhythm of day and night. 

The roaches here are not dark brown. In the continuous bath of wet sodium light and steam, their shells have lightened to a sickly, semi-transparent horn color, almost the shade of scalded pork rind. Their life cycles have accelerated to a fever pitch; they molt twice as fast as their surface cousins, leaving the gravel littered with millions of pale, discarded skins that do not dry or crumble, but rot into an oily, grey pomade that greases the floor. 

They share the hanging blankets with house centipedes—*Scutigera*—that have grown monstrous on the endless meat. In the lower basements these creatures are fast and fragile, but down here, gorged on roach nymphs, their bodies have swollen into thick, segmented thumbs three inches long, their multi-jointed legs dragging through the moisture with a dry, hair-comb rustle. They do not hunt with their usual darting precision; they crawl slowly over the wet cloth, heavy with their own venom, grazing through the nurseries of infant cockroaches like cattle through tall grass.

Lower down, where the water gathers in the stepped catch-basins, a different, quieter order has taken hold in the lime milk. 

The run-off is thick with calcium hydroxide leaching from the fresh shotcrete—alkaline enough to burn human skin—yet the sumps are alive. The isopods that drift through these cloudy, stagnant pools are ghostly things, completely bleached of pigment, their carapaces thickened and chalky from the minerals they ingest. They look like carved soap. They move across the grey-green floor of the basins with a slow, marine grace, browsing on the dense, rubbery biofilms that grow in the currentless warmth. 

These films are not green; they are the liver-red and sulfuric yellow of deep-crust extremophiles. They form undulating, mucus-like sheets two inches thick that anchor to the galvanized bolts and wrap the lower rungs of the maintenance ladders in greasy, wet rags. When a drop of lime-water falls from fifty feet above, it punches a neat, round crater into the bacterial jelly that slowly closes over again like grease in a skillet.

Around the edges of these pools, where the gravel meets the sump walls, there are things that have come down the drainage pipes as eggs or microscopic cysts and found themselves in an accidental tropics. Tiny, jet-black midges that do not fly—their wings stunted into useless, wet stubs by the constant downdrafts from the upper vents—hop through the sludge along the waterline in dense, vibrating drifts. They feed on the moldering grain-sprouts that have withered into grey hair upon the rat dung. 

There are no voices here, only the respiratory machinery of ten thousand small, soft bodies fighting the wet air. Sometimes, when a heavy freight above crosses the frogs at the surface junction, the whole chamber shudders through its rock anchors. The vibration is immense, deep enough to make the water in the sumps jump in concentric rings of alkaline milk. 

When that happens, the vault does not panic. The rats do not scatter; they only turn their blunt, scalded heads toward the ceiling in unison, their whiskers twitching as the fine lime-dust drifts down through the sodium light like hot flour. Overhead, across twenty thousand square feet of wet steel and curing stone, the pale carpet of roaches shifts an inch to the left, a single, lazy ripple of greasy lacquer adjusting itself against the heat, and then the dark settles back into its wet, panting work.
