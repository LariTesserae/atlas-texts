They have no name for themselves, nor any apparatus that could frame one, but in the acoustic geography of their skulls, the extruded city is not a labyrinth or an enclosure; it is an immense, rigid reef. 

They are called up out of the tectonic fissures by the current in the foundational struts. That hum—the seven-hertz thrum that keeps the brine from crystalizing—is identical to the frequency generated by continental subduction zones, the grinding of wet basalt against wet iron miles below the lightless sea floor. To them, the city is merely a colossal, cold-welded shelf of bone that has suddenly punched up into the mist, offering infinite footing, infinite guttering, and an endless, sheer plane across which their hides can shed grease without snagging on the silt of the trenches.

The great ones—the three-meter bulls and matriarchs that form the main body of the crush—are built like tallow ingots. Beneath the thick, non-cellular leather of their outer skin lies a blubber that does not burn lipids for warmth; instead, it is densely packed with mineral platelets, like wet mica, which insulate them against the cold by simply refusing to conduct heat inward or outward. They operate at the exact temperature of the brine. Their blood is rich in copper-protein complexes that turn it a deep, oily violet, carrying dissolved nitrogen at tensions that would liquefy human marrow. 

When one dies, it does not collapse so much as it settles. Its spiracles seize open, whistling like vented boiler valves as the gases of its intestine escape, and the herd simply climbs over it. The weight of thousands of blunt, webbed claws presses the carcass into the virgin fullerene floorplates until the hide splits. Within hours, the body is not decomposed—for there are no rot-bacteria here, no airborne spores to turn meat into soup—but mechanically pressed, flayed, and stripped by the blind traffic until only a flat parchment of hide and a basket of cartilage remain, polished into the black gutters like a watermark.

Yet the herd is not uniform. Look closely into the vapor where the drainage ribbons hang suspended by the null-fields, and the hierarchy of this deafening migration begins to resolve.

Riding the flanks of the larger animals are the *crawlers*—crustaceous, flattened things no larger than a human hand, pale as bleached lard, their eight legs tipped with curved carborundum points that can grip the slick fullerene without slipping. They do not feed on the great beasts’ flesh; they feed on the thick, alkaline grease the giants secrete to lubricate their crawl. The crawlers are sanitation workers of the skin: they scrape away the salty crusts that form where the brine evaporates against the spiracle fringes, their multi-jointed feeding palps working with frantic, mechanical precision. When two bulls press together in the narrow bottlenecks of the terrace stairs, thousands of these crawlers are sheared away, falling like dropped coins into the runoff below, where they tumble without injury, rolling with the current until their legs catch the lip of a gutter and they scuttle back up the sheer pylons to find another moving mountain of fat.

Higher up, along the under-ribs of the suspended skyways where the draft from the abyss blows fiercest, live the anchors. 

These do not move at all. They are molluscan or brachiopod in habit, though their shells are not calcium—which would dissolve in this heavy, acid-tinged moisture—but folded sheaths of tough, vulcanized keratin. They have clamped themselves directly to the virgin expansion joints, using chemical excretions that etch slightly into the non-reactive composite, anchoring their muscular pedestals against the updrafts. From these hanging shells, long, translucent feeding filaments trail down into the thoroughfares, some stretching twenty meters toward the street level. 

The filaments are nearly invisible, swaying in the convective wind like monofilament lines. They do not sting; they are coated in an ultra-viscous, conductive mucus. As the blind herds surge beneath them, their venting spiracles puff up plumes of moisture rich in ammonia, cast-off epithelial cells, and dense lipid droplets. The filaments catch this biological exhaust, reeling it upward in slow, rhythmic hitches, digesting the vaporized dross of the city’s floor before it can settle and sour the air.

And beneath everything, in the deepest troughs where the water moves with the force of hydraulic rams, are the scrapers.

They are elongated, eel-like things, two meters long but no thicker than a man’s thigh, blind and entirely jawless. Their mouths are wide, circular suckers armed with concentric rings of diamond-hard rasps. They live entirely submerged in the black, weeping channels, navigating the zero-gauge gutters by sensing the slight electrical eddy-currents generated where the water shears across the fullerene corners. They do not hunt the great beasts. They hunt the current itself, or rather, the metallic sediment that precipitates out of the over-saturated water as it passes through the city’s low-frequency magnetic zones. Their stomachs are full of fine, black magnetic sand; they grind it endlessly in their gizzards, extracting the faint chemical charges imparted by the struts, living on the literal physics of the platform’s construction.

There is no mating season because there are no seasons in the cloud. Reproduction is continuous, silent, and indifferent. 

In the quietest alcoves—the interior hollows of the five-tiered city where the wind cannot reach and the roar of the water drops to a hollow, resonant purr—the pregnant females peel away from the press. They crawl into the blind geometric corners, wedge their snouts into the razor-edged angles where three planes meet, and remain motionless for days. When they drop their litters, the young do not emerge as soft, helpless things; they are expelled in hard, leathery pods, perfectly spherical, about the size of a barrel. 

These pods do not hatch immediately. They are washed down the terrace gutters, bouncing and clattering against the black steps like ball bearings, rattling down five tiers of pristine engineering until they wedge into the drainage grates at the very bottom of the plateau. There, submerged in the fastest, coldest torrents, bathed in the strongest electromagnetic runoff from the foundations, the pods cure. The skin of the egg absorbs the conductors from the brine, hardening, turning matte-black, until after three cycles of the strut-resonance, the capsule splits from within.

The neonate crawls out: thirty kilograms of slick, pale muscle, already equipped with its blunt claws, its blind, sensory-tilted snout, and the deep, hollow chest cavity designed to breathe the fog. It does not look for its mother. It does not need to. It simply turns against the flow of the water, sets its claws against the virgin fullerene, and begins the long, wet, deafening crawl upward into the dark.

They are not an infestation; they are an equilibrium. The city, cast by whatever cold intelligence designed it, was perhaps intended to be empty, a clean engine of unknown purpose waiting for a signal that never came. But biology is a pressure, like the water condensing out of the black sky. It finds every crevice that does not burn it. It fills every void that provides a foothold against the drop.

And so the city groans under their mass, lubricated by their tallow, cleaned by their parasites, its mathematically perfect canyons resonant with the colossal, wet sighing of a hundred thousand lungs that know nothing of architecture, and care only for the friction of wet skin against cold, unyielding floorplates.
