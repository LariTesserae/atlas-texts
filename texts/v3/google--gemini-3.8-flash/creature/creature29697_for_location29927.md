To understand what lives here, one must first abandon the prejudice that life is something that happens in time.

On the plateau, life is an estate. It is not an event that begins, consumes, excretes, and finishes; it is a structural condition, like the crystalline grid of the slate beneath it or the angle at which dolomite shears when struck. The creatures do not inhabit the plateau; they *are* its outermost strata, the final, organic sedimentary layer of a world that has finished settling.

If you sit beside them long enough for the sound of your own pulse to become background noise, you begin to see that this carpet is not uniform. It has lineages, orders, and quiet hierarchies that have long since grown into one another.

### The Braided

The uppermost layer belongs mostly to the horned beasts—the ones whose spiraled crowns have knit into a continuous canopy. They are not all of one kind, though fifty thousand years of standing flank-to-flank have rubbed away the sharp distinctions of breed. There are great-horned sheep whose fleeces are as dry and dense as pressed felt hats; there are straight-legged ibex whose coats have the sheen of buffed horn; there are broad-nosed cattle whose lips are perpetually parted, exposing teeth as square and yellowed as old dice.

Their horns do not merely touch; they have grown through and around one another in an intricate joinery. Where two tips have pressed against each other for millennia, the keratin has cold-welded, fusing horn to horn until the entire herd forms a single, vast suspension bridge of bone and hair. If you strike a horn at the southern lip of the plateau, the dull, wooden *thock* will not travel through the air, but the vibration will run through the lattice for miles, carried from skull to skull, through the dry neck vertebrae, down into the flint-like hooves, and into the rock, dissipating without an echo.

Their eyes—those polished beads of jet—are not blind. They are focused on the horizon, but they do not track. They hold the sky. If you pass your hand an inch before a cow’s eye, the pupil does not contract; yet if you peer into it, you see the reflection of the bleached sky with a clarity impossible in a living, weeping eye. There is no film of moisture to warp the image. The eyeball is dry glass, set in a socket of supple leather that neither shrinks nor rots.

### The Interstitial Kinds

Beneath the bellies of the great ungulates, in the dim, cool crawlspaces between four-footed pillars, lives the secondary floor. This is where the smaller forms have gathered, fleeing an ancient wind that ceased to blow before their hair could fall out.

Here are the rodents with scales of translucent mica. They do not burrow, for the slate will not yield to them, but they have wedged themselves into the crevices between hoof and stone, between knee and knee, forming tight, interlocking pavements. Their bodies are flattened, like loaves left unbaked, their ribs hinged to fold nearly flat without breaking. They possess no claws—their feet terminate in small, dry suction-pads, pale as discs of dried turnip, that adhere to the stone with a grip that requires no muscle tension, only the seal of absolute vacuum.

Among them are the wingless birds. They have forgotten how to sing, not because their vocal cords are gone, but because air has no purchase in a throat that holds no moisture. When you look closely at their plumage, you see that the barbs of their feathers have fused, turning from plumage into continuous, paper-thin plates of grey and buff horn. They rest their chins on the haunches of the mica-rats; the mica-rats rest their snouts against the ankles of the sheep.

And everywhere, filling the gaps where two curved hides leave a triangular vacuum, are the jointed things. These are not insects in any modern sense, but heavy, slow-minded arthropods made of unglazed earthenware. They have no antennae; those broke off long ago or were drawn back into the head-shields. They have twelve or fourteen jointed legs that fold flush against their bellies, fitting together like the pieces of an ivory puzzle-box. When picked up—if one had the strength to pry them from the stone—they would feel like tiles of sun-baked silt. They do not bite, nor do they run. If placed back, they settle into their precise indentation with a small, dry *tink*.

### The Coincidents

The true mystery of the plateau’s population lies in the depressions—those wide, shallow threshing floors where the arithmetic of space has collapsed.

In these places, the creatures do not merely crowd; they *coincide*. In a hollow sixty paces across, you can walk among twenty thousand stags. They do not crush one another. A stag’s shoulder passes cleanly through the ribcage of an elk; an owl’s dry beak sits precisely in the center of a goat’s braincase, neither displacing the other. Both are solid. If you press your thumb against the goat’s brow, it resists with the density of cold tallow; if you slide your finger an inch to the left, you feel the hard, sharp quill of the owl’s wing-joint, equally firm, sharing the same coordinates in the world without friction.

Here, a third kind of inhabitant has emerged: the things that exist only in the overlap.

These are thin, leaf-like entities—neither animal nor fungus—that have taken root in the shared volume where two bodies interpenetrate. They have the consistency of dried vellum, pale ivory or the color of tea-stains. They have no organs of their own; instead, they borrow the geometry of the collision. Where an elk’s lung passes through a ram’s shoulder, this paper-thin creature traces the boundary between the two, spreading its lace-like veins along the impossible seam. They do not eat the animals. They eat the *fact* that two things are one. They are the skin of the paradox, feeding on the small, silent friction of two masses occupying a single point in space.

If you shine a lantern through them—though the fixed sun leaves little room for lanterns—they cast a shadow that is neither sheep nor deer, but a shifting, complex knotwork of ribs that belongs to no beast that ever breathed.

### The Shepherds

They are still here.

They are not skeletons, nor are they mummies in the way lowlanders understand the word; they have not shriveled, their lips have not pulled back from their teeth, and their bellies have not fallen in. They have simply undergone the same mineral conversion as their charges.

There is a shepherd leaning against the low slate wall at the northern wash. He wears a tunic of coarse, undyed wool that has long since become indistinguishable from the fleece of the ram whose back supports his right elbow. His beard is long, parted by an ancient breeze that left it swept slightly over his left shoulder, stiff as carded hemp. His skin is the color of parchment that has spent three centuries in an attic, dry and fine-grained, with every pore distinct.

His eyes are open. They are brown, clouded with a faint, bluish ring around the iris—the *arcus senilis* of a man who was already old when the clock broke. He holds a crook of peeled ash, its grain polished by the grease of a palm that has not sweated in a hundred eras. The wood has not checked or split. It has simply lost its sap, becoming as light as balsa and as hard as bone.

Between the thumb and forefinger of his left hand, he holds a copper chisel. It is pressed against the horn of a heifer that stands knee-deep in his own shins. The cut is halfway through a mark—the fourth in a cluster of five parallel lines. The metal has not oxidized; in this air, copper stays as bright as an orange peel.

If you speak to him, the sound of your voice falls flat, swallowed by the wool around his knees. He does not turn his head. But if you look into the pupils of his eyes, you realize he is not absent.

He is engaged in an act of infinite duration. He is not waiting for the world to resume; he is holding it where it is. The nomads who built these slate walls did not come here by accident; they drove their herds up from the green, rotting valleys because they were tired of things dying. They climbed until the air became too thin for decay, until the sky lost its weather, until the sheep ceased to drop their lambs and the rams ceased to butt their heads until they cracked. They came here to finish.

The shepherd’s mind is not dark. It is simply wide, cold, and level, like the sky above him. He knows the count of every horn within five leagues. He knows where the horns have knit, and where the lattice needs the pressure of an elbow to keep the geometry from sliding. His thought does not move from premise to conclusion; it rests upon the herd like the sun rests upon the dolomite.

### The Pith-Lice

Deep within the bodies, in the cavities where intestines once churned and hearts once beat, there is no rot, but there is an inhabitant.

If you were to take a saw to the dried flank of one of the great oxen—though the sound would set your teeth on edge for days—you would find the interior filled with that white, elderberry-like pith. It is dry to the touch, odorless, smelling only faintly of flour and old stones.

Living within this pith are the smallest of the plateau’s citizens: white, blind lice, the size of caraway seeds, with six legs that terminate in microscopic hooks. They do not eat the pith. They have no mouths; their heads are smooth, rounded knobs of ivory chitin. Instead, they move through the dry cellular channels of the animals' interiors, traveling along the paths once cleared by arteries and nerves.

Their movement is inconceivably slow. A single louse may take a human lifetime to travel from the hip-joint to the hock of a sleeping heifer. They do not reproduce; their numbers were set when the plateau dried, and not one has been added or lost. They are the keepers of the channels. Where a thread of dry pith threatens to break and sever the continuity between a horn and a hoof, they anchor it with their bodies, hooking leg to leg to form a living bridge of bone-white shell.

They are the reason the beasts remain firm. They are the internal scaffolding of a world that has replaced the wet hurry of life with the eternal architecture of the crust.

***

When the sun does not move, there is no evening to wait for. The creatures will not wake, because they are not asleep; they are simply complete. They have survived the need for water, the need for green grass, and the terrible, wearing labor of breathing. They have become part of the mountain's long memory, a fleece of bone and dry skin spread over the limestone, keeping the old earth cool beneath its coat.
