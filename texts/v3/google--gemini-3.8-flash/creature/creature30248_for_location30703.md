To live here is to develop a wide, testing step. 

Because the raw limestone floor shears into flat lozenges under any sudden strike of the heel, the people walk with their knees slightly bent, planting their soles flat and dragging the weight forward from the pelvis rather than the ankle. They move without the bounce of plainsmen. Their feet are broad, splayed by weight-bearing on bare laths and jagged rubble, the soles yellowed and stiff as sole-leather, though crisscrossed with hundreds of pale, bloodless cicatrices where fresh shards have nicked the skin.

They are not subterranean people—not yet. Their eyes are still the eyes of scrub-herders and plain-dwellers, squinting against the flat glare of noon when the white cut fills with direct sun. But their skins have taken on the specific, dry pallor of the cellar. It is not an unhealthy gray, but the tint of river-silt dried on an arm: chalk dust has worked so thoroughly into the grain of their hides, into the pores of their forearms and the crevices around their nostrils, that washing does not lift it. It stays like flour in the seams of a sack.

Because iron is hoarded for the tips of things that must pierce, the people smell almost entirely of grease and cold larch. To keep the skin from cracking in the cellar draft, they rub themselves with mutton fat brought down in bladders from the sheep-runs on the plain above. The tallow, mixed with the fine limestone flour that hangs suspended in every breath of wind, forms a smooth, water-repellent crust over their forearms, shins, and the napes of their necks. When they work—straining against the breast-straps or levering the wet larch baulks into place—they do not smell of hot horse-sweat, but of cool, sour grease and peeled bark.

Their speech has shortened to fit the acoustic of the cut. In a place where a dropped cedar bucket or a cracked stone sounds like an axe-blow, loud talk is treated as a clumsy, dangerous waste of breath. They speak in the back of the mouth, clipping their vowels and leaning on soft palatal stops that die three paces from the lips. A man directing a four-ton block will not yell; he communicates through the tension of a willow wand held between his two forefingers, or by clicking his tongue against the roof of his mouth—sharp, dry taps that tell the rope-haulers whether to slacken or lean.

Family life is arranged vertically, by moisture and draft. 

The elders and the small children live on the highest shelves, three or four tiers up from the floor, tucked directly under the roof-slabs where the rising animal warmth collects and the stone is driest. Even so, the small children are tethered. You see them pegged out on short leashes of braided osier on the flat roofs of the middle tier, sitting among the salt-crusted hides like pale, fat grubs. They are quiet children. They are taught before they have all their milk teeth that the edge of a slate slab is an absolute end; they play with chips of calcite and smooth knuckle-bones, never running, scooting on their buttocks with their heels tucked under their skirts.

The younger, heavier men and the uncoupled women sleep lowest, in the "keels"—the narrow, box-like spaces left between the dry-stone footings and the sweating bedrock. Here the air is cold enough that a sleeper’s breath hangs in the tallow-light all night long. They sleep packed four or six to a sheepskin pallet, spooned tight to share marrow-heat, their clothes tucked beneath their heads to keep the bedding clear of the damp that creeps across the stone like oil. 

A peculiar caste of people has already coalesced around the water-gutters: the *trough-watchers*. They are mostly older women and maimed quarrymen whose thumbs or arches have been crushed by settling stone. They sit throughout the long twilights on low stools carved from larch knots, armed with small wooden scrapers and bundles of dried marsh-grass. Their entire business is the maintenance of the lead-lines. If a hairline fissure begins to weep in a new spot along the cliff face, they must chip a tiny diversion lip with an adze-horn to catch the drops before they run into the foundations, guiding the runnel into the nearest cedar chute. They know every seam in sixty paces of wall; they know which cracks yield sweet water that tastes of lime and which ones produce the bitter, gypsum-tainted seep that curdles lard and rots the osier lashings within a fortnight.

Food comes down from the rim in wicker hampers that swing between the gin-poles like black wasps. It is dry food: cakes of parched barley meal, smoked mutton as hard as larch heartwood, dried wild leeks, and sacks of small, bitter field-peas that must be soaked in the cistern water for two nights before a tooth can dent them. The town has no stoves; fire is a terrifying thing in a chimney made of dry larch poles and bundled reeds. What cooking exists is done in deep stone pits dug into the rubble at the wider points of the cut, where charcoal burned on the plain above is kept glowing beneath lids of heavy slate. Food is simmered rather than roasted, boiled down into dense, glutinous gray broths thickened with pea-meal and fat, which the people dip out with flat horn spoons and swallow without chewing.

Beneath the houses, in the crawl-spaces where human grease and wood shavings have accumulated in the cold, the crickets have multiplied. They are not the chirping field crickets of the scrub; they are an inch long, entirely white, and soft-bodied. They have long, thread-like antennae that tremble continuously in the draft. When a man rolls over on his floor-slabs, you can hear hundreds of them shifting in the dark below like dry corn husks stirred with a stick. The boys catch them in bladder-flasks and dry them on the slate roofs until they are crisp enough to be ground into powder with salt; the quarry-gangs rub this powder into the chafed raw spots left by the hemp hauling-harnesses to dry the skin.

There are no rats yet—the plain is too lean to support them, and the rift is too new for them to have followed the scent of grease down the ropes. Instead, the domestic vermin are the small, mouse-colored lizards that crept in from the rim fractures during the first summer. They have fattened on the crickets and the gnats that drift down from the wind-rim. They are semi-domesticated by indifference; they are so sluggish from the cold that they can be plucked from the wall timbers like overripe berries. Children keep them inside their leather tunics, against the ribs, where the lizard stays torpid and keeps the fleas down by snapping at anything that crawls across the skin.

At night, when the fat-lamps are doused to save the tallow, the silence is not absolute, but geological. 

The people do not toss; they lie still beneath their stiff wools, their ears tuned through the timbering to the mountain itself. The rift is still finding its balance. Every few hours, a sharp, metallic *ting* sounds through the foundations—not an echo from the town, but the bedrock three leagues below snapping off another micro-burr as the two sides of the valley settle a millimeter deeper into the earth. When this happens, a low, collective murmur runs through the roofs—a thousand sleepers exhaling at once, checking without waking that the lintel has not dropped, that the cedar gutter is still dripping into the bucket, that the world above them has not decided to pinch itself shut.
