To understand what lives here, one must first look at the depth of the water.

Across millions of square yards, the water is rarely thicker than a fingernail. It is not a pond or a creek; it is a sheer, tension-bound membrane sliding over slick lime. Because of this, the first true inhabitants of the floor are not creatures of depth, but of the surface film—animals that have dispensed almost entirely with a third dimension.

In the glassy catchment pans, where the water gathers to the depth of an ankle, the surface is occasionally broken by what look like wandering oil-slicks. They are colonial siphonophores or something adjacent to them—radial, tissue-thin discs of pale, translucent amber, no thicker than wet parchment, measuring three to four paces across. They float suspended in the top four millimeters of the water, sustained by the high surface tension. Their upper sides are dry, dusted with microscopic silica prisms that reflect the yellow-white sun away, preventing them from being cooked in their own shallow broth; their undersides are a dense carpet of microscopic, alkaline-resistant cilia that sweep the ceramic beneath them clean. They do not eat prey, for there is none; they graze on the curing chemistry of the floor itself, metabolizing the dissolved calcium hydroxide and the free minerals leaching out of the fresh stone before the squalls wash it all away. 

When the sun burns the pans dry in the late afternoon, these discs do not perish. As the water recedes, they simply settle onto the dry ceramic, glue their margins to the trowel-marks with a crust of dried albumin, and go inert. They become indistinguishable from faint, circular stains on the stone—rings of dried salt. When the next wall of rain advances, drumming across the leagues, the sudden film of water lifts them again within thirty seconds, softening the crust, and they resume their slow, circular drift across the pans.

Away from the catchments, where the water is only a momentary sheet on its way elsewhere, life must move or be baked.

The hairline seams between the plates—the coarse-sand packings—are the nurseries. If you kneel and peer into the crack, where the shadow is narrow as a knife-blade and relatively cool, there are things that look like lengths of silver thread. They are blind, ribbon-flattened myriapods, their bodies made of overlapping, glassy plates that can withstand the hydraulic pressure when the multi-ton ceramic slabs expand and groan together. They do not burrow down, because the stone is solid below; they live horizontally, threading through the grout for miles like living stitching. They feed on the pioneer lichens, scraping the olive crust down to the bare lime with diamond-hard, chitinous rasps, and leave behind trails of calcified, pearl-like frass that slowly cements the sand into place.

During the downpours, these thread-creatures come up. When the rain drums down and the floor turns into a single rushing mirror, thousands of them slip out of the joints. For ten minutes, while the water sheets over the plains, they swim through the runoff—writhing laterally, glittering like needles dropped in mercury, mating, shedding eggs into the warm current, and darting back down into the nearest fissure the instant the sheet breaks into beads and the heat begins to radiate from the stone again. Their eggs are swept into the sumps, where they settle in the sand-drift around the edges of the pans.

Then there are the drifters, brought by the localized meteorological cycle. The great thermal drafts that pull air inland from the unseen perimeter act as a conveyor for whatever lies beyond the terrace. They bring aerial plankton—spiders that balloon on long threads of silk, tiny winged seeds, desiccated rotifers, and occasionally, larger things carried aloft in the violent updrafts of the squalls.

Most of what falls from the sky dies on impact or cooks against the noon ceramic. But around the monolithic white pillars—those ten-foot-tall, unperforated blocks that cast the only shade for leagues—a parasitic ecology has formed around this continuous sky-fall.

Huddled against the eastern base of the monoliths during the morning glare, and shifting with excruciating precision to the western side as the shadow sweeps across the deck in the afternoon, are creatures that look like low, broad-shelled river stones. They have no common name, for no one has been here to name them. They are flat, four-legged scavengers with wide, snowshoe-like pads composed of fibrous horn that insulate them from the ceramic's radiant heat. Their shells are pale, hydraulic-white, mimicking the lime-stone perfectly, covered in tiny, cup-like depressions that catch windblown moisture.

These shadow-dwellers are tethered to the monoliths like goats to a post. They cannot leave the shade for more than a few minutes without their blood coagulating in the radiant furnace of the floor. Their whole lives are lived in a slow, clockwise waltz around the stone base, keeping pace with the penumbra. 

As they march in their eternal, tight orbit, they lower their flat, soft snouts into the angle where the monolith meets the floor. Here, the wind deposits the dried flotsam of the sky: the dead spiders, the blown chaff, the tiny airborne beetles that suffocated in the thermal clouds and dropped like hail. The shadow-dwellers vacuum this drift with a rhythmic, wet clicking. When the squall hits, they do not drink from the pools; they press themselves flat against the vertical face of the monolith, anchoring with leathery suction-pads, and drink the rain as it sheets down the clean white wall, filtering the lime through bristled lips.

They are few. Around some monoliths, there are three or four; around others, none at all—only the empty shadow sweeping across empty stone, waiting for a seed or an egg to blow in from the edge of the world.

And higher up, in the air that hovers two fathoms above the radiant heat, something has begun to take advantage of the sheer, uninterrupted level. 

They are kite-like, paper-thin organisms, perhaps related to the discs in the pans, but evolved for the boundary layer where the rising heat meets the descending cool of the squall clouds. They have no muscles, no true organs; they are sails of sun-toughened cellulose, three spans across, weighted at the trailing edge by a bead of heavy calcium carbonate they secrete from atmospheric dust. They do not fly so much as float on the cushion of thermal air that hovers two feet above the deck, skimming perpetually without ever touching the ground. 

They travel with the wind-fronts, racing five miles ahead of the rain squalls like scrap paper driven before a broom. When the shadow of a cloud passes over them, the thermal cushion collapses and they drop gently, skimming along the damp plates for a few yards before the next gust catches them, lifting them back into the pale, heat-bleached sky.

None of this life has modified the place. There are no burrows, no mounds, no paths worn into the plates. The trowel-marks remain as sharp as they were when the pourers left; the razor seams remain unchipped. The floor is still essentially clean, sterile, and immense. The living things here are like the dust that settles on a freshly painted table: light enough to be ignored by the surface they occupy, living entirely on the margins between the stone's chemical heat and the cloud's sudden water, waiting for the first true crack to appear.
