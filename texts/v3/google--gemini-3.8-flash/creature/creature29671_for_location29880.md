The first true residents are not the things that were planned, but the things that came glued to the undersides of the coir mats: colonies of minute, ghost-white symphylans and soil-mites whose generations are currently turning over every six days in the uninterrupted eighty-degree wash. 

They do not stay in the coconut fiber. To them, the vast, newly turned copper faces are warm cliffs of salt and tallow. They graze on the skin of curing lacquer, carving delicate, serpentine tracks through the milky haze that coats the metal seams. Where an Allen bolt forms a shallow basin with its recessed head, five or six of them will gather around the dark rim of grease, suspended upside down on the surface tension of a single trapped drop of distilled water, twitching their thread-like antennae in the downdraft of the atomizers.

Higher up, where the galvanized struts meet the glass vault, are the ones who were brought intentionally: three dozen dwarf day-geckos, introduced from breeding jars two weeks after the pumps were fired to keep the midges down. They have not yet settled into territories. They are vivid, violent emeralds against the gray industrial zinc, skittering across the rivet heads with dry, adhesive clicks. Because there is no real night here—only the blinding cobalt daylight of the high peaks, followed by the dull amber glare of kerosene heaters hung along the catwalks to prevent cold spots—their pupils remain pinned to narrow, black slits. They have discovered that the copper water-mains are warmest two inches downstream of the bronze expansion joints; there, three or four will flatten themselves flank-to-flank, their soft bellies soaking up the boiler’s vibration until the brass nozzles hiss overhead. Then they snap their heads back in unison, lapping at the falling mist before it hits the floor.

And then there is the tender.

Her name is rarely used because there is no one within earshot to speak it; the shift cable-car only swings out from the lower terminal once every sixteen days with fresh filter-coarse, lard, and salt. On the registry at the valley station she is entered as Vane-Cutter Third Class, but in the Core she is simply the body that displaces eighty liters of vapor.

She does not wear the heavy boiled-wool serge of the pylon workers outside. Here, the positive pressure and the thick, lung-saturating steam make cloth an irritation. She wears only a pair of oiled canvas trousers cut off at the knee, held up with a strip of split hide, and thick-soled river-shoes made of woven hemp that give her purchase on the slick diamond-tread of the iron grating. Her skin has the peculiar, waterlogged pallor of a turnip left in a cellar bucket; the skin of her palms and the balls of her feet have stayed permanently creased into white, dead-looking ridges since her second week on the mount. 

She sleeps swung in a low webbing sling slung between two exhaust columns near the north flue. It is the only place where the air moves fast enough to dry sweat. To sleep, she binds a strip of black grease-cloth across her eyes to shut out the eternal, milky mountain glare that pours through the vault. Above her, the condensation collects on the wire-glass, shivering with the *chuff-thump* of the steam beam until a drop grows too fat to cling and falls, warm and greasy, onto her collarbone. She does not wake when it hits; she simply rolls an inch to the side, her breathing heavy, long, and wet, timed unconsciously to the four-minute rising and falling of the brass atomizers.

Her work is not agriculture; it is clearance. The Core’s vitality is an aggressive, mechanical threat to its own function. Every five hours, she takes her scraping blade—a curved piece of spring-steel with a file-sharpened edge—and walks the narrow lead gutters beneath the floor.

Down here, between the iron grating and the sloped lead pans, the heat is suffocating. The warm water runs over her hemp shoes in a continuous, lukewarm wave. The *Philodendron* roots are the enemy: they possess a blind, hydraulic strength, driving their white, fibrous tips into the packing-glands of the reciprocating pumps, seeking the tallow-grease, which they drink like milk. If left alone for three cycles, a single root can pry the packing out of a bronze stuffing box and drop the pressure across the third sector by two atmospheres.

She crawls through the dark with a brass-cased carbide lamp hooked between her teeth, its jet burning with a steady, blue hiss that smells of lime and wet stone. When she finds a root wedged into a seam, she saws it through with three quick jerks. The severed tip bleeds a clear, viscous slime that smells faintly of rhubarb and hot machine-oil. She flings the rubbery lengths behind her into the drainage pan, where the current carries them down toward the intake screens.

She has developed a way of moving that belongs entirely to the Core. Because the air is held at one-and-a-half atmospheres above the thin mountain wind outside, her voice, when she clears her throat or curses a rounded bolt, sounds curiously flat and small, stripped of its echo by the wet greenery. Her ears click constantly. She moves with a deliberate, low-slung economy, never raising her head too near the glass where the air pockets hit fifty degrees, never lingering long beneath the atomizers when the brass valves begin their metallic *tink-tink-tink* of thermal expansion.

When her crawl is done, she climbs to the central staging platform. There, where the great wooden-handled blow-off cocks project from the floor, she sits on an overturned grease drum to eat her cold peas and dried tongue out of an enameled cup. 

She does not look at the plants; she has ceased to see them as green. To her, they are simply a heavy, self-propagating felt that muffles the voices of the steam valves. But sometimes, while she chews, she will extend a white, water-softened thumb toward a freshly emerged shoot of *Monstera*. The leaf is still rolled into an oily, pale spike, glistening with drops of pure lacquer-sweat. She will lay her thumb against it, feeling the tiny, frantic pulse of the sap driven up from the coir by fifty pounds of hydraulic pressure from the pumps below, measuring its heat against her own.

They are both being pumped full of water at high pressure. They are both suspended in a glass cage on the rib of a dead granite cliff. When the four-minute squall begins at the south bulkhead, she does not look up; she merely drops her chin to her chest, lets the fine, hot fog settle into her cropped hair, and listens through the soles of her hemp shoes to the single, massive iron heart rocking on its grease-soaked pins beneath the deck.
