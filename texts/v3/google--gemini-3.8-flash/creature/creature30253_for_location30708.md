At first, it seems proper to agree with the eye that took the survey: nothing lives here that has lungs or a name. 

Yet the absence of birds and flies is not an absence of appetite. It is only that the threshold of entry has been set at a temperature that denatures ordinary blood. Life in the trench does not arrive from the sky; it rises from the split floor, or it rode in on the grease of the drilling rigs and found, in the boiling gloom, an ancient home it remembered from the Archean.

Begin in the lagging around the intake pipe. The wet insulation, sodden with warm mineral water and laced with degraded tallow-grease, is not merely decaying; it is being digested by a dense, felted mat of thermophilic hyphae. This mold produces no caps or brackets. It grows as a pale, glistening rind, greasy to the touch, that throbs imperceptibly with the pulse of the pump inside. Deep inside its fibrous folds, where the temperature sits at a steady 58 degrees Celsius, live colonies of blind, eyeless mites—tiny as cornmeal, translucent as paraffin. They do not walk so much as swim through the wet glass-wool, grazing on the sulfur-blooms. Their bodies are round, droplet-shaped, and covered in fine, hydrofuge setae that trap microscopic jackets of cooler air against their carapaces. They have no lungs; they breathe the scalded air through pores in their skin, and their metabolic rate is so furiously elevated by the ambient heat that their entire lives—hatching, breeding, starving, dying—are compressed into forty-eight hours. Their shed skins drift down continuously into the black wash like a fine, gray meal.

Beneath the running water, the life is older, heavier, and slower. 

In the margins where the chartreuse cyanobacteria tangle into long ribbons, there is a grazer that does not look like an animal until it moves. It is a flatworm, perhaps an inch long, though they vary with the flow. In the open sunlight they appear slate-black, almost indistinguishable from the wet basalt grit, but when lifted into the sulfur-haze, their tissue is clear amber, veined with a branching digestive tract colored vivid emerald from the slime they consume. They have no circulatory system to boil. They cling to the rock by sheer capillary tenacity, sliding across the stone beneath the laminar flow of the water, insulated from the worst of the heat by a self-secreted sleeve of heavy, clear mucus that smells faintly of boiled turnip. Where the current is swiftest over the white silicate bed, these worms cluster in shingles, twenty or thirty overlapping, their edges fluttering in the draft of the water like the petals of a dark, drowned flower. 

They are deaf to the noise, or rather, the roar of the subterranean vent is the only world they have ever known; their nervous systems are tuned entirely to the thrum of the basalt. If the steam trap stops its *clack-thump*, they contract into hard, rubbery buttons, waiting for the vibration to return before they extend their sensory lobes again.

Higher up, where the lead-sheathed cables droop from the stanchions, there are things that depend on the failures of the iron.

Around the severed copper cores that have crusted into turquoise carbonates, a species of crustose lichen has established itself. It does not belong to the mountains outside. It is an opportunistic hybrid of an alga that blew in on the blasting dust and an extremophile yeast that lives in the condensation lines of geothermal stations throughout the valley. It grows thick as dried lard, chalky and pale yellow-green, crusting over the split insulation. The lichen secretes an acid that leaches the copper, concentrating the heavy metal in swollen, bead-like blisters along its margin. When the afternoon sun strikes the wall directly, these blisters turn iridescent, weeping tiny droplets of a metallic, emerald syrup that the heat bakes into a resinous varnish. 

Underneath this crust, sheltered from the caustic steam by the dead copper salts, live small, legless larvae. They are the offspring of a midge that never takes flight—a creature whose females are entirely wingless, resembling bloated, six-legged lice with skins like wet parchment. The males possess vestigial, paddle-shaped wings, useless for the dense, saturated air, which they use only to fan the steam away from the female’s brood-pouch during the few minutes of their mating. They live on the underside of the cables, suspended above the deafening abyss, their whole existence pinned to the thirty-centimeter zone where the air has cooled to forty degrees but the rock is still wet enough to prevent them from drying into husks.

And deepest of all, where the concrete plinth sinks its massive foot into the gravel wash, something has come up out of the fracture itself.

It is not an animal that belongs to the sun. It is troglobitic, washed up from the deep hydrothermal cracks by the bypass manifold's venting. They are amphipods—bleached, milk-white crustaceans the size of a pumpkin seed, completely without eyes or pigmentation, their carapaces so thin that the pale churning of their hearts is visible through the dorsal plates. In the subterranean lakes from which they were violently blown, they scavenged on ancient bacterial mats in the cold dark. Here, swept into the trench, the survivors have gathered in the eddy behind the pump plinth, where the water is turbulent, hot, and continually replenished with dissolved minerals. 

Most were boiled when the steam first breached, their curled, pinkish carcasses paving the gravel three months ago. But the few that survived have adjusted with horrifying speed. They have colonized the slack-water pockets between the plinth and the basalt wall, where the runoff cools slightly as it pools against the sulfur-leached concrete. They crawl over the submerged bolts with a stiff, scuttling gait, grazing on the white silicate skin that is slowly entombing the machine. Their antennae are extraordinarily long, like gossamer threads twice the length of their bodies, sweeping the current constantly for the shower of dead mites falling from the pipe above.

When one dies, it does not rot in the normal sense; the water is too sterile for putrefaction. Instead, it is slowly encrusted. Within a week, the little crustacean is coated in a fine, sparkling armature of mineral salts, becoming a tiny, perfect fossil of calcite and sulfur, cemented to the gray plinth alongside millions of its fellows.

There are no voices here. Nothing calls out to mark territory, because sound is obliterated by the piston-stroke of the valve and the iron groan of the rock. The communication is entirely chemical, written in trails of slime that do not wash away in the current, in peptides left on the rust, in the pheromones of fear and hunger that dissolve into the clear, scalding sluice and are carried downstream to the waiting, blind mouths below the intake. 

The trench is not empty; it is a crowded, frantic nursery. It is simply that the things that live here have made their peace with the disaster that made the place. They are made of water that is nearly boiling, their shells are formed from the poisons of the mine, and they would freeze to death, stiff and shattered, in the cool, quiet grass forty feet above the rim.
