The people do not have names for years, because nothing here has ever lasted long enough to gather an age, yet nothing has ever ended. Their language has fifty words for the degree of hardness in setting lime, but no word for *bark*, no word for *shade*, and no word for *tomorrow*. 

They are squat, thick-ribbed, and heavy in the heel, built with the low center of gravity required to live on an inclined, slippery shelf. Their skin is not brown or pale in the manner of people who know sunlight; it is a dense, water-logged grey, like the flesh of a cleaned skate, tinted faintly yellow around the throat and knuckles by sulfur steam. Because the mineral water is violently alkaline—capable of scouring the skin to raw beef in three days of steady wading—they go through life lacquered from the scalp down in bird-fat and the thick, yellow oil rendered from river-dogs. It gives their bodies a dull, wet sheen, like oiled slate. Where the grease wears away—at the creases of the elbows, the groin, behind the ears—the lime forms dry, white scabs that crack and seep watery blood. 

They do not wear clothes to keep warm, for the steam never allows the air to cool below the heat of a fresh carcass, but to keep from being shredded by the stone. A fall on an unworked step of fresh travertine will open a forearm like an oyster. Their aprons and thigh-guards are made of the sewn bellies of the bottom-feeders: thick, pebbled hide, cured not with tannins (for there is no bark) but steeped in boiling brine until it turns as stiff and horn-like as hoof.

Their hearing is largely deadened. The roar of the adzes, bouncing off millions of wet domes, combined with the endless clatter of the diving birds and the hiss of subterranean steam, leaves them nearly immune to subtle sound. They do not whisper. When they speak to one another across an alley, they bark from the diaphragm—short, harsh, vowel-heavy shouts that carry over the screeching rafts of fowl. At close range, within the dim, sweating interiors of the domes, they communicate through a rapid, percussive slapping of their palms against the wet lime walls, feeling the vibration through the floor-slabs with their bare, calloused feet.

To understand their lives, one must understand that the city is not built upon a mountain, but upon a slow, expanding lung of scalding rock.

Every few days, a blister rises. 

A family or a work-gang will feel the floor of their cell grow hotter, until the grease in their tallow-bowls begins to smoke. The lime underfoot will bulge upward in a smooth, hair-cracked dome, three or four cubits across, sighing hot vapor through its fissures. When this happens, there is no panic; there is only a scramble of work. The adze-men come running, bare-chested and reeking of vinegar, driving heavy wedges of river-slate into the fracture lines while the stone is still soft, before the calcium has fully crystallized into flint-like hardness. They peel the rock back in steaming layers, like the rind of a melon, working fast before it seizes. By evening, they have hollowed out the blister, channeled its sulfur-spring into the central run-off gutter of the alley, and turned the swelling into a sleeping-niche or a sunken vat for curing meat.

Death here produces no graveyards, because there is nowhere to dig. The earth does not yield; it only pushes outward. When a man or woman dies, their bones are the most valuable thing they leave behind. In a world without timber or metal, bone is the only structural anchor. The long femurs are scraped clean by the river-dogs, dried in the steam-flues until brittle, and shaped into pegs to pin the clay bricks together or carved into the barbed gaffs used to haul the blind-eels from the cisterns. The flesh is dropped straight into the sea-trenches, where the river-scorpions wait in their billions, their broad, pale claws clicking against the submerged cliffs.

Beside the people live their beasts, entirely shaped by the warm, plantless soup.

The river-dogs are not like the hounds of dry land. They have lost their ears; in their place are round, sphincter-like holes that seal watertight when they slide off the terrace edges into the alleys. Their skin is hairless, dead-white, and folded in loose, rubbery dewlaps that protect them from the bites of the river-scorpions they hunt in the shallows. They do not run; their legs are short and paddle-shaped, terminating in five webbed claws of clear cartilage. They live in the lower niches of the domes, half-submerged in the grey run-off, whining with a wet, bubbling sound in their throats, waiting for their troughs to be filled with the mash of crushed bacterial foam and chopped eel-heads. Every household keeps four or five; they are heat-sinks for the children to sleep against, grease-factories to keep the skin intact, and, when their joints grow stiff with lime deposits, meat for the pot.

Above them, on the curving tops of the domes, are the birds. They have the bulk of geese, but their feathers have devolved into a greasy, matted shag, impervious to the boiling sprays that drift from the vents. They cannot fly—their wings are merely flat, muscular paddles edged with horn—but their legs are massive, ending in splayed, serrated feet that can grip the slickest bacterial film without skidding. They are foul-tempered, territorial, and entirely devoid of fear. They lay their pale, leathery eggs directly upon the warm lime, where the subterranean heat incubates them without the need of brooding. The roofs are an endless, squabbling carpet of them: millions of grey backs, snapping beaks, and yellow, cataract-filmed eyes. A child cannot cross an upper roof-way without carrying a club of slate to beat off the drakes, whose bone-tipped beaks can shear a finger to the knuckle.

And between the stone and the flesh crawl the scorpions. They are pale as lard, broad as platters, and nearly translucent; through their carapaces one can see the dark, grey paste of sulfur bacteria churned in their guts. They crawl from the water at dusk—though dusk is only a slight thickening of the pearl fog—climbing the wet lime-wash of the domes to hunt the blind, broad-winged moths that drop exhausted from the ceiling of steam. The children harvest them with wicker tongs, snapping off the harmless, fat tails to squeeze the white, crab-like meat straight into their mouths, still twitching with the heat of the stone.

Nothing here has ever seen a leaf. If a branch of oak or a sprig of pine were dropped upon the travertine terraces, the people would treat it with the same bewildered caution they might reserve for a dropped meteorite: a dry, fibrous curiosity, possessing neither meat nor grease nor lime, completely useless to a race whose only world is the living mineral under their feet, the gray soup in their bellies, and the heavy, boiling fog that keeps them all alive.
