To find what lives here, you have to unlearn the signs of movement. 

In an atmosphere rushing upward at seventy miles an hour, where the cold glazes everything in an invisible skin of dry, unfreezing lubricant, soft motion is impossible. Muscle would freeze into glass; tendon would snap like dry spaghetti. Nothing here crawls, nothing slithers, and nothing flies.

Instead, the first true inhabitants appear as litter—debris you would swear was left behind by the fitters who bolted the gussets together forty years ago.

Lying in the dead corners of the diamond-tread plates, just outside the blast of the laminar air-shear, are small, circular disks. They look like punch-outs from a drill press or discarded zinc shims, no thicker than an envelope, five to eight centimeters across. They are dull, nickel-gray, and their edges are sharp enough to shear paper. For hours, they lie pinned against the deck by the acoustic pressure, vibrating at the exact four-hundred-hertz frequency of the gas engines. 

Then, when an acoustic node shifts—when a valve closes three miles down the line and moves the standing wave an inch to the left—a disk will buckle.

It does not crawl. It flexes with a sudden, violent *clack*, like a child’s bimetallic jumping-popper, leaping eighteen inches through the dry air to slap flat against the sheer manganese wall. It clings there by electrostatic adhesion. These are *lacquers* or *tappets*. They are not mechanical; they are laminated biological wafers of dry mica-plate, bound with fossilized resins and veins of native copper that grow through them like capillary beds. 

They have no insides, no wet viscera. Their "digestion" is a slow, cold galvanic process: their underbellies are coated in a fine, velvety felt of conductive graphite hairs that absorb the thin film of aerosolized diester oil coating the steel, while their upper surfaces bleed the continuous static charge off the air. When two of them overlap on a plate, they do not fight; they weld slightly at the rim, sharing charge through micro-arcing until the mechanical vibration shakes them apart again. They live for decades in the roar, thickening by fractions of a micron every year, slowly laying down rings of brittle silicate until they are as hard and dead as the armor they cling to.

Higher up, where the cross-bracing forms narrow, windless wedges against the outer skin, are things that have achieved articulation.

They are known only by their silhouette against the blinding quartz-iodide floor-troughs: long, spindly geometries called *calipers*. A caliper is built like a drafting compass crossed with a harvestman, but it is composed entirely of hollow, quill-like segments of wire-heath that have incorporated manganese shavings into their joints. They have no eyes; their long, arching limbs are acoustic sensory horns, tuned to different registers of the station’s structural scream. 

A caliper lives perpetually wedged between opposing surfaces. It spans the gap between a secondary manifold and a vertical support truss, its three needle-sharp leg-tips seated firmly into the microscopic pits of the laser welds. It does not move to flee; it moves only to maintain tension. If the station flexes under the torque of an engine cycle, the caliper adjusts with tiny, ratchet-like clicks of its flexure-joints, keeping its central body suspended in absolute harmonic stillness. 

Its mouthparts are fine, spinning drills made of modified quill-teeth, powered by the continuous mechanical shudder of the metal it rests on. It does not consume the steel; it bores into the dense, felted clumps of wire-heath that anchor in the bolt threads, mining them for the sulfur and copper they have accumulated from the air. The caliper is so dry, so light, that if it were knocked from its mount into the main wind-shear, it would not fall; it would be drawn up through the intake cowls into the high stratosphere, tumbling into the upper blue like a handful of dry thistle-down.

And then there is what rests in the deep null-zones.

Every hundred meters, where the geometry of the compression housings creates an acoustic dead-spot—a pocket of air only six feet wide where the sound cancels itself out into a terrifying, vacuum-like silence—something larger sits.

It looks like an abandoned pressure-suit or a roll of heavy, rubberized insulation that has been wedged behind an expansion joint. It has no recognizable face, but it possesses orientation. Its outer hull is a thick, pleated mantle of layered asbestos cloth, vulcanized synthetic rubber, and dried grease that has absorbed so much manganese dust it has turned a uniform, velvety blue-black. 

It does not breathe the air; it lets the laminar wind blow through it. Across its dorsal hump are rows of narrow, louvered slits lined with fine brass-wire gills that collect the diester mist and trap the dry airborne particles of zinc wash. It has been there so long that the wire-heath has rooted into the folds of its joints, its yellow quills sprouting from the creases behind its knees and under its neck-ring like stiff lichen.

It is barely mobile, moving perhaps three inches over the course of a seasonal atmospheric cycle. When it does shift, the sound is like heavy canvas being torn in a frozen room. It possesses hands, though they are not fingers so much as a cluster of flat, spring-steel pincer-blades that have grown together into a specialized pry-bar. With these, it reaches into the grating beneath its perch, clearing away the fallen, needle-sharp debris of the wire-heath before the drainage slots can choke.

It is difficult to say whether it was built by the station’s makers and forgotten, or if it was once a man who came up into the interstitial spaces to inspect a weld, stayed through the cold, and let the grease, the static, and the howling pressure replace his blood with something that doesn't freeze.

It sits in the absolute center of its acoustic pocket, bathed in the white halogen glare, listening through its iron-shod frame to the engines miles below. Around its head-cowl, where the metal has been ground smooth by decades of vibrational friction against the structural ribs, the blue corona discharge flickers continuously—a cold, soundless candle-flame that never wavers, burning on the dry breath of the sky.
