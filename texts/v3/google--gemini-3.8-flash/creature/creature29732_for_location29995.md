The mouths that would crowd your skin are not organs belonging to a single kind of beast. They are the youngest things in the room, still unanchored to any anatomy, like small scabs of appetite that drift on the light until they find an edge worth testing. When they find your knuckle, they do not bite to feed; they bite to take an impression, the way a key-maker presses soft wax into a lock. They leave faint, hexagonal rings of cold moisture that stay pale long after your hand is withdrawn, marking where they tried, and failed, to find the grain of the world you came from.

Among those that have achieved a shape, the water-mice are the most numerous, though they are less like rodents than like tears that have learned to steer. Their bodies are held together solely by the high surface tension of the room’s air. Because there is no wind and no heat to stir them, their amber beads—the small, hard inclusions that click when they bank—are their only weight. These beads are not bone. If one of the mice drifts too near the unbaked doorframe and is drawn through, the beads do not simply rattle; they arrange themselves in a brief, strict constellation, like the lead pellets in a level, before settling back into a disorderly knot. They are measuring something about the threshold. They do not feed on the stone or on each other; they seem to drink the sour brightness itself, swelling slightly over the course of days until their skins become so taut that they split with a sound like a wet finger run over parchment, releasing two smaller, perfectly clear replicas of themselves and three new amber pellets that roll across the tallow floor until the flat-moths come to scrape them clean.

The flat-moths are the housekeepers of the curd. They have no bellies, only an underside that is slightly more porous than their backs, operating like dry tongues against the rising mineral. They glide always in pairs, never touching, but maintaining between them a strict gap of three or four inches, as if carrying an invisible pole between their shoulders. Where they pass, the wet fat of the wall ceases to sweat; it glazes over, taking on the brittle, porcelain sheen of an eggshell that has dried in the shade. When two pairs cross paths, they do not turn or yield. One pair simply thins itself until it is a grey film no thicker than an oil-slick on water, allowing the other to slide across its back without a rustle, leaving a faint grease-track that the glasswort prisms immediately begin to hum against.

Those prisms—the solitary, blunt stems that rise from the sediment—are where the life of the pocket touches what might eventually become mineral law. They do not grow from seed. They are excreted from below by the silver horsehair eels that weave through the soft limestone sludge. 

The eels are the only things here that possess an ancient patience. They are older than the air that surrounds them. They do not breathe the light; they shun it, surfacing only when their long, looped bodies have collected enough cold from the deep, uncured strata beneath the skiff-hold. When an eel’s snout pokes through the curd, it looks like a drop of solder resting on butter. It will wait for an hour, perfectly motionless, until a drop of the upward-falling moisture gathers upon its tip. The moisture does not drop up into the void from the eel; instead, the eel drinks it backward, drawing the room’s inverted gravity into its marrow until its whole length goes stiff as a needle. Then it dies, or seems to die, petrifying where it stands into one of the glasswort stems. Over weeks, the lower half of its body is digested back into the white sludge by the centipedes, while the prism at its tip continues to ring softly, singing down into the mud the news of whatever altitude it has managed to measure.

And there is something larger, which does not swim and does not graze.

Near the left wall, where the fired-clay plumb-bob aims its sideways desire, the stone is not tallow-colored. It is darker, bruised with the grey of slate that has almost remembered how to be heavy. Here, pressed flat into a shallow recess that it has softened for itself, lies the Auditor.

It is roughly the length of an oarsman’s forearm, but it has no limbs to speak of—only a series of broad, overlapping plates of uncalcified horn, like the plates of an artichoke or the segments of a boiled prawn, grey-white and translucent at the margins. It has no face, but between its front plates there emerges, at long intervals, a bundle of white, hair-fine feelers that do not move with the air, but twitch with the same dry cadence as the plumb-bob’s thread.

The Auditor is the only thing in the pocket that has weight in the ordinary sense. It is extraordinarily dense. Where it rests, the curdled wall does not harden; it groans, weeping a steady, slow runnel of yellow oil that never reaches the floor, because the Auditor’s feelers are constantly weaving back and forth through the drip, turning the fat into dry, crumbly pellets that it tucks beneath its belly.

It has been there since before the slabs were set. It may have made the slabs; beneath its shell are traces of that same unbaked siltstone, smeared like grey clay across its underside. It lives by hesitation. It does not allow itself to settle into flesh, nor does it permit its plates to become shale. Every few hours, it heaves itself half an inch to the side, breaking the bond it has just formed with the rock, leaving behind a smooth, moist hollow that smells faintly of river water and cold iron. 

If you were to watch it long enough, you would see that it is not idle. It is waiting for the doorframe to become true. The plumb-bob points to its heart because the Auditor is the only anchor the chamber possesses—the one point of stubborn, unformed mass that refuses to fall upward into the bright non-space above. The water-mice know this; when their amber beads click, they turn their blind heads toward the grey plate of its back, realigning their small interiors against its density before they venture through the stone trilithon again.

Nothing here sleeps, because there is no dark to invite it, but occasionally the Auditor will draw its feelers all the way into its plates and emit a long, shuddering pulse that travels through the soft stone like a ring through water. When this happens, the centipedes freeze on their needle-legs; the flat-moths drop flat against the curd, sticking fast like wet leaves to a flagstone; and the red water that springs from their punctures does not evaporate. For three or four seconds, the room remembers weight. The beads on the floor stop rising and roll together into small, trembling puddles; the light dims to the color of wet tallow; and the whole chamber feels small, narrow, and cold, like a chest buried under six feet of ordinary dirt, waiting for a shovel that has not yet been forged to strike its lid.

Then the Auditor twitches. Its side-plates click. The plumb-bob swings an eighth of an inch wider, and the world resumes its drifting, unfinished rehearsal.
