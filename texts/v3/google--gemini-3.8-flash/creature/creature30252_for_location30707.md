The people who inhabit the spans are called the Bark-Brood or the Ring-Men by none but themselves, for they have no neighbors to name them. To themselves they are simply those who stand. 

Because the world screams without pause, they have ceased using voice for ordinary business. Their speech has dropped down into the throat—a language of short, glottal clicks and swallow-cries that carry no further than an arm’s reach—or it has moved entirely into their limbs. At ten paces, two hunters converse through the snapping of knuckle-joints, the rhythmic clapping of palms against thigh-chitin, and the tap-tap of wood-pegs struck against the grain. Sound does not travel well through the wind-sheared air, but the petrified timber carries vibration with terrifying fidelity. A woman pressing her ear to the floor of a cleft can feel the skitter of a bore-grub six hundred yards away through twelve feet of solid cedar, or tell by the dull, dragging thrum across three miles of heartwood that a dead-fall beetle has touched down on the upper ridge.

Physically, they are thick-set and bowed, their shins curved slightly outward from a lifetime of bracing against the camber of the logs. Their feet are wide, the soles not soft like water-dwellers’ feet, but split and padded with cracked, yellow calluses half an inch thick—tissue that has learned to key into the microscopic grooves of the grain like stone-gripping moss. They wear no shoes; a shod foot slips on wind-polished wood, and to slip on the sunward side is to slide for sixty yards down a slope that steadily steepens until it drops into two leagues of lavender mist. Their toes are long, muscular, and perpetually curled, capable of hooking into an ancient beetle-track with the reflexive tenacity of an osprey’s talon.

Their skin, darkened by the savage ultraviolet of the thin altitudes, is coated from crown to heel in *smear*: a foul-smelling grease rendered from the abdominal blubber of the great ox-beetles, boiled together with pulverized liverwort and bitter resin. It preserves them from the dry winds, which would otherwise mummify a living man in two seasons, and turns them the color of cured horse-hide. Over this, they wear little: aprons of beaten bast stripped from the rare, damp seams of cambium; harnesses of braided sinew that are never removed, day or night; and greaves carved from the glassy, iridescent elytra of dead mantids, laced tightly to the shins to turn the thorny kicks of skyline fowl.

Theirs is an architecture of anchoring. Nothing they make is free-standing. A house is not a box built upon a floor; it is a pouch or an eyelid, lashed down flat against the hollow of a grain-scar. They use the shed husks of the droning beetles as roofs, overlapping the curved black plates like slate tiles and caulking the seams with chew-gum made of cedar-pitch and bird-down. Within these husks, the air is still, dark, and smells of old dried hives. A family does not sleep on bedding, but in woven nets suspended from dowels hammered deep into the wood. They sleep strapped in; an infant who kicks free of its swaddling must not be permitted to roll.

Water dictates their movements, though they never move far. The knot-hole cisterns are sacred, not with prayer, but with strict, violent protocols of conservation. To foul a knot-hole is to die by the gaff. Each morning, before the dry sun has climbed two fingers above the east spans, the water-gatherers go down on the shadow-side. They travel suspended in fiber-baskets on long, grooved wooden tracks pegged into the vertical drop, descending into the damp under-fleeces where the traveling clouds shear against the burls. There, in the cold, dripping dimness, they scrape the greasy liverworts with bone spatulas, wringing the gray, spongy mats into bladders made of bird-craws. 

Down there, beneath the arches, the world is entirely different. It is cold, greenish-black, and silent compared to the howling sunward crests. Here cling the *blind weavers*—pale, soft-bodied spiders as large as sheep-shields, who do not build orbs, but stretch single, vibrating trip-lines between the splintered fibers of the lower bark, waiting for the dead or disabled fliers that tumble endlessly from the daylight wars above. The Ring-Men do not hunt these spiders; they harvest their silk with notched sticks, winding the sticky, silver cables to twist into heavy mooring ropes that do not rot, even when soaked in beetle-fat.

Above, the sky remains an engine of wings, and it is from the sky that everything else is drawn. The people do not sow; they intercept. 

When the spine-winged martins return from their circuit of the lower cloud-banks, flying in tight, rippling skeins of twenty thousand birds that sound like a hailstorm on sheet iron, the skyline-hunters take their posts on the wind-flutes. They anchor their feet in bored peg-holes, lean out over the void at an angle of forty-five degrees, and work the *gallows-rakes*. These are thirty-foot poles of dried reed-wood, balanced on bone fulcrums, tipped with clusters of barbed beetle-mandibles. With short, practiced jerks, the hunters sweep the edges of the cloud. The work is bloody and mechanical: the hooks tear through the dense flock, snagging wings, necks, and breastbones, hauling the thrashing, screeching shapes down onto the timber-shoulders where children wait with wooden clubs to crush their skulls.

The birds provide almost everything: flesh that is black, dry, and tastes of iron and bitter seed; needle-bones that are turned into awls, hooks, and dowels; and feathers that are chopped fine to mix with resin for insulation. The stomach-stones of the larger gannet-like divers—smooth, pebble-sized quartz spheres swallowed by birds in some far-off, mythic world where the earth has broken into gravel—are treasured above all else. They are the only stone the people ever touch. They are passed down through six generations as family heirlooms, used to hone the teeth of the push-knives, or kept in the mouth by the dying to give their tongue something heavy to press against as their breath leaves them.

Beyond the hunters, other things share these wooden highways. 

In the long, cavernous dry-rot flutes—tunnels fifty yards wide that bore through the center of the greatest spans—live the *gall-monks*. Whether they were once the same stock as the skyline hunters is unknown, for they have grown long-limbed, chalk-skinned, and nearly blind. They do not visit the sun-slopes. They dwell in the rotting dust of the interior, harvesting the giant bracket-mushrooms that grow in shelf-like stairs inside the hollows. These mushrooms are as dry as cork, tasting of stale flour, and must be chewed for hours to yield any moisture. The gall-monks have no ropes; they climb the perpendicular growth rings using curved finger-claws made from the dried leg-spurs of mantids, gummed to their own nails with amber-pitch. They are tolerated by the hunters because they trade dry mushroom-flour and a bright blue rot-grease that glows cold in the dark for bird-meat and the bitter liverworts of the cloud-drop.

And then there are the things that belong neither to the sky nor the settlement. 

Along the high, sun-baked crests, where the grain has split into canyons twenty fathoms deep, prowl the *resin-wolves*. They are not mammals, but six-legged, wingless orthopterans, covered in a coarse, hair-like bristle that collects drifting thistle-spores until they resemble moving clods of dry fleece. Their jaws are blunt, chisel-shaped levers capable of shearing through an ox-beetle’s horn as if it were a dry reed. They are solitary, slow-stepping, and blind, navigating entirely by the heat-gradient radiating from the wood. They follow the Ring-Men by the warmth their bodies leave behind on the bone-colored grain; a hunter who pauses too long in one depression must look back to see if the gray fleece is creeping along his track, sniffing the wood with antennae thick as walking-staffs.

When death comes to a dweller of the spans—whether through the slip of a foot, the sudden, snapping strike of an aerial serpent sweeping low through the noon glare, or the simple drying-out of the lungs that takes the old—there is no digging of graves. There is nowhere to dig. The grain is harder than copper and will not take a spade.

The dead are dressed in their harnesses. Their kin coat them one final time in the beetle-grease, fill their mouths with a lump of sweet-resin, and carry them to the absolute verge of the leeward drop, where the wind does not rise, but falls in a long, smooth sheet toward the lower lavender.

There is no weeping; the roar of the sky would swallow it anyway. A hunter simply takes his push-pole, places the notch against the dead man’s ribs, and gives a short, steady thrust. The body does not tumble immediately; it slides along the polished marble-curve of the wood for fifty paces, picking up speed, silent against the white fiber, until it clears the lip of the grain and hangs for a fraction of an instant against the empty, five-mile gulf. 

Then it is gone, taken by the air, falling down toward whatever floor the world forgot ten thousand centuries ago, while above, the mantids drum their forelegs against the cedar, the great beetles vibrate the bones of the living, and the smoke from the cambium-pots rises straight into the sun, thin and gray and utterly ignored.
