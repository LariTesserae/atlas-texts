The skin of the people who walk the slate is not cracked, as an outsider might assume of an air without humidity, but cured. It has the consistency of clean sheepskin parchment that has been kept in a dark chest with dry sage: flexible, thick at the knuckles, and entirely without pore-glaze. They do not blush easily; their blood runs deep within their limbs, shielded beneath subcutaneous layers of firm, dense lard that insulates the core from the radiant heat of the feldspar floors. 

Because they cannot afford to lose water through the mouth, their speech is lateral and compact. They speak without widely opening their lips, driving consonants against the front teeth with small, sharp puffs of breath that are inhaled again almost immediately. To strangers from lower crusts, their conversation sounds like the settling of cold shale or the click of bone counters on an abacus. They have few songs that use vowel-calls; their music is rhythmic and percussive, played upon tuned zinc plates with small lead mallets, or hummed through closed nostrils so that the chest cavity acts as a resonator against the iron floorboards.

They wash themselves with dry fuller’s earth and cakes of pulverized talc scented with sulfur-pitch. At night, in the low adobe tenements where the heat of the noon hours is trapped and slowly given back by the three-foot-thick walls, husbands and wives use scrapers of polished ox-rib to clean the day’s tallow from each other’s shoulders. It is an intimate, quiet labor: the dull horn sliding over the yellowed skin, gathering up the mixture of paraffin grease, dust, and discarded skin cells into a grey paste that is not thrown away, but rolled into pellets and dropped into the small bronze urns by the door. In the morning, these pellets are taken down to the block-stoker to be melted into candle-grease. Nothing that contains carbon is permitted to blow away.

Reproduction here is an event of long engineering. A woman carrying a child is relieved of all tram-line duties and moved to the lower galleries near the pneumatic hubs, where the air is thickest and the escape-valves from the cracked-oil stills maintain a constant, measured heaviness. Her rations of sealed water—heavy, flat-tasting distillate drawn from the deep asphalt fissures—are doubled. The child, when it arrives, does not cry with the wet squall of a sea-born infant; its eyes are sealed with a waxy vernix so tough it must be softened with warm carillon-marrow before the lids can part, and its first breath is drawn against the mineral rasp of air that tastes of tin and brick.

Above these settlements, in the open stretches of swept stone where the iron rails narrow into the horizon, the *carillons* live out their centuries. They are not warm-blooded in any sense that a wet lung would understand. Inside their enamel shells, there are no red organs; their bodies are divided into dry, compartmentalized cauldrons where sulfurous compounds are broken down by colonies of anaerobic, lithotrophic microbes that ferment without moisture, producing a dry, hot gas that keeps their limbs light.

When a carillon grazes, the rasping of its belly-plate sounds like a shovel working into gravel. It does not look where it goes; its small, amber-colored eyes are countersunk behind defensive skirts of horn, useful only for registering the angle of the white sun. They have an uncanny sense for the stress-lines in the bedrock. A herd will halt as one animal, sixty tons of jointed porcelain standing motionless for hours upon a seam of gypsum, waiting for a micro-fault three miles below to slip and release its faint wisp of subterranean methane, which they draw into their lateral vents with a long, wheezing groan that sets their rib-casings ringing like bell-metal.

Between the plates of the carillons live the *scritches*—small, flattened crustaceans the color of dirty paraffin, with ten jointed legs that end in diamond-hard carvers. They do not feed on the beast itself, but upon the brittle crusts of salt and sulfur that flower in the seams where the animal’s joints grind together. A carillon does not scratch; it tolerates the scritches as a ship tolerates barnacles, though sometimes, when the tickling of their tunneling becomes sharp, the great beast will lean its bulk against an iron tram-pylon and slowly, methodically grind its flank against the metal until the small things are pressed into a fine, yellow meal.

The sky, though clear, is never empty. The cartilage kites do not descend to the stone if they can avoid it; their ribs, though light, are brittle as dry macaroni, and a rough landing on the slate can shatter the struts of their wings. They live upon the updrafts generated by the heat of the zinc foundry-roofs. 

To the machinists who look up through the roof-gratings, a kite against the yellow sun looks like a geometrical drawing made in clear horn. Their bodies are no larger than a child’s torso, but their ribs extend into vast, parabolic frames across which is stretched a skin as thin as bladder-paper, slightly milky, through which the slow, amber pulse of their dorsal artery can be tracked. They have no feathers, no hair, and no beaks; their mouths are round, puckered rings of gristle, lined with small, inward-pointing teeth made of pressed chitin. 

They fish the lower air. When the warm air rises off the massifs at mid-afternoon, carrying with it millions of dried beetle-shucks, flakes of mica, and the ballooning silk of the rock-spiders, the kites tilt their wings, drop their four-fathom lines of sticky, amber mucus, and troll through the heat-shimmer. When a line grows heavy with caught vermin, the kite does not reel it in; it simply twists its body in an aerial barrel-roll, bringing the line across its mouth-disc, stripping off the dried chitin and the fat-rich beetles in a single, wet slurping sound that is lost in the vastness of the plateau.

There are things, too, that live wholly within the machines. 

In the long conduits that carry compressed air from the Great Drop, there exists a species of blind, white slug, known to the line-tenders as *gasket-rot*. They are tough as cured leather, four inches long, and feed exclusively on the graphite-grease used to pack the pipe flanges. They do not drown in the violent drafts of high-pressure air; they thrive in them, anchoring themselves to the interior rivets with four suckered feet and extending fringed, feather-like mouths into the gale to catch the microscopic talc-dust that the compressors draw from the plains. When a gasket fails and a pipe begins to scream, the line-walker knows what he will find when he unbolts the sleeve: a tight, writhing ball of these pale rubbery grubs, jammed into the breach, having sealed the leak with their own tough bodies while they gorged on the escaping lubricant.

The men do not hate the gasket-rot; they pry them out with copper chisels, toss them into grease-cans to be used as emergency packing elsewhere, or dry them on the flat stones until they are hard as dried plums, to be chewed during long watches on the tram-ways to stimulate the salivary glands.

Death among the human settlements is treated with the same dry, mechanical respect as a broken gear. When a man dies, his family does not lay him in earth—there is no earth to take him, only the unyielding granite that turns a pick-point in three blows. Instead, the body is carried to the desiccation houses behind the bauxite roasters. 

There, in long chambers through which the dry, superheated flue-gas passes constantly, the corpse is laid upon a slatted rack of red fir. In forty-eight hours, the warm, thirsty wind has removed every dram of fluid, drawing it up the flue to be captured by the condensation-rings at the chimney-head—for even the last breath of a father must return to the community’s water-stills. What remains is light and dark as cedar-wood. The bones are pulled from the dry sinew, ground in horse-head mills into a fine, calcium-rich meal that is added to the mortar for the adobe walls, while the dried hide and muscle are pressed into fuel-blocks for the copper furnaces. 

"He has gone into the brick," the elders say when a companion is laid down. And indeed, walking through the tenement streets of the fifth massif, one can run a dry thumb along the red mortar between the bricks and know that the wall stands plumb because the people who walked it fifty years ago are holding it against the weight of the air.

At the edges of the massifs, where the stone drops away into the blue haze of the uninhabited depths, live the *tally-men*. They dwell in round iron huts perched directly upon the lip of the abyss, tethered to the bedrock by chains each link of which is as thick as a bull’s thigh. These men are solitary, chosen from those whose speech has failed entirely with age, or who have developed the "slate-stare"—a quiet, harmless madness that overtakes those who spend too many seasons tracking the flight of the kites.

The tally-man has only one duty. He watches the great counterweights of the aerial tramways as they hang over the void. These weights—cylinders of hollow iron filled with seventy tons of lead punchings—rise and fall by inches as the day warms and the three-mile spans of steel cable expand and sag, then shrink and tighten in the late afternoon. 

From his round window, the tally-man reads the height of the weight against brass markers driven into the cliff-face. When the heat is great and the steel grows slack, he turns a cast-iron wheel that operates a release-chute, dropping twelve pounds of lead shot into the lower world with a sound like a single drop of rain. When the night-drafts chill the cables and they threaten to snap under their own shrinking tension, he pulls a lever that engages the ratchets of the basalt flywheels, lifting the slack before the iron can part.

He lives on lard-biscuits, dry pemmican made of carillon-heart, and his two canisters of grey water per week. He sits in his iron basket between the sky and the lower crust, listening to the iron hum beneath his chair. Below him, forty miles down, the floor of the world is purple and brown, crossed by ancient salt-faults that look like the cracks in old glaze. He does not think of what is down there; he knows that down there the air is heavy and cold, thick with poisonous mists that would rot a man's lungs with wetness in an hour. 

He prefers the kiln. He likes the smell of his own knuckle-skin when the sun hits it—the warm, sweet grease of it, like an old harness hung in a forge. He takes his rib-bone scraper and runs it down his calf, collects the white flake of skin, and taps it into the wind. It does not fall; the updraft from the great cliff catches it immediately, carries it up into the yellow light, and an amber kite, drifting twelve yards above his chimney, swerves slightly on its cartilage spars to receive it.
