The creatures who shape the bone-pale city are neither entirely insect nor spider, though they share the hard, spare geometries of both. 

At the surface, in the blinding white wash of midday, the most common inhabitants are the *litho-weavers*—the blind, eight-legged laborers whose sheer numbers blur into a single, crawling tide across the fluted parapets. Up close, a weaver is the length of a man’s forearm, yet weighs astonishingly little. Its exoskeleton is not glossy chitin, but an unpolished, porous composite of calcite and dried protein, identical in tone and texture to the stone beneath its tarsi. Their front pair of limbs does not touch the ground for locomotion; instead, they have flattened into broad, spatula-like scraping tools, serrated along the outer rim. 

They have no eyes. Where a face might be, there is only a cluster of sensitive, quill-like antennae that vibrate in harmony with the city's thermal wind, and a recessed, triple-hinged mouthpart. Deep within their thorax lies a churning, muscular gizzard where dry chalk-grit, masticated shed skin, and the waxy resins gathered from the creepers are blended with a fast-curing, alkaloid saliva. When a weaver patches a wall, it does not build as an artist builds; it regurgitates a thick, foaming paste and trowels it into micro-fractures with rhythmic, shivering strokes of its forelegs. The paste sets in seconds under the sun, hissing faintly as it sheds its heat, crystallizing into seamless parity with the underlying rock. 

Moving alongside them are the smaller castes—creatures no larger than coins, whose legs are like needle-thin bone pins. These are the smoothers. They follow behind the trowelers by the ten-thousand, vibrating their abdomens against the setting slurry at ultrasonic frequencies, compacting the mineral matrix before it sets so that no air pockets remain to give purchase to the desert wind. 

Among these endless swarms of pale legs move the *cart-lizards*—the soft-scaled, heavy-bodied reptiles that serve as the beasts of burden across the elevated viaducts. 

These beasts have the mass of draught oxen, but their profiles are low and broad, like river stones smoothed by water that never came. Their skin is not plated with hard scutes, but covered in millions of microscopic, velvet-soft scales the color of dry salt, which trap a layer of still, dead air against their flanks to insulate their organs from the sun. Their feet are vast, round pads, soft as unbaked dough, which splay outward over the masonry to distribute their multi-ton weight without chipping the crystalline parapets. 

The cart-lizards are nearly inert in the cool before dawn, their heartbeats dropping to three or four per minute. But as the chalk towers bake, their black blood warms, and they begin their slow, unstoppable hauls. They are not harnessed. They are guided by columns of biting midges that live in their ears and along the creases of their heavy dewlaps. By swarming or resting against specific nerve clusters, these commensal insects direct the lizards along ancient, pheromone-greased routes, towing sledge-runners loaded with harvested sap-blocks or boulders of dense, mineralized limestone hewn from the under-city. The lizards move in a trancelike state, their flat, leathery muzzles scraping the stone just inches above the ground, their dry tongues occasionally flicking out to taste the dust for salt.

In the vertical canyons where the euphorbias and waxy creepers cling, life takes to the air. 

These are not birds—feathers would shred against the razor-sharp spires, and the energy required for sustained warm-blooded flight is impossible in this desiccated economy. The fliers are *felt-moths*, creatures with wingspans the size of shields, covered in a dense, mossy pile that absorbs sound completely. They drop from high roosts within the shaded chimney-flutes, catching the rising thermal walls that boil off the southern facades. They do not flap so much as steer, tilting their broad, crescent-shaped wings to bank through the narrow alleys without a whisper. 

The felt-moths are the city’s hydrators. Their abdomens are swollen, translucent bulbs filled with heavy, bitter plant lipids gathered from the spindle-trees. They possess long, horn-tipped siphons capable of puncturing the rubbery stems of the euphorbias. When a brood-comb in the lower canyons reaches a critical temperature, hundreds of these moths settle upon its perforated surface, weeping drops of cool, emulsified grease from their abdominal pores directly into the cells. This grease seals the dormant larvae from evaporation, coating them in a fragrant, golden rind until they are ready to emerge.

Below the sunlight, down where the spires plunge into the original salt-flat and the air becomes cold, powdery, and mineral-heavy, the ecology turns inward and ravenous. 

Here dwell the *grinders*. They are blind, multi-segmented myriapods the thickness of tree trunks, armored in thick, ivory rings. They have no venom and no need for stealth; they are the living jaws of the metropolis. When a section of the city becomes old, clogged with dried secretions, or structurally unsound due to shifts in the subterranean strata, the grinders are drawn to it by the sound of its stress-strains. They eat the city itself. Their jaws are solid plates of biological quartz that rotate in opposite directions, pulverizing dried calcium, ancient silk, shed reptile scales, and brittle root systems into a fine, flour-like meal. 

Behind them, they leave tunnels bored with absolute, circular perfection, their walls glazed smooth by a friction-melted slime that hardens into glass. The weavers follow directly behind the grinders, laying down fresh cross-braces and arches inside the freshly bored channels, transforming old destruction immediately into new foundation.

Nothing in this place dies into soft putrefaction. When an ox-lizard finally halts upon a ramp, its heart stopping after a century of hauling, there is no smell of carrion. 

Within minutes, the papery murmur of the city shifts pitch. Small, flat-bodied scavengers with carapaces like overlapping roof tiles pour from the crevices in the masonry. They do not tear flesh; they desiccate it. They coat the carcass in a caustic, chalky dust stored in their crops, which draws every drop of bound moisture to the surface where it is instantly drunk by specialized mouthparts. Within three days, the great beast is reduced to dry, odorless fibers and salt crystals. The fibers are chewed into felt for the moth-nurseries; the bones are broken down by the grinders; the scales are incorporated into the plaster of the nearest tower. 

There is no ruling brain, no queen in a central chamber, no blueprint buried in the chalk. The city’s terrifying coherence is purely acoustic and chemical. 

The spires themselves act as a vast, non-thinking nervous system. When the hot midday winds blow across the plain, the perforated domes and fluted parapets act like flutes and sirens, emitting infrasonic vibrations that travel down through the stone foundations. A high, dry whistle in a western tower automatically triggers the weavers in that sector to thicken its leeward buttresses; a hollow, rumbling hum in the floor of an alleyway calls the cart-lizards to bring more weight to stabilize the base. 

The metropolis is a single, slow-breathing crystal whose cells happen to have legs, mandibles, and leathery lungs, endlessly reproducing their own fossilized home across the dry white floor of the world.
