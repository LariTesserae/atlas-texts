The life here does not begin with flesh, but with the habit of stone to accept skin.

Where the vertical laths stand closest—an inch apart, or half an inch, where no light reaches the base and the condensation gathers in long, shivering seams—there are things that are neither worms nor lichens, but ribbons of gristle that have taken the exact cast of the joints. They are called by no name because no one speaks here, but they behave like living shims. They have no faces, no segments, only a pale, tallow-like density that sweats out an acid so dilute it takes decades to etch a grip into the slate. They hang suspended between the blades by lateral pressure alone, their sides pressed hard against the ironstone, fattened by the lime-wash that runs over their upper edges. When a drop falls ten feet and strikes the top of a blade, the shock passes downward through the rock and into these ribbons, which do not flinch; they feed on the vibration, their internal tissues arranged in tiny, parallel fat-folds that liquefy under percussion and set solid during the brief pauses between impacts.

Higher up, where the blades are dry at the crests but greasy six inches down, there are small, pale things shaped like the blades themselves. They have the thickness of a fingernail and the length of a butcher’s knife, clinging flat against the shale faces with suction discs made of hardened mucus. They are so thin that when the gray light strikes a stone sideways, their shadows are no wider than a pencil line. They do not crawl. They shift their mass by allowing the sheet of cold water behind them to slide them downward an eighth of an inch at a time, or they dry slightly at the margin, curling away from the rock like peeling paint until a gust of acoustic air catches them and flips them onto the neighboring slab. Their diet is the grit suspended in the fog and the dry skin-cells shed by whatever climbs above.

And something does climb, though it does not use feet.

In the upper thirds of the forest, where the stone is notched and broken by ancient frost-shatter, there are creatures whose bodies have been broken and mended so many times by the stone that their joints are like walnuts of scar-tissue. They are roughly the size of sheep, but flattened laterally until they are no broader through the shoulders than a man’s hand held sideways. Their ribs do not encircle the heart; they are vertical slats of heavy, dense marrow-bone that slide over one another like the leaves of an iron spring, allowing the animals to flatten themselves to the thickness of three fingers to pass through the jams.

They have no hair. Their hide is an oily, rubbery hide like the belly of a sturgeon, continuously grazed and scored by the knife-edges until it has formed an external rind of pale, keloid cart-grease. They do not have eyes; their skulls taper to a hard, chisel-shaped horn of compressed hair and lime that they use to lever themselves between the slabs. They move by bracing: two legs set against the left slab, two set against the right, walking the gap as a chimney-sweep walks a flue. 

Their ears are strange. Because the air is an engine of metallic clatter that would tear a delicate tympanum to rags in an hour, they have no external openings. Instead, their hearing is hydraulic. Thick, fluid-filled sacs hang beneath their lower jaws like double dewlaps, dragging along the wet stone or swinging free in the gaps. These bladders pick up the ringing of the ironstone directly from the rock-faces. They know where an alley is blocked three hundred yards away by the drop in pitch; they know when an upright is cracked through its foot because the stone sounds "fat," like wet wood, rather than "clear," like an anvil.

They feed on the shield-backed crawlers in the floor-gutters, but they do not descend into the black water if they can avoid it. Instead, they drop stones.

It is a slow, mechanical hunting. A flank-walker will find an upright that has been weathered loose at its root by the red iron-rot. Working with its horned snout and the wedge of its shoulders, it will rock the stone—sometimes for days, a quarter-inch this way, a quarter-inch that—until the resonant frequency matches the drumming of the water from above. When the drop-rate and the rock’s natural swing align, the slab shears off with a sound like a ship’s plate snapping. The fall is short—everything is wedged too tight for a long tumble—but the weight drives downward, crushing the shield-crawlers in their troughs beneath five tons of dropped slate. Then the flank-walkers go down into the red-frothed muck to work their narrow snouts into the cracks, licking out the pale, grease-rich pulp of the broken crawlers with tongues as long and coarse as rasp-files.

The makers of the causeways and the bone-wedges are not gone, but they are fewer than the stones, and they are not men. 

They are the ones who put the granite there. They are gray-skinned and long-armed, with fingers that have four joints each, terminating not in nails but in flat, spade-like studs of horn that can find purchase in a hair-line cleavage. They do not talk because their lungs are small and their throats are crowded with thick, fibrous cartilages that exist only to damp the vibrations of their own ribcages; if they opened their mouths to shout, the harmonic violence of the basin would set their teeth ringing until the enamel cracked. 

They communicate by wedge-work.

A family group consists of three or four, rarely more, living in the "hollows"—places where several slabs have fallen against one another like a collapsed house of cards, creating a three-cornered tent of stone six feet high, lined with dried biofilm that has been peeled from the troughs and cured into a tough, sulfur-smelling leather. They do not hunt with weapons; they do not have the room to swing an arm or cast a line. 

Instead, they are quarrymen of the silence.

Their entire culture, if it can be called that, is an effort to quiet small pockets of the plain. They spend generations driving single wedges of fossil bone into specific uprights, not to fell them, but to change their note. They know that a blade that rings at a certain high chime will bring down sheets of cold lime-water that rot their bedding-hides; a blade that hums low and wet will shelter the shield-things they gather for grease. They work foreign boulders—heavy, rounded lumps of pinkish granite dragged from the basin’s rim miles away—down into the slots with lever-poles made of the rib-bones of their dead, wedging them with immense labor until the ironstone groans and the screech of the upright stone is choked down to a dry clatter.

Where they have succeeded, there are small "deaf spots"—clearings no larger than a horse-trough where the percussive roar drops to a tolerable, heavy thrum. In these pockets, they sit together for weeks without moving, their bellies pressed against the granite stones they have dragged in. The granite does not ring; it is cold and dense, a dead weight in the teeth of the slate. They lean their foreheads against it to rest their brains from the iron vibration.

Their skin is bleached the color of lard, and their eyes are milky, covered by thick, semi-opaque nictitating membranes that they never retract; they see only the distinction between the pale fog above and the black water below. They have no fire. They do not know what smoke is. When they die, their fellows do not bury them—there is no earth to dig, only the drowned slots—so they stand the corpse upright in a narrow crack where the iron-water runs fastest. 

Within five years, the corpse is no longer flesh. The water enters the pores, replacing the soft marrow with lime, then with iron, until the body is a black, greasy lath among the other laths, perfectly vertical, shoulder to shoulder with the stone, its chin lifted toward the blind sky, helping to muffle the sound of the water that made it.
