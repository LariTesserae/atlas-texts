At first, it seems impossible that anything could share this silence without disturbing it. To live here, an organism would have to produce no refuse, cast no frantic motion against the pale vapor, and leave no tooth-marks on the thick, glossy foliage. 

And so, what lives here does not live in the manner of beasts.

The inhabitants are there, but they are easily mistaken for the architecture itself, or for the long, low slabs left resting in the meadows between one sector and the next. They are people—if that word can be stretched to contain beings whose pulse is slower than the drift of the mountain’s weeping water—but they are people cast in the physical register of the basalt.

They are broad, flat-bodied, and heavy, rarely rising much taller than a man’s chest, but twice as wide across the shoulders and hips. Their posture is low and horizontal, their joints thick and smooth, moving with the slow, terrifying torque of hydraulic stone-presses. They have hands—wide, square, spade-like tools of flesh, devoid of distinct fingernails, tipped instead with broad, horn-hard pads that have been polished glassy by centuries of grinding against wet grit. 

Their skin is not furred, scaled, or feathered. It is entirely naked, thick as sole-leather, and the color of river slate that has dried halfway in the sun: a muted, greenish charcoal that deepens to absolute black wherever the film of water washes over them. It possesses no sweat glands, for they do not overheat; their internal fires burn with the cold, steady efficiency of slow chemical accretion. Water does not bead on them. It spreads out across their broad backs and shoulders in the same unbroken, microscopic sheets that coat the basalt pavements, so that when one lies motionless in the shallows of a ceremonial avenue, the surface of its body is indistinguishable from the drenched flagstones.

They do not breathe through nostrils or mouths with the ragged, panting bellows of lowland creatures. Across their flanks, beneath the shelf of their heavy ribs, run horizontal slits lined with pale, moist folds of tissue—gills that drink the thick, warm vapor of the high air, extracting water and dissolved minerals directly from the atmosphere. Because they do not need to inhale or exhale violently, their chests never heave. They stand or kneel in the sunken canals for days without the slightest tremor of respiration.

They are the ones who built this place, and they are the ones who keep it unruined. 

Their work has no end, not because it falls apart, but because the work is simply their state of being. You will see one crouched at the base of a cyclopean retaining wall, holding a heavy, egg-shaped nodule of diorite in its two hands. It does not swing the stone with sudden fury; it lifts it four inches, drops it, lifts it, drops it, with a steady, metronomic cadence that might continue for forty years on the same single block of basalt. It is not carving an image or dressing an edge to a line; it is abrading the face, grain by grain, until the rock matches the microscopic curvature of the water that flows down it, ensuring that the film of moisture never gathers into a turbulent droplet, but remains forever a silent, glassy skin.

Because sound carries here with such flat, unsoftened clarity, they do not speak. They have no vocal cords, nor any ears that can be seen from without. Instead, they sense the world through the soles of their feet and the broad, calloused planes of their palms laid flat against the bed-rock. 

If one of them, three kilometers away across the rift bench, shifts a three-ton lintel into place with a wooden lever, the deep, shuddering *thrum* travels through the unbroken basalt shelf. Every inhabitant on the terrace feels it instantly in the marrow of their bones—a low, tonal geography of their city’s balance. They know where the masonry is settling, where the moisture has thickened, and where a canal bed has worn smooth, entirely through the vibrations transmitted by the stone.

They do not hunt, nor do they cultivate crops in the furrowed, violent manner of men. In the courtyards where the leathery ferns grow in tight, geometric rosettes, an inhabitant will occasionally approach a mature frond. It does not tear or bite the leaf. It takes the thick, fleshy base of the stem between its broad palms and presses, exerting a slow, crushing pressure until a dense, pale, gelatinous sap oozes from the pores of the plant. The being licks this weeping liquid away with a flat, muscular tongue that leaves the skin of the plant entirely unbroken. The fern does not wither; it simply exudes its excess moisture and continues to grow, glossy and intact.

They have no houses because they have no need to keep anything out. The pavilions are roofless because the sky brings no harm—only the constant, warm daylight and the ceiling of vapor that keeps their skin slick. When they rest, they do not sleep as animals sleep, closing their eyes against the dark; they simply cease their abrasion of the stone. 

They lower themselves into the shallow gutters where the water runs clearest, resting their heavy bellies and chins against the flagstones, their broad limbs trailing downstream. There they remain for seasons at a time, entirely submerged except for the slight ridge of their spines, their gills drinking the current, their minds settled into a wide, neutral attention that tracks the slow wearing of the plateau.

They are few, but because they do not die of cold, rot, or famine, their numbers are unchanging. A meadow between two urban sectors may sit empty for three centuries, until one day, two of them rise from a drainage ditch, lift a balk of dark timber on their shoulders, and begin to roll a freshly split prismatic column toward an avenue that has not yet been finished. 

They move with a vast, terrifying gentleness. When they walk across the saturated turf, their broad feet leave deep depressions in the club moss, but the moss is so resilient, and their tread so steady, that the green pile simply springs back after the water has settled, leaving no path, no trail, and no sign that anything has passed except the quiet, continuous murmur of the water finding its way along the stones they laid down yesterday, or ten thousand years ago.
