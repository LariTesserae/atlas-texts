In the drill-flutes along the lower rock walls, where the cylindrical stone has been bored clean and left open to the damp, the toads sit vertically. 

They are small, rib-sprung slate toads, grey as wet asphalt, with skin like boiled leather. They wedge their rumps into the round groove of the bore and hold themselves three or four feet above the concrete culvert by pressing the pads of their splayed toes against the curved stone. When the diesel in the shed turns over—that heavy, jaw-rattling *thung-clack*—the rock transmits the shudder straight through their bellies. They do not drop. They close the thin, golden rings of their eyelids against the concussion and let the stone shake them, their throats pulsing with an identical, slow register. They live off the stone-midges that breed in the weeping seams, and because the wall is so vertical, they never walk; they shift up and down the grooves in three-inch hitches, like lineworkers climbing telegraph poles.

Above them, the swallows have made something wholly industrial out of nest-building. Under the iron web of the high gantry, their mud-jugs are not grey river-clay, but a stiff, dark mastic compounded from crushed coal-dust, lime-wash flakes, and the grease-black grit thrown off by the drive belts. The nests are glued directly into the ninety-degree corners where the web of the steel joins the flange. In the nests, the chicks sleep with the metal singing beneath their ribs: three-phase alternating current vibrating through the ironwork with a steady sixty-cycle purr that keeps the floor of the nursery permanently blood-warm. When the young fledge, they do not swoop into broad country arcs. They drop straight into the trench, drop sixty feet like lead sinkers, then snap their wings out flat and shoot along the sheer rock face, their wingtips whispering an inch from the slate flutes. They fly down the railway cuts as if the stone were air-ducts, banking hard against the corners of the compressor houses without shedding an ounce of speed.

The snails on the dynamo cowlings are third- or fourth-generation mechanics. Their shells are not brittle like garden snails; the lime in the concrete dust, blown continuously into the air by the exhaust fans, has thickened their whorls until they are chalk-heavy, marbled like fossil stone. They form pale, overlapping rosettes on the cast-iron casing where the metal stays at the temperature of a fresh cup of tea. They do not eat leaves. They graze down the mist of aerosolized cylinder-oil, fine engine soot, and the microscopic green scum that seeds itself in the grease rings. Where they feed, they leave behind broad, winding tracks of clean, naked iron that gleam like newly minted pence against the grime. 

Beneath the duckboards and along the cable trays, the weasels have given up the brown coat of their hedgerow cousins. The air down here has no mud, only shale-grit and oil, and the weasels are thin, greasy-backed animals with coats the color of lead pipe. They hunt the conduit tracks in total darkness, running their paws along the cool PVC-sheathed cables with an engineer’s familiarity. They know the dead-end junction boxes from the through-runs; they know that if they chase a vole into the belly of an idling turbine, the beast will foul in the copper windings and be crushed when the rotor takes its four-minute kick. They wait at the exhaust vents for the vole to be driven out by the stink of hot varnish, their eyes glowing like wet quartz in the low grey light.

And then there is the tender. 

He lives in the upper room of the brick pump house, behind the round-arched windows where the iron mullions divide the sky into narrow, vertical slabs. He has been in the cut long enough that the immense noise has ceased to be an obstacle; it has become his weather, the atmospheric pressure against which his inner ear maintains an equal, outward lean. If the stationary diesel were to miss a stroke, the silence would bring him out of his bunk faster than a shot.

He wears boots with three-quarter-inch soles of dense white rubber, grooved across the ball so he can grip the wet shale without rattling a single pebble. In his pocket he carries a brass wire brush, a horn-handled oiler with a long, needle-pointed snout, and three clean cotton waste-cloths. His skin carries the yellow tint of high-pressure sodium and the pale, pickled scrub of cold vapor, but his chest is wide and his breathing is completely flat. 

He descends the iron exterior stairs twice every eight hours, down to the level of the ballast. His work is neither urgent nor heavy; it consists entirely of arbitration between the stone, the steel, and the green things. 

When the bindweed creeps toward the turning-pad of the south counterweight, he does not poison it. He kneels down on the ballast, takes out his pocket-knife, and neatly trims the tendrils back three inches from the tooth-race, winding the severed green wires into a loose hank and tucking them under a sleeper where the voles will find them. He clears the drainage culverts of the heavy green ground-beetles, lifting them by their broad, lacquered flanks—they click against his thumbnail with the weight of small crabs—and setting them up on the dry brick footings of the substation where the grease-water cannot douse their breathing holes.

He knows the dynamos by their temperatures. He will lay his bare palm against the dynamo casing, just behind the ring of snails, letting his skin tell him if the bearing has begun to pull toward the left. He does not touch the snails. He lets them work their rings around the bolts, pleased with the clean, scored circles they leave behind.

At midday, when the sun is directly above the slot of the trench and the shadows vanish entirely from the quarry floor, the tender sits on an upturned grease firkin between the rails, eating cold ham and mustard between two thick rounds of crust. Around him, the basin works its giant, unhurried clockwork. The four-minute arm rises, pauses with an oily sigh of pneumatic valves, and drops its balance-weight with a dull, subterranean *whump* that makes the water in his enamel tea-mug ring in neat, concentric ripples. 

The swallows drop past his forehead, snapping midges out of the exhaust mist so close he can feel the draft of their primary feathers against his temple. A weasel slips out of an inspection hatch four feet from his boot, stops, stands on its haunches with its oil-stiffened paws tucked against its ribs, and looks him directly in the face. The tender does not toss it scrap; the weasel does not ask for it. They share the same mineral smell, the same cold draft from the rock-face, the same three-phase thrum vibrating through their skeletons. After ten seconds, the iron counterweight begins its slow, four-minute ascent, and the weasel flows back into the lead-lined trough as silently as poured mercury, leaving the tender alone with the swallows and the great iron wheels turning their slow, unwearied circles against the stone.
