The first life to reckon with is not human, nor is it strictly animal, but it possesses an appetite that governs the walls.

Beneath the tarred hemp lagging, where the high-pressure mains bleed their constant, lukewarm sweat, lives a pale, unsegmented fauna that has no names in surface manuals. There are flat, milk-white planarians that do not swim, but glide across the slick belly of the iron, feeding on the gray velvet of bacterial slime that carpets every seam. They are blind, flat as knife blades, and their bodies are so thin that the dark, colloidal silt they ingest can be seen digesting within them, branch by branch, through translucent skin. They drift along the thermal gradient, never straying more than three inches from the hottest rivets. If an arm of lagging falls away and exposes them to the dry draft from above, they do not shrivel; they simply liquify, turning into a clear, oily smear that the rust drinks within an hour.

In the armpits of the iron framing, where dead leaves from the great bronze aroids drop and rot without ever truly drying, there are colonies of false-scorpions no larger than flax seeds. They have shed their pigment entirely. In the dull orange glow of the caged maritime bulbs, they look like droplets of paraffin wax given claws. They live out their years within the space of three bricks, preying upon the minute, wingless springtails that graze the fungal blooms on the mortar. They move with an unnatural, hydraulic smoothness, never hurrying, because there are no birds here, no hunting spiders, no drafts strong enough to dislodge them. Nothing falls from these walls that does not wish to fall.

And behind the vitrified brick—inside the stacked tenements that recede into the cut limestone—are the people who built the tags and hung the hemp.

They are not a colony of ghosts, though they share the room’s disinclination to make a sound. When they come out onto the red-lead walkways, they do so with a gait that belongs entirely to the Sump: a low, heavy-heeled tread, their bare feet flat against the wet, checkered plate of the catwalks. The soles of their feet are remarkable—yellowed, half an inch thick, and cured by contact with warm scale and vegetable tannin until they are as tough as boiled saddle-leather. They do not wear boots; iron against iron makes a sharp, ringing report that sets the nerves on edge in a space where all other sound is deadened, and boot-leather rots into green jelly here within two weeks.

They are broad-ribbed people. Because the air is heavy with sulfur, damp lime, and the exhausted breath of the boilers, their chests have a pronounced, barrel-like arch, giving even the children a look of ancient, stoic thickness. Their collarbones sit high, and their necks are short and corded, built to carry the lungs through air that has the consistency of warm broth. 

Their skin does not tan, but neither does it have the papery whiteness of cellar-bred things. It has the tint of lard that has been kept in a dark pantry near an oil lamp—a pale, tallowy amber, slicked always with a film that is not entirely sweat. It is a protective mantle of natural grease and condensed vegetable oils that they do not wash away; to scrub oneself raw with lye in this humidity is to invite the red mold that eats the mortar to settle in the pores of the groin and armpits.

They emerge from the deep rooms in the quietest hours—though "hour" has little meaning where the gray slit of grating sixty feet above never brightens beyond the color of a wet slate. They emerge when the thoracic hum of the subterranean engines drops half an octave, signaling that the surface factories have banked their fires for the night shift.

A man will come out through a low-lintelled door, carrying an iron scraping-chisel and a small, square-sided tin of rendered fat. He does not stretch or yawn; the atmosphere does not permit the luxury of deep, gasping inhalations. He moves with a deliberate, conservationist economy, his shoulders rounded against the heavy drops that fall intermittently from the overhead cables. 

His relationship with the vegetation is neither horticultural nor adversarial; it is closer to the work of a harbor master tending a slipway. The vines are essential. Their dense, tuberous growth binds the loose masonry against the limestone wall, their roots drinking up the dangerous hydrostatic pressure that would otherwise blow the mortar out of the lower courses. But they are also relentless. If left alone for six weeks, a single creeper will work its waxy tip into the packing-gland of a gate valve and split the brass casing with the hydraulic force of its sap.

The man works with the small chisel, paring away the corky, blistered collars where the vines have bitten too deeply into the lagging of the steam mains. His hands are stained a permanent, deep walnut-brown from the bitter milk of the aroid stems. He works without gloves, his fingers broad and blunt, their nails worn down to the quick by years of clearing wet grit from thread-races. When he cuts a vine, the severed stump does not bleed water; it weeps a thick, clear gelatin that smells intensely of bruised watercress and machine oil. He catches this sap in his tin of fat—it is what they use to grease the hinge-pins of their shutters and the slides of their coal-scuttles, a lubricant that neither turns rancid nor washes away in the steam.

Inside the rooms behind him, life is lived in the cool, stable breath of the rock. The limestone keeps a steady temperature of fifty-eight degrees, twelve feet back from the retaining wall, and so the tenements are carved deep. They are narrow, barrel-vaulted tunnels rather than chambers, lined with the same dark vitrified brick to keep the rock-sweat from dripping onto the bedding. 

In these recesses, the silence is total. The people speak in low, level monotones, using only their throats and chests, rarely allowing their breath to hit the teeth or lips; consonants are swallowed or smoothed down until their dialect sounds like the tumbling of smooth river stones in a wooden trough. To raise the voice here is considered an obscenity, an act of unmeasured violence against the close, damp equilibrium of the court. 

Their domestic life is shaped entirely by the avoidance of rot. Their bedding is made from the beaten, fibrous inner bark of the great creepers, boiled in brine and dried on the warm pipes until it forms a stiff, springy mattress that neither hosts lice nor absorbs water. Their children are quiet, serious, and extraordinarily sure-footed; by four years old, they can run along an eight-inch catwalk rail forty feet above the floor without looking down, their prehensile toes finding the gaps between rivet-heads with an instinct like that of the blind crickets.

They have few possessions that are not made of iron, stoneware, or lead. Paper cannot survive here; an ungreased book becomes a spongy mass of felt in three months. Their records—the dates of child-bearings, the tallies of steam-taps, the deaths from the "White Lung" that comes when the boiler tubes burst—are scratched with an awl directly into the soft lead flashing that lines their door-frames. 

They do not look up at the grates. If an iron cart rattles over the street sixty feet above, showering down a fine, glittering spray of horse-grit and dried dung, they do not flinch or tilt their chins. That upper world is simply a roof that leaks grit instead of leaves, an unpredictable weather system of iron tires and draft-horses that has nothing to do with the heavy, eternal engine-stroke that keeps their floor warm.

Toward the bottom of the Sump, where the retaining walls meet the bedrock and the black water from the weeping pipes gathers into the lead gutters, an old woman sits on a low stool of cast scrap. She is sorting dried roots into wicker baskets. Her fingers are crippled with the thick, knotty nodes of gout—the common ailment of those who live on well-slurry and salted pork—yet she works with the unhurried precision of an instrument. 

She does not look at the light, which is failing as the filament in the maritime lamp across from her begins to sputter, its carbon loop thinning toward its end. She knows the shape of every valve, the sound of every joint weeping, the precise interval between the falling of the water-drops from the central telegraph bracket. When the bulb finally dies with a small, sharp *tink*, she does not pause. The dark here is not an absence; it is merely another surface to be worked, thick and wet and familiar as wool.
