At the floor of the kerf, down where the iron lag bolts bite into the heart-ring, life begins as friction.

Because there is no moisture to harbor bacteria, there are no films, no slimes, no soft decays. The smallest inhabitants are dry-rot analogues that are not fungal at all, but sub-arthropods whose bodies are largely composed of pressed stearates and silicate flakes. The *planer-lice* are no larger than a grain of ground pepper; they do not eat the wood so much as bevel it. Millions of them crowd the expansion joints, vibrating at the frequency of the overhead drive-shafts, their tiny, diamond-hard mandibles planing the edges of the floor-planks to keep them flush. They leave behind trails of talc-fine meal that never turns sour. When a man steps on a drift of them, there is no wet pop, only the dry, powdery crunch of fine chalk breaking under heel.

Higher up, where the trunks shear and form those half-mile fences of standing, glass-sharp splinters, the *harness-moths* nest. They have no wings—in an atmosphere pumped by rhythmic bellows-gusts, broad wings are merely sails that drag a creature into the industrial linkages. Instead, their abdomens trail four or six ribbons of spun cellulose, tougher than binder twine and dry as mummy-cloth. They anchor these ribbons to the splinter-points and hang suspended in the cool halide glare, their parchment-textured bodies swaying like dry husks in the twenty-minute gale. They feed on the aerosol esters venting from the wood's tears, their mouthparts functioning as fractional condensation plates: as the draft cools across their copper-colored antennae, heavier pinene droplets coalesce, run down microscopic grooves in their horn-plated faces, and are sucked into a crop that stores fuel rather than water. A harness-moth torn open spills nothing that could stain a clean cuff—only a spoonful of clear, aromatic oil that evaporates in seconds, leaving behind a pale ring of rosin.

The larger predators—the iron-skinned coursers mentioned in the logbooks—do not build dens. In an unyielding world where you cannot dig into earth, shelter is entirely architectural. They sleep wedged into the cold clearance beneath the elevated rail dowels, their ribs hinged so flatly that a beast six feet in length can compress itself into an aperture no wider than a man’s hand. 

Up close, the coursers are less like reptiles and more like clockwork engines whose springs are made of dry sinew. Their blood is an amber, high-viscosity silicone that barely circulates when they are at rest; their body temperature sits precisely at the cellar-chill of the floor. When they hunt the sugar-weevils, they do not pant. They have no tongues. They kill by percussion: their forelimbs terminate in swollen, hammer-shaped spurs of dense horn that shatter the weevils’ vitreous carapaces with the sound of a pistol shot against timber. The coursers do not lap at their prey; they scrape the dry, crystalline fats out of the weevil’s hollow segments with spoon-like palps, leaving behind clean, empty shells that look like lacquered crates abandoned on the loading docks.

Then there are the people—or what has persisted under the name.

They are tall, their limbs long and narrow, their movements shaped by the acoustic economy of the clearance. Because the dry heartwood swallows voices at twenty paces, they do not waste breath shouting across the empty squares. They have developed a complex, rapid vocabulary of foot-taps—percussive heels struck against the resonant grain of the structural ties—that sends low-frequency signals through the floor for half a mile. A worker standing on a freight platform feels the approach of a maintenance squad as a series of rhythmic thrums in his arches long before he sees their gray smocks emerge from the pale haze.

Their bodies have undergone a quiet, desperate chemical realignment. True water is an imported sacrament or an industrially cracked byproduct, kept strictly inside closed loops, never allowed to escape into the desiccating air. To sweat here would be suicide; the basin would drink a man dry through his shirt in an afternoon. Thus, their skin does not perspire. It has grown thick, dull-surfaced, and pale as tallow, continuously treated with heavy animal-grease and refined pine-pitch to seal the pores. Where their sleeves end, the skin does not crease into soft wrinkles; it folds in stiff, clean pleats, like oiled canvas.

Their faces behind the breather masks are hairless and narrow. The masks themselves are not merely filters; they are condenser traps. A complex arrangement of tin reed-valves sits under the chin, collecting the vapor of every exhalation, running it over cold copper plates to recover the moisture before it can reach the ambient nitrogen draft. To take off one's mask in the open square is considered an act of obscene waste, a violent bleeding-out of the body’s sealed interior into the thirsty timber plain.

In the settlements—those mortised, three-story barracks that sit bolted into the living cambium—they live in a state of suspended preservation. Their domestic life is entirely devoid of slops, sinks, or steam. Food is dry-pressed meal: wafers of compressed starch harvested from the inner bark of the great horizontal trunks, dipped into clarified oils that smell faintly of camphor. They do not cook with fire; a flame in this vast, continuous tinderbox is a horror beyond contemplation. Their tools are bronze, cold-forged iron, or compressed lignum vitae—metals and woods that will not spark if dropped against a rail or a lag bolt.

A child born here learns three things before it learns to read: the sound of a shear-fault beginning in the grain miles away (a sharp, musical *ping* like a snapped piano wire); the twenty-minute pulse of the ceiling bracts, which requires one to lean thirty degrees into the dry wind to keep one's footing; and the discipline of the joints. In a place where no moisture eases the movement of things, knees and elbows must be watched. The settlers move with a deliberate, oiled cadence, their gait mechanical and low to the timber, their leather soles pegged with horn rather than iron so they glide across the planed ash without catching.

When one of them dies, there is no question of burial; the heartwood offers no spade-depth. Nor can there be cremation. 

Instead, they carry the body out past the sublimation refinery, to the margins where the floor splits into those white, glass-hard bristles of sheared tension. They strip the dead of their apron, their breather, and their grease-impregnated wools. They lay the body bare across the splinter-bristles. 

Within two cycles of the overhead bracts, the dry nitrogen wind has flayed every molecule of humidity from the flesh. Within a week, the skin has turned the exact color and texture of the surrounding ash timber—smooth, matte, and hard enough that if struck with a dowel, it would ring like seasoned boxwood. The harness-moths come and trail their binder-twine ribbons across the ribs; the planer-lice settle into the seams of the ears and the fingers, gently leveling the contours. In time, the dead do not decay; they are simply incorporated, planed down by the wind and the dust until they become part of the floor, another smooth, dry foot of heartwood beneath the boots of the shift-workers walking out to the freight-flats.
