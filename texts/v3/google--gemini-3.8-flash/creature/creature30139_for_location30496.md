If there is life where there is no breath, it cannot have ribs that rise and fall. Ribs that yield would be crushed by the broth of the air, and any throat that opened to pull in that scalding reek of copper and mint would blister down to the cartilage in a single hour. 

What lives here does not inhale. It drinks through the grain of its hide, soaking up the hot, mineral grease that sweats from the mud-brick and the underside of the kidney-leaves.

They are inside the tenements. In the lower rooms, where the orange lichen is so thick it covers the thresholds like deep, wet carpets of fleece, they sit in clusters of five or eight, wedged into the corners where the palm-worked walls meet the stone of the mountain. At first glance, beneath the flat, shadowless light that filters into the doorways, they appear to be part of the masonry—heaps of river silt and river gravel bound together with the same pale, gummy sap that holds the lintels above their heads. But they are meat. They are dense, solid, and violently heavy, with the specific gravity of drowned swine or waterlogged timber.

Their bodies are broad, low-slung wedges, lacking waists or necks, tapering from shoulders wide as millstones down to hips that rest flat against the damp earth. They have no fur, no scales, and no shell; their skin is a thick, rubbery rind, pale as scalded tripe, perpetually slicked with a thin, clear fluid that smells of crushed weeds. Across this skin run endless whorls, ridges, and concentric furrows—not wrinkles of age, but the permanent impressions of the human-sized palms that smoothed the city into shape. Whether they were pressed into this flesh when it was soft, or whether their hide naturally mimics the tamped walls they have leaned against for eras, cannot be known.

They have no ears. External ears would have been torn away or beaten to pulp by the falling mountain of water miles above. Instead, their hearing is skeletal. The sound of the cataract—that flat, teeth-loosening roar—enters them through their broad, flat feet and the thick pads of their palms, passing directly into a solid, unjointed wishbone of dense marrow that spans their chests. They do not listen to the noise; they are anchored by it. If the cataract were ever to stutter for a split second, their internal hydraulics would lose their counterweight, and their capillaries would rupture outward through their hide. The roar is their atmospheric pressure; it is the stone laid on top of their lid.

Their faces are blunted and unworked, like soap that has been used under hot water until its edges are gone. There are no nostrils, only a pair of narrow, vertical slits beneath the brow through which they exude excess brine, and eyes that are mere buttons of cloudy, horn-colored amber, lidless and unblinking beneath the vertical daylight. They do not turn their heads to see, because their heads do not turn; they perceive the world as a tactile vibration through the soles of their limbs, sensing the weight of the air, the density of the steam, and the slow, circular crawl of the gray water in the troughs.

They move, but their movement is so slow, so deliberate, that it shares more with the flow of cold pitch than the scamper of an animal. To cross an alleyway that widens as it recedes—to climb three steps that lead only back to the base of the stair—takes them days of steady, hydraulic shifting. They extend a broad, five-lobed limb, press it against the wet mud until the sap-glue bites, and draw the rest of their vast weight forward with a wet, sucking sound that is instantly swallowed by the roar.

When they reach the plazas, they assemble. They do not speak—they have no lungs with which to push a column of air, and against that cataract, any voice would be less than the snapping of a dry hair—but they communicate by displacement. Two of them will meet on a terrace of river-smoothed cobblestones and lean their wet flanks together, pressing skin to skin until their respective tallow-layers deform and lock. In that compression, through the shuddering of their fused breastbones, they trade the frequency of the rock. They know how many inches the basalt tableland has tilted toward the drowned lowlands; they know the exact thickness of the orange lichen on the south-facing aqueducts; they know the consistency of the mud thirty stories down where the foundations root into the mountain’s veins.

There is another thing that shares their courtyards, though it is difficult to say whether it is an animal, an organ, or an excretion of the city itself.

In the upper tiers, where space wraps tightly back upon its own corners, the circular water troughs are never empty. In the gray, seamless ring of water that turns without current, pale, tubular cords lie coiled like submerged cables. These cords are as thick as a man's thigh, formed of an elastic, veinous gristle that neither rots nor grows. Every few hours, one of the thick-skinned dwellers will step out onto the terrace, approach the trough, and reach a heavy, fingerless foot into the water. The cord responds not by swimming, but by tightening: it knots itself around the limb with an immense, slow constriction, drawing out the excess copper that has settled into the dweller’s tissue and returning it into the endless, looped flow of the basin. The water darkens for a moment to the color of dried liver, then clears again as the mineral is absorbed by the rock beneath.

There is no youth among them, and there is no rot. No one has ever seen one of them die, nor has any small, soft thing been seen crawling from beneath their flanks. When the violent thermals rise from the abyss, they do not hide; they merely flatten themselves against the tamped ramps, their pale backs taking the brunt of the heat until their skin dries to a glaze like baked bread, waiting for the ambient sweat of the stone to soften them once more.

They are not prisoners of the ledge. They could not exist anywhere else. Were they brought down into the thin, cold, empty winds of the lowlands, their flesh would slough from their bones for want of the sound’s crushing weight; their bellies would distend and split without the copper broth to push against them; their minds, attuned only to the eternal, violent completion of a world that cannot move, would shatter in the presence of an echo.

They remain where the earth forgot to finish its dying, pressed into the wet corners of their finger-smoothed halls, warm as fresh blood, heavy as graves, vibrating in unison with the three-mile drop of the water that will never wash them away.
