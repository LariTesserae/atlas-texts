To understand what lives in the basin, one must begin with the fat, because nothing here survives on heat it cannot carry in its own skin.

The goats that supply it are not pasture animals; there is no pasture. They live four hundred feet above the tower roofs, upon the raw steps of the western needle where the phonolite is so steep that even the black ferns cannot find purchase. The herdsmen call them *kites*, though they are four-legged, cloven-hoofed, and broad in the chest. What the townspeople call their “wings” are not feathered limbs, but enormous, double-hinged scapulae—shoulder blades that have lengthened over centuries into wide, flat shovels of thin bone, connected to the rib cage by thick webs of greasy, hairless skin. When a goat leaps across a forty-foot shear to reach a shelf of liverwort, it does not merely jump; it locks these high shoulders upward, creating a stiff, triangular cowl behind its horns that catches the cold chimney-drafts and lets it glide downward in a long, controlled fall, landing with a soft, dull *thud* of splayed horn-tips and rubbery hooves. 

Their meat is dark and stringy, smelling of swamp-moss, and the people rarely eat it. They keep the goats for the marrow and the pale grease that pools in the pockets beneath those wing-bones. A single full-grown ram yields three stone jars of lard that never rancidifies; it remains clear as glycerine in the cold, smelling only of crushed hemlock and wet slate.

Without this lard, the people would have died out in the first two generations after the mountain broke. 

The two or three hundred human inhabitants are small-boned and unusually short in the waist, their shoulders rounded forward as if they were perpetually shielding an ember between their collarbones. Their skin has the greyish, milk-washed cast of river stones, translucent enough at the temples to show the branching blue of their veins. They do not wash with water—water here does not dry, but sits upon the skin until it rots the wool against it—but rub themselves from crown to heel with warm goat fat once every seven days, scraping the rind of it away with flat knives carved from sheep-ribs. This leaves them water-tight, smelling faintly sweet and mutton-greasy, their clothes gliding silently over their limbs without the rustling friction that would disturb the air.

Their voices have withered down to a register that the swifts ignore. An elder of the basin speaks by pressing the flat of his tongue against his back teeth and squeezing air through his lips: a dry, rustling hiss that carries no farther than the width of a larch bridge. For long-distance speech, they rely on the stone itself. Because the phonolite is so dense and crystalline, a sharp tap with the knuckle of a copper ring against a corner-block will travel straight down through the masonry of one tower, across the bedrock floor, and up into the hearth-stone of a neighbor eighty yards away. A child lying on her pallet can hear her mother returning from the grease-cistern five towers over—the three light clicks of her sheep-gut soles on the lower steps ringing through the pillow like the tapping of a woodpecker inside a hollow log.

Inside the towers, life is vertical, stacked, and layered with vermin that have been turned into household furniture.

The ground floors are kept empty for the mountain draft; no one sleeps where the iron smell rises raw from the cracks. The living quarters occupy the middle floors, where the larch beams are thickest. Here, the walls are entirely concealed behind triple hangings of mountain-goat felt, pinned to the stone with copper skewers. Behind these hangings, in the dark four-inch pocket between the stone and the wool, live the blue-smoke dormice. 

They are blind, or nearly so, possessing only tiny, jet beads where their eyes should be, but their hearing is so acute they can track the passage of a slug across the outside of a phonolite block. They live entirely on the grease-crumbs dropped from the felt tables and the dried midges that drift in through the window-slits. Because they do not have tails to lose heat through, they are nearly spherical—warm, purring balls of grey-blue down, no larger than an apricot. In the evening, when the penumbra turns from slate to ink, the children reach behind the felt curtains and pull them out by the handful. The dormice do not bite; they tuck their small, hairless paws into their bellies and submit to being sewn into quilted linen pockets that line the inside of the people’s sleeping-sacks. A grown hunter will sleep with six of them arranged along the length of his spine and two beneath his feet. Throughout the night, as he shifts, the dormice shift with him, their tiny furnaces working at a steady hundred and four degrees, keeping the marrow in his shins from aching against the frost-breath of the floor.

Higher up, where the towers terminate in the flat roof-slabs, the world belongs to the white marmots.

They are not wild, nor are they domestic; they are an architectural necessity. The phonolite roof-stones, if left bare, would gather condensation until the weight of the water collapsed the rafters, or else the cold would strike downward through the stone ceilings like an iron wedge. The marmots prevent both. They lie three deep, packed into an interlocking pavement of living fur that covers every square foot of the slate. Their velvet coats are so dense that moisture cannot reach their skin; the droplets bead on their guard hairs and roll off into the stone gutters, channeled down into the narrow communal cisterns before it can pool. 

To the marmots, the town is simply a geothermal reef. They draw the faint, rising warmth of the towers through the floorboards, and in return, their triple-jointed limbs and heavy, lard-thick flanks form a living thatch three feet thick. They have forgotten how to dig; their claws have flattened into wide, horn chisels that fit perfectly into the stone joints. When a tower family wants to light a tallow wick, they do not climb to the roof; they reach up through a small trapdoor in the ceiling and gently push an iron skewer into the marmot-pack, feeling for the fat-pocket behind a shoulder blade. The marmot does not even startle; it merely gives a slow, oily shudder, and the skewer comes down dripping with clear grease ready for the hemp-cord.

Beneath this domestic layer, down where the air stays in the low forties and the light is never more than a greasy smudge, the scavengers keep the stone from choking on its own life.

Because there is no rot, a dead swift does not decay. If it falls from its chimney-niche, it strikes the flagstones and lies there, perfect and downy, its four grey eyes open to the mist. Within twenty minutes, the silver-haired woodlice arrive. They do not swarm chaotically; they march out from the vertical joints in strict, radial spokes, twelve or sixteen abreast, guided by the magnetic grain of the iron-heavy phonolite. They look like moving ribbons of mercury. They do not tear the bird; they dissolve it from underneath with an alkaline grease they secrete from their leg-joints, softening the bone until they can shear through it with their translucent mandibles. In three days, nothing remains of the swift but an outline of dull grey dust that the town’s children sweep up with their fern-brooms to mix with goat-tallow for caulking the larch bridges.

The pale, legless lizards are the only things that hunt with haste. They are six feet long in the oldest chimneys, blind and smooth as peeled elder-twigs, their belly scales modified into broad, suctioning shields that can grip phonolite even when it is sheeted with fern-oil. They have no teeth; they have hardened, horn-edged gums that shut with the flat, dead snap of a spring-trap. They live in the deep chimneys behind the towers, where the drafts are fastest, waiting for the wind-reversal.

When the reversal comes—that sudden, six-hour drop when the cold milk-fog pours over the rim—the entire basin holds its breath. It is the only time the townspeople stop moving. They sit on their stools with their knees pulled up into their down rolls, their hands over their mouths, watching the line of white vapor swallow their bridges level by level.

The swifts do not fly during the fog. They retreat into the spaces between the floor joists, wedging themselves four and five deep into the crevices, their forward eyes shut, their upward eyes wide and glassy, watching the timbers above them. The white marmots on the roofs stop their rhythmic claw-ticking; they flatten their skulls against the slate and let the fog bury them until the roof looks like a field of snow-drifted hummocks.

In this twenty-minute smother, the rift belongs entirely to the moisture.

The water does not fall; it emerges. It beads upon the copper spikes until the green verdigris looks like wet lichen; it coats the black ferns until their heavy fronds bow down and lay their tips against the bridge-timbers; it fills the eyes of the four-eyed swifts until they blink away tears that taste of cold iron. 

And then, as the last of the milk-fog settles into the floor-cracks and is sucked down into the boiling bowels of the mountain three miles below, the sound begins again: that vast, dry *tick-tick-tick* of ten thousand horn claws resetting their grip on the phonolite, the soft, fat-choked cough of a sleeping ram up on the scree, and the high, thin wheeze of a woman in the third tower singing three notes through her teeth to tell her daughter that the damp has passed, and that the hearth-grease may be stirred once more.
