They do not arrive from outside; the city sweats them out. 

Where the square root-blocks of the tenements weep their thickest amber, the resin does not merely run down into the gutters. It pools in the corners of doorways, gathers mass, and hardens into translucent, amber-jacketed pods the size of steamer trunks. When the pressure in the vascular curbs peaks—marked by three long, shuddering thumps of the subterranean bulbs—these pods split along neat, dry seams like chestnut husks.

What steps out belongs entirely to the logic of the street.

They are bipeds, mostly, because the stairs and the boardwalks demand two points of purchase, but their anatomy is an illiterate translation of the human form, pieced together from hearsay. Their limbs are peeled hickory, split and braided into dense, muscle-like ropes that squeak when flexed, wet with yellow resin that lubricates their joints instead of synovial fluid. They have no faces, only smooth, rounded finials of pale pith, like the newels of a staircase, yet they cock their heads toward one another as if listening for footsteps or the ringing of an absent shift-bell.

Over their wicker ribcages, their outer bark grows in stiff, overlapping plates that uncannily resemble the heavy woolen coats of dockers and mill hands—complete with thick, flared collars of shelf-fungus and rows of hard, circular root-knots running down their chests like horn buttons. They do not shed this rind; it is their dermis, worn against the cellar cold.

They move in squads of six or seven, their gait rhythmic and unhurried, their feet—flat, cleated blocks of lignum-vitae—clacking sharply against the cobblestone galls. They have no eyes to see the street, but their heels register every hydraulic tremor coursing through the floorboards. 

They appear to be on their way to work. 

They walk down the avenue in silence, turn without hesitation where the cobblestones curve upward at a right angle, and continue walking up the sheer vertical face of the wall. Their stride does not change; their coats do not fall backward toward the void. Near the vertical masonry, gravity is merely a local agreement between the wood and the stone. They climb toward the wet dark of the ceiling, fade into the steam-drizzle, and then—an hour later—descend again along an adjacent facade, having carried nothing, altered nothing, and touched nothing, their peeled-bark shoulders glistening with condensed moisture.

Inside the tenements, behind the flawless mica panes, a different caste keeps the domestic vigil. 

These are smaller, flatter things, pressed thin as pressed flowers, made of papery sheets of dried bast and birch-like velum. They do not leave the rooms with the pith floors. Instead, they crouch beside the miniature reed gearworks, their fingers—twelve to a hand, delicate as dried pine needles—working the spools. They do not weave garments; they weave long, uniform bandages of pure cellulose, winding them around their own ribs until they become thick, swollen spindles of white fiber. When one becomes too fat with silk to move, it wedges itself into the open window arch. There it remains, letting the updrafts catch the free ends of its thread, slowly unwinding it into the chasm, letting its own accumulated body drift out across the dark to bridge the gulfs between the viaducts. As it unfurls, it shrivels back to a parchment husk, waiting for the spindles to turn again.

Down on the narrow wooden rail tracks of the suspension bridge, there are the Listeners. 

They are taller than the street-walkers, ten or twelve feet high, balanced on stilted legs as thin and springy as dry osier rods. They possess no mimicry of clothing; they are naked poles of iron-wood, topped with immense, fan-shaped ears made of pleated hosta-leaf membranes that stretch four feet wide. They stand at the dead end of the rails, rooted by fine, hair-like suckers into the gaps between the ties, leaning out over the three-hundred-foot abyss where the slow, fern-scented draft rises.

They do not move for days. They are waiting for the train that the tracks imply. Because the tracks exist, the city knows a train must come; because no train has ever been grown, the Listeners must remain, tuned to the absolute lowest frequencies of the lianas. Sometimes, when a heavy drop of cold condensation falls from a mile up and strikes a cable with a musical *ping*, a Listener will tremble throughout its entire length. It will emit a soft, pressurized whistle through a reed-valve in its sternum—a clear, lonely note that mimics the steam-whistle of a locomotive five miles away in a fog—and the other Listeners down the line will take up the call, passing the phantom approach from throat to throat until it dies out in the hollows beneath the mills.

Deepest of all, inside the textile husk where the dynamos idle, live the Stokers.

They are the only things here that possess true bulk. They are shaped like massive, squat tubers, several tons of waterlogged cambium resting on rows of short, thick root-knees. Their hide is the coarse, furrowed cork of ancient oak, perpetually sweating a cold, alkaline brine. They have no heads, but along their crests run double rows of deep, rhythmic vents that open and close with a wet *clack-whuff*, sighing out clouds of the refrigerated steam that feeds the city’s weather.

The Stokers do not shovel; there is no coal. Instead, they embrace the conduit pipes. They wrap long, fleshy root-tendrils around the translucent veins running along the curb, squeezing them with rhythmic, peristaltic pulses to help the underground bulbs force the sap up into the flywheels. When the dynamos spin fast enough to warp the local space—causing water drops to orbit their polished drums—the Stokers press themselves tighter to the wood, shivering with a slow, narcotic pleasure, their cork skins groaning under the torque. 

They do not eat, nor do they hunt the pale mantises or the papery isopods that crawl across their hides. Everything here is sustained by the slow, pneumatic tide of sugar-water and cold starch circulating through the floorboards. 

There is no malice in any of them, nor is there curiosity. If you were to stand upon the damp boardwalk as the shift-walkers passed, they would not swerve to avoid you, nor would they strike you down. Their iron-wood heels would simply step onto your instep with the unyielding, crushing weight of a falling beam, their finial-heads tilted slightly upward toward the empty rafters, their fungal lapels stiff in the draft, carrying out the perfect, punctilious pantomime of a morning that has never broken, for an empire that never was, in an empty lung that never exhales.
