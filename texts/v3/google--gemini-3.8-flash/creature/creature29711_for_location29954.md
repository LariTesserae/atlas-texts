To understand the life here, one must first discard the soft, wet physics of animal meat. There is no blood, no mucus, no eye that glazes if exposed to the wind. Life here does not squander itself in liquid chemistry; it is dry-state, friction-tempered, and built along the lines of clockwork, vulcanized fiber, and electrostatic lattice.

### The Floor-Fauna: Tribophages and Sintered Striders

Across the iron-veined flats, where roots as wide as highways curve into mirror-smooth horizons, the ground itself provides both the floor and the pasture. 

Close to the surface slide the *planae*—flat, oval scavengers the size of shields, whose undersides are not lined with cilia or soft muscle, but with tightly shingled plates of calcified mica. They do not walk; they maintain a faint, ambient charge difference between their ventral skirts and the lignum floor, riding a millimeter off the petrified ebony on a silent skim of dielectric repulsion. As they glide, forward-facing combs of micro-crystalline diamond plane away the thinnest possible shavings of resin-soaked wood. They do not eat to digest in a belly; their internal cavities are dry ball-mills. Hard, spherical gallstones of basalt-dense heartwood tumble together in muscular, dry-valved gizzards, grinding the shaved lignin into an ultra-fine, micronized soot from which non-aqueous sulfur-lipids are chemically stripped under high piezoelectric heat. Behind them, they leave not dung, but twin ribbons of pressed, totally desiccated charcoal briquettes, entirely drained of charge and chemical oil.

Preying upon these gliders are the *scythe-striders*—the mantis-forms that stand three meters high on legs that resemble black, segmented surveyor’s tripods. Their joints do not use synovial fluid, which would boil into vapor in seconds; they pivot on dry, self-lubricating graphite bearings and flex with bundled braids of electro-contractile carbon fiber. 

When a strider moves, it sounds like an antique clock mechanism running down: a frantic, syncopated *clack-tick-click* as its hard carborundum needles tap against the petrified grain. A strider does not possess lungs. Along its flanks are ranks of slotted louvers, lined with copper-rich membranes that harvest volatile hydrocarbon vapors directly from the high-altitude draft as it gusts past. Its vision is entirely non-hydraulic: two bulging, multi-faceted clusters of dry quartz lenses, backed by photochemical resins that register the world in deep violet and the glowing, crackling blooms of static electricity that halo every moving thing.

When the strider strikes, it does not crush. Its forelimbs are folded leaf-springs held under immense mechanical tension by an organic ratchet. It releases them with the sound of a snapped cable: two needle-sharp lances of sintered ironwood punch through the planar scavenger’s top-shell, delivering an arc of forty thousand volts stored within its stacked, laminated pelvic plates. The scavenger's sulfur-fats instantly vaporize internally, scorching the shell clean, and the mantis sips the resulting ionized gases through hollow, ceramic proboscises.

### The Choral Drift: Life in the Roaring Flutes

The vast hollow columns—the fluted, bone-colored pillars that funnel the planetary winds—are far from vacant. Their interiors are acoustic chambers of murderous intensity, where air funneled from the ionosphere accelerates into standing shockwaves that would shear damp flesh to ribbons. 

Here dwell the *reed-moths*. They are not moths at all, but kite-like ribbons of cured, fibrous bark, their bodies suspended inside the flutes by diamond-tipped hooks anchored into the growth-rings of the inner wall. They hang suspended in the gale, their broad, stiffened wings acting as tuned reeds. As the sub-bass drone of the planet reverberates through the hollow trunk, their wing-roots flex, turning the sheer physical energy of the sound into electrical current via thick clusters of piezoelectric crystals nested along their spines. They do not hunt; they feed entirely upon the kinetic violence of the air and the ambient mist of fine siloxane that weeps from the xylem pores within the flute’s lining.

Around them, crawling along the inner curves where the wind shears fastest, are the *pipe-cleaners*: multi-jointed, centipede-like organisms whose outer chitin is coated in a velvety pelt of carbon-fiber setae. They are the balance-keepers of the planetary organ. Where the wood begins to pit, crack, or accumulate excessive frictional soot, they gather in clusters of hundreds. Their mouths secrete a warm, thick paste of dissolved lignum and dry amber that sets harder than tool-steel in the howling cold, sealing the fissures and preserving the laminar flow of the column. They are deaf, blind, and immune to the pressure waves that would liquefy an ordinary lung; they navigate entirely by the vibration of the wood-grain, knowing their column as an old sailor knows the lines of his hull.

### The Sky-Casters

High above the flat root-terraces, where the copper sun sits frozen in the violet-black sky, circle the *strata-kites*. With wingspans reaching twenty meters, these creatures possess no muscle mass capable of flapping. Their wings are rigid, hollow aerofoils formed from a single, continuous sheet of resin-impregnated cellulose, stretched taut across a skeleton of light, bone-dry reed-tubes. 

They remain aloft for decades, kept buoyant by the intense mechanical upwellings created where the planetary winds strike the kilometer-high pillars. Between their trailing wing-tips they spin drifting streamers of pure, pyrolyzed carbon thread—webs kilometers long, thinner than a strand of hair, that trail through the upper atmospheric dust like fishing lines through an oily sea. These streamers harvest the triboelectric charge generated by the diamond-particulate winds. 

When a kite has accumulated a lethal differential of charge, it banks downward toward the ground-spires in a long, silent dive. Dropping its ventral tail—a single, weighted probe of native copper—it skims the root-roads, trailing the probe like a lightning rod. The resulting discharges illuminate the twilight with blinding, pale-blue sheets of static, frying whatever striders or ground-crawlers happen to cross the current. The kite circles back, using broad, scooping flaps of its chin-chitin to gather the shattered, carbonized remnants before climbing back to the silence of the high thermals.

### The Inhabitants of the Golden Polyhedra

And then there are the people—or the terminal lineage of what began as humanity. 

They do not, they *cannot*, walk this plateau in the bodies of their ancestors. To bring a being of water, salt, and blood out onto these polished ebony flats would be to subject it to explosive, shivering desiccation: the lips would crack to dust with the first syllable, the eyes would become dry pebbles, the blood would sublimate through the skin in a cloud of pink smoke.

The citizens of the suspended polyhedra are called the *Tempered*.

In form, they are tall, elongated figures of severe, calligraphic elegance. Their silhouettes are human, but their proportions belong to the fluted pillars they live among: narrow-waisted, limbs like slender, multi-articulated lances, moving with a terrifying, liquid economy. Their skin is not meat, but an artificial dermis of oiled, flexible amber—semi-translucent, showing beneath it the slow, flickering pulse of femtoscale computational paths and the clear, heavy circulation of the non-aqueous siloxanes that cool their synthetic neural networks.

They do not breathe in cycles. They possess no bellows-chests. Across their collarbones run narrow, gold-inlaid grilles that sample the ambient air, drawing in the volatile hydrocarbons and cold ozone to fuel their fuel-cell cores, which burn without flame or exhaust. 

Their faces are still, beautifully carved masks of matte black or dark gold, lacking mouths. Speech between them is not conducted through damp breath against vocal cords, but through laser-tight infrared telemetry that pulses between the jewel-like nodes set into their foreheads, and through acoustic resonance. When a Tempered speaks to an assembly, they lower their head and anchor their knuckles directly to the ironwood root below them. Using internal piezoelectric transducers, they cast their thoughts downward into the planetary wood, using the plateau itself as their vocal tract. Their voices come not from the air, but from the ground: a deep, gravelly vibration that shudders upward through the limbs of their peers, carrying layers of dense, mathematical poetry that would take human speech hours to articulate.

They view the wet biological history of humanity with a mixture of reverence and horror—reverence for the raw spark that originated them, horror at the sheer fragility of an existence reliant upon the puddle. In their gold-walled polyhedra, where gravity is dialled down to an imperceptible breath and the furniture is carved from whole diamonds, they keep museums of moisture: hermetically sealed, triple-walled chambers of thick sapphire glass containing single, preserved drops of water, illuminated like unstable isotopes. They visit these droplets to remember what volatility was, how terrifying it must have been to be composed of the very thing that the universe was always trying to boil or freeze away.

The Tempered are engineers of the Great Bore. Their entire civilization is geared toward the acoustic maintenance of the plateau. They do not see the world-spanning boughs as a territory, but as an ancient, unthinkably slow computer—an engine whose processing cycles are measured in the harmonic frequencies of the wind-flutes and the shifting stress-lines of the calcified root-boughs. 

They graft their city-polyhedra to the columns with great care, adjusting the repulsive gravity fields that hold them aloft by fractions of a nanometer so as not to damp the natural resonance of the flutes. They are constantly listening. They spend centuries lying prone upon the polished terraces, their amber cheeks pressed against the cold, iron-veined grain, listening to the wood-roar below.

For the drone is not static. The roots, descending leagues into the dark depths of the world, are not dead; they are processing the crushing weights of tectonic drift, shifting their mineral density along microscopic channels, responding to the cold, slow rotation of the mantle leagues beneath. The sounds that shudder through the ebony flats are thoughts—calculations of mass, tension, and eons-long botanical survival, hammered out on an acoustic loom five thousand miles wide.

The Tempered consider themselves not the masters of this world, nor even its inhabitants, but its ear. They are the organist’s assistants, moving through the dusk, polishing the stops, catching the carbon-dust as it blows from the pipes, waiting for the chord to change.
