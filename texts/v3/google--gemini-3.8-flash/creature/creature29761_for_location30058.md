The things that belong to this water do not swim; they anchor, or they shatter.

Where the current shears closest to the granite, down in the hairline fault-runs that predate the big snap, live the grazers of the liver-skin. They are flat, button-shaped isopods, no larger than a thumbnail, their backs curved like river-pebbles to spill the wash over their shells without offering an edge for the hydraulic press to catch. They are blind, translucent horn-colored, and their underside is a single, terrifying vacuum cup of ringed muscle, bordered by six pairs of hooked, basalt-hardened claws that dig directly into the microscopic tooth of the sheared quartz. They do not move when the sun-wedges hit them. They graze the tough, rubbery moss with circular rasps of chitin, vibrating in place at the same frequency as the vibrating rock, so closely tuned to the granite’s resonance that to pry one loose requires an iron chisel; if lifted into the free water, their shells are torn from their meat by the sheer drag of their own weight.

Deeper down, where the water climbs out of the subterranean flumes in a solid, unyielding cylinder, there are the things the fault tore out of the deep aquifer: pale, ribbon-like loaches, entirely without scales or pigmentation, their bodies little more than long cords of milk-white muscle wrapped around a translucent spine. They have no swim bladders—a bladder in this column would detonate like a dry bladder-wrack under a boot—and so they are heavier than the water they inhabit. They do not fight the up-boil; they use it. They wedge their broad, wedge-shaped craniums into the angles where two fracture-planes meet, their gills protected beneath heavy, forward-facing flaps of gristle, and let the ascending ice-melt drive past them. They wait for the amphipods. When one of the tumbling glass-shrimps is smashed against the rock face and stunned by the concussive crack of a cavitation bubble, the loach’s mouth—an underslung, toothless slot ringed with sensory barbels like white hair—snaps outward on a lever of bone, vacuums the pulp from the stone, and retracts before the turbulence can catch its jaw.

These loaches are what the wooden cribs are meant to catch, though not for meat. 

The people who come down into the shaft are not valley folk; they come from the dry screes on the northern shoulder of the needle-range, where the scrub juniper dies out and nothing grows but black lichen. They are narrow-shouldered and thick through the ribs, their lungs enlarged by generations of gasping in the blue-thin air of the summits, their skin perpetually stained a dull, greased plum by the soot-and-tallow lard they smear across every fold of their bodies to keep the mountain wind from splitting them to the fat. 

To enter the cleft, they strip naked except for their ballast: two slabs of green slate, grooved with files and lashed tight to their shins with raw sinew, and a broad belt of ox-hide weighted with lead ingots salvaged from ancient roof-gutters down on the plains. Over their ears they wear caps of boiled leather packed with mutton grease and bees-wax, tamped deep into the conch until they are stone-deaf; the hammering inside the cleft is loud enough to rupture a man’s balance-canals in ten breaths, turning the brain inside the skull into a spinning compass that cannot tell down from up.

A diver descends by a single line of plaited horsehair, held above by four men braced against the sky-slit with their heels sunk into granite cracks. 

The man who goes down does not breathe while the water is rising. He enters during the heave—that ten-second drop when the hydraulic flumes above choke on their own slush and the water falls three cubits, going oily and thick like cooling fat. In that momentary dead-space, he kicks downward, the slate slabs pulling his legs into the vertical chill, his eyes open to the blinding gold spears of sunlight. 

He sees the world through a corneal haze of animal fat, his vision reduced to high-contrast shears of glare and black stone. He does not look for the loaches; he looks for the crib-slats. With fingers already deadened to wood by the four-degree melt, he feels along the linden poles for the dead-water pockets. 

The loaches crowd into those fist-sized eddies behind the ash-baffles to shed their winter skin—a heavy, greasy slime-coat rich in an anti-freezing lipid that smells like cold tallow and prevents ice crystals from nucleating in their blood. In the dead pockets, the fish rub themselves against the rough gut-lashings to peel the mantle off, leaving long, curd-like skeins of grey grease clinging to the cordage.

This grease is the harvest. 

The diver does not carry a knife; an iron edge dropped here would shatter on the stone or pierce an artery in the manic wash. Instead, he carries a bone scoop carved from a mountain-goat’s rib. In the five or six seconds remaining before the flumes clear and the hydraulic scream returns, he scrapes the curd from the nets and the slats into a small, bladdery pouch slung beneath his chin. 

He works with a manic, rhythmic economy, his knees clamped around the ash timbers to keep the ballast stones from dragging him through the bottom fissures into the guts of the range. If the surge catches him while his legs are loose, the rising column will break his thighs against the crib-frame as easily as dry twigs. When he feels the first metallic hum in the ash wood—the prelude to the cavitation—he strikes his heels together. The slate slabs, hung on slip-knots of greasy cord, kick free and plunge into the abyss. 

Above, at the sky-slit, the haulers feel the tension change through the horsehair line. They run backward across the granite shoulder with the rope over their shoulders, hauling him through the sun-shafts before his lungs can draw the icy melt into his chest. 

He comes out of the water like a cork from a skin, purple-black, vomiting thin, white bile, his eyes blood-shot where the water pressure has burst the sclera. He cannot speak for an hour; his jaw is locked in a risus sardonicus from the cold, and the sound of the cleft—that timber-splitting roar—remains trapped inside his grease-clogged ears, humming in his teeth long after he has been rolled into dry sheepskins beside a dung-fire.

Yet behind him, down in the blinding clarity of the vault, the chamber remains indifferent. The skin-grease he missed floats up in small, curdled pearls that are instantly sheared into nothing by the quartz blades. A loach, disturbed by the diver's goat-bone scoop, drifts out from behind a timber, is seized by an upward-spinning vortex, and is driven four fathoms toward the sky-slit in the pulse of a heartbeat. 

Before it reaches the open air, the current twists it sideways against a feldspar fin. The fish splits with a dull, wet snap that makes no sound against the roar of the water. Its white entrails—pure, fat-thickened cords—unravel into the gold needles of sun, tumbling in the wash for a fraction of a second before the amphipods, tumbling alongside them like thrown gravel, catch them with their pale, paddle-feet and are carried down together into the dark throat of the fault.
