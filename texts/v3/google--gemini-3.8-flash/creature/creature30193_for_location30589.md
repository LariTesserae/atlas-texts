In the total absence of a sky, the eye is useless, and so the creatures here have largely abandoned it. What remains of vision is mostly an irritation: milky, lensless globes that register only the dull, orange-red cherry of an overheated resistance coil or the brief, violet snap of a shorted terminal. 

Instead, the basin is mapped in the belly, the pads of the feet, and the sensitive skin between the nostrils. The creatures perceive the world not as shapes against an horizon, but as a topography of temperatures, frictions, and low-frequency oscillations. 

The dominant life along the lower pipelines is the *mattress-mouse*, though it is less a mouse than an unbroken tissue of rodent flesh that has forgotten how to be solitary. They do not maintain burrows; there is no loose soil to dig without striking a root or an iron rib. Instead, they nest in living quilts—hundreds of soft, blunt-snouted bodies stitched together by interlocking claws and the greasy tangle of their tails. A single cluster may span twelve feet across the top of an insulated steam pipe, rising and falling in a collective, staggered respiration that sounds like a blacksmith’s bellows worked through a wet sponge. The individuals on the bottom, pressed directly against the corroded lagging, absorb the mechanical heat until their bare bellies are chapped and pink; when the heat becomes lethal, they wriggle upward through the mass, displaced by cooler bodies from the surface diving down to warm themselves. They feed without leaving the clump, passing down pellets of dried fungal crust and grease-soaked lichen from mouth to mouth like a bucket brigade. 

Above them, where the pipe-hangers and corroded iron brackets bite into the rubbery wood of the cycad trunks, live the *shear-bills*. They are birds only by lineage; their primaries have long since melted together into rigid, horn-like paddles used to wedge themselves between tree-boles. Their plumage has lost its barbs, resembling instead the dark, lacquered hair of an animal that has spent its life swimming through oil. They do not sing—a call would vanish three feet from their beaks into the muffling moss. Instead, they communicate by snapping their heavy, scissor-like mandibles against the steel conduits. A sharp *clack* against the iron can be felt a quarter-mile down the line by another bird whose chest is flattened against the same pipe. Their chicks are born naked, blind, and covered in a thick, amber-colored lard that repels the constant drip of acidic condensation from the leaves above.

Between the boughs and the ground, where the plant life gives way to the dense, writhing floor, life exists under a rule of absolute pressure. There is no room to run, and so there are no sprinters. 

The primary grazers of the peat are *bellows-hogs*—great, low-slung things shaped like wet flagstones, whose ribs have broadened and flattened into a protective shield beneath their skin. They do not walk so much as lever themselves forward on four thick, clawless paddles that work through the mud with the slow, hydraulic persistence of pistons. Their skin is corrugated like tractor tread, hosting colonies of pale barnacles and leeches that feed on their sluggish blood without ever prompting a reaction. A bellows-hog moves perhaps twenty yards in a calendar year. It simply lowers its broad, shovel-shaped lower lip into the stratum of coal-sludge and rotted leaves, straining out the fist-sized weevils and the blind, fatty grubs that gestate in the grease-traps of the eccentric gears. Its teeth are not enamel, but dense plates of bone that grind continuously against one another, producing a faint, rhythmic rasp that blends with the hum of the dynamos.

Where there are grazers so heavy and dull, the predators are equally patient. Nothing stalks; stalking implies an approach through empty air. Here, predation is an act of prolonged displacement. 

The *sleeve-snakes* take up residence inside the dead conduits—lines where the copper wire has rotted out or been stripped by short-circuits. A snake will back its tail into an empty three-inch pipe and remain there until its body thickens to fill the bore entirely, its scales locking into the interior rust like the teeth of a ratchet. Only its head protrudes, resting flat on the iron rim, disguised by a fleshy crest that mimics the rubber gaskets used to seal pipe flanges. It does not strike with a lunge. It merely waits for a cluster of shrews or a blind, foraging opossum to shift against its snout during their sleep. Then its jaws part—very slowly, so as not to cause a panic in the press—and it draws the animal into its gullet over the course of an afternoon, its throat expanding within the iron casing until it fits the pipe like a piston in a cylinder.

Down in the sloughs, where the cast iron gives way to deep sumps of black, mineral-tainted water, life has adapted to the taste of refined hydrocarbons. 

The *grease-skimmers* are pale, disk-shaped salamanders with skin so thin their organs can be seen through their ribs as dark, pulsing shadows. Their mouths are round, sucker-like sphincters lined with fine, hair-like filters. They cling to the underwater packing glands of the massive line shafts that still turn beneath the silt, feeding on the heavy, mineral-based tallow that leaches out around the brass bushings. They do not metabolize the oil for calories in the ordinary sense; rather, their livers have become massive, lipid-storing vats that use the petroleum compounds to synthesize a foul, buoyant slime that keeps them suspended in the warm, stagnant soup. If one is dragged from the mud, it smells not of swamp, but of machine oil, paraffin, and old kerosene.

The invertebrates are the only things that seem to possess numbers beyond counting. They are the mortar between the bricks of larger bodies. 

There are roaches with carapaces five millimeters thick, grooved along their backs like the teeth of the gears they nest in; when a gear tooth turns into its mate, the roaches do not leave, but allow themselves to be caught in the mesh, their shells tough enough to withstand hundreds of pounds of pressure without cracking, using the cold cast iron to scrape the parasitic mites from their bellies. There are blind, white millipedes six feet long that live inside the high-pressure water mains, feeding on the bacterial mats that coat the interior steel; they have lived in total dark for so many generations that their nervous systems have adapted to the water’s laminar flow, navigating through thousands of feet of pipe by the slight variations in pressure caused by distant, leaking valves.

And deep in the low swales, where the hum of the 60-cycle current is strongest and the earth feels like the hood of a working truck, there are signs of things that move only when the current alters.

These are the *ground-calves*—pale, massive heaps of muscle and blubber that have no recognizable faces, only a snout like a leather boot and two broad ears that are kept pressed flat against the damp earth. They are entirely hairless, their hide covered in a thick, gray mantle of grease and river-silt that has dried into a hard, flexible crust. They lie directly over the main buried feeder cables, their bellies nestled into the trenches where the peat has been cooked to a warm, sour mulch. They do not eat plants, nor do they hunt the things that walk on them; they are sustained by the enormous beds of sulfur-eating bacteria that bloom in the heated mud beneath their bodies. They live in a state that is neither sleep nor waking, but a continuous, drugged trance induced by the low vibration of the electrical field. When a transformer miles away shifts load—when the pitch of the 60-cycle hum drops by half an octave—a ground-calf will shudder, let out a long, bubbling sigh through the mud, and roll its four-ton bulk three inches to the left, finding the fresh edge of the heat.

Nothing in the basin ever starves, because nothing ever burns its fuel in a hurry. The heartbeats are slow, five or six to the minute; the lungs expand with the reluctance of cold leather; the blood is thick with fats and mineral salts that keep it from spoiling in the heat. It is an ecology that has reached its terminus. There is no wildness left in it, if wildness means freedom of movement or the sudden terror of the chase. It is an enormous, breathing engine made of meat, running on the heat of an older engine made of iron, both of them slowly rusting and fattening together in the warm, unbroken dark.
