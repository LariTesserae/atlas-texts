To survive upon an unyielding floor of cold razor-edges, an animal must either be hard enough to skate upon flint or so soft that a laceration is of no more consequence than a wrinkle.

The things in the bottom-most layer, where the wet moss meets the bare black glass, are built for the razor. They do not have paws or soft pads; their undersides are armored in overlapping shingle-plates of oily horn, like the bellies of iron-scaled pangolins, but wider, flatter, and oiled with an amber grease that resists the continuous wash of lukewarm water. These are the *pavement-grubbers*—though they are not grubs, but broad, jointless disc-crustaceans, wide as washbasins and barely two inches tall. They move with an imperceptible, hydraulic slide, their thousands of short, chisel-tipped false legs gripping the microscopic pits of the obsidian. They have no faces. Their heads are merely forward-facing slits fringed with serrated, sweeping plates that rasp the acidic lichen directly from the glassy crests. When two of them collide in the dark beneath the moss, there is no fight; their skirts grind together with a flat, dry *clink*, like stoneware plates shifting in a sink, until the heavier one slowly slides over the carapace of the lighter, bearing it down against the glass without pausing in its chewing.

Beneath them, wedged into the vertical blowholes and split blisters where the basalt cooled into jagged pockets, live the *tallow-hides*. 

They are amphibians only by courtesy of their slime, having long since surrendered legs, hearing, and the burden of vision. A tallow-hide is four to six feet of bloated, pale, semi-translucent muscle, pale as lard boiled with turnip greens. They do not possess lungs that expand against ribs; instead, their whole dorsal surface is a thick, pleated bellows of vascular fat that rises and falls against the roof of the lava tubes in which they wedge themselves. They wedge tight—deliberately so. A tallow-hide packs its body into a slit lined with obsidian razors until its skin puckers around the rock edges; the stone cuts them, but their blood does not run like red water. It is a thick, milky lymph that hardens the moment it meets the scalding moisture of the air, forming an instant, rubbery callus that binds the animal into the stone. 

They are the basin’s filters. Their mouths are simple, gaping funnels lined with gelatinous teeth like blunt corn kernels. They do not strike. They sit, three or four jammed into a single vent, mouths upturned toward the dripping under-mat of the moss, swallowing the steady drift of shed carapaces, decaying fern-slime, and the drowned run-off that drains through the floor. When they spawn, they do not couple. They simply vent ribbons of tallow-colored beads into the hot water that sheets down the rock; these beads are sticky, adhering to the glass within inches of their parents, where they begin to pulse within an hour, their translucent embryos already shivering in the persistent warmth.

Between the stone and the light, suspended in the three-foot mattress of water-logged liverworts, there is no air to speak of—only an emulsion of vapor and rotting vegetative matter. Here, the life is legless, blind, and soft.

Huge, ribbon-bodied flatworms, purple-brown and rimmed with frills of electric orange, thread through the moss like living intestines. They do not hunt with speed; they are simply wider than the spaces between the plant stems. As they creep, their ventral surfaces weep an alkaline jelly that dissolves the cellulose of the moss behind them, leaving hollow, collapsing tunnels that immediately fill with warm water and pale, thread-like rotifers. Following in their wakes are the *spindle-crabs*—not true crabs, but spindly, soft-shelled arachnomorphs with legs like wet reeds. Their bodies are transparent; through their hulls, one can see the steady peristalsis of their stomachs, dark green with digested moss-pulp. They are harmless, defenseless, and so numerous that every handful of wet vegetation contains five or six of their tangled, kicking clusters. They are the bread of the basin. Everything eats them, and they eat everything that dies before it can reach the tallow-hides below.

Higher up, where the ferns flatten their skiff-sized ribs to catch the pale, yellow-green skylight, the creatures must deal with the heat not as a subterranean steam, but as a suffocating roof.

Here live the *bellows-beetles*. They are the size of sheep, but flat as shields, their shells dull and greasy, pitted like pig-iron. They have lost the power of flight—their elytra are fused into a single, seamless dome that deflects the continuous, heavy drops of condensation falling from the canopy above. Underneath this dome, in the humid hollow between shell and abdomen, they cultivate beds of yellow fungus. They do not eat the leaves they walk upon; they are too large, their jaws too heavy. Instead, they prune the thick, liver-colored vascular ferns with their front pincers, stuffing the masticated fronds into their own shell-cavities to feed their private, steaming gardens. A bellows-beetle does not sleep. It walks a circuit of three or four fern-crowns, its jointed, clawed stilts puncturing the rubbery leaves with wet *thwacks*, leaving behind an oily trail of scent that warns others of its weight. When two bulls meet on the broad rib of a fern, they do not rear up; they lower their front skirts, engage their iron-colored horn-spurs, and push. There is no triumph of display—only the dull, wooden groaning of their legs until the weaker beetle’s claws tear through the leaf flesh, and it drops three feet into the yielding moss below, where it lands with a soft *flump*, rights itself without urgency, and begins to climb the nearest stalk once more.

And hunting among the ferns are the things that have abandoned silence.

Because there are no shadows and no horizons, concealment is an impossibility; therefore, the basin’s predators have abandoned stealth entirely. The *mantis-crawlers* do not look like orchids or dried leaves; they look like what they are: seven feet of pale, muscle-banded gristle encased in a skin like wet parchment. They have no eyes—only two pairs of long, quivering antennae that end in fleshy, cupped discs. These discs do not smell; they feel heat-signatures through the steam with such precision that they can detect the opening of a beetle’s breathing-pore across twelve yards of dense brush. 

A mantis-crawler’s movements are horrible in their lack of momentum. It does not spring. It simply extends an arm—an arm like three joined pickaxes wrapped in wet pork—with a slow, continuous extension that takes twenty minutes to cross the space between two fronds. Its claws do not snap shut; they sink into the prey through hydrostatic pressure, slowly driving through the chitin or the hide until the joint locks. Once locked, it does not kill immediately. It begins to digest the prey where it stands, exuding clear, scalding acid from pores along its forearms, dissolving the limb it holds while the prey continues to chew its own fern, too dense or too dulled by the eternal heat to panic.

Nothing screams here. The air is too heavy for screams; high frequencies are choked out by the suspension of grease and water within twenty paces. 

The only things that make voice are the *bell-worms*. They live in the canopy, anchored by three-pronged suckers to the high, swollen trunks that support the leafy roof. They are nothing more than skin-pipes, twelve feet long, hanging straight down like gray sausages into the humid gloom. As the thermal currents rise from the hot basalt below, they pass through the hollow interiors of the bell-worms, causing them to hum—a low, flat, vibrating tone like a wet finger dragged across the rim of an enormous earthen jar. Because there are tens of thousands of them, and because the heat never changes its draft, their humming does not rise or fall. It is a single, continuous chord that sits beneath the crackle of the beetles and the scrape of the horn-plates, a subterranean vibration that travels through the water, through the moss, and through the marrow of everything living there, so that even if one could close one's ears, the liver would still shudder with it.

There are no generations here, not in the way cooler lands understand them. There is no spring swelling of youth, no winter die-off of the old. An organism in the basin simply expands until it is torn or infected, and then it is consumed while its outer margins are still feeding. 

Near the stagnant center of the basin, where the basalt crust is thickest and the obsidian has cooled into unbroken, black mirrors forty feet wide, the vegetation cannot find a seam to pinch. Here, the glass is bare, swept clean by the eternal sheets of scalding run-off. 

Yet even this is not empty.

Across these black mirrors moves the *film*. It is animal, but it has no limbs; it is plant, but it has no fiber. It is a yellowish, liver-like skin, no thicker than a wet canvas, that creeps across the obsidian at the speed of a shadow’s edge. It moves by surface tension, rolling its leading edge forward over its own drowned tissues, digesting the micro-algae that coat the glass and leaving behind a trail of pristine, sterile obsidian that is re-colonized by the green slime thirty minutes later. Trapped within the body of the film are small things—spindle-crabs that were not fast enough, dead larvae, the detached legs of beetles—all suspended in its golden transparency, slowly disappearing into the organism’s vast, un-segmented belly like flowers pressed into tallow.

On the margins of the mirror, where the film reaches the moss again, the tallow-hides poke their snouts out of their cracks to lick at its trailing edge with tongues like gray leather strips. The film does not retreat; it simply grows faster on the other side, rolling endlessly across the glass that will not break, fed by the rain that will not drop, in the light that will never become morning.
