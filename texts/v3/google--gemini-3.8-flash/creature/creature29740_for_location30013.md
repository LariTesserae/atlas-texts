Between the mated faces of the iron, where the millstones-broad hex-heads pull the flanges together with twenty tons of dry tension, there is no air, but there is an inhabitant. 

These are the shims: ribbon-thin, jointless creatures no thicker than a leaf of feeler gauge, made of a tough, translucent cartilage that resembles oiled vellum. They do not burrow; they were simply present when the plates were drawn down, having slid between the cold surfaces while the cranes were still swinging. They survive on the ancient, lardaceous tallow that coats the threads, extending fine, capillary threads into the micro-gaps where the planing tool skipped or chattered. When a nut settles half a degree under the shifting thermal mass of the deck, the shim registers it, pulsing once, a silent, hydraulic squeeze that extrudes a droplet of clear, bitter water from the bolt-hole. A man walking the seams can tell where they lie by the dark, circular damp around certain washers—a ring of cold moisture that never evaporates, even under the full dry wash of the white zenith.

Beneath the belly-plates of the great flat-bodied lead-beasts, another order lives. These beasts—the ones whose snouts drink the structural vibration—are seldom clean. Their undersides, pressed so hard against the diamond-tread that their skin takes the crisscross pattern of the embossing, are pasture for colonies of blind, six-legged lice the size of copper coins. The lice do not drink blood; they scrape away the damp grey film the beasts leave behind—an emulsion of shed epidermis, zinc efflorescence, and the fine, airborne graphite that floats up from the uncured concrete below. They have no shells, only a stiffened hide of greasy leather, and when a lead-beast shifts its quarter-ton bulk to face a new stress line, the lice roll with the movement like ball bearings, rattling dryly under the meat, keeping it slick, allowing the great creature to slide without tearing its skin against the fresh-milled edges.

The owls—if they are owls, though their hemp-colored plumage has no barbs, only dry, fibrous strings that hang like unraveling rope—nest where three beams meet to form a dead corner. Their young are not round or downy. They hatch as angular, wedge-shaped things, stiff-necked and flat-headed, their bones composed of an unusually heavy, mineralized matrix rich in the zinc salts they preen from their parents’ quilts. Because the spatial lines are fractured, their nests often appear to be built against a vertical plane, the twigs of zinc-wire-grass glued to the sheer uprights by a mortar of regurgitated graphite. 

These birds do not hunt by sight. In the white, unblinking glare, their eyes are covered by third lids of thick, pale skin that look like boiled horn. Instead, they wait for the *shear*. When an atmospheric fold passes over a deck—one of those places where the air suddenly tastes of bruised tin and a stanchion’s second shadow appears—the air pressure between two invisible planes changes sharply. Small things get caught in the fault: the pale crustaceans lifted from the drainage wells by rising drops of water, or the fragile, four-winged midges that hatch from the curdled lead caulking. When the shear opens, the bird simply leans out into the emptiness, locks its wings at that strict ninety-degree square, and falls *sideways* along the crease of the world, mouth agape, gathering the floating debris of the rift before returning to its perch with a single, un-flapped, geometry-defying glide.

And there are walkers.

They are few, spaced so far across the endless mile-wide terraces that one might travel for days along a single bevel without crossing their sign, yet they are the true tenants of the assembly.

They are tall, dry-jointed things, neither men nor mechanisms, though their silhouettes carry the miserable, round-shouldered stoop of boiler-tenders. Their bodies are wrapped closely in bands of heavy, rubberized drilling-cloth, yellowed by tallow and tied off with copper wire at the knees and elbows to prevent the jagged burrs of the gangways from catching their clothes. They wear no shoes; their feet have broadened into wide, callous-soled pads, tough as vulcanized tire-carcasses, split into three splayed toes that can grip the lip of an open I-beam even when the deck tilts underfoot into an impossible vertical.

They do not look up into the blinding zenith. To do so is to invite a dry, blistering blindness that leaves the eyes crusted with white matter like salt on a battery terminal. Instead, they wear vizors fashioned from thin sheets of planished lead, pierced only by two horizontal needle-holes. Through these, they watch the steel.

They have no language of vowels and teeth. When two walkers meet at a convergence—where three identical gangways terminate at a single bulkhead that shouldn't be there—they do not speak. They stand ten feet apart, their long arms hanging until the tips of their knuckles, clad in rings of turned iron, rest against the floor plates. Then they drum.

It is a short, rhythmic percussion: three raps with the middle finger, a pause to let the harmonic run through the web of the girder, then two answering taps with the heel of the palm. They are not asking after each other’s health or where the horizon went. They are comparing the tension of the bay. One reports a loose rivet-line three tiers down; the other returns the signature of a buckling stay somewhere out in the glare where the perspective fans into flat, un-foreshortened drawing. 

Their food is the crustacean from the wells, which they take with wire baskets dropped down the square vertical conduits. They crack the soft, graphite-streaked shells with their thumbs and eat the meat raw; it has the cold, chalky consistency of fresh lard, faintly flavored with lye. To drink, they carry small scrapers made of slate. They run these along the underside of the girders where the sweat hangs thickest, catching the bead in small, lidded cups of pressed sheet metal. It tastes of zinc and wet iron, but it keeps the joints from seizing.

In their pockets—deep sacks sewn into the skirts of their oilcloth coats—they carry the things they have found in the unfinished parts of the works. Not tools, for there is nothing left to build, but small geometric anomalies that have fallen out of the seams: iron nuts whose threads run in two directions at once; sheared rivets whose two halves are made of metals that reject one another; and lead plumb-bobs that, when hung from a string, refuse to point toward the deck, pointing instead with a stubborn, silent urgency toward some lateral point twenty miles out in the white glare where there is nothing at all to see.

Sometimes, when the great valve five levels down releases its cold, mechanical sigh, a walker will pause. He will not turn his head, but his foot will rise an inch from the plate, feeling the long, slow displacement of the air as it lifts the hair on his ankles. He waits until the valve seats itself again—until the single dry *clack* of the metal plug meeting the bronze ring-seat travels up through his left heel.

Then he sets his foot down, checks the angle of his shadow against the nearest missing sun, and steps out along the edge of a flange that is two inches wide and sixty miles long, his weight carried easily, perfectly balanced between the steel below him and the steel that is already beginning to assemble itself above his head.
