The pallet is not empty. 

To a creature accustomed to the heave of wet lungs, it appears to hold only another bundle of the golden kapok-fiber, tucked neatly against the basalt wall. But look closer, where the braided loops of cord take the strain against the flint pegs: the fiber gives slightly under a concentrated weight, settling into a small, rigid trough no heavier than a sack of dried lentils.

It is folded upon itself like a carpenter’s rule.

The dweller is small, its spine curved into a tight, defensive spiral that brings the knees against the sternum and tucks the forehead down toward the shins. It does not sleep, because sleep belongs to things that exhaust themselves through chemical combustion and must lie down to clear their blood of acid. Here, where there is neither night nor cool air to mark an interval, it simply ceases to move. For months—perhaps for the duration of a minor seismic epoch—it remains locked in that knot, suspended an inch above the glass floor, hummed into a stupor by the deep-mantle drone that trembles through the hanging cords.

When it does uncoil, it does not stretch. There is no elastic rebound in its fibers. 

First comes a sound like the bending of dried cane—a hollow, fibrous creak from the ball-and-socket joints of the hips. The legs extend in deliberate, ratcheting notches. They are impossibly thin, the shins flattened like laths of split ash, the skin stretched so sheerly over the tibia that the amber translucence of the bone shows through, lit from within by the dull copper glow of the roof. There are no veins standing out against its flesh; where blood ought to run, there is only a resinous, viscous marrow that does not pulse, moving through microscopic tubules by capillary pull alone. 

It has hands, but they are not the hands of a digger or a climber. The palms are long, rectangular, and utterly flat, devoid of the soft pads at the base of the thumb that give a mammalian hand its grip. Instead, the underside of each finger is surfaced with transverse ridges of thick, horn-like keratin, precisely like the tread on a gecko’s toe, but hard as tortoiseshell. They do not sweat. When it reaches out and places a palm against the vitrophyre, the contact produces a light, dry *clink*, like a teacup placed upon a marble table.

It does not touch the razor-sharp flakes of the floor. It knows the architecture of the cleavage intimately, stepping only on the obtuse ridges where the stone broke with a blunt hinge. Its foot is narrow, three-toed, the heels long and bony, tipped with a small spur that slots into the natural pockets of the basalt.

Its face is an economy of survival. 

The creature has no nose; where a bridge and nostrils might have been, there is only an oblique, triangular vent covered by a tight lattice of bone-colored bristles. This is its intake. It does not draw breaths; the atmospheric convection of the rift—forced upward by the baking floor—pushes sulfurous fumes through the lattice, which extracts the volatile minerals and passes the spent, parched air back out through paired slits beneath the jaw.

Its eyes are neither wet orbs nor the clustered jewels of an insect. They are twin plates of clear, amber-colored selenite, flat and flush with the skull, grown over whatever optic nerve still lingers beneath. It does not blink. It does not turn its gaze. It navigates almost entirely by the absolute roar of the stone, feeling with the plates of its skull how the sound shadows shift as it moves between the ledges. To this creature, the iron-drone of the earth is not noise; it is topography. An overhanging shelf sounds dark and thick; a shear drop rings with a shrieking, metallic clarity; the small alcove where its bed hangs has the muffled, domestic tone of an enclosed bell.

Watch how it feeds.

It moves toward one of the feathery, golden mounds at the edge of the drop. Its movements have none of the hesitant twitching of the mica-shelled beetles; it possesses the terrifyingly slow confidence of an animal that has no predators and no prey to chase. 

Reaching into the thicket of spun-glass hair, it does not pull with its fingers. It takes the rib-scraper from the stone beside the basin. 

Holding the blackened bone with both hands—pressing its calloused thumb into that polished depression worn by decades of identical gestures—it draws the edge firmly up the stalks of the wire-moss. It does not cut them down. It grazes them, shaving away the fine, sulfurous efflorescence that coats the brittle canes like yellow frost. 

The shavings fall onto the flat ledge in a tiny, pale heap, smelling powerfully of heated flint and burned feathers. 

The creature lowers its head. Its mouth is a horizontal slit, completely lipless, lined with overlapping plates of yellowed enamel that slide past one another like the blades of shears. It sweeps the shavings into its mouth with the side of a horned palm. 

There is no chewing of a wet mash. It grinds the sulfur-crystals and the silken shards dry, an audible, rhythmic *scritch-scritch-scritch* that cuts cleanly through the drone of the walls. It does not drink. It has never known the sensation of a liquid slide down an open gullet. The water it requires is locked permanently into its tissues, bound up in the crystalline structure of its salts, cycling endlessly in a hermetic interior that allows not a single molecule of vapor to escape into the baking air.

Yet, despite this total, mineral completeness, there is an ache in the creature. It is small, but it is not ancient; it is continuous.

When it has finished feeding, it sweeps the stray shavings into the narrow fissure—an act of tidy, obsessive stewardship—and returns to the alcove.

It does not immediately climb back onto the kapok pallet. 

Instead, it sits upon its haunches before the chipped obsidian basin. It reaches out with both hands and lifts one of the three river-stones.

The stone is grey-green, veined with white quartz, its surface rounded by thousands of miles of cold, rushing water that ceased to exist before the basalt split. It is everything the rift is not: it has no edges; it has no tooth; it does not hum; it was made by softness and weight, not by heat and violent shear.

The creature brings the river-stone to its face. 

It does not smell it—there is nothing to smell on a rock that has been dry for a century. It presses the curved surface against the selenite plate of its left eye. Then, slowly, with an agonizing, meditative rhythm, it rolls the stone across its cheek, along the curve of its jaw, and down the parched skin of its neck. 

It is searching for an impossible thing. It is looking for the slip.

Its skin grips the stone, dry and unyielding, horn dragging against quartz with a faint, dry squeak. It pushes harder, rolling the egg-shaped pebble until the friction warms the stone to the exact temperature of the room. It does this until the tactile memory of water—carried down in the shape of the stone like a word preserved in an extinct alphabet—has been thoroughly ironed into its flesh.

Then, with infinite care, it sets the river-stone back in its depression between the other two, so that their smooth flanks just touch.

It reaches for the edge of the pallet. It hooks its three-toed feet into the braided cordage, draws its knees to its chest, and folds itself down into its triple-jointed spiral. The creaking of its joints ceases. The horn plates of its fingers interlock over its shins. 

The liver-red light from the roof glints off the flat crystal of its brow. Below it, the mica-shelled things tick through the wire-ferns, their little mica claws tapping against the obsidian, but the sleeper does not hear them. It has already sunk back into the mantle’s roar, becoming one more dry, unshifting node in the great, baking circuit of the stone.
