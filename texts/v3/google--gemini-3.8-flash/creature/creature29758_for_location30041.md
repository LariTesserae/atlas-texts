The water in the cooling ditches and sumps does not harbor true river fish, but things that have abandoned both river and sea to master the chemical soup. The eel-like creatures the heron pulls from the ditches are swamp eels—scaleless, blunt-snouted, and nearly blind, their sides coated in a heavy, viscous amber mucus that shields their flesh from the copper salts. They spend their days knotted together by the dozens inside the drowned housings of centrifugal pumps and the hollow cores of cast-iron valves, breathing air through the vascular lining of their mouths when the oxygen in the sulfur pools drops to nothing. They taste of creosote and river silt, and they grow thick as a wrist on the endless fallout of crab larvae and swift guano.

Alongside them in the mud-shallows are the sleepers—stout, black-skinned gobies that crawl across the exposed flats on stiff pectoral fins when the tide goes out, dragging their bellies through the rust aggregate to hunt the smaller fiddlers. Their eyes have migrated to the tops of their heads, covered in opaque, cloudy lenses that protect them from the lime-crust, hunting more by the vibration of a million moving legs than by sight.

The crabs themselves are not uniform. While the gray flats belong to the small mud-dwellers, the labyrinths of the cracked concrete settling tanks are held by larger, solitary blue-claws that have grown monstrous and sluggish on the warm outflow. They do not run. They sit in the shadows of sunken I-beams, their shells encrusted with gray barnacles and iron scale so dense they resemble lumps of broken masonry. When a dying cormorant drops from a girder above, these heavy crabs converge upon it not in a rush, but with the glacial, rhythmic persistence of iron machinery, dismantling feathers, tendon, and hollow bone until nothing remains to snag the silt.

Above the mud, where the metal stays clear of the corrosive tides, the ecology turns dry, brittle, and vertical.

Between the diagonal struts of the catenary towers, where the salt-damp breeze stirs only slightly, the spiders have constructed a continuous canopy. They are long-jawed orb weavers, but here their silk is not clear; it is yellow-gray, dusted with the airborne fallout of pulverized slag and dried guano. Their webs are not discrete wheels, but sprawling, tangled sheets that bridge the gap between rail gantries and steam headers. They feed on the chironomid midges that hatch by the billions from the dead cooling ponds—soft, mouthless insects that rise at twilight in columns so thick they look like steam venting from cold stacks. The spiders grow fat and pale-bellied, their webs sagging under the weight of their own silk and the dry husks of a thousand generations of flies.

Higher still, inside the hollow box girders and the inverted domes of the blast stoves, live the bats. These are little brown bats and free-tailed bats, but their colonies have taken on the character of the iron. Their fur is stained a dull mahogany by the iron oxide dust that coats the roosting plates. In the suffocating, unventilated heights of the old open-hearth roofs, the heat never leaves, and the bats do not truly hibernate; they exist in a state of perpetual, twitching torpor, dropping out of the steel gaps at dusk to rake the midge-swarms above the ditches, their high-pitched clicking lost against the steady hiss of the crustacean floor below.

And nestled into the highest, dryest voids—the former crane operator cabs perched eighty feet over the yards, their glass long blown out, their floorboards replaced by solid plugs of dried dung—are the night herons and white-faced ibises. They build their nests without a twig, for there is no wood to be gathered for miles. Instead, their platforms are woven out of what the basin provides: fraying braided-wire rope, copper grounding cable stripped of its insulation by weathering, dried reeds brought down from the freshwater reaches miles upriver, and the stiff, bleached wing-bones of their own dead. The young sit in these wire baskets under the gray bell of the sky, their bills open, panting in the wet furnace heat, waiting for their parents to regurgitate the yellow oil and shredded eel-meat dredged from the ditches.

Nothing here travels by ground if it can help it. The lime and slag eat the pads of mammals; ordinary dogs and feral cats died out decades ago, their feet turned to running sores by the caustic dust. 

Yet there are a few mammals that have persisted by taking to the pipeline highways. High above the crab-flats, running along the continuous bundles of three-foot steam pipes that link the mill buildings, are the raccoons. They are long-legged, lean, and unusually silent. They never set foot on the gray mud. They travel the pipe racks like elevated roads, moving with deliberate, claw-ticking care over the rusted rivets and insulated sleeves. They hunt the roosts, reaching into the rivets and crevices for sleeping bats, or climbing into the lower crane platforms to raid heron nests for thick-shelled, chalky eggs. Their scat, left in neat piles on the tops of the junction boxes, is composed entirely of crab chitin and bird-bone, stained the dark, purple-black of dried blood.

Farther in, in the cavernous gloom of the central motor rooms where the roofs are still intact and the rain never reaches, there is another kind of presence. 

Men live here, though they are so few and so quiet they are easily mistaken for the shadows of the overhead cranes. They do not belong to the town upriver; they have drifted down the drainage canals in flat-bottomed punts made of corrugated tin, and they have stayed. They live in the elevated foremen’s shacks—small, green-painted wooden boxes perched on steel stilts inside the machine halls, reachable only by iron ladders with half the rungs rotted away.

They wear boots cut from truck tires, bound around their shins with split inner tubes to keep the brine from their skin. Their hair and beards are stiff with salt, their skin cured by the sulfurous damp into the dry, yellowish gray of tallow. They do not speak to one another across the gulfs of the mills; the wet air carries sound too far and ruins the quiet they have grown used to. 

They live on the eels and the big blue-claws, which they catch using simple drop-traps fashioned from expanded-metal walkway grates, baited with the entrails of pelicans they knock down with lengths of pipe when the birds roost low. They boil their food in cut-down freon tanks over small, smokeless fires fed by bituminous tar scraped from the roof joints and the ancient, oil-soaked cedar shoring from the lower basements. The smoke is greasy and sweet, vanishing almost immediately into the vast, amber ceiling-vaults where the swifts nest.

These men move with the tide. When the culverts back up and the water rises over the dead rail tracks, they do not leave their shacks. They sit in the doorways, legs dangling over the thirty-foot drop, watching the gray tide of crabs migrate toward the higher footings. They know the names of the pipes; they know which lines still weep fresh water condensed from the upper valley’s fog, and which ones drip green liquid that leaves a sore where it touches the hand. They have lived here long enough that the birds no longer shift their wings when they pass beneath the catwalks. 

When one of these men dies, he is not buried—the ground is impenetrable, an armored carapace of slag and rebar. His companion simply slides him into the hot outflow canal at the turn of the ebb tide. The eels take the soft parts within an hour; the gray shore crabs pick the ribs clean by nightfall; and by morning, the bones are swept down the blind, brick-lined tunnels toward the estuary, white and smooth as chalk, leaving the steel and the birds to their work.
