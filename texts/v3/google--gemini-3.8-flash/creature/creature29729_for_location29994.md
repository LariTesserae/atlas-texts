To understand what lives in the vault, one must first discard the soft mechanics of the wet world: there are no membranes here that require bathing, no juices held behind vulnerable barriers of skin, and no cellular walls that would not instantly fissure and turn to meal in the desiccated argon drift. Life here is not fluid; it is an arrangement of dry ceramics, dense tallow-waxes, and interlocking plates of self-lubricating slate. 

The baseline of this ecology begins with the copper-colored wire-growth—the *needle-moss*—though it is closer to an open-air crystal matrix than any lichen of the upper crust. It does not drink from the stone; it roots into the microscopically shattered edges of the pyroxenite via catalytic chelation, leeching trace lead, silicon, and iron directly out of the quarry faces. To drive its slow, brittle expansion, it relies on two sources of ambient force: the thermal gradient between the floor and the roof, and the fierce triboelectric charge generated by millions of hard feet scraping against vibrating pig iron. If you hold a hand near a patch of these wires (though no hand will ever reach them), you would feel a prickling, dry static draw, like wool pulled over amber. Their hexagonal spores are not seeds in the organic sense; they are dry, hollow capsules of faceted silica containing a pinch of reactive dust. When one settles into a hot, fluorocarbon-slick fissure on a walking beam, the ambient friction and heat snap it open, and the filament begins its slow, stiff climb toward the dark.

Crawling through these wire meadows are the knuckle-crabs—known to the geometry of the room simply as *grinders*. 

Seen closely, a grinder possesses neither eyes nor antennae. Its front is a blunt, wedge-shaped shield of slate-colored apatite, polished along its leading edge to the brilliance of an obsidian mirror by decades of scraping against raw casting marks. Beneath this cowl, its mouth is an exposed triple-drum: three cylindrical burrs of sintered corundum that spin against one another with an audible, raspy chatter. They do not eat organic mass because none exists in a soft state; they eat the fine, super-fluid graphite dust that coats the machinery, along with the brittle tip-sheddings of the needle-moss and the shed hulls of their own dead.

Because a grinder has no blood, its interior pressure is maintained by a dense, unctuous wax that only softens at thirty-six degrees. If an engine slows, or if a draft from some forgotten rock-vent should drop the temperature of a crank-pin by five degrees, the grinders upon it freeze in mid-stride, their internal tallow seizing into solid white paraffin until the friction heat rises again. They move on six pairs of legs that terminate not in claws, but in hard, tungsten-tipped chisels. When millions of them move in unison across the flank of a descending beam, their chisels strike the iron like the chatter of a thousand clockwork escapements, producing a static hiss that rides just underneath the boom of the solid-state valves.

They reproduce without moisture. When two grinders meet upon a warm bearing race, they do not mate in the fashion of soft things. Instead, they lock their carapace-flanges together with a dull *clink*, their grinding plates working in reverse to plane down a pocket between their sternums. Into this gouged pocket they deposit a pair of dry, lead-stabilized discs—dormant, disc-shaped larvae that will not awaken until the host engine reaches a specific vibrational frequency, shaking them loose to roll into the clearances of the nearest journal.

Hunting the grinders are the cling-bats, though they are bats only by virtue of their suspension. 

Their bodies are narrow, triangular spindles of porous bone, no thicker than an ax handle and roughly forty centimeters long. They have no fur; their hide is a cured, paper-thin leather that feels like parchment dipped in boiled linseed oil. It does not stretch. Their wings do not flap in broad, sweeping arcs; rather, they click and fold like collapsible measuring rulers, utilizing spring-loaded bone hinges that snap outward with sudden, pneumatic violence. 

The cling-bats spend days hanging motionless from the high valve manifolds, where the fluorocarbon lines run at their hottest. They roost by driving curved, iron-hard calcified spurs directly into the leaded seams between pipe segments. Their eyes are milky, lensed beads that see only in the deep infrared, registering the world as a shifting map of dull-red machinery against the cold, black geometry of the pyroxenite ceiling.

When a cling-bat drops, it does not glide; it falls like a plumb bob, its wings rattling like dry slats of lath. It strikes a walking beam at the height of its arc, drives its chisel-beak through the seam between a grinder’s carapace plates, and extracts the hot, dense tallow within. There is no blood to spill. The prey does not struggle with limbs; it simply seizes as its pressure-lipid is drawn out. The cling-bat’s scissor-jaws snip the empty shell into four or five flat shingles with that fast, dry *snip-snip-snip*, letting the fragments drop into the engine pit where other grinders immediately gather to reduce the shards back to dust.

Yet there are things older and larger still, dwelling in the spaces the engines do not touch.

Deep in the lateral recesses where the pyroxenite was sheared away to make room for the flywheels, the convection drafts form dead zones. Here, the air is stagnant, heavy with the stench of scorched slate, and cooler by three degrees. In these shadows move the *caliper-striders*. 

They are stick-thin, radially symmetrical creatures with three jointed limbs that span four meters from tip to tip. Their frames are built entirely of hollow, carbonized cartilage that has absorbed so much silicate dust it rings like porcelain when struck. A caliper-strider does not walk so much as it measures the stone, placing its three needle-sharp feet into the fracture fissures with surgical slowness, moving only when the engine’s master-stroke strikes its concussive blow. In the split second that the iron rockers hit bottom and the rock floor hums with the transfer of sixty tons of inertia, the caliper-strider shifts one leg, its movement entirely masked by the seismic thud of the vault.

These striders are scavengers of a different order. They do not feed on the living tallow of the crabs or the metal-oil of the pipes. They are spun-glass trappers. From their undersides they extrude miles of microscopically thin, boron-doped filaments that they string between the rock teeth of the ceiling and the support gantries. The wire is invisible, stiff, and brittle, but under the static tension of the chamber, it hums at a pitch that makes the cling-bats veer away. 

When a cling-bat misses its strike on a falling rocker arm—deflected by a sudden burst of heated gas from an unjacketed vent—it tumbles into the web. It does not stick; there is no glue on the filaments. Instead, the wire shears through the thin parchment of the bat's wings like a hot wire through cheese, cleaving bone-rods and dry sinew until the creature breaks apart and falls into the lower crannies. There, the caliper-strider descends with its three clicking stilts, kneeling over the wreckage to harvest the sulfur-dense organs, leaving behind only a neat pile of notched bone segments that look like dismantled drafting tools.

At the very bottom of the vault, in the four-meter clearance beneath the engine beds where the red incandescence never dims, there is a crust that is neither rock nor shell. 

It is the *scale-mat*. It is three inches deep in places: an agglomeration of billions of microscopic, shield-backed acari that have abandoned individual movement entirely. They have fused edge-to-edge into a continuous, flexible floor of biological tile. The scale-mat absorbs the steady rain of graphite silt, crushed carapaces, and bat-shingle that drifts down from the engines. It breathes through tiny, slate-lipped spiracles that open and close with the cyclic pressure waves of the walking beams. 

The mat has no brain, no face, and no voice. It is simply an organ of return. It processes the falling silt, extracting every trace of organo-lead, every fragment of bone-wax, and every grain of fluorocarbon polymer that settles from the air, concentrating them into dense, heavy pellets that it excretes from its margins directly into the thermal updrafts. These pellets are dry, hollow, and light as pumice; the engine drafts catch them, carry them back up thirty meters into the amber glow of the rafters, and shatter them against the iron beams, renewing the dust that feeds the wire-moss, which feeds the grinders, which feed the bats.

Nothing here sleeps. There are no cycles of rest because there is no dark and no cold to enforce them. If an organism dies without being crushed, it does not rot; it simply stands where it stood, drying further until the graphite dust cakes its eye-slits and its joints weld together with mineral deposit. Eventually, a descending beam or the foot of a wandering strider knocks it over, and it breaks with a dull, clean snap, joining the fine gray drifts that bank against the foundations of the machines.

In this place, the concept of life has been honed until it matches the pyroxenite that contains it: hard-edged, mineral-rich, completely waterless, and sustained entirely by the dry, furious prayer of three walking beams that never cease to turn.
