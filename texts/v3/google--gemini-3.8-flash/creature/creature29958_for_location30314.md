They have no names for each other, nor for the cleft, because they have no larynxes that can shape a vowel. The large ones—the ones who sit by the anvil-stones—communicate by pressure and heat, and by the low, seismic drumming of their heels against the saturated silt. When one of them strikes the bench, the vibration travels thirty yards through the moss-sponge to register in the belly-plates of the others, who answer with a slight tightening of the gut or a shift of their rump against the stone. 

To look at their faces is to see the human form after five hundred centuries of drowning in grease and silence. The forehead has crept forward, heavy and smooth, a thick shelf of spongy bone that protects the frontal lobes from falling grit. The eyes have not vanished altogether; they have retreated beneath translucent folds of fatty skin, resembling two cloudy pearls sunk in lard. They can tell the difference between the cold-tea green of the foxfire and the absolute black of a moss-tunnel, but little else. Instead, the bridge of the nose has widened into a ridged, leaf-shaped organ of tactile cartilage, ribbed like a mushroom’s underside, constantly moist, twitching with the passage of every damp eddy of air.

Their coats are not true hair, but a dense, downy under-wool, silvery-ash in color, through which long, pale guard-hairs protrude like the whiskers of a catfish. This pelt is so heavily charged with tallow that water cannot wet it; when one of them wades through a travertine basin, the liquid pearls and rolls off its flanks like mercury, leaving the pale hide beneath bone-dry and warm. Under the pelt lies four inches of white, solid blubber, an armor against the four-degree chill.

They are matrilineal in the way that lichen is: an unbroken continuity of skin-to-skin contact. A female does not drop her litter onto the moss; she sits upright against the cleft wall, her legs splayed, and the newborn—blind, pink as a scraped turnip, no larger than an apple—climbs upward through the soaked tallow of her belly fur by pure instinct, guided by the smell of the two milk-pores beneath her collarbones. There it stays for four of the valley’s seasons, buried so deeply in the guard-hairs that an outsider would see only an occasional twitch in the mother’s chest, like a second, erratic heart.

They do not hoard food. To hoard in this dampness would be to invite the molds that burn the skin. Instead, they cultivate the shelves. With their broad, spatulate fingers—the nails are flat, spade-like shovels of amber horn, two inches wide—they turn over the wet slabs of detritus, weeding out the bitter, sulfurous liverworts to make room for the pale, gelatinous *flesh-ear* fungi that swell to the size of trenchers on the droppings of the lower runs. 

Sharing the bench with them, wedged into their armpits and sleeping between their splayed thighs, are the loaf-things. These are rodents only by ancestry; in habit, they are more like living cushions. Their bodies are short cylinders of immense density, weighing twenty or thirty pounds, with legs so reduced they barely clear the turf. They live in a state of semi-torpid contentment, their blunt heads buried in the fur of the larger beasts, nibbling mechanically at whatever scraps of beetle rind or crushed snail drop from the fingers above. Their breath is sweet and smells faintly of turnip greens. The large ones do not eat them. There is an unspoken truce that has lasted longer than the languages of the plains below: the loaf-things provide a perimeter of small, twitching ears that can detect the subtlest change in air pressure—the whistling drop of a dislodged stone from the choked roof eighty feet up—and their dense, boiling body heat warms the nursery depressions. In return, they are allowed to scavenge the anvil-crumbs and die of old age, their ribs slowly flattening into the peat where they lie.

Lower down, in the gutters where the water moves with a lazy, glass-like sheet flow, live the giants of the cold. 

These are the great welters—salamanders that measure eight feet from the spatulate snout to the rudder-like paddle of the tail. They are older than the tool-users. Their skin is not slimy, but has the texture of wet, pebbled pigskin, slate-gray along the spine and fading to the sickly yellowish-white of curd on their wide, soft bellies. They have no lungs; their sides are fringed with triple clusters of external gills that hang into the water like skeins of scarlet thread, vibrating with an almost mechanical constancy to strain the sparse oxygen from the trickle. 

A welter may not move twelve paces in forty years. It wedges itself between two sunken slabs of limestone, resting its broad, chin-less throat upon the stone, its pale, lidless eyes staring into the dark. Its life consists of waiting for the slow, drifting descent of dead organic matter: a loaf-thing that died in its sleep and was nudged down the runnel by its kin; the soft, pulpy carapaces of giant wood-lice; or the dense mats of cave-cress that break free when the water swells. When something touches the sensory pits that line the welter’s jaw, the immense mouth yawns open with a sudden, hydraulic *thwump*, sucking in thirty gallons of water and whatever drifts within it, then snaps shut with the sound of a wet cellar door slamming.

Below the welters are the cleaners—the pale necrophores. They are blind, ghostly isopods, broad as saucers, with shells as thin and flexible as wet parchment. They move by thousands through the sub-surface of the moss, an endless, pale machinery of legs that keeps the rift sweet. Nothing that dies ever touches the bare stone beneath the peat. Within six heartbeats of an animal’s death, the necrophores are there, rising through the bryophyte turf like bubbles through dough. They enter through the nostrils, the eye-sockets, the soft folds of the groin, working with a quiet, rustling industry that sounds like dry husks being stirred in a basket. Within three days, even the teeth are gone, dissolved by the acid secretions of their mouthparts, converted into the fine, chalky silt that the tool-users gather to line their sleeping hollows.

Between these layers, there is no cruelty. There are no hunts, no flights, no cries of terror. The system is too cold for rage, too dark for display. A mother tool-user will occasionally feel the blind, blundering snout of a welter brush against her foot in the shallow runnel; she will not kick it, but simply lift her heel, allowing the pale, ancient river-log to wedge itself deeper into its silt-furrow. 

At the very rear of the cleft, where the ceiling drops until it is no higher than a man’s chest, the stone walls are smooth. They are not smooth from water. For sixty feet along the rock face, at the height of a sitting creature's shoulder, the limestone has been buffed until it reflects the foxfire like gray butter. It is the grease-mark of tens of thousands of generations who have leaned their backs against that specific seam of rock to sleep, rocking slightly from side to side to work the damp out of their spines. 

If you were to press your ear to that spot, you would not hear the mountain. You would hear, through the sixty-foot thickness of the stone, the faint, rhythmic *clack-clack* of the anvil-stones being lifted and set down, a mile above the valleys where nations are born, burn, and turn to dust without ever sending a single echo up through the wet, plugged throat of the cleft.
