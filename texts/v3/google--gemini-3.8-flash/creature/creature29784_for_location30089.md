To look for eyes or feet in this place is to misunderstand what survival becomes when space has no corners and time is measured in piston strokes.

What lives in the matting underfoot is not separate from it. If you were to peel back three meters of the pale, compressed fiber—past the point where the daylight dies and the warmth becomes an aggressive, greasy fever—you would find lengths of wet, white cord that appear to be taproots until they are touched. They do not snap; they contract with a sluggish, hydraulic stubbornness, weeping the same alkaline brine that coats the tree trunks. 

These are the ribbons. They possess no joints, no sensory horns, and no heads. They are simply tubes of tough, rubbery parchment, three fingers broad and thirty paces long, threaded vertically and horizontally through the pulp like the basting stitches in a quilt. They have no lungs and no heart; they do not need them. When the central piston strokes—that deep, subterranean *thump* that shudders through the iron floor far below—the sudden hydrostatic shock compresses the matting by half an inch. The ribbons use that universal compression as their systole. The machine beats, and five thousand yards of blind, buried gut are emptied into the sump; the machine rests for its three-minute inhalation, and the ribbons expand by capillary action, drinking the grease-slick water and iron salts out of the fiber around them. If the engine ever ceased to turn, they would not starve; they would simply suffocate in the dead weight of the floor, unable to draw their next breath without eighty tons of cast iron to squeeze it out of them.

Higher up, where the flywheels turn their endless, grinding half-revolutions through the collars of crushed lichen, the life becomes thicker, heavier, and more settled. 

In the clearances between the turning rims and the lime-scaled brickwork, there are things shaped like split hams or loaves of wet dough. They have the color of parsnips that have rotted under snow, blotched here and there with the purple-brown stains of iron tannates. They have wedged their broad backs firmly against the brick, anchoring themselves with short, woody pegs that grow straight out of their skin and into the old mortar courses. They have no faces, only an anterior margin that rests against the flywheel’s rim like a brake shoe. 

As the wheel groans through the wet lichen, it produces a steady, porridge-like paste of pulverized moss, rust flakes, and degraded mineral oil. The creatures do not hunt; they do not even reach. Their front lips—calloused, black, and as hard as sole leather—remain pressed against the turning metal, continuously skimming the hot wet meal from the iron face as it presents itself. They make a soft, bubbling noise, entirely lost beneath the wheeze of the manifold, as the friction of the wheel keeps their bellies at a steady, feverish heat. When an iron bolt shears nearby with its gunshot snap, they do not flinch. Nothing in their world has ever fallen upon them; nothing has ever pursued them. They simply absorb the iron-scum, growing denser and more mineral each decade, until their skins are so heavily plated with precipitated hematite that they can no longer expand, at which point their centers slowly soften, rot out, and become fresh troughs for liverworts to colonize.

Near the upper rocker arms, where the air is thickest with aerosolized grease and the heat hovers near the point of scald, the life is different. It is suspended.

Here, in the dense hammocks formed by the broad, bronze-green leaves, hang the sheaths. They look, at first, like discarded overalls or the sloughed skins of large cattle, dangling by their heels from the vine-girders. They are dull grey, hairless, and oily to the touch, water skittering off their flanks in greasy peas just as it does from the leaves. 

These are organisms that have abandoned the burden of internal organs. Inside their tough, tallow-scented hides, there is little more than a spongy mesh of fibrous marrow soaked in clear, bitter fat. They have no limbs, though their lower edges fray into dozens of thin, prehensile cords that trail down into the lower foliage, searching constantly for the warm trickles of sweat running out of the knots in the wood. They do not eat the wood; they siphon the copper-smelling gum. 

Occasionally, during the great splash that follows the piston’s concussive stroke—when the overhead canopy shivers and unloads its hundredweight of hanging water—one of the sheaths will shift. The motion is not sudden. It is a slow, muscular heave, like a sleeper turning over under heavy blankets. A fold in the hide opens, exhaling a puff of air that smells faintly of boiled tripe and machine oil, and draws itself three inches higher along the girder to escape a sudden pocket of steam, or to lay its belly against a patch of cambium that has swollen with fresh brine.

And down in the very heart, inside the cold-water wells and the drowned steam jackets where the metal never cools below the temperature of fresh blood, there is a singular, continuous thing.

It cannot be called an animal, because it has no boundaries, and it cannot be called a fungus, because it possesses long, slow bands of red, twitching fiber that resemble unstriped muscle. It has grown into the cast iron itself. Where the cylinder walls have cracked under the frost of two centuries ago, this flesh has welled up through the fractures like dark pitch, sealing the gaps, holding the cylinder together against its own internal pressures with a living gasket of dense, gristly meat. 

It is this thing that tastes the steam. When the high-pressure manifold vents its long, whistling breath through the moss jackets, the steam passes through miles of its microscopic, labyrinthine passages. It extracts the volatile paraffins from the cylinder oil; it drinks the lime that boils out of the old well-water; it filters the soot. It is the engine’s soft lung, laid over its iron ribs. 

It has no thoughts that could be translated into language, but it has a state of being: it possesses an enormous, vegetative awareness of pressure. It feels the stroke building in the boilers half a mile away under the sod; it feels the expansion of the piston like a deep, satisfying ache in its own flanks; it knows the precise friction of every bearing by the temperature of the sap running through the wood that grips it. 

Nothing here dies in a way that leaves a skeleton. When one of the dough-like things behind the flywheel finally ceases to skim the metal, the lichen does not wait for it to cool; it simply climbs over it, its pale threads stitching into the hide while it is still warm. The iron salts that the creature spent eighty years concentrating from the machine’s rust are taken up by the creeper roots, turned into that violet, chisel-blunting wood, and built back into the struts that hold the engine upright. 

If someone were to climb down through the broken lantern roof, they would find no footprints to follow and no voices to hear. They would feel only the warm, persistent downpour, the heavy air pressing into their ears like hot wool, and the great, regular *thunk-whuff* of the floor beneath their boots. 

And after twenty minutes of standing in that lime-green light, drenched to the skin in sweet, copper-scented brine, they would notice that their own clothes were beginning to stick to the pulp under their heels—not with mud, but with a fine, grey-green felt that was already beginning, with infinite patience and mechanical regularities, to draw them down into the stroke.
