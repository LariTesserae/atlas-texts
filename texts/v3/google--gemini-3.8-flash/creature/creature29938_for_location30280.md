The life here does not begin with water, nor does it end with rot. To rot requires moisture, and moisture has not existed on this shelf for an age. When an organism here ceases to function, it does not dissolve; it simply cools slightly, its joints stiffening until it becomes another small, unmoving casting upon the lead-grey stone, waiting for the talc-fine dust to bank against its ribs and erase its profile.

Because of this, the things that dwell among the blast stoves and the arrested flywheels possess a biology that leans heavily toward mineralogy. They have traded flesh for vulcanized fiber, blood for heavy, non-volatile oils that do not evaporate in the thin air, and bone for a dense, cellular silica that rings like porcelain when struck.

The most common of them are the *tallow-gleaners*, though to give them a name implies a community that does not exist; each is entirely alone. They are small, broad, and flattened to the thickness of a thumb, moving with a dry, mechanical flutter across the sheer iron faces of the silos. Their legs—twenty or thirty pairs—are fine as needle-wire, tipped with microscopic claws of corundum that find purchase in the oxidized hematite skin of the metal. They live by grazing the ancient, polymerized residues of lubricants left behind when the machines fell silent: petroleum jellies that have hardened into dark amber varnishes, cakes of graphite tallow packed into the housings of split-bearings, and the faint, bitter crusts of sulfur that bloom around old valve-packings. 

They do not have mouths that chew. Instead, their undersides are fitted with vibrating rasp-plates of chert that hum against the metal, grinding the dried grease into an impalpable soot, which is then drawn upward into their flat cavities by capillary action. As they feed, they leave behind them bright, hair-thin lines of exposed, silver-grey iron—the only part of the city that ever shines. But these trails are quickly extinguished; within a century, the dark plum-skin of oxide reclaims the scar, and the surface is uniform once more.

Far larger, and infinitely slower, are the creatures that have taken the place of boilers and pipe-lagging. 

They are masses of dense, segmented pelt, ten or twenty cubits long, shaped like halved logs or broad, collapsed bellows. They have no limbs. Their ventral sides are composed of thick, foot-deep slabs of fibrous keratin that form a seamless, molecular contact with the warm basalt of the causeways or the low decks of the reciprocating engines. They do not hunt; they do not forage. They are thermal parasites of the planet’s deep fire, drawing the unchanging heat from the stone below and converting it, through an imperceptible, solid-state metabolism, into a sluggish, internal life that barely registers as living.

To look at one is to look at a length of discarded gasket-material or an old roll of lead flashing. Their backs are armored with overlapping tiles of a substance like horn impregnated with iron filings, matte-black and rough to the touch. If one were to lay an ear against the flank of such a creature, nothing would be heard for three days; on the fourth, there might come a single, deep *clonk*—the contraction of an internal chamber shifting a thimbleful of dark, viscous mineral fluid from one side of its mass to the other, a heartbeat measured not by the pulse, but by the transit of tectonic warmth. They will remain in a single swale of the rock, draped over an iron girder, for six hundred years, creeping forward only when the heat beneath a particular flange falls by the fraction of a degree.

There are no eyes on the plateau. To grow an eye here would be an impossible waste of mineral resources; the starless black overhead has never yielded a single photon to reward an optic nerve. 

Instead, the creatures see with their feet, their bellies, and the long, stiffened feelers they sweep through the graphite dust. The entire city is an acoustic resonator, and they inhabit it as an instrument.

Every several miles, perched atop the high gantry towers or clinging to the dead cableways, sits an organism built entirely of angles: the *strikers*. They are tall, emaciated tripods of jointed chitin, standing twenty feet high, their bodies reduced to little more than a dry, central hinge from which their long, stilting struts radiate. They weigh almost nothing; the air does not move them, and their balance is an absolute, motionless art. 

At the tip of their foremost limb is a heavy, spherical bulb of solid, iron-rich bone—a mallet grown for no other purpose than percussion. 

Every few weeks, or perhaps once a season, a striker will shift its weight. It does not descend. It lifts its mallet-limb, holds it suspended in the thin, dead gas for several minutes as if calculating the precise tension of the steel beneath it, and lets it fall against the flange of an I-beam.

*Clang.*

The sound does not travel through the air; the thin atmosphere swallows it instantly, reducing the strike to a dull, dry snap. But through the ironwork—through the interlocking trestles, the riveted plates, the vitrified slag causeways, and the lead-grey granite beneath them—the strike runs like a current. The sound travels for forty miles through the bones of the city, a sharp, pure ring that hums at the exact resonant frequency of the frame.

Across the dark, fifty other creatures receive the vibration. The flat gleaners pause their rasps, feeling the tremor lift their carapaces a thousandth of an inch. The great, dormant slabs on the boilers absorb the shockwave into their dense fiber, their mineral fluid shuddering within them. And three miles away, another striker, balanced upon the rim of a blast stove, feels the tone rise through its stilts. 

It waits. It allows the iron to ring down until the silence is total again. Then, with the same cold, geometric precision, it lifts its own limb and strikes the stove’s throat an answering fifth below.

They are not speaking of hunger, or territory, or fear. They have no language for these things; they do not have the brains to house such concepts. The strikes are structural. They are sounding the integrity of the world. They are measuring the plateau—checking if an engine has settled, if a girder has sheared, if the iron remains plumb, or if the cold from the sky has crept down an inch toward the stones. So long as the tone returns clean, without the rattle of a cracked joint or the dead thud of collapsed masonry, the striker remains still. Its task is complete.

Inside the deep, vertical silos, where the air is even stiller and smells of ninety-thousand-year-old lime, there are other, more phantom-like existences. Things that hang like plumb-lines from the darkness of the high roofs: threads of dry silk, hundreds of feet long, weighted at the bottom with a tiny, calcified drop that hovers an inch above the floor. They do not move. They are waiting for the dust to rise. But the dust never rises, so they hang, year after century, plumb and true, keeping the vertical line of the void.

Nothing here is young. Reproduction is an event so rare and prolonged that it resembles the growth of a stalactite more than birth. A striker may spend four centuries extruding a single, needle-straight rod of bone from its underside, millimeter by millimeter, letting it cure in the dry air until it touches the iron deck. Then, and only then, will the rod divide, its tip splitting into three small claws that grip the rivet-heads with an unyielding, dead tension, ready to begin its own ten-thousand-year watch.

There is no violence here. There are no predators, for there is no meat to be taken that would repay the expenditure of movement, and no blood to drink. The tallow-gleaner can pass directly beneath the three-yard-wide leg of a striker, brushing its wire limbs against the creature’s chitin, and neither will acknowledge the other. They are both merely components of the same assembly, moving parts within a mechanism that forgot its work so long ago that the work itself has dissolved into the stone.

They do not belong to the natural world of fields, cycles, and seasons, nor are they machines. They are the third thing: the life that comes after everything that was ever soft or green has been burned away, leaving only the frame, the heat, and the long, ringing dark.
