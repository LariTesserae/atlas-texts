The bare corridors are not barren by accident; they are kept. 

A lane through the ballast stone does not stay clear under such aggressive foliage unless something repeatedly grinds it flat, or grazes it down to the iron before the roots can bite. 

Watch the apron of the dead steam pump, where the condensation gathers into thick, greasy tears and trickles into the silt. For an hour, there is only the *tock* of the high pipe and the slow swelling of water beads. Then, from the black slit beneath the pump’s eight-foot counterweight, a seam in the silt widens. 

What pulls itself into the cherry-red gloom is neither bug nor rat, for neither could endure the oil-soaked heat or find nourishment in vulcanized sap. It is broad, low, and flat as a cobbler’s last, moving with the heavy, hydraulic deliberation of a piston stroke. Its flesh is the color of wet newsprint or lard, thick-hided and completely hairless, glistening with a slick mantle of mineral grease that shields it from the caustic run-off of the ceiling. 

It has no face to speak of—no eyes to take in the sullen coals of the resistors, no nostrils to flare at the stink of boiled metal. Instead, its blunt leading edge is a sensory collar: a pale, fluted fringe of cartilaginous frills that tests the temperature of the floorplates. It seeks the heat lines, navigating by the thermal grain of the iron and the shale, steering toward the warmest flagstones as an ox leans into a familiar furrow.

It does not walk; it flows by muscular contraction, a dry, heavy peristalsis that produces a faint, rasping sound against the gravel, like canvas dragged over cinders. This friction is what keeps the lanes open. Wherever these creatures travel, the tallow-leaves are sheared away at the base, their rubbery stems clipped clean by a hardened, horn-like plate on the creature's underside. 

Yet they are not merely grazers. As this one reaches the flywheel, it halts. Its body broadens, spilling outward like dough left to rest on a warm board, until it has flattened itself against the iron block. There, it feeds. 

It does not eat the metal, but the grease that has cooked out of the packing glands for thirty months, and the microscopic colonies of iron-fixing microbes that bloom in the thin sheath of condensate. Its underside works with microscopic slowness, a carpet of thousands of tiny, rasping cilia that lick the engine block clean, leaving behind a polished, gunmetal gleam where there was once dark scum. In return, it leaves a trail of clear, viscous mucus that smells intensely of spearmint—the distilled essence of the pale spade-leaves it has crushed and processed through its blind, chemical gut.

There are perhaps half a dozen of them in the vault, though they rarely meet. When their paths cross in the narrow gravel passages, they do not fight or flee; they simply press against one another with the indifference of two slabs of beef hung on the same hook, sliding past with a wet, suctioning sigh before resuming their separate circuits. 

In the long, shallow settling tanks, another order of life has found its station. 

The water there is lukewarm, hard with lime, and undisturbed by currents. Suspended in it, just beneath the mirrored surface that doubles the red coils, are translucent, disc-shaped bladders the size of dinner plates. They resemble the vitreous humor of some colossal eye, ribbed with fine, milk-white vessels that radiate from a dark, knotty core. 

These are not active wanderers. They are anchorless, buoyant organs, tethered only by hair-thin strands of fibrous tissue to the iron rivets at the tank's bottom. They do not breath through gills or lungs; instead, they act as passive thermal engines. The upper surface of each disc absorbs the ambient warmth of the vault’s stagnant air, while the lower face rests in the cooler, mineral water. That slight differential in heat generates enough micro-electrical current through their ribbed tissues to drive a continuous, almost imperceptible pulse. Every twenty or thirty seconds, a disc will ripple from its rim to its center, sending a tiny, concentric ring through the red-lit water to lap against the steel walls.

They are the distillers. They draw the dissolved iron and sulfur from the water, binding it into heavy, metallic nodules that form slowly at their cores over years, until one day the weight exceeds the disc’s buoyancy. Then, quietly, without a struggle, the organism sinks to the bottom of the tank, where it dies in the cold silt and leaves its iron stone behind—a small, spherical tribute to the machinery that gave it birth.

And what of the sound—the clean, ringing *tock* that falls into the hopper?

Look closely at the metal receiver where the drop strikes. The iron is not bare; it is padded with a thick, living cushion of dull grey felt. It looks like industrial lagging or wet asbestos, but it is vascular. It drinks the impact. 

Every time the heavy drop falls from the hanging pipe, the felt shudders, drinks the drop instantly into its porous weave, and yields no splash. It lives entirely upon the momentum and the minerals of that single, mechanical heartbeat. The *tock* is muted now, softer than it was when the iron was new, because the growth has learned the precise cadence of the pipe. It stiffens a second before the drop falls, bracing for the weight, then relaxes as the water is drawn inward through fine, capillary veins toward a swollen stem that anchors it deep inside the hopper’s throat.

Nothing here dreams of the surface. If an entrance were cleared, if the cool, thin air of the outer world were to spill down the drainage shaft, it would be a poison of frost and dry wind. The pale leaves would shrivel to wet paper; the tallow-crawlers would curdle into inert suet; the pulse-discs in the tanks would freeze in their lime. 

They are the children of an unrecorded blast, born of coal-oil, trapped steam, and the seeds carried down on the boots of long-dead miners. They have forgotten the sun entirely, finding their heaven in eighty degrees of stagnant wool, three red resistors, and the wet, eternal sigh of cooling iron.
