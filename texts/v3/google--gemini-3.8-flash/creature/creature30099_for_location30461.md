At first, there appear to be only the scavengers of the grain—creatures whose entire existence is an exercise in adhesion.

The flat-shingled grazers that slide across the damp under-ribs are called *scutes* by the few who watch them, though they have no true name for themselves. They are broad as bucklers, segmented like horn, and pale with the translucence of parboiled lard. They possess no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the amber fog that would justify the cost of sight; instead, their undersides are lined with thousands of velvet-tipped cilia that taste the fungal tallow lining the seams. When a scute dies, its grip does not slacken. Its rim of micro-barbs dries into the cambium, its shell hardening into a pale, hollow blister that other creatures use as a cistern, drinking the sweet, warm condensation that wells within its upturned belly.

Higher up, where the river-willow cables run parallel across three miles of empty drop, live the spinners—not spiders, but long-bodied, soft-fingered things no thicker than a boy’s wrist, covered in a pelt of pale, greased down that repels the mist. They have four limbs, each ending in three opposing, leathery digits that never fully open; their joints are locked into permanent hooks, like dry roots. They spend their generations upon a single guy-line, crawling hand-over-hand along its curve, never touching the floor of the world. They feed by peeling the loose, water-rotted fibers of the outer hemp, chewing it into a grayish pulp, and plastering it back down over the structural flaws, sealing the ropes against the deep rot that would otherwise snap the world’s balance. They are not intelligent, but they are faithful; they have the slow, mindless fidelity of white blood cells patrolling a colossal, braided vein.

In the emptiness between the tiers, where the void is thickest and gravity begins to turn like water in a wide, slow eddy, drift the *plana*.

They are the pale rays that graze the vapor. Up close, their skin is not smooth, but ribbed with thousands of hollow, hair-like capillaries that breathe the warm mist straight into their blood. They have no skeleton; their rigidity comes entirely from the hydraulic pressure of their juices, pumped by seven small, rhythmically pulsing hearts that line their margins like a string of wet pearls. Because they have no bone to break, they are immortal until they dry or tear. Sometimes, caught in a thermal column rising from a particularly hot, fermenting truss miles below, a planum will drift upward into the thin amber heavens, spinning with the slow, languid grace of a sycamore seed, its pale skirts rippling against the pearlescent sky until it is nothing more than an eyelash against the white. 

They do not prey upon the wood-dwellers, but the wood-dwellers prey upon them. 

When a planum drifts too near an arch, caught in the lateral gravitational drift that draws bodies toward the great haystack knots, the *splicers* take it.

The splicers are what became of the people who tied the ropes.

They are unmistakably human, though their bodies have been reshaped by ten thousand years of sheer, perpendicular living. They are long—their torsos narrow and their ribs deep to draw life from the thin, warm atmosphere. Their feet are no longer platforms for standing; the heels have drawn back, the insteps have curled into strong, calloused arches, and the big toes have migrated outward, thick as wrists and capable of bearing a man’s full weight from an overhead liana. Their skin has the gray-white cast of peeled birch, mottled with faint brown freckles where the sap-sweat has stained it. They wear nothing woven of loom, for cloth holds water and rots the groin; instead, their loins and joints are wrapped in bands of cured bast, oiled with the tallow of boiled scutes until it is supple as chamois.

They live in the "graft-pockets"—the hollows left behind where three or four ancient oak-timbers have met, swollen, and grown together into a single, cavernous burl.

Inside these knots, the world is dark and smells of cellar-dirt and old beer. Here, gravity does not lean; it folds inward toward the dead center of the cluster, so that a family can sit on the walls, the floor, and the ceiling of a single spherical chamber, looking across at one another with their chins resting on their knees, passing bowls of crushed wood-grub across three feet of air that has no "down." 

The splicers are quiet. They speak in a register so low it seems less like human speech and more like the wood’s own digestion—a murmur of clicks and throat-rumbles that die within four paces. To speak loudly in the void is considered an obscenity, a waste of moisture, and a danger: sound causes the delicate, thread-thin fungi that anchor their lichen-gardens to shatter and fall into the drop.

When they communicate across the gulfs—from an arch to an outrigger, or down to a lower shelf suspended half a league below—they do not shout. They use the wood.

A splicer will find an exposed run of heartwood, clear of moss and bark, and strike it with a mallet made of fossilized burl. The blow makes no sound in the air, but the vibration enters the timber like a stone entering water. Miles away, an elder sitting with his ear pressed to the willow-line will feel the tap in the bones of his skull. *The fifth runner is dry,* the wood will say to him. *The lashing at the three-way joint is slacking. Bring the wedges.*

Their children are born with eyes wide and milky, taking weeks to adjust to the dim, grain-bent light. From their first moon, they are tied to their mothers’ hips with lengths of two-ply line that have been soaked in the mother’s own afterbirth to make them supple and familiar. A child learns to test tension before it learns to reach; it knows that a rope that sings when plucked is a road, while a rope that thuds like dead gut will drop you through four tiers of air before you have finished your breath.

There is a strange, holy trade carried out by the eldest among them—the *Tensioners*.

These are the men and women whose bodies have grown too stiff to swing between the trusses. They take up residence in the spatial distortions, where the joinery was botched or perfected in the dawn of the world. In the triangles that do not close, they sit beside the openings that swallow distance. They watch the pebbles they cast out an hour ago return from behind their heads, noting how many minutes the stone took to complete the loop, and whether the condensation on its surface is sweet or alkaline. By this, they know whether the world is stretching or sagging.

If the world sags, the Tensioners call for the sap-drives.

Then, hundreds of splicers descend along the guy-lines into the damp, green-gray haze of the lower tiers, where the air is so thick with warm vinegar-steam that a man must wear a sponge of damp moss over his nostrils to keep his lungs from burning. There, in the foundations where the light is green as pond-water, they find the *mothers*—the colossal, unpeeled trunks, five hundred feet in girth, that were planted when the world was young to serve as the pillars.

These trunks do not grow upward; they grow sideways, twisting around one another in an endless, agonizing spiral that compresses the lower frameworks. The splicers do not chop them. Instead, using bone adzes and stone wedges, they open shallow cuts in the bark, driving in wedges of dried ash. When the wet sap enters the ash, the wedges swell with irresistible, hydraulic force, levering the great timbers apart by an eighth of an inch, tightening the lines that reach through five leagues of mist to the upper sky.

As the wedges take the strain, the whole horizon groans. 

The splicers on the high bridges feel the ground rise beneath their feet—a slow, buoyant lift that takes their stomachs, as if the entire continent of wicker were a raft riding the crest of an invisible wave. The amber light shifts, brightening by a fraction as the upper thatch opens its vents. The drops of water clinging to the under-ribs stop falling for an hour, caught between the upward surge of the timber and the leaning drag of the knots.

And then, the work is done for another generation.

When a splicer reaches the end of their life, they are not dropped into the mist. To drop a body into the void is considered an act of waste so grave it borders on madness; every ounce of marrow, every foot of gut, every scrap of salt belongs to the framework.

The dead are brought to the seams where the old hemp cordage is beginning to rot within the bark. The body is laid along the tongue-and-groove joint, between the cleft oak and the living ash. Its limbs are pulled straight; its long, prehensile toes are tucked into the adze-scars; its hair is braided into the hemp lashing. 

Then, the living take small bone scrapers and shave the bark of the host tree until it bleeds a thick, cloudy resin over the corpse.

Within three seasons, the sap has done its work. The amber crust hardens, then turns gray, then softens into cambium. The flesh is drunk by the wood; the minerals of the bones are dissolved by the slow, relentless acids of the river-willow. A generation later, a traveler walking the spine of the arch will see only a pale, human-shaped swelling in the grain—a knot where a shoulder was, a long, straight flute where the thigh-bone held the seam together while the tree forgot the difference between the bone and the bough.

The world remembers them this way: not as names, but as extra grain, laid down along the load-lines to help the timber bear the terrifying, weightless height of its own impossible span.
