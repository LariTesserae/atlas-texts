The corrals are not empty. 

To look for an occupant in the way an eye usually searches—scanning for a silhouette that detaches itself from the scenery, for the twitch of a limb or the silver flash of a flank—is to find only stone, timber, and the drifting black sheets of rotten weed. Nothing swims out. Nothing raises a head.

Yet the pens are tended.

Within the deep, fermenting sludge banked against the wicker hurdles, the temperature is three degrees warmer than the surrounding brine. In an ocean that hovers on the razor-edge of freezing, three degrees is an enormous thermodynamic country. The heat is generated by rot: millions of tons of shed cellulose breaking down under dense, anaerobic blankets of sulfur-reducing slime. 

Buried at the bottom of these thermal pits, pinned beneath the weight of decaying kelp, are the builders.

They are not creatures of flesh. If split with an axe, they would show no marrow, no red channel of blood, no grey knot of brain. They are made of the same materials as the forest, but organized with an architectural density that approaches petrified heartwood. 

They appear, at first, as massive, gnarled burls of lignified root-tissue, flattened like river boulders, five or six paces across. Their surfaces are plated in thick, overlapping scales of silicate bark—grey-blue and greasy to the touch, heavily encrusted with white deposits of barite and pyrite. They have no faces, no sensory stalks, no orifices for breathing. They do not breathe; they seep. 

Their bodies are vascular engines powered entirely by osmotic differentials and the violent percussion of the water. Deep inside their dense, fibrous cores runs a labyrinth of pressurized chambers lined with semi-permeable membranes. As the concussive internal waves slam into the ridge, the sudden spikes in hydrostatic pressure force brine through these internal sieves. The water enters cold, leaves mineral-stripped, and drives slow, hydraulic pistons of tightly coiled cellulose.

They do not crawl so much as they wedge. 

From the margins of their bark-plated discs extend heavy, jointed thalli—thick as human torsos, but textured like cured cordage or the vulcanized stipes of the great kelps. These appendages do not rely on muscles; they move through turgor. By shifting saline fluids between internal vascular bladders, they can stiffen a limb until it has the tensile strength of iron, or soften it until it slumps into the silt like a severed vine.

When the deep-sea pulse passes through—that regular, titanic surge that lifts the entire forest toward the unseen sky—they awaken into motion. 

The roar of the rising current is deafening, dragging at the plateau with enough force to shear steel, but the builders are anchored. They do not fight the shear; they harness it. As the great ribbons of weed pull taut, these beings use the upward draft to lever their heavy masses out of the mulch. 

A jointed thallus uncoils from the muck. It does not terminate in fingers, but in a dense, spiral basket of calcified thorns—hard, flinty protrusions of biogenic opal that have grown out from the vascular bark. With slow, rhythmic precision, timed exactly to the frequency of the subterranean waves, the basket scoops through the drifting debris, catching the vast, sloughed sheets of kelp that are tumbling in the current.

They do not eat the weed. They feed on the heat and the chemical bath of its dissolution. 

Working in the bruised green murk, two or three of these great wooden discs will gather at the mouth of a weir. Moving with a terrible, patient momentum—taking twenty minutes to drag a single timber three feet across the slate—they pack the caught weed into the stone pens, treading it down under their multi-ton bulks, compressing the mulch into dense, fermenting silage. 

They are the cultivators of their own climate. Without these pens, the currents would scour the ridges clean, carrying the shed carbon away into the dead abyssal trenches, and the cold would turn these highland summits into sterile plains of bare, scouring gravel. The pens are stoves; the builders live clamped over them like hearth-stones, drawing the warm, sulfurous leachate up through porous pads on their undersides.

Their masonry is not an ancient art that was abandoned; it is an ongoing, seasonal necessity. 

When a slab of slate is needed, they do not quarry it with iron. They locate a hairline fracture in the bedrock, work a slender, fibrous shoot deep into the stone, and drink. As the shoot absorbs mineral salts, it swells with hydraulic pressure that exceeds thousands of pounds per square inch. Over weeks, the rock groans, cries out through the oily brine with a sharp, percussive *ping*, and finally shears off cleanly along its bedding plane.

To shape the stone, they use the ocean itself. An appendage tipped with dense nodules of chert clamps the slate against the bedrock during the height of the internal storm. When the concussive vortex rolls over the crest—that grinding sub-surface hammer that rattles the gravel—the builder leans its dead weight into the shockwave, using the ocean's own violent downward blow to fracture the slab into manageable, rectangular balks.

The braided ropes lashed around the timber posts are not dead artifacts. They are alive. They are the shed, fibrous exuviae of the builders themselves—tendrils that were grown around the joints, allowed to mineralize until they achieved the toughness of hemp, and then severed from the parent body to slowly cure in the cold brine. 

Because they lack nervous systems, their communication is physical, acoustic, and wide. 

They have no voices, but they are not silent. Within their hollow interior chambers, they maintain suspended spheres of heavy, mineralized spar. When a pulse lifts them, they can contract their hydraulic chambers sharply, snapping these internal stones against their hard basal plates. 

The resulting sound is not a call, but a sharp, dry *clack* that punches through the hyper-dense, near-freezing water with terrifying velocity. In this brine, where sound travels five times faster than in air and retains its edge for leagues, that concussive snap carries for sixty miles along the drowned spine of the cordillera. 

Across the ridge, miles away in another basin, another burl of bark and fiber feels the shockwave rattle through its tap-roots. It shifts in its pen of rotting leaves. It waits for the pressure to equalize, for the kelp to fall back against the stone with that vast, sodden slap. 

Then, deep within its wooden throat, it releases its own stone, answering into the dark.
