To touch the skin of one of the plate-dwellers is to lay a palm against wet, vulcanized tire-stock that has spent forty years at the bottom of an oil sump. There is no warmth in it, but there is a slow, elastic give—a dense, hydraulic turgor that pushes back against the fingers with the steady displacement of heavy grease.

They are called nothing, for there is no one here to name them, but their bodies belong to an anatomy of pure tolerance. 

The largest of them—the ones that crowd the thirty-foot granite shelves—possess no necks, no ribs, and no limbs in the terrestrial sense. Beneath their dull, plumbous hides runs an uninterrupted mantle of cartilaginous honeycomb, three inches thick, saturated with a clear, sulfurous lymph. This is their primary organ: a living crumple zone. When the rock ledge finishes its stroke and slams home with a shock that would crack a horse’s shins to powder, the shockwave passes into the beast’s underside, is broken apart within the maze of liquid-filled cells, and vents out through a pair of muscular chimneys positioned between its shoulder-calluses. The collective sigh that follows each collision is not breath from a lung; it is the dumping of compressed fluid and gas, an exhaust cycle that preserves the animal’s central knot of viscera from cavitation.

Their undersides do not have bellies. They have *runners*. 

Where the leaden hide meets the polished graphite of the stone, the tissue changes character. It becomes white, vitreous, and grooved with microscopic chevron-patterns that channel the passing film of cold water directly across the contact patch. The creatures do not cling with claws; they plane. By shifting the volume of lymph within their lower pads, they can induce a sudden, near-total vacuum that locks them to the nickel-iron plate like a machinist’s magnetic clamp, or they can exude a pale, fishy lipid that lets them slide thirty feet sideways with no more resistance than a pat of butter on wet glass. When the stone accelerates, they clamp; when the stone bottoms out, they uncouple and drift forward on the momentum of their own immense, blubbery bulk, grazing as they slide.

Their mouths are not for biting. A mouth here is a scraper, a broad, horizontal slit rimmed with plates of self-sharpening horn that wear down against the stone and grow back from the gum in continuous, laminated sheets, like the fingernails of a corpse. They do not seek food; the food is delivered to them by the sheer velocity of their environment. 

Every square inch of the basin’s stone is coated in an endolithic pelt: not moss, but an ancient, rubbery crust of chemotrophic bacteria and iron-precipitating fungi that draws its calories from the raw, freshly sheared faces of the plates. As the rock grinds, it mills its own minerals into a digestible, colloidal flour; the moisture and hydrocarbons turn it to paste; the pelt consumes it; the plate-dwellers scrape the pelt clean down to the bright, silver scoring of the iron. 

They are deaf to voice, but they are entirely composed of hearing. In place of ears, they possess two long, lateral trenches running the length of their spines, filled with suspended grains of magnetite suspended in thick jelly. Through these, they read the health of the floor. They can feel the spalling of an axle-concretion three miles away; they know the stroke-length of every slab in their territory as an engine knows its own camshaft. If a fault line begins to hitch—if the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-slam* of a shelf loses its cadence by even a sixteenth of a second—a ripple of panic goes through the herd. It is the only time they move with haste. They do not run; they *ooze* down the gutters and slip into the drainage seams, abandoning the shelf before the seized stone fragments into shrapnel.

Deeper in the workings, where the light of the zinc sky cannot reach and the plates are stacked three and four deep like the leaves of a carriage spring, the life grows stranger, harder, and more strictly specialized.

Here live the wedges. 

They are small, rarely longer than an arm, and shaped precisely like cold-chisels. Their carapaces are not leathery, but are composed of an organic iron-chitin compound so heavy that they sink in liquid mud like lead sinkers. They have no digestive tracts to speak of. Instead, their bellies are hollow sleeves that fit directly over the stone lips where two opposing slabs shear across one another. They live by sacrificial wear. They insert their wedge-shaped heads into the closing gap, allowing the stone to shear off their horn-plates, feeding on the microscopic jets of nutrient-rich marrow forced out of the friction-zone under ten thousand pounds of pressure. They grow rapidly from the rear, constantly extruding new horn forward to replace what the rock consumes, living their lives as living bushings, perpetually ground away at the snout and perpetually regenerating from the flank.

Between these extremes are the scavengers, who are also the only predators the basin knows. 

There are no chases here; speed is meaningless where the ground itself is moving at fifteen miles an hour. The predators are creatures of the *catch*. 

One variety, long and flat as a strip of belting, anchors its tail into the natural perforations of the slate beds using barbed, calcified anchors. Its long, ribbon-like body lies slack in the runoff troughs, nearly invisible beneath the churning froth of grease and water. It waits for an animal to misjudge its timing—for an old plate-dweller whose lymph-sacs have hardened, or a young one that failed to plane when the shelf rebounded. When a carcass or a mangled cripple comes tumbling down the flume, the ribbon-beast does not strike; it simply stiffens. Its body, constructed like a ratchet, allows the debris to slide over it in one direction, but when the stone cycles back, the barbs catch, pinning the meat against the return stroke of the slab. The rock does the killing; the rock does the chewing. The ribbon-beast merely drinks the pressurized slurry that pumps out from between the plates as the carcass is milled into the seams.

Even their reproduction has been captured by the stroke. 

They do not mate in pairs. At certain intervals, governed not by seasons—for there are none—but by the slow, harmonic alignment of the tectonic pumps, when several major faults stroke in rare, absolute synchronization, the herds enter a state of total muscular rigidity. As the vast, simultaneous shockwave travels through the platform, their chimney-vents do not vent lymph; they fire thick, grey clouds of oily spores and milt into the air. 

For ten minutes, the freezing fog turns so dense and greasy that it coats the moving stones in a yellow, curdled wax. The aerosolized seed is drawn down by the pneumatic breath of the earth, pulled into the deep suction-wells and forced through the microscopic channels beneath the bedrock. There, in the dark, warm, hyper-pressurized cavities where water boils at freezing temperatures under the mechanical pumping of the world, the larvae hatch.

They do not look like their parents. They are minute, hydrostatic needles of clear gristle. For their first years, they do not crawl; they are the lubricant. Billions of them inhabit the high-pressure water films, their microscopic, frictionless bodies rolling between the moving plates of stone like living needle-bearings, feeding on the rock-meal, growing thicker, flatter, and more leaden, until the day their mass becomes too great for the crevices. Then, squeezed out through the weeping basalt lips like black putty from a grease gun, they emerge onto the upper flats, find their place in the flank-to-flank press of the herd, align their bodies with the throw of the plate, and begin their long, rhythmic slide toward the hammer.
