To understand what lives here, one must first understand that to an animal native to the spires, the wind is not weather. It is floor, wall, and pulse. It is as firm and predictable as bedrock, and far more dependable.

### The Prow-Winged

Those pale shapes hanging in the gaps between the towers are called, if they have a name at all, the *shearmen*.

Close to, their plumage is not feathers in the downy sense, but a dense, interlocking shingles of modified horn, slick as sealskin and completely waterproof. Underneath lies no blubber—fat is too soft for this dynamic pressure—but a dense, fibrous cartilage that encases their organs like a barrel. Their rib cages do not expand outward to draw air; to do so against the three-hundred-knot blast would tear their pleural cavities. Instead, they are vented. Four hard, oval intakes set beneath their wing-roots funnel the slipstream straight through passive, non-collapsing lung-tubes lined with micro-vascular honeycombs. They do not pant; they simply exist as living wind-tunnels, oxygenating their copper-rich, nearly black blood purely through the violence of the air rushing through them.

Their wings have no elbows. The joint is a massive ball-and-socket of calcified cartilage that locks into place with an audible snap when extended. A shearman cannot tuck its wings; once grown, it is condemned to the sky forever, or to the narrow, lip-hung niches of the downwind rock.

Because there are no fish in the abyss and no seeds on the towers, they feed upon the sky’s drift. The great planetary jet is not clean; it is a global conveyor. It carries aloft millions of tons of dry high-altitude plankton: desiccated ballooning spiders from the lowlands, pollen grains blown out of lost sub-continents, dry chitinous shells of dead cicadas, and dormant lichen spores. The shearmen open their lower mandibles—long, flexible spoons of horn edged with a comb of stiff bristles—and strain the blast. They feed as blue whales feed, drifting through a river of invisible grit, closing their gullets every few hours to swallow a pellet of compressed, nutrient-dense dust.

They never touch the sunlit windward face of a tower; the pressure there would flatten them against the granite like wax. Instead, when they must rest, they sweep into the turbulent slip-vortices in the lee. Here, the world turns inside-out. The air does not roar; it rattles and thumps like a loose sail. 

In these vertical dead-zones, they nest in the ancient pebble-hollows. Their young are born not from brittle eggs, which would vibrate to powder on the rock, but from leathery, adhesive pouches that the females cement into the stone with an anal gland secretion that cures in the dry wind like epoxy. A shearman chick does not have wings when it hatches; it is a grotesque, three-clawed thing, mostly belly and hooked beak, that clambers through the guano crust with blinding speed, its skin an oily slate-gray to absorb the ambient UV without burning. It will spend its first three years inside a two-foot-wide hole, staring out into the roaring cathedral, watching its parents hang frozen in the light, waiting for the day its shoulder-clasps harden and it can drop belly-first into the shear.

### The Groove-Runners

Beneath the birds, flat against the granite marrow, lives that which does not fly.

In the flute-grooves—the vertical channels eaten into the towers by millions of years of sand-wash—there is a creature shaped precisely like the space it inhabits. It is the *scaph*, a mammal that has surrendered all pride of form to become a wedge.

No wider than a man’s forearm and nearly five feet long, the scaph is entirely flattened, like an eel carved from salt-cured hide. It possesses no neck; its skull tapers directly into a blunt, chisel-nosed wedge plated with thick, shovel-like keratin. Its limbs are set so far to the sides that it cannot walk on flat ground; it does not crawl so much as it keys itself into the fluting. Its four feet are not soft pads but clusters of recurved, cat-claw talons that match the micro-fractures of the coarse granite crystal for crystal.

The scaph lives with its belly pressed flush to the stone. It never lifts its head; to lift its head even half an inch would allow the wind to catch underneath and peel it off the wall like a dry leaf, sending it down into the two-mile white below.

It feeds upon the liver-colored lichens and the tough mats of alpine felt. Its mouth is on the bottom of its throat, like a sturgeon’s—a rasping circular file that grinds down both lichen and stone together. It has no external ears; its auditory nerves run directly into the bones of its jaw, which remain in continuous physical contact with the granite. Through the jaw, the scaph does not hear the roar of the wind. It hears the *mountain*. It hears the granite column groan under the lateral torque of the atmospheric jet; it hears the subtle crack of microscopic fault-lines expanding in the cold; it hears the claw-fall of another scaph three hundred feet above it, moving down the fluting like a shadow.

They are solitary to the point of horror. They pass one another in the channels only during the brief season when the sun aligns with the tower’s vertical axis, blinding the gulf with a noon so bright the shadow disappears entirely. When they meet, they do not fight; they crawl over one another, one pressing flat while the other scrapes across its back, their horned hides squealing against each other in the gale, exchanging musk and scars, before continuing their vertical browse toward the clouds.

### The Eyeless Pools

In the small, perfectly circular water-cups—the holes gouged by pebble-drills where the aerosol mist settles—there is a life of absolute stillness.

These pools are never more than a few inches deep, yet they are tiny oceans that have never known a wave. The surface tension is so high, and the air immediately above the rim so carefully diverted by the curve of the stone, that the water seems more like clear resin than liquid.

In them live the *trench-mites*. They are pale, translucent arachnids the size of a fingernail, with legs so thin they are invisible except where they cast thread-like shadows on the white sand at the bottom of the pot. They do not swim; they walk the floor of the basin with a solemn, agonizing slowness, grazing on the film of blue-green bacteria that coats the submerged quartz grains.

Their entire universe is ten inches across. To them, the vast, roaring world of granite towers and hanging aeronauts does not exist; their sky is the mirror-underside of the water’s skin. If one is blown out of the pool by a freak eddy, it dries into a salt-flake before it has fallen twenty feet. If a shearman dips its bill into the cup to drink, the mite freezes, clamping itself to the sand like an anchor, waiting out the disturbance with the patient non-being of a creature whose ancestors have inhabited the exact same depression in the stone since before the surrounding valleys were emptied by the wind.

### The Bone-Whistlers

And then there are the ones who climb.

They are not native to this altitude, though they have been coming here long enough that their bodies have begun to narrow and drift toward the conditions of the rock. They are humans, though the lowlanders who live in the swamps beneath the cloud-floor would hardly recognize them.

They do not speak. Even within the lee-cracks where the wind drops to a hiss, they communicate with their hands—rapid, percussive gestures that mimic the tail-flicks of the shearmen. Their throats have adapted to save water; their tongues are permanently coated in a bitter, astringent grease made from boiled scaph-tallow that prevents the dry, cold air from cracking their mucous membranes.

They are called the *Stitchers*, or the *Bone-Ties*.

They come up from the deep cracks where the roots of the towers merge into the subterranean dark, following the ancient seams of soft pegmatite that can be worked with hand-drills. They do not climb for territory; there is nothing to govern here. They climb to maintain the *lines*.

Every tower is linked to its neighbor not by bridges—no bridge could survive the torque—but by single strands of braided sinew, dried under tension and greased with mineral wax. These lines are not for walking; they are instruments. Tied to them are hundreds of carved bone whistles, like the one left in the cleft: hollow marrow-reeds with finger-holes stopped with wooden pegs.

The Stitchers move along the vertical fluting using slings of ibex-hide and pairs of long, horn-tipped adzes. They climb in pairs, tied so closely together that if one slips, the other must instantly drop his center of mass into a groove to act as a counter-cam. They are impossibly lean; their chests are disproportionately deep, bulging like wine-skins to house lungs that can harvest the thin, blue air, but their limbs are ropy and dry as willow roots.

They are here to listen.

To a Stitcher, the roar of the wind is not noise; it is a text. It is a single, continuous chord that shifts pitch depending on the season, the density of the air, and the approach of the deep, planetary compressions that run through the troposphere like tidal bores. By anchoring whistles in the fractures—whistles tuned to distinct fourths and fifths—they can tell by the changing harmonic whether a tower is settling, whether the moisture in the mist is failing fifty leagues to the west, or whether the foundations of the world are tilting.

When a Stitcher dies, they do not drop the body into the void. To fall through the lower cloud-deck is to be lost in the blind, wet mud of the world below—an ignominious end for those who have spent their lives in the white-hot teeth of the sun. 

Instead, they wedge the dead into the deepest lee-side chimneys, facing the wall, their knees pulled to their chins. They pack the gaps around them with dried felt-mat and leave them there. Within three days, the dry flint-wind has pulled every drop of water from the flesh, turning the body into light, hard leather. In a century, the sand carried on the wind will begin to polish the shoulder blades, cutting through the skin to the white bone beneath, flute-grooving the spine until the person becomes indistinguishable from the marrow-stone itself: another rib of granite, whistling in the blast, perfectly shaped to let the roaring air slip past without purchase.
