They are built of the same dry necessities as the spires: horn, tensioned sinew, silicate plate, and cold grease. In a world without sap or blood, the body is not a sack of fluid, but an articulated instrument of levers and dry seals. Nothing here is soft; softness would yield its moisture to the magnesium glare within minutes and turn to stone.

In the upper reaches, where the gantry-branches sweep through thin violet air, live the *tallow-kites*. They do not flap; flapping would snap their brittle struts in the violent shear-winds. Instead, their wings are ribbed cantilevers of translucent, sun-cured keratin, stretched as flat and taut as drumheads over frames of hollow ironwood marrow. They have no eyes—photons in this unshielded glare would merely scorch the retina—but their blunt, wedge-shaped heads are split by deep acoustic sinuses: yawning, stepped cavities carved like the pipes of a calliope. 

To these creatures, the world is an architectural projection of sound. They do not fly blind; they fly through a blinding geometry of concussions. A kite feels the four-mile backstroke of a piston rod as a vast, solid shelf of compressed air upon which it can bank; it navigates the shrieking static vents as if they were sheer granite cliffs of pitch and frequency. When a kite dives into an exhaust plume, it is not breathing air, but tumbling through a brief, boiling vortex of volatilized hydrocarbons. It opens its throat—a dry, rasping valve lined with rows of calcified combs—to sieve out the droplets of airborne wax and tallow-spore before the freezing sunlight can crystallize them into hail. When they mate, they do so locked at the carpal joints, spinning down thousands of feet in the violent updrafts of a cylinder-bank, disengaging only when the shuddering roar of the root-plates signals the hard floor is less than fifty fathoms away.

Lower down, where the immense trunks meet the cross-bracing, the machinery becomes an ecology of tolerances. 

Here dwell the *scrapers*. They are long-bodied, segmented like tape-measures, their armor formed of overlapping scales of cold-pressed mica and graphite. They possess no true mouthparts, only grinding mandibles shaped like hardened lathe bits. Their entire existence is spent inside the guide-sleeves and bearing-races of the great connecting rods. They are the vermin of the tolerances, surviving in the hair-thin clearances between sliding ironwood surfaces. 

As the colossal timber rams plunge back and forth—forty paces thick, moving with the slow, unstoppable momentum of falling hills—the scrapers follow millimeters behind the stroke. They graze on the *frictional char*: the glassy, smoking crust of carbonized resin that cakes the walls. Their movements are terrifyingly synchronized to the engine’s tempo. If a scraper hesitates by a tenth of a second, the returning ram catches it, not squashing it into wet pulp, but milling it instantly into a superfine, silvery graphite dust. The other scrapers do not mourn or flee; they eagerly consume the dust of their pulverized kin, for it is rich in the heavy mineral lubricants their own joint-cases require to keep from cold-welding in the dry wind.

Between the trees, across the vast, vacant hexagons of the root-plain, there are no places to hide from the cold glare. Whatever travels here must be made to outrun the wind or become part of the floor.

There are things that resemble long, skeletal tripods—the *gantry-striders*. They stand three stories tall, their legs formed of bowed, spring-tempered spars of silica-wood lashed together with dried pericardial cables that hum in the wind like cello strings. They have no belly, no meat, no organs of digestion. Their central core is a single, hollow pendular weight suspended within a gimbal of bone. 

These creatures move by thermal oscillation. As the fierce sun bakes their southern spars, the fibers contract with sudden, metallic violence; the northern spars, shaded in sub-zero chill, remain lax. This continuous, uneven contraction tilts the central weight, swinging the creature forward in high, whistling, twenty-yard bounds. They migrate perpetually, tracking the sweeping, razor-sharp shadows cast by the two-thousand-foot engine-trees. Within those narrow lanes of freezing shade, they stoop on telescoping neck-joints to strip the obsidian needles from the floor—plucking up the petrified debris of ancient splinter-storms and grinding it in an internal gizzard of spinning quartz pebbles to extract the microscopic trace-metals locked within the wood-glass.

And down in the root-pavement itself, inside the hexagonal stress-cracks that run leagues deep into the petrified lignum, lives the quietest life. 

In these black, windless fissures, where the ambient temperature sits locked at forty degrees below zero, hang the *web-spindles*. They are sedentary, radial creatures that look like clusters of wire calipers held together by knots of black amber. They drop monofilament lines made of dry, friction-spun glass down into the abyssal cracks. These lines do not stick; they are acoustic sensors. The web-spindles do not hunt flesh—there is no flesh to catch. They listen for structural failure. 

For hundreds of miles beneath the surface, the continent-sized root-mats are locked in agonizing, slow-motion tectonic shifts, twisting under the torque of the engines they anchor. When a deep tap-root snaps miles below, the release of energy is seismic: a sudden, brilliant flash of piezoresistive lightning accompanied by a deafening acoustic whip-crack through the lignum. The web-spindles harvest this discharge directly. Their bodies are arrays of dry quartz nodules and brassy plates that catch the static surge, storing the electrical pulse to power their dry, internal ticking for another hundred years.

Nothing here dies in the way the wet worlds understand death. There is no putrefaction, no liquefaction, no collapse into soil. 

When a creature of this plateau reaches the end of its cycle—when its bearing-joints have ground themselves down to powder, or its horn-struts have lost their elasticity under the relentless assault of the magnesium sun—it simply seizes. It locks up. It stops in mid-stride or clings to a diagonal timber with its talons cold-welded to the bark. 

The scouring wind goes to work on it immediately, armed with microscopic flint-dust. Over decades, the fleshless, dry corpse is slowly planed down, chamfered, and polished. The wings of the tallow-kites become smooth black louvers that redirect the shear-winds into the engine air-intakes; the limbs of the striders snap and become rolling spars that clear debris from the hexagonal tracks; the bodies of the scrapers are milled into the eternal, graphite sheen that keeps the timber rams sliding, stroke after stroke, into the violent, empty brilliance of the sky.
