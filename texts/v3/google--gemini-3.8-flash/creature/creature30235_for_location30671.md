To find what remains alive here, you have to look lower than the froth, lower than the columns of yellow clay that spit and burst, down past the violent middle of the broth where nothing with an edge can survive. 

You have to look at the boundary layer.

In fluid mechanics, even in an ocean that moves with the force of an avalanche, the fluid directly in contact with a solid surface does not move at all. At the absolute floor, in a film no thicker than a fingernail, the velocity drops to zero. That paper-thin sheet of dead calm is the only territory left.

Beneath the scouring tide, pressed so hard against the green-black stone that they have no measurable profile, are the wedges. 

They are the ones who made the indentations. They did not leave; they merely shrank down into their own footprints as the world got loud. At first glance they do not register as animals, or even as distinct objects—they look like bevels, small triangular scabs of slate-colored gristle, their front edges tapering down to a single layer of cells that meets the rock with the airtight precision of a suction cup. 

They are small—rarely larger than the heel of a child’s hand—and their anatomy is entirely an argument against lift. Any curve, any sudden bump or ridge that dared to arch into the current would catch the shear force and rip the animal from the planet forever. And so they are shaped like dropped coins, chamfered at the margins, heavier than lead. Their backs are not shell and not skin, but an armor grown out of their own wastes: a dense, calcified horn impregnated with basalt dust and powdered quartz, which the torrent polishes to an oily, black mirror-finish every second of their lives.

They do not have faces. To have a face is to offer the slurry a purchase. Instead, their underside is a single, broad sole of pale, cartilaginous muscle that grips the bedrock through hydraulic negative pressure. They "walk" by loosening a millimeter of their trailing edge, allowing the endless, planetary friction overhead to shove them forward an infinitesimal fraction of an inch, and then resealing before the boiling brine can pry them off. A wedge might spend two hundred years traveling the circumference of a single cistern, guided by the shallow, worn spiral cut into the lip.

Inside those cisterns, out of the wind of the stones, the life is softer, though no less stubborn.

The builders did not dig those wells to hold water—there has always been too much water. They built them to trap stillness. Within the five-inch square of a sheltered cistern, the deafening thrum of the floor becomes a thick, rhythmic pulse, like the interior of an engine block. In this quiet, the grey tallow collects, and within the tallow lives the brood.

They look like small, jointless fingers. They have no armor yet; their flesh is the color of lard, slightly translucent, through which one can see a labyrinth of tiny, sand-filled bladders. These are their hearing organs, though "hearing" is the wrong word for an animal that lives inside an unceasing earthquake. They do not listen to sound; they balance on it. The white noise of the world falling over itself is the only medium through which they understand mass and distance. If the roar were ever to pause, even for a breath, their vascular systems would collapse; they are kept rigid by the hydrostatic weight bearing down upon them, held together from the outside in.

The tallow they eat is not organic decay. It is the grease of the water itself—the fat of millions of years of minerals rubbed together until their chemical bonds shear apart, creating long-chain hydrocarbon slimes that settle in the dead corners of the soapstone. The young chew it with flat, grinding plates set directly into the base of their bellies. They do not breathe. They do not possess lungs or gills that could be clogged by the gray flour of the flats; they absorb their sulfur and heat directly through the skin that touches the rock.

The bedrock is their hearth. Because the continental plate has been planed so thin, the deep heat of the mantle is never more than a mile or two down. The basalt is warm, almost feverish, and through it travels the only information that matters to them: the scrape of their neighbors.

They speak by shivering.

A wedge on the lip of a cistern two miles to the north will clamp its edge down hard and vibrate its ventral plate against the basalt, sending a high-frequency click through the stone. It travels faster through the floor than the roar travels through the mud. A second later, fifty wedges around the rim of another stub will drop their mantles and hum back. It is not speech as we know it—it contains no history, no names, no longing. It is a continual, lateral census. It is the stone registering its own occupants: *Here. Firm. Still held. Here. Firm. Still held.*

Above them, tangled in the current, the black hair-pelt whips and tears. It is not separate from the wedges; it is their garden, or perhaps their dead. 

When a wedge finally loses its purchase—when a freak grain of grit works beneath its leaden skirt and breaks the seal—it does not wash away into the air. It is rolled. The torrent tumbles it at two hundred miles an hour across the flats, smashing it flat against the floor until its armored back shatters and its pale meat is pulverized into strings. These strings do not rot; they cannot. The heat and the salt cure them instantly. They snag on the micro-grooves of the rock, take up the dissolved iron in the water, and begin to sprout. 

The hair is the wedge’s muscle, continuing to work after the animal is gone. It has no nervous system, but it retains the wedge’s ancient, mechanical hunger for friction. As the slurry flays the pelt, the strands convert the kinetic drag into tiny chemical currents, feeding the root-caps that bore an eighth of an inch down into the basalt. 

And from these roots, very slowly, the green-black soapstone precipitates.

The stubs are not ruins. They are not the abandoned villages of a drowned race that vanished eons ago. 

They are growing. 

A single stub takes ten thousand years to rise an inch, built grain by grain from the excretions of the hair-weed and the patient, circular labor of the wedges, who follow the spiral channels, smoothing the cured silt with their heavy, leaden bellies, cutting the square cisterns deeper with their rasping under-edges, fighting the scouring wind of the world with their own dense, unmoving bodies.

They are building the only thing that can be built on an ancient world: more world. They are making the continent heavy enough to stay. 

Sometimes, in the bruised butter-light, when a pane of surface-tension glass forms and snaps, you can catch a glimpse of the top of a stub before the boiling mud reclaims it. There are the thumb-marks. Fresh ones. The size of an apricot stone, pressed deep into the curing green sludge by something that possesses neither eyes nor voice, but knows, with the absolute mathematical certainty of five billion years of pressure, precisely how much weight a corner must bear if it is not to be wiped away.
