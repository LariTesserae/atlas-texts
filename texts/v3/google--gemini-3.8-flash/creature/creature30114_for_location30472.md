To survive in the cleft, the body must first change its relationship to weight. 

The people who tread the larch platforms have developed feet like broad wedges. The calluses on their heels and the pads of their toes are white, thick, and permanently sodden, resembling the water-logged rind of salt pork. They walk without an up-and-down swing, sliding the soles forward in a low, dragging shuffle that keeps their center of gravity pinned to their hips; to lift a heel suddenly on the wet, algal sheen of the timber is to invite the void. Because the air is starved of thickness, their ribcages are unnaturally wide, flared out like split casks beneath skin that is fish-belly pale and perpetually cold to the touch. 

They are nearly all stone-deaf by their twentieth winter. No human ear can endure the cathedral of falling water without the tiny bones inside the skull breaking or calcifying into silence. The old ones carry a milky, unblinking stillness in their eyes, but they do not experience this as a loss; to them, hearing is a vulnerability of the lowlands, a softness that lets the world leak in. To preserve what little sanity remains during their first years on the scaffolds, children have their ears plugged with plugs of rolled wool soaked in melted mutton tallow and spruce-gum. By the time the wax shrinks and falls away, the stone has already done its work: the ear canal is dead, and the world has narrowed to the vibration in the soles of the feet, the rattle of the ribs against the chest wall, and the twitch of knuckles.

Their speech is entirely physical. It takes place below the level of the chin, so as not to distract the eyes from the path ahead. It is an insectile language: two fingers tapped against the tendon of the wrist means *slack the rope*; a flat palm pressed hard against the hip-bone means *grease*; a rhythmic flutter of the knuckles across the thigh indicates that a stone has detached from the high rims and is currently dropping through the mist. For things of great moment—a birth, a break in the retaining pins, an approaching gale—two people will step close, wedge their shoulders together against a rock-bolt, and press their foreheads bone-to-bone. Through the skull, the voice can still carry: not as sound, but as an abrasive, wet hum passed directly through the frontal plates.

They clothe themselves against the dampness with an obsessive, greasy ingenuity. Nothing made of dry wool or tanned cowhide lasts here; the moisture rots sheep’s fleece into black porridge within months, and raw leather softens until it pulls through the eyelets like boiled fat. Instead, they wear smocks sewn from the oiled intestines of river oxen hauled up from the plains, lined with layers of hammered felt that has been boiled in pine-pitch. These garments do not drape; they stand stiff as wooden armor, reeking of rancid lard and turpentine, shedding the spray so cleanly that an inhabitant coming in from an eight-hour shift at the winches can be wiped dry with a handful of lichen in three strokes.

Underneath the timber stages, where the larch logs butt against the cold mirrors of schist, lives the settlement’s only true companion: the pale, spindle-legged salamanders, which the people call *crawlers* or *finger-fish*. They are blind, translucent things, scarcely thicker than a willow switch, with external gills like fringes of blood-red moss behind their blunt heads. They do not swim—the water moves too violently for swimming—but cling with six-clawed digits to the hairy underside of the beams, grazing the velvety coats of bacterial gel that feed on the mountain’s mineral sweat. 

The inhabitants do not eat the crawlers; their flesh tastes of brimstone and wet tin. Instead, they use them as indicators of decay. A crawler will not walk on timber that has begun to develop the red, crumbly heart-rot. When the greasy grease-gatherers see the salamanders abandoning a high platform, moving in long, sluggish white lines toward the stone shelf, they know the oak pegs inside the rock have sheared, and the crew goes out with split-wedges and sledges before the shelf drops into the void.

Food is an affair of ropes and grey paste. It is impossible to cultivate anything in the spray except the liverworts, which are scraped from the rock with horn spades, soaked in lye to draw out their blistering bitterness, and boiled into a thick, mucilaginous gruel that tastes of peat-silt and iron. Protein comes up from below, or it drops from above. Twice a month, the great hand-spokes are manned, and the hemp lines that disappear into the grey belly of the cleft groan under the weight of willow hampers. From the valleys far below come round loaves of hard, black rye, blocks of grey salt, and slabs of dried goat mutton that have the consistency of pine boards. 

Sometimes, the draft brings gifts. When an autumn blizzard drives flocks of high-altitude finches or migrating thrushes against the rock-split, the birds are sucked into the down-draft, their hollow bones shattered against the wet quartz by the acoustic pressure before they even strike the walls. They fall through the scaffolds by the hundreds, soaked bundles of feathers that the children snatch from the safety nets strung beneath the walkways. The boys who work these nets are light, wasted things, seven or eight years old, tied to the main larch timbers by ten paces of braided hemp around their waists; they scramble across the wet cords on all fours like harvestmen spiders, gathering the dead birds into gut-bags slung between their teeth.

There is no dirt in which to bury those who die. To wedge a corpse into a rock fissure is unthinkable; the dead would bloat, rot, and foul the hairline channels that deliver the sweet, drinkable condensation to the wooden troughs. 

When an inhabitant ceases to feel the drone—when the heart fails or a knee splits on the grease-timbers—the ritual is brief and silent. The community does not gather; there is no space for an assembly on shelves four feet wide. The family strips the body of its intestine-coat, unlaces the tallow-treated boots, and removes the iron-pegs from the belt. The hair is shorn to be twisted into bowstrings and winch-packings. Then, without a prayer—for no deity could hear through three miles of solid, singing quartz—they slide the body off the lowest scaffold into the blue-black gradient of the mist.

It does not drop cleanly. The gale that licks the rift catches the body, turns it, tosses it out toward the center like a leaf caught in an oven’s draft, and then rolls it down into the dark. Within five seconds, it is indistinguishable from the spray, erased by the light that bounces off the wet mirrors of stone, absorbed into the unending percussion of the water. 

Those left on the ledge do not watch it vanish. They have already turned their backs to the emptiness, their broad, soft feet feeling for the raised grain of the pale larch, their fingers searching for the next cold, wet peg that needs driving.
