The people who occupy the four-story brick stacks do not descend to the flags except on wooden pattens shod with strips of cured belting. To step into the basin floor in ordinary boots is to risk having the horn scrapers of the spindle-whelks test the welt within five minutes; to stand still for twenty is to have the leather rasped down to the sock.

They are small-boned people, narrow in the shoulders, with the pale, thick eyelids common to those who spend their lives under low arc-light. Because the basin never warms and never freezes, their clothing has no seasons. They wear tight-sleeved smocks of coarse unbleached twill, saturated with boiled linseed until the fabric is as stiff and waterproof as dried skate-skin, under heavy aprons of lead-riveted split cowhide. Around their throats they knot rags of greasy calico to turn the cold drip from the I-beams above.

They are almost entirely deaf, though they do not consider this an infirmity; it is merely the state of adulthood. Children are born with hearing as sharp as that of any hill-dweller, but between their eighth and twelfth years the ear canal gradually thickens, the drum calcifying behind a protective plug of dry, waxy tallow until the roaring of the walking beams is reduced to an internal, rhythmic pulse felt only in the lower jaw and the coccyx. They speak very little with the tongue. When they communicate, they strike the ironwork: a short tap with the brass heel of an oil-can or the flat of a spanner carries through three hundred paces of interconnected casting and girder far more clearly than a shout could travel through the roaring air. A mother summons a child by striking three brisk triplets into the web of the nearest gantry stair; the child, feeling the iron bite against their palm four floors below, climbs the rungs without looking up.

Inside the brick houses, there is no furniture made of wood. Timber rots or softens in the cellar draft; instead, everything is cold-rolled plate, slate slab, and cast iron. Their beds are iron wire cradles hung from the floor joists by heavy coiled springs, not for comfort, but to isolate the sleeper from the continuous fifty-cycle tremor of the basin floor. A sleeper whose bed touched the brick would wake with aching kidneys and blood in the urine after three months.

Their diet is narrow, pale, and entirely dependent on the engines. 

Twice daily, the older children—whose weight is not yet great enough to crack the whelk-crust on the flags—crawl out along the overhead condenser pipes carrying long wicker scoops shaped like wind-socks. They thrust these into the gray columns of rising midges. A single pass yields three or four pounds of the blind, hairless insects, which have no wings to speak of, only fat, milky bellies full of the fatty distillate they skimmed from the condenser sumps. These are brought into the kitchens, washed in the alkaline drip from the lead gutters to cut the mineral taste, and pressed in screw-presses into firm, grayish loaves that smell faintly of machine-oil and parsnips. When sliced and fried in the tallow skimmed from the steam cylinders, the bread has the texture of pressed curd. 

To supplement this, they keep *tallow-coots* in wicker coops hung beneath the eaves. These are domestic offshoots of the zinc-skinned gulls, shorter in the wing, with blunt, scoop-like bills. The people feed them the larger midges and the tough, yellowish grubs that breed in the leather packing of the pump pistons. The coots lay small, perfectly spherical eggs with shells as brittle and dark as graphite, each containing a yolk the color of axle-grease that tastes strongly of sulfur and cold marrow.

Water is drawn from the dead-end cisterns where the overhead condensation collects. It is so hard with dissolved lime and iron scale that it leaves a white crust on the teeth of those who drink it unboiled; every kitchen hearth features a shallow lead pan that simmers perpetually, casting off a bitter, flat steam that leaves a frost of salt on the window panes.

Beneath the flags, in the spaces where the foundations of the ashlar walls step out into the cold bedrock, lives the third partner of the district’s economy: the *pipe-rats*, though they have neither fur nor tails. They are soft, long-bodied salamandroids, eighteen inches from snout to vent, with skin the translucent, bluish-gray of skim milk. Their eyes are absent, replaced by two pits of sensitive, shivering vibrissae that read the pressure changes in the steam vents. They live in the warmth of the subterranean flues, grazing upon the thick, white felting of mineral-digesting bacterial mats that line the wastewater culverts. 

The people do not hunt the pipe-rats for meat—their flesh is bitter with copper salts—but prize them for their fat. Once every quarter, when an engine is stopped for an hour to replace a worn bronze shim, men drop through the manholes into the dry sumps with iron tongs and heavy sackcloth bags. The rats they draw up are thrown into copper rendering-pots over coal fires. Their fat does not smoke; it melts into a heavy, greenish oil that remains liquid even in the dampest cellar air. This is the oil that fills the lamps and lubricates the delicate toggle-joints of the arc lights.

Nothing leaves the basin, because there is no road out that does not require an ascent up three tiers of iron ladders bolted into thirty feet of sweating ashlar. The people have no carts; they have no wheels larger than the hand-trucks they use to roll barrels of re-cast shims from the domestic forges to the plinths. 

When a person dies, there is no burial in earth, for there is no earth to receive them. The corpse is stripped, washed in the cold condensate, and laid out upon the low iron platform directly beneath the counterweight of the easternmost walking beam—the one the people call *The Weaver*. Over the course of twelve hours, the spindle-whelks find it. They do not tear or mangle; their horn scrapers are small, each stroke removing only a speck of tallow-rubbed skin no larger than a grain of salt. Within three days, the bone is scraped clean to the blue-white calcium, polished as smooth as the piston rods themselves. 

The family then gathers the dry, gray cordage that the whelks have excreted around the perimeter of the platform. This material, composed of the dead person's subcutaneous fat combined with the horn-dust and iron filings of the machine plinth, is extraordinarily dense. It is tamped into molds with an iron pestle, dried over the flue-mouths, and used to repair the fire-brick linings of the boiler hearths.

The clean bones are taken up into the houses. The long thigh-bones are ground into fine, white abrasive powder used to lap the seats of the high-pressure relief valves. The smaller knuckles and vertebrae are threaded onto iron stay-wires to act as rattle-stops, keeping the high-voltage lines from chattering against the brickwork when the north wind blows across the lip of the basin.

In this way, the district’s mass remains constant. A child is born; forty pounds of midge-bread and sixteen gallons of cistern-drip become ninety pounds of gristle and bone; the bone becomes valve-paste; the valve-paste helps hold thirty atmospheres of dry steam inside a cylinder that has not been cold since the grandfather of the oldest woman was lowered down the north wall in a coal-basket.

Around noon, when the overhead clouds take on a slightly yellow cast that passes for bright day, the children sometimes sit on the lowest rungs of the ladders, their pattens dangling three inches above the lead-colored shells of the whelks. They hold pieces of wire with bent pins at the ends, dropping them into the crevices between the flags to fish for the small, blind crabs that scavenge the scraps dropped by the gulls. When a crab takes the pin, the child jerks it up, bites off its soft, pale legs with their lime-crusted teeth, and spits the shell into the lead gutter, where the cold, alkaline drop washes it, inch by inch, down into the silence of the cisterns.
