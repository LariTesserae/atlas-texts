To inhabit a place that has forgotten the habit of falling, one must have very little use for an interior. 

The chalk-white crawlers upon the undersides of the lintels are the most visible of these dwellers, but they are not the architects, nor are they vermin. They are more like the dry pins that hold a folding rule together. Seen from beneath, as they span a gap of three hundred feet of indigo air with four of their ten limbs anchored into the mortarless joints, they appear flat—no thicker than the split flagstones they mimic. They have no bellies. Their undersides are concave troughs of dry, pale horn, grooved like the underside of a millstone. 

They do not hunt in packs, yet they are never entirely apart; if one shifts its grip three miles away on the face of a sheared terrace, the vacuum created by its moving pad draws a minute, compensatory shudder from another resting on the flank of an inverted stair. In their hollow carapaces, they harbor no organs, only chambers of nested vacuum. When they breed—which occurs once in several hundred of the city’s silent shifts—they do not touch. Two will hang opposite each other across an alleyway, extending their longest, needle-tipped calipers until the shadows of their limbs overlap on the pavement below. Where those dry shadows cross and remain crossed for a year without the light shifting, an efflorescence begins to rise from the flags: a tiny, brittle node of calcium, perfectly square, which peels itself up from the shale after nine decades and begins to crawl toward the nearest seam.

They feed upon the city’s double-occupancies. Where an avenue folds in on itself during a barometric drop, creating two parallel geometries that share a single line of stone, the crawlers wedge themselves into the seam like chisels. They draw the redundant dimension into their bodies, flattening the fold until the street snaps straight again with a cold, ringing shudder. A crawler that has gorged itself on excess space does not fatten; it simply grows pale, its plates turning from gray-shale to the color of powdered bone, and its gait slows until it cannot be pried from the masonry by a wedge of steel.

***

In the tenements themselves, behind the doors eight feet high and two fingers wide, the occupants are of another order entirely. 

They are things of one profile. If you were to look into one of the windowless basalt cells from the lintel, you would perceive it as entirely empty, washed by the dim, sourceless twilight. But if you were to step inside and turn your shoulder to match the door’s extraordinary taper, you would find that the chamber is crowded.

They stand like upright blades of mica, dry and stiff, rooted by their own weight to the floor. They are taller than men, but have no depth; their thickness is that of a heavy vellum or a flake of split slate. Because they possess no third dimension, they cannot be seen frontally; they exist only as an edge, a vertical seam of dark gray that fractures the light behind it. 

Yet they are not incorporeal. When the floor slides, their bases click against the flags with the sound of dropped coins. They have no faces, though near the upper third of their length there are fine, vertical slits that open and close like the leaves of a dry shutter, tasting the mineral drift that rises from the floorless abyss. 

They do not sleep, because they have no darkness to close out; their eyes, if those dry slits can be called such, look only sideways, perceiving two entirely different districts simultaneously—one eye watching the endless fall of the shale-drop to the west, the other fixed upon a blank, unmortared wall three inches away. 

These are the measurers. It is their weight, multiplied across millions of stacked cells throughout the five-hundred-tier blocks, that governs the lateral migration of the districts. They do not think as animals do, through the transit of chemical fires across salt membranes; their minds are systems of balance, like the escapements of heavy, unlubricated clocks. They stand for millennia without swaying. Then, as a single mass, an entire tenement of them will lean three degrees to the south. 

The basalt below them responds. The friction line yields, and the entire half-league terrace glides forward into the void, its movement as smooth as a heavy drawer sliding into its bureau. They hold the angle until the city’s center of mass has corrected itself against the pull of some unseen, distant body in the trough below, and then, with a dry, unison *tink* like needles falling on stone, they right themselves. The terrace stops. A puff of dry mica-flour rises from the seam, hangs motionless in the cold, and refuses to drop.

***

Between these high, upright blades and the crawlers of the lintels, there are the drift-things—beings that have no fixed form, but are simply the aggregate consciousness of the mica storms.

They are not spirits. They are made of the sharp, triangular flakes of gray shale-leaf that rise from the basement pits when the air thins to a vacuum. In the plazas, when the pressure drops until the blood would boil in a man's veins, these flakes do not merely whirl; they *cohere*. They form into vast, multi-jointed ribbons thirty yards long, turning through the air with a crisp, dry rustling like the turning of a million parchment pages.

These ribbons have no head or tail, but they possess an appetite for continuity. They thread the needle-bridges. They slip through the narrow apertures of the city, weaving in and out of the tenements like gray thread through a loom. As they pass the planar dwellers in their cells, the friction of their stone-scales against the edge-beings generates faint, silent discharges of pale violet light—the only fire this city has ever known, a cold static that leaves the stone smelling of nothing at all.

The mica-ribbons are the messengers of the high gates. When one has wound its way through forty miles of vertical labyrinth, gathering the precise configuration of the shadows and the degree of tilt in each terrace, it climbs. It coils around the sheer, unscaleable towers that scrape the black ceiling of the world, shedding flakes as it rises, until only a tight, whistling spiral of dust reaches the summit.

There, it throws itself into the empty frame of the lone, floating cyclopean gate that hangs anchored to nothing in the twilight.

What receives it on the other side of that arch is neither god nor machine, but the city's future self. 

Through the gate, where the patch of air is blacker than the indigo around it, something sits upon the lip of the threshold. It has no shape that the eye can reconcile with gravity; it appears by turns as a heap of dry plumb-bobs, an arrangement of unhewn megaliths that balance on their sharpest points, or an immense, folded pair of stone compasses whose legs span five miles of nothingness. 

It does not move. It has never moved. But it breathes—not air, for there is none in the high gate, but the sheer, geometric possibility of the void below. As the mica-ribbon dissolves into its shadow, the thing adjusts a joint. A hinge of dry slate, eighty cubits thick, groans without sound. 

And miles away, far down in the trough where the eye stopped looking, a plinth of gray stone that has not yet been named rises five inches through the dust, feeling for the cold, unyielding soles of the blades above it, waiting for the habit of falling to break once more.
