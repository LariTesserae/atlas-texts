To understand what moves through the stone flutes, one must first understand that to an organism here, gravity and water are the same force. They are not distinguished. Downwardness is an unbroken, dragging weight that never relents, compounded by the frictionless glaze of the stone and the vibration that tries constantly to shake anything loose from its moorings.

Nothing here is soft in the way lowland creatures are soft. There is no tissue that can afford to swell or billow; an edge caught by the water is a lever that tears the animal away into the dark lower reaches where the needles close into grinding mills. Everything is flat, laminar, and hydraulic.

The creatures that inhabit the intermediate depths—twenty to forty feet down, where the sun is reduced to a thin, cold blade of silver that shifts only an inch an hour—are built like hydraulic wedges. They are neither fish nor salamander, though their skin has the dense, rubbery resilience of cartilage. They have no necks. Their heads taper directly into wide, spade-shaped thoraces that are thicker in the center and thin out at the edges to a knife-sharp margin of flexible, muscular gristle. When the water sheets over them, it does not push against them; it presses them harder against the quartz-diorite. The faster the current runs down the pillar, the more immovably they are sealed to it.

They have abandoned limbs in favor of walking ribs—dozens of paired, chisel-pointed spurs of modified dentine that protrude through the underside of their lateral skirts. These spurs do not reach forward and pull; they ratchett. They engage the micro-fissures in the slate, biting into angles invisible to the human eye. To watch one climb is not to watch an animal walking, but to watch an ivory-toothed zip slowly closing upward against the cataract. They can move only up or down along the vertical cleavage lines. To cross horizontally from one facet of a needle to another requires an act of contortion so violent and exposed that it seems to happen only once or twice in a lifespan, usually when an individual has outgrown the width of its cleft and begins to suffocate against the converging rock.

They are milky, entirely without melanin, but they are not translucent. Their skin is packed with microscopic platelets of guanine or an allied mineral, giving them the dull, dense luster of wet soapstone or lard. Because there is no light to see by, they have no eyes, not even the sunken pits or vestigial nerves of cave fish. Their entire sensory universe is the stone’s acoustic load. 

The needles, vibrating at different pitches according to their girth and the volume of water rushing down their flanks, are individual bells. A creature resting in a joint can feel the drone of the entire plateau through its breastbone. It knows when a windstorm has struck three miles to the west because the fundamental frequency of the ridge shifts a half-step down under the increased wind-shear; it knows when an insect has hit the water thirty feet above because the flutter of six tiny legs against the wet prism produces a treble stutter that travels down the stone faster than the insect itself is washed down.

They do not hunt with jaws. Their mouths are ventral, placed far back beneath the shield, consisting of an oval vacuum-disk lined with revolving rows of silica-reinforced plates. When they reach an area where the water weeps from an iron seam—leaving behind a rust-colored smear of chemotrophic sludge—they do not pause. They plane it off. They plane down to the raw diorite, leaving a track so clean the rock looks newly fractured. When a dead thing washes down—a high-flying hawkmoth battered by the summit squalls, or a migrant locust with its wings glued together by surface tension—the scraper-plates do not chew it; they draw it into the vacuum and shear it into hair-thin strips against the rock itself, using the mountain as an anvil.

Farther down, where the needles press so close together that a sheet of paper could scarcely pass between them, the character of the life changes. The water here does not fall as a veil; it is squeezed. It moves under immense hydrostatic pressure, forced through capillary gaps in a deafening hiss that is higher in pitch than the wind-roar above.

Here live the thread-dwellers. They are long, white, and stiff, like wires of extruded bone. They do not cling with skirts; they are permanently wedged. They have evolved an internal skeleton that acts like a scissor-jack, expanding outward against opposing faces of slate to lock them in place across the void. They live horizontally, bridging gaps no wider than a finger, held rigid by their own lateral thrust. Their bodies are grooved along the back, forming miniature conduits that channel the water through their gills without allowing the current to hit their flanks. 

They are passive interceptors. Between their splayed, bracing tips, they spin small, incredibly tough webs made of a keratinous mucus that does not dissolve in cold water. These nets are rarely larger than a coin. They catch the fine organic detritus that escapes the upper scrapers: the scales of moth wings, bits of chitinous leg, microscopic cysts of dry-land spores blown into the stratosphere from forests hundreds of leagues distant. Every few hours, a thread-dweller contracts its lateral jacks, rotates ninety degrees within its groove to clear the net with its mouthparts, and then springs its ribs back out, wedging itself anew into the vibrating stone with an audible *tink*.

In this lower dark, there is no season. The cold water is forty-one degrees Fahrenheit in July and forty-one degrees in December, kept liquid by its own velocity and the perpetual cloud blanket that insulates the plateau’s root. Because there is no change in light or temperature, their lives have no rhythm. Growth is so slow that a creature the length of a forearm may have witnessed the erosion of an inch of stone beneath its pads—an existence measured not in winters, but in the slow deepening of the flutes.

Reproduction is the only event that breaks the vertical solitude, and it is terrible in its economy. There can be no free-swimming larval stage; any egg laid into the current would be cast out over the cliffs within seconds, falling into the warm valleys below to burst or dry. 

Instead, the females do not release their young. They are viviparous, but in a manner that resembles mineral deposition more than birth. A female will climb to the highest point her broad shell will permit—just to the edge of the glare, where the water is thinnest and the air begins to suck at her margins with desiccating power. There, where the rock is warmest from the trapped solar heat of midday, she wedges her calcified spurs into a seam and simply remains. She allows the wind and the light to take her edges. As her margins dry, they adhere to the slate with the permanence of a fossil. She dies upright, pinned against the stone by her own desiccated rim, creating a hollow, water-filled blister beneath her corpse.

Within that sheltered pocket, where the current is diverted by the dead mother’s dorsal keel, two or three young feed upon her internal soft tissues until their own scrapers are hard enough to cut iron. When the winter gales finally tear the mother’s empty shell away, the young are already anchored, flat and white, their little dentine spurs sunk deep into the grain of the rock where she stood.

Above them all, at the summits of the needles—the sun-blasted tips that poke out of the spray into the burning azure—there is only one inhabitant, and it belongs neither to the stone nor the water.

They are small, long-tailed spiders, pale as bleached salt, that live in the dead-zones behind the windward faces. They do not venture down into the wet; the surface tension would pull their dry bodies flat and drown them in an instant. They live entirely on the needles' parched, sun-baked leeward facets, where the rock reaches ninety degrees while the water two inches away is near freezing. 

They run across the glare with erratic, vibrating speed, their feet tipped with dense brushes of hydrophobic hairs that allow them to skate across the edges of the falling water sheets as if they were ice. They live by salvage. When the updrafts cast thousands of lowland insects against the wet mirrors of the summits, the spiders race to the edge of the wet zone, snatch the drowning flies from the water film before they are drawn into the descending flutes, and haul them back to the dry quartz tips.

They build nothing. They have no silk for webs, only a single, heavy drag-line that they cement to the very pinnacle of the needle. When the afternoon wind rises to gale force, shearing across the plateau with enough violence to strip feathers from an eagle, the spiders do not seek shelter. They gather on the highest stone blades, lift their abdomens into the roar, and pay out lines of thick, coarse cable into the void until the drag is immense. 

Then they let go.

They do not float away. They are so heavy, and their lines so stout, that they are held suspended horizontally in the jet of the wind, trailing out from the stone needles like hundreds of white pennants, vibrating so fast in the gale that they disappear into a blur. They hang there for hours in the blinding white light, suspended over thousands of feet of space, held rigidly motionless by the balance between gravity and the mountain’s breath, drinking the cold spray that flies off the teeth of the plateau until the wind drops at twilight and sets them down again upon the stone.
