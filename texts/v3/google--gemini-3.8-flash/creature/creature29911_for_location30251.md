To understand what lives in the split, one has to understand that they did not arrive from the lawn outside, nor did they crawl down through the floorboards from the daylight world of the pump house. 

They were already inside the siltstone.

For several million years, their ancestors existed as minute, desiccated cysts—microscopic lenticular capsules wedged between the paper-thin laminae of the shale, dormant in an anoxic freeze that smelled of compressed mud and dead ferns. When the bedrock fractured under the unbalanced thrust of the pump’s eight-ton piston, the rock didn't just break; it unsealed a fossil horizon. The first warm, grease-thickened condensate that seeped into the rift was not a contaminant to them; it was an amniotic wash.

They are known, if they are known to science at all, as a species of aberrant subterranean isopod—though to call them woodlice is to ignore the degree to which they have surrendered ordinary crustacean anatomy to the architecture of the seam. 

### The Carapace-Smiths

Individually, they have no names, but their work divides them more sharply than sex or age. 

Their dorsal shields are not rounded like those of garden sowbugs; they are pressed flat, squared off at the margins like slate floor-tiles, with chamfered edges that allow them to slide under one another without catching on the razor-sharp stone terraces. Their natural chitin is a pale, tallowy horn, translucent enough that, in the grubs, one can see the dark pump-oil moving through the branchial loops of their digestive tracts. 

In the adults, however, this horn is invisible, buried under layers of masonry.

They are mineral-hungered. They do not merely tolerate iron; they are geophagous for it. Their fourth pair of thoracic limbs has been modified into heavy, pincer-like rasping tools tipped with silica-hardened calcified teeth. With these, they gnaw at the weeping base of the foundation bolts, swallowing the rust flakes and the microscopic particles of swarf cast off by the pump’s cross-head guides. 

This metal is not excreted wastefully. Their gastric glands extract the iron salts and pump them into the soft dermal tissues just beneath the cuticle before a molt. When the new shell hardens in the tallowy heat, it does not set as horn; it precipitates as a brittle, hematite-rich crust, dull orange and magnetic. 

The swarf they wear on their backs is not gathered by accident. A specimen will spend hours beneath the viaduct of the *11/16* wrench, carefully turning a curled brass shaving with its first three pairs of walking legs, tasting the metal with the sensitive sensory hairs along its mouthparts. It prefers brass to cold iron, likely because copper resists the mold that occasionally blooms in the stagnant corners where the tallow pools. Once satisfied, it presses the shaving into a bed of thick, sulfurous cement exuded from glands behind its head. The cement cures in minutes under the radiant heat of the pump’s underbelly, turning the animal into a low, angular mound of metallic detritus.

When hundreds of them crawl together, their brass and iron dorsal plates chime against one another—a continuous, low, dry rattle like dried peas shaken in a copper kettle, nearly lost beneath the thudding pulse of the piston.

### The Strata of the Seam

Because the fracture is narrow—a wedge that tapers from two inches to a hairline—their society is strictly bathymetric.

1. **The Floor-Raspers:** At the cold, narrow bottom of the rift, where the siltstone is still gray and fresh, live the newly emerged juveniles. They are small as turnip seeds, unmineralized, blind, and milky-white. They graze exclusively on the bacterial skins that form where the grease meets the cool rock. They are the stone-cleavers. By scraping away the soft clay binders of the siltstone to reach the mineral salts, they undermine the laminae. Over three months, their grazing has already widened the wedge floor by three-sixteenths of an inch. They are carving their own world larger, grain by microscopic grain.

2. **The Gutters:** The middle tier is composed of the mature non-breeders. They form a living shingle. Their legs are short, thick, and peg-like, hooked at the tips with curved dactyls that can grip the slickest iron plate. They are flat-bodied and broad. When they lock their margins together, they form a vast, sloping deck that spans the gap between the rock ledges and the rusted foundation bolts. They are a plumbing system. Their dorsal plates are grooved with sub-millimeter channels that draw the warm condensate away from the greasy ceiling by capillary action, directing it down into the middle of the cluster where the relative humidity must be maintained at precisely ninety-eight percent to prevent their leaf-gills from drying out.

3. **The Crown-Tenders:** The uppermost layer lives directly against the cast iron of the pump. The iron here is hot—not scalding, but hot enough that a man could not comfortably keep his palm against it for a minute. The lice here have lost their metal carapaces entirely; their backs are covered in a dense, velvety felt of modified setae that insulate them from the metal. Their sole function is to manipulate the eggs.

### The Brood-Clutches and the Fuse

The eggs are not laid in dirt. They are fastened with tallow-glue directly to the iron engine belly, arranged in neat, concentric rings that follow the spiral tool-marks left by the lathe that faced the pump’s cylinder head forty years ago. 

Each egg is the size of a bird-shot pellet, jacketed in a tough, rubbery membrane that resists the grease. The Crown-Tenders stroke them continuously with their antennae. These antennae are not simple feelers; their segments are tipped with micro-spatulae that spread a thin layer of liverwort oil over each capsule, protecting the embryos from the anaerobic slime that would otherwise choke them.

Inside the glass fuse cylinder—the one wedged into the northern corner—the situation is different. 

The glass is an anomaly in their world: perfectly smooth, chemically inert, and clear. The three grubs inside did not crawl in through the brass end-caps; they were deposited there as eggs through a hairline fracture in the seal by an elder whose ovipositor was long and hair-fine. 

These three are giants compared to the others. Sheltered from the crushing weight of the tiers, bathed in the amber light from the inspection port that concentrates through the glass like a burning-lens, they have grown smooth and fat. They have no swarf on their backs. Their skins are the color of cold bacon fat, translucent and segmental. 

They do not graze. They lie on their sides, their limbs curled against their bellies, sustained entirely by the oil that wicks through the fuse's broken cap. They are tuned to the machine in a way the others are not. When the pump hits the top of its stroke—that brief, half-second hesitation where the valves flip with an internal *clack-whump*—the three grubs twitch their thoracic rings in unison, three small, white pendulums keeping time with five hundred horse-power of steam. They are being fattened. In time, when the glass is finally broken by the shifting of the shale, they will emerge not as scrapers or gutters, but as the thick-shelled queens whose salivary cement will anchor the colony to the foundation bolts when the winter cold sets in.

### The Outliers: The Thread-Spinners and the Blind

The isopods are not alone. A crevice this rich in heat and fat inevitably breeds its own satellites.

In the dead spaces between the foundation nuts—where the liverwort forms a black, oily felt—there live colonies of minute, blind collembolans, pale as dandruff. They do not jump like their surface kin; in this density, a leap would mean being pinned and crushed between the moving plates of the lice. Instead, they run through the hair-roots like lice through pelt, feeding on the shedding skins of the isopods.

Deep in the back-crevice, where the light from the amber bulb cannot reach and the seam narrows to the thickness of a knife blade, lives the only thing in the pocket that has legs longer than its body. 

It is an arachnid, but one that has forgotten silk and venom. It is no larger than a flax seed, flattened until it is scarcely thicker than a sheet of grease-proof paper. It has ten legs instead of eight—two pairs having evolved into long, multi-jointed whips that it inserts between the plates of the sleeping gutters. It does not eat the lice. It drinks from the lateral gutters along their flanks, siphoning off the emulsion of water, iron salts, and engine oil that the lice have spent hours collecting from the ceiling. 

The lice tolerate it. When the whip-legs tap a gutter’s flank, the louse simply slows its crawl, tilts its dorsal plate by a fraction of a degree, and allows the creature to drink. It is a tax paid for maintenance; as the long-legged thing drinks, its minute chelicerae pick away the choking iron crust that threatens to freeze the louse’s leg-joints, cleaning the hinges with the delicate precision of a watchmaker.

### The Cycle of the Engine

Life in the fracture has no morning. It has only the *Duty* and the *Blow-Down*.

When the pump is working under load, pumping thousands of gallons from the sump wells below the city, the iron overhead is drum-tight, hot, and weeping. The pocket is an engine of production: carapaces are thickened, stone is excavated, eggs are turned. The world is small, warm, and secure, lit by the steady amber sun of the work-bulb above the mesh.

The true terror of the pocket is the *Stand-By*.

Once every three or four weeks, the engine room above goes quiet. The piston slows, shudders, and comes to rest with a hollow, brassy sigh. The steady *thump-thump-thump* that has formed the heartbeat of thirty generations of lice ceases.

When the vibration stops, the pocket freezes. 

The lice do not move. They do not rasp. They drop their bellies flat against the rock, dig their claw-tips into the laminae, and interlock their squared plates until the entire floor becomes a single, continuous sheet of cold-rolled iron and brass swarf. The heat leaves the iron pump belly first; the droplets stop falling; the amber bulb in the port above is switched off by an engineer’s thumb, and darkness drops into the seam like a stone.

In the black, cooling silence, the only sound is the contracting of the stone—a series of sharp, dry *cracks* that sound like pistol shots in the four-foot space. 

The lice wait. They can wait for months, their metabolisms dropped to near-zero, their gills sealed behind a meniscus of hardened grease. They wait through the bitter subterranean chill, through the dry drafts that find their way down through the pump-hall floor, until at last, deep in the earth, a valve is opened. 

A tremor runs through the siltstone. A drop of warm, tallow-scented condensate falls from the overhead flange and strikes the rusted plate of a gutter with a hollow *tock*.

The plate tilts. The antenna snips the water in two. And the terrace begins to move again.
