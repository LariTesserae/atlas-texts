To begin to understand who lives here, one must first look at the eyes. 

In this chasm, the light does not shine down upon faces from an angle; it rises from the white floor, bounces between opposing slabs of chalk, and fills the hollows beneath brows and cheekbones. There are no shadows to rest the vision. Consequently, the people have developed a heavy, horizontal hooding of the upper lid—a thick fold of cartilage-stiffened skin that lowers like a visor, leaving only a slit no wider than the crack between two courses of shale. Their irises are not dark, as one might expect of those who dwell in deep shade, but a startling, washed-out hazel or clear whey-gray, the pupils contracted to the size of needle-pricks from the fourth hour after dawn until the sudden fall of twilight. When they look at one another, they do not turn the head; they turn the torso in a slow, oil-smooth pivot, shielding the gaze with a raised palm or the edge of a wet rush-mat.

They are small-bodied—seldom taller than the shoulder of a lowlander—with dense, heavy bones shaped by a lifetime of hauling themselves up vertical notches. Their ribs are wide and barrel-shaped to gather the heavy, low-oxygen air that sits thick as pond-mud in the bottom fifty cubits, while their fingers are broad-tipped, spatulate, and perpetually damp. The skin of their palms is not calloused into yellow horn like a farmer’s, but water-bleached, white, and pebbled like the belly of a river chub. It possesses an astonishing tactile sensitivity: an artisan here can tell the grain of a cedar lintel by the back of their knuckles, or estimate the water-depth of a cellar-pool to within a barleycorn by pressing a thumb against the tufa sill.

They call themselves nothing that translates to a nation or a clan; in their low, breathy grammar, they refer to themselves merely as *the ones who sit under the lip*. 

Because a sneeze or a shout would be an intolerable physical assault in an alley six handspans wide, their larynxes have softened. They speak entirely in the front of the mouth—a clicking of the tongue against the palate, the whistling of air through the incisors, and soft, humming glottal stops that mimic the sound of water dripping into full stone pots. When two people meet on the foot-lanes, where the slate overhang is so narrow their chests brush, they do not exchange names. Instead, the one who yields leans their back into the moss-cushioned cliff, exhales a soft *tsik*, and lets the other slide past, belly-to-belly, their felt tunics dragging together with the sound of dry grass rubbing against stone.

Their clothing is composed almost entirely of mouse-hide, feather-felt, and the beaten fibers of the dwarf-willows that scrape the upper rim. To make a single winter smock, nearly three hundred of the small, blind mice that inhabit the foundation walls are trapped, skinned, and their pelts cured with stale urine and tallow until they are as supple as chamois. These skins are sewn together with hair plucked from the necks of the elders, stitched so tightly with bone awls that no seam is visible. Over these, they wear mantles woven from the down of the chalk-swifts—pale, ash-colored garments that repel the cold condensation that constantly weeps from the ceilings.

They do not sleep lying flat. Space does not permit it, nor does the stone tolerate a body stretched out upon its chill. Instead, they sleep tucked into stone niches hollowed out behind their hearthless kitchens—sitting upright with their knees pulled against their chests, their chins resting on a crossbar of seasoned ash, wrapped in heavy, damp-proof rush bags. A whole household of nine or ten adults will nest thus in a chamber no larger than a farmer’s grain-chest, their breath mingling in a warm, sour vapor that smells of masticated water-parsnip, wet pelt, and the mineral chalk that cakes their teeth.

And then there are the other lives—those that are not human, yet are stitched into the same civic rhythm so thoroughly that the city could not stand without them.

The blind mice are not pests; they are the herds of the cleft. They are long-bodied, hairless-tailed creatures with milky, undeveloped nubs where eyes should be, living in the deliberate interstices left between the shale foundation stones. The city feeds them on a paste made from crushed liverwort and the dried maggots of the lime-flies; in return, the mice are culled on a strict lunar cycle. Their fat fills the stone lamps that burn with a clean, scentless blue flame; their blood is dried and beaten with chalk to make a hydraulic lime that mends cracked cisterns; their bones are scraped into the needles and pins that hold the felt together. They are handled with a strange, familial tenderness. A child will carry a breeding mouse in the pouch of its smock, feeling its cool, hairless feet scuttling against the skin, feeding it bits of chewed fern from the lip.

Above them, in the upper tiers where the light is most piercing, live the swifts. These birds have forgotten the wide sky. Though their ancestors may have ranged the open steppe, these small, pale variants never rise above the rim of the fissure. They spend their entire lives within the vertical shaft, fluttering from beam to beam like white moths, their wingbeats so soundless that one only knows they have passed by the brief, cold puff of air on one’s neck. They build their nests not of mud, but of a thick, gelatinous saliva mixed with the downy seed-heads of the saxifrage, gluing them directly to the dry-stacked slate. The inhabitants harvest half of every clutch of eggs, using long willow poles tipped with sheep-horn spoons, but never take the second egg. It is a treaty of three hundred years’ standing: the swifts yield food and down; the city leaves the upper fifteen cubits of wall undisturbed and sweeps the lower ledges clean of anything that might harbor the black-beetles that prey on swiftlings.

The beetles themselves are the city’s slow clockwork. They are massive, flightless things, the length of an old man’s finger, with carapaces as black and lustrous as anthracite. They live along the cedar shoring poles, moving with a ponderous, mechanical dignity, their claws making a faint *tick-tick-tick* as they grip the weathered grain. They do not feed on the wood, which is bitter with resin and salt, but on the translucent bracket-fungi that sprout wherever the humidity gathers in the timber joints. The people treat them as sacred meters of dampness. Where the beetles congregate, the timber is rotting; where they abandon a cross-beam, the wood has dried too quickly and is brittle enough to crack under the stone’s pressure. A carpenter will sit for hours on a slate shelf, his knees folded beneath him, watching the path of a single beetle across a tie-rod before deciding whether to drive a fresh cedar wedge into the wall.

Food is not something that is pursued or reaped in seasons; it is collected with the patient, monotonous persistence of dripping water. The primary nourishment is the *white-cress*, which grows in the terraced tufa basins that step down the center of the ravine. These are not soft, peppery water plants like those of lowland streams, but thick, fleshy weeds whose leaves are heavy with absorbed calcium. When harvested, the leaves are soaked for days in sour water-parsnip vinegar to leach out the bitter stone-milk, then pounded into a cold, dense mash that tastes of iron and river-silt. 

This mash is eaten three times a day, cold from soapstone bowls, seasoned only with the powdered carapaces of the water-fleas that are skimmed from the pool margins. There is no fire for cooking. To light a wood fire in this pocket would fill the chasm with suffocating blue smoke that would linger for three days, blind the swifts, and foul the drinking troughs. Everything is eaten raw, salted, or cold-fermented in the dark stone jars that line the cellar floors.

Because of this diet, and the mineral-heavy water they drink from the floor-spring, the bodies of the people change as they age. By their fortieth year, their joints begin to accumulate lime. Their knuckles swell into gray, knotty burls, looking remarkably like the tufa stones in the basins; their spines lose their flex and fuse into rigid, upright pillars. An elder here does not lean on a staff—to carry a stick in these narrow alleys would block the passage of others—but moves with a stiff, deliberate glide, using the palms of both hands against the walls to guide their progress. 

When an old one dies, they are not taken up to the prairie. The people have a profound, superstitious horror of the open sky—a realm they view as an empty, violent madness where the air has no walls to hold it and light comes from no fixed surface. To be laid under the wide sky is, to them, to be scattered and lost forever in the thin, blowing nothingness. 

Instead, the dead are washed in the coldest of the seep-pools, bound into the sitting posture with cedar-bark thongs, and placed into the lowest water-cellars beneath the tenements. There, the snowmelt, saturated with dissolved limestone from its journey through forty cubits of karst, trickles over them in absolute silence. Within six months, a milk-white skin of calcite begins to film over the mouse-skin smock. Within three years, the flesh is gone, replaced molecule by molecule by the heavy, crystalline carbonate, until the ancestor is no longer a corpse, but a pillar of pure, shining spar—a structural block, tongue-grooved and squared by the masonry of water, ready to be slid into place under the foundation of a new stair or used as the threshold of a daughter’s house.

In this way, the city does not merely occupy the stone; it produces it. The living stand upon the petrified laps of their grandmothers, their feet polishing the calcite brows of people who spoke the same clicking, breathy tongue ten generations ago. 

Above, on the lip of the karst, the prairie wind runs wild through the dwarf-willows, carrying the scent of dry dust, horse dung, and lightning. It screams across the open earth, violent and free. But down here, three yards into the cleft, the air remains dead, sweet with the smell of wet liverwort and mouse-oil. A woman sits on a slate stoop, trimming a square of cushion-moss with a flat bone scraper. A blind swift flutters from a beam, circles the white flare of noon, and returns to her shoulder. She does not look up. Her hands, white and damp and sure, move along the edge of the stone with the unhurried precision of the lichen itself, knowing that nothing will ever come down from the sky, and that nothing will ever leave.
