To survive here, a body cannot possess an interior. There is no marrow, no gut cavity to retain an atmosphere, no soft core that could endure the differential between the cold nitrogen vacuum and the kinetic violence outside. Whatever has an inside simply bursts on its first descent.

So the things that dwell upon the limestone ribs are flat, laminated, or woven through.

Under the copper fronds, where the shudder of the rock never ceases, live the *tine-ticks*—though they are not ticks, but something closer to self-striking iron filings held in a leaf-spring arrangement. They are no thicker than a playing card, broad as a dinner plate, and constructed of alternating leaves of black shale and natural foil. They do not walk; walking is too slow for stone that is constantly shedding its skin. Instead, they cock their dorsal spines against the tremors of the cliff. They read the harmonic signature of the approaching force-pulses through their anchor-pads—three diamond-tipped prongs that bite four inches into the graphene-veined limestone—and when the snap arrives, they use the shockwave itself to reset their internal ratchets. Between pulses, they shear along the mica terraces, scything the fresh sprouts with mandibles made of brittle boron carbide. They do not chew or swallow; they press the pulverized glass and copper flakes into their abdominal laminations, where the dry galvanic friction strips electrons from the dust and charges their running-arcs. When two meet, they do not fight; they spark across a three-inch gap, welding their margins together for a fraction of a second to trade strip-spores—stiff little cassettes of silicate memory—before the wind tears them apart again.

Farther down, where the nave narrows to a throat so sheer that sunlight only glances it for twenty minutes at zenith, the cliff-face ceases to be stone and becomes a living felt of *thread-worms*. These are single-molecule filaments, kilometers long, extruded by microscopic sessile cyst-nodes that drill into the ancient compressor pylons. The threads do not fall slack; they hum. Taut as piano wire, they bridge the drop from wall to wall, cross-hatching the canyon in a web so fine it is completely invisible until the oil-mists from the vents condense on it in microscopic beads. 

The things that navigate these strings are the *pendulums*. 

Seen from above, a pendulum appears only as a dull leaden sphere the size of a human skull, perfectly smooth, suspended from a single high-tensile braid. It swings across the kilometer-wide abyss in a majestic, terrifying arc that takes twelve seconds to complete. But as it nears the apex of its swing, the sphere opens like a split plum, revealing six segmented, caliper-like limbs tipped with sapphire pulleys. With a dry, shrieking *whir*, it catches one of the thread-lines, runs its length for eighty yards in the blink of an eye, casts off, snaps shut, and plunges into another great ballistic dive. 

The pendulums are the heavy scavengers of the mid-gulf. They do not bother with the living ribbons or the flying polyps; they wait for the kinetic field to fire. When that great invisible sledgehammer strikes through the nave, snapping the quartz-scaled flyers into thousands of crystal shards, the pendulums dive through the rain of shrapnel with their hulls parted, scooping the heavier chunks into their internal combustion chambers. Inside them, small, dry catalytic fires smolder constantly—not carbon fires, but high-temperature pyrophoric reactions fueled by titanium flour and the thin volatile gases of the vents. They are the only warm things in the nave. If you could hold one in your hands, it would feel like a hot flatiron, trembling with the violent, unbalanced rotation of an internal flywheel that keeps it upright as it cuts the void.

Deep in the canyon floor, three thousand meters below the highest gardens, the talcum silt lies seventy feet deep. Here, the deafening roar of the upper reach is muffled, transformed into a continuous, heavy thrum that vibrates through the soles like the deck plates of an icebreaker. 

Nothing flies down here; the air is choked with a dry, blinding fog of pulverized quartz and zinc dust that would scour wings to ribbons in an instant. This is the realm of the *sifters*. 

They are massive, long-bodied things, shaped like blunt-ended ingots of rough casting iron, fifty or sixty feet long and segmented like freight cars. They do not have legs; they swim through the powdered mineral drifts using broad, rhythmic contractions of their belly-plates, which are covered in millions of microscopic cilia made of grown diamond. The silt does not resist them; dry, cold, and entirely free of moisture, it acts more like a heavy liquid than earth. The sifters plow through it at shocking speed, leaving boiling wakes of grey dust that collapse behind them without a sound.

Their life is entirely blind and tactile. They possess no eyes, no light-sensing organs; the upper sky does not exist for them. Along their dorsal ridges run rows of hollow, flute-like chimneys that project just above the surface of the silt. As they cruise the depths, the screaming downdrafts from above blow across the mouths of these pipes, producing deep, organ-like tones that travel through the silt for miles. This is how they school. A dozen sifters will align themselves in parallel, half a mile apart, their pipes singing in eerie, interlocking fifths, sweeping the floor from wall to wall. They ingest the fallen silt by the ton, extracting the residual electrostatic potential stored in the shattered plant-springs, leaving behind tracks of slag—sintered ribbons of fused silica that mark the bottom like ancient, forgotten tram lines.

And then there are the ones who inhabit the dark polyhedrons.

They are not descendants of the builders, nor are they machines, though their anatomy makes the distinction meaningless. They are called the *anchor-folk*, though they have no name for themselves, possessing no language other than the shifting patterns of high-voltage arc-discharge they throw across the hulls of the abandoned housings.

They are roughly the height of a man, but built like tripods of jointed carbon pipe, wrapped in dry, grey pneumatic bladders that hiss slightly as they vent cold nitrogen to adjust their balance. They have no faces; their upper bodies terminate in heavy, toroidal coils of dark alloy that mimic the geometries of the compressor housings they tend. 

They do not leave the cradles. To them, the void is an uninhabitable desert of raw kinetic force. They cling to the micro-machined facets of the floating polyhedrons with electromagnetic footpads, moving with a jerky, stop-motion precision that seems entirely out of step with the hurricane around them. They are slow where everything else is fast; they will stand motionless for three weeks, their coils faintly glowing with violet Saint Elmo's fire, watching a single monomolecular stay-cable vibrate until their sensors detect a microscopic phase-slip in the weave.

Then, with agonizing care, one will lean down. From its chest-strut it extrudes a slender needle of synthetic sapphire, heating it to white-hot ductility with a burst of internal static, and draws a single, thread-fine bead along the cable’s root, retuning it. 

They do not know what the compressor does. They do not know who anchored it there, or why the pulse must strike the nave every eleven seconds to clear the gardens from the limestone. But they are bound to it by an absolute, non-conscious piety of maintenance. They feed on the high-frequency leakage of the engines, drinking the inductive fields directly into their toroidal brains. When one of them finally wears down—its carbon joints ground to powder by the fine quartz dust, its solenoids burnt out by some sudden surge—it does not drop into the abyss.

Its fellows assemble around it with dry, clicking efficiency. They strip its coils, its sapphire needles, its pneumatic bladders. They weld the empty structural pipes directly onto the framework of the housing itself, adding another strut, another buttress, another small geometric reinforcement to the machine that keeps the air cold, dry, and screaming.

Then they turn back to the cables, their cold lenses reflecting the razor-sharp white daylight, waiting for the next snap to clear the sky.
