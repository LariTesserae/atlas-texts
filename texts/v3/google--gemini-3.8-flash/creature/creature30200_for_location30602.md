To survive here, a creature cannot borrow from the sky. It cannot rely on the bellows-action of a breastbone, nor the delicate lattice of a tracheole; the air is too thick with suspended paraphins and heavy sulfur to be pulled into any fragile wet sack without choking it to tallow. 

Whatever moves in the vault must take the environment’s own terms: it must be hydraulic, it must be blind, and it must be built of the same greasy, petrifying gristle that holds the dynamos to the floor.

The first of them are the *pipe-leeches*—though they are leeches only in their slow, lip-like locomotion. They live in the long, dripping shadows beneath the suspended conduits, clinging to the rust-swelled joints where the condensate hangs heaviest. They are the gray of boiled lard, flat as cobblers’ soles, perhaps two spans long, and thick-skinned as sea cucumbers. They have no lungs, nor even gills in the conventional sense; their entire dorsal surface is a corrugated bed of vascular felt, saturated constantly in the trickling runoff of iron salts and machine-sweat. They drink the runoff through capillary action, extracting microscopic sulfur flakes and the paraffin oils that coat the stagnant droplets. When a drop falls above them with that hollow *tock*, they do not flinch; they have no organs for hearing air-borne vibration. Instead, they feel the iron beneath them ring. To them, the vault is not an expanse of space, but a map of shivering tensions, hot veins, and cold rivets.

Where the condensate gathers into those palm-sized, copper-rimmed depressions on the floor plates, there is a different sort of tenant. In these shallow, chemical baths dwell the *scale-weavers*. They are colonial, crusting organisms that look, at first glance, like an irregular boil of greenish solder or a blister in the iron plate. 

If you watch them over the course of hours, the blister breathes—not with air, but by shifting its plates. They are soft-bodied, multi-lobed animals that secret a carapace of lime, copper-salts, and oxidized lead, which they leach directly from the corroding casings. They live half-submerged in the scalding, verdigris-green puddles. Their mouths are down-turned rasps, grinding endlessly at the dead iron beneath them, widening those tiny cisterns millimeter by millimeter over the centuries. They defecate a fine, dense powder that sets underwater like hydraulic cement, slowly walling themselves into tiny, volcano-shaped redoubts that shield their soft, tallowy undersides from the falling scale of the ceiling. 

Yet the vault is not merely an aquarium of grease-pools; there are things that travel across the iron. 

High up, where the sagging ribs of soot-black iron meet the fractured limestone seams, move the *caliper-crawlers*. They are larger—some the length of an engineer’s forearm—and their bodies are entirely dry, covered in an armor that resembles the charred bark of a hemlock, though it is composed of keratin reinforced with silica and precipitated iron. They have no faces. Their heads terminate in a blunt, horn-plated wedge used for prising open the rubbery fronds of the iron-eating plants, seeking the fat, pale root-nodes that drill into the bolt-holes. 

The calipers move with a heavy, deliberate, three-stroke gait. Their limbs are not jointed like an insect’s; they are muscular hydrostats encased in telescoping rings of hardened scale, ending in blunt, peg-like hooks that fit into the pitted hollows of rusted girders. They do not breath through their mouths, which are simple vertical slits on their undersides, but possess lateral pores plugged with dense, wax-like hairs that filter out the greasy soot while allowing sulfurous steam to pass over internal, chemo-synthetic pads. 

The caliper-crawlers are utterly dependent on the vault’s deafening noise. The constant, marrow-shaking roar of thermal expansion is their clock and their engine. Because their carapaces are dense and heavy, they cannot easily generate internal body heat beyond what they absorb from the rock; instead, they press their broad, flat bellies against the vibrating beams. The relentless, 40-hertz shudder of the expanding iron acts directly upon their digestive tracts, churning the fibrous vulcanized leaves and mineral paste they have swallowed. They use the iron’s mechanical agony to grind their food. 

During the great *CLANGS*—when an expansion joint finally yields and slips three millimeters in its seat—the caliper-crawlers go rigid. The shockwave through the frame lifts them an eighth of an inch off the metal; they hold fast with their hooks, their pale bellies flush against the shuddering plate, absorbing the violent release of kinetic energy like a sunbather catching a sudden break in the clouds. To them, that terrifying sound is not noise; it is a pulse of ambient life, a sign that the tap far below is still boiling, that the iron is still working, that the ceiling will not yet settle entirely into the silt.

Deepest of all, back toward the western corner where the single heat-pipe still glows with the dull, cigar-red ember of the earth’s gut, resides the vault’s apex. It does not wander. It has no need to.

Inside the shattered housing of an eighty-ton reciprocating steam pump—where the piston has seized midway through its stroke, leaving a cavernous, grease-slicked cylinder twelve feet long—lives what might be called the *Accumulator*. 

It is a mass of dense, liver-colored flesh, folded in upon itself like the cerebral cortex of some immense, buried thinker, filling the bore of the iron cylinder almost to the brim. It has grown around the connecting rod, swallowing the cold, machined steel in a knot of fibrous, vascular gristle. It has no eyes, no ears, no limbs. Its skin is not moist with water, but slicked in a thick, amber-colored grease that smells of engine tallow and old tallow candles. 

The Accumulator is a single organism, or perhaps an ancient colony that has lost all boundary between its parts. Its pale, fibrous tendrils extend out through the cylinder’s weep-holes, tracing the paths of the dead lubrication lines, following the copper tubes for hundred of feet through the vault’s skeleton. It has grown into the walls, into the boiler drums, its thread-like hyphae dipping into every condensate pool and wrapping around the roots of every rubber-fronded stack.

It is the master of the vault’s acoustic mass. The vibration of the entire room converges upon the mass of the seized pump, which acts as a great, cast-iron resonator. The Accumulator sits in the center of that acoustic focus. The endless hum passes through its dense, liver-colored folds, keeping its thick, petroleum-based fluids in constant, slow circulation without the need for a heart. 

When the daylight bruises through the high ceiling crack at noon, the Accumulator does not notice. But when the deep thermal tap shifts miles beneath the earth—when the muffled, rhythmic hammering against the dead valves changes its beat by even half a measure—a slow, rippling spasm runs through the great gristled body in the cylinder. 

One of its fat, greasy lips will peel back from the edge of the iron bore, releasing a heavy bubble of foul, sulfurous gas that rises through the still, suffocating air to join the soot above. Then it will settle again, pressing itself tight against the cylinder’s bore, its pale skin drinking the heat from the metal, perfectly still, perfectly deaf, waiting with the iron for the next hundred years of cooling.
