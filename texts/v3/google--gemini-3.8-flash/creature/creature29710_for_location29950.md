To understand what lives here, one must first discard the notion of individuals. 

In this basin, flesh is not an exception to the stone; it is a second, softer geology that has settled over the dolomite like a continental silt. There are distinct forms, yes, but they interlock so tightly, and depend so utterly upon the secretions, sheddings, and cavities of one another, that the entire floor acts as a single, sprawling organ of digestion and turnover.

At the very bottom, in the microscopic film between the polished bedrock and the grinding bellies of the great carapaced herds, exists an entire fauna of two-dimensional things. They are known only by the slick they leave behind: ribbon-leeches and pale, leaf-thin plates of gristle no thicker than wet parchment, lacking blood vessels, bones, or eyes. They cannot survive unless pressed between two immense weights. They inhabit the sheer shear-zone. As the twelve-foot crawlers drag their horn-plated bellies across the dolomite, these flat-worms feed upon the microscopic debris of pulverized shell and the salt-frost sweated out by the stone itself. They exude a cold, curdled grease that reduces friction, allowing the massive crawlers to slide more smoothly through their ancient grooves; in doing so, they lubricate the very pressure that would crush any other living creature to paste. When a crawler dies, these flat things do not flee; they are simply scraped away and consumed by the mouths of the next generation following directly behind, their substance folded seamlessly back into the under-tread.

Within the Great Crawlers themselves, life multiplies again. Their bellies do not merely drag; they breathe. Along the lateral margins of their twelve-foot horn-shields are rows of wet, valve-shutting orifices that open with a heavy, sucking sigh every few minutes to take in the cold, ammoniac air. These spiracles are warm caverns compared to the freezing mist outside. Inside the moist, ribbed tunnels of their breath-pipes dwell the *cilia-thieves*: pale, jointless things shaped like plucked poultry, blind and rubbery, that anchor themselves with bone spurs into the crawler’s respiratory lining. They do not drink blood; they wait for the crawler to expel its bronchial mucus—a thick, gray froth heavy with trapped mist-minerals and dust—and ladle it into their bag-like gullets with long, fringed forearm-spades. When the crawler exhales in its great, fog-spewing heave, these parasites brace against the hurricane of foul air, their pale bodies fluttering like rags in a storm.

Above them, upon the broad, scarred tables of the crawlers’ backs, is where the middle ecology roams. The tallow-colored tripods and quadrupeds that pick at the herds are not purely scavengers; they are groomers and harvesters of a strange, continuous garden. The backs of the crawlers are not smooth horn; they are deeply furrowed by decades of friction and chemical seepage, forming artificial canyons of dead chitin. In these ruts live the *spindle-ticks*—six-legged, brittle-shelled creatures the size of dinner plates, whose legs are fused into single, chisel-like picks. The spindle-ticks pry apart the molting plates of the host to lap at the raw, soft skin beneath. 

In response, the taller, pale scavengers pace along the crawlers’ backs, their long, multi-jointed limbs stepping delicately from shell to shell across the shifting herd like herons wading through a swamp of living horn. When a scavenger’s rosette-snout detects the sharp, vinegar tang of a spindle-tick prying into living meat, it strikes down like a trip-hammer. It does not eat the tick’s body; it pierces the tick’s shell with a needle-like feeding tube and drains its cold, yellow marrow, leaving the empty husk to be kicked into the slurry between the crawling beasts.

These tall scavengers have no lungs in the traditional sense; their skin is loose, folded like heavy linen, and perpetually damp. They absorb the oxygen carried in the wet fog directly through their tallowy hide. Because of this, they are terrified of the dry drafts that occasionally slip over the rim of the basin. When a rare breath of dry air descends, they squat flat against the crawlers’ shells, pressing their delicate faces into the moist dung and condensation pools that gather in the carapace hollows, waiting until the heavy, cold soup of the normal mist swallows the valley once more.

Higher still, anchored into the stone like ruined towers, are the sixty-cubit pillar-beasts. Seen closely, their legs do not seem to be made of jointed bone, but rather of hundreds of thick, fibrous tendons bound together in a tight, twisting cable of hide, like the trunks of ancient wisteria. Their feet have no toes; they have broad, calcified pads that have grown into the dolomite, chemically fused with the stone over centuries of immobility. They do not walk. They are born, they settle, and they petrify in place. 

The sides of a pillar-beast are an ecosystem of altitude. At the ground level, its massive leg-pillars are smooth, rubbed bare by the thousands of crawlers that grind past them, polishing the gray hide until it looks like wet river-slate. But thirty feet up, beyond the reach of the moving herds, the skin erupts into a violent topography of horizontal ridges, shelf-like calluses, and deep, draining gutters. 

Here dwell the *channel-drinkers*: creatures that resemble long, segmented centipedes, but made entirely of pale gristle and soft fat, without a shell. They wedge their bodies into the moisture grooves of the pillar-beast, aligning their open, scoop-shaped mouths directly into the trickle of falling mist-water. They act as living valves. If they drink their fill, their swollen, translucent bellies overflow, allowing the water to continue down to the next tier; if the mist is thin, they clamp their bodies shut, hoarding the fluid in their distended guts, becoming bloated, quivering water-skins that smaller, agile climbers will rip open with hooked claws to survive.

Near the crown of the pillar-beast, fifty cubits above the grinding bed, the air is slightly warmer, heated by the slow, furnace-like metabolism of the creature’s immense thoracic cavity. The beast's head is not a brain-case; it is an upward-facing funnel of spongy, cartilage honeycomb, thirty feet across, that simply drinks the low sky. There are no eyes, no ears, no teeth. Inside the dark, labyrinthine chambers of this funnel-head live the only airborne things in the depression: the *drift-fleas*. 

These are not true insects, but light, bladder-bodied things that possess no wings, only vast, sail-like fins of translucent membrane. They cannot fly against a current; they merely float upon the warm, rising plume of the pillar-beast’s breath, drifting like ash a few yards above the funnel-head, feeding on the rich, bacterial steam that vents from the beast’s interior. When the beast’s breath pauses, they drift back down, clinging with barbed filament-legs to the moist edges of the honeycomb, waiting for the next long, thermal exhalation to lift them into the gloom once more.

And beneath everything—beneath the crawlers, beneath the grease-worms, beneath the feet of the pillar-towers—are the *fault-borers*. 

In the rare places where the dolomite basin has cracked under the unimaginable weight of millions of tons of shifting flesh, there are fissures that go down into black, dead rock. The fault-borers live in these gaps. They are blind, jawless, and utterly white. They do not eat flesh; they eat the thick, black runoff that drains from the surface—the concentrate of ammonia, shed skin, pulverized bone, and biological oil that trickles perpetually down into the dark. They are essentially living siphons, tubular and leagues long, that pack the fissures until the cracks are caulked with their rubbery, pulsing bodies. They process this dark slime, extracting whatever trace minerals remain, and exude a chalky, mineral paste that slowly cements the fissures shut, preventing the valley floor from collapsing into whatever voids lie beneath.

There is no season here, no time of day, and no concept of an outside. 

Generations do not pass in a sequence of births and burials; they pass as a slow, continuous extrusion of material. A crawler drops an egg-mass—not a clutch of fragile shells, but a dense, greasy slab of hardened cartilage containing hundreds of dormant embryos, flat as coins. The slab drops into the biological ditch behind the mother. It is immediately trodden upon by the beasts behind her, stamped into the slurry, ground under thousands of spade-feet, soaked in the ammonia and oil of the trough. The embryos do not break; they are designed to be pressed. The pressure of the herd’s passage is what activates them, like seeds that require fire to germinate. By the time the slab has been dragged a league through the dark by the friction of the herd, the young have absorbed enough grease and stone-dust through their porous casings to hatch—not into the air, but into the thin, greasy space between the stone and the bellies of their elders, already blind, already hard, already gnashing at the rocks.

The acoustic roar of the basin is the heartbeat of this existence. It never stops. It is so loud that the smaller creatures do not have ears; organs of hearing would simply be ruptured and turned to liquid by the sheer sound-pressure. Instead, they sense the world through *receptors of contact*. A tripod knows a crawler is dying not because it sees it falter or hears it cry out, but because the specific frequency of its belly-friction changes—the rhythm of its spade-legs shifts from an eight-beat grind to an uneven, six-beat scrape that sends a distinctive shudder through the carapaces of all who stand upon it. 

That shudder is the signal. It spreads through contact alone, bone against horn, horn against hide, hide against stone. Long before the great beast has even ceased its sliding, the swarm above has already begun to shift its weight, aligning its mouths, turning its blind, questing rosettes toward the single joint that has begun to cool.
