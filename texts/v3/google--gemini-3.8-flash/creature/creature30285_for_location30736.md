To see what lives on the shelf, one has to look past the scale of the trees and become accustomed to the heavy, concussive rhythm of the water. Life here does not compete with the sound; it has built its nervous systems around it.

The isopods on the underside of the leaf-boats are the first to show this. They are larger than they appear from the pavement—some the length of a man’s thumb, their carapaces not chalky like garden woodlice, but clear as river ice, yellowing slightly toward the margins where the chitin thickens into fluted shields. They have no eyes. In this light, eyes are an unnecessary expense; instead, their long, jointed antennae lie flat against the jade skin of the cistern-cabbage, sensing the rising turgor of the leaf. 

Three seconds before the rubbery leaf groans and collapses under its load, the isopods do not scatter; they flatten. Their legs are fringed with micro-hooks that slot into the microscopic pores of the leaf’s epidermis. When fifty gallons of water tear down the face of the wood, the isopods become part of the hull. They drink during the drop—siphoning thin films of moisture through mouthparts that vibrate in sympathy with the falling weight. Once the leaf snaps back up with that wet, hollow boom, they resume their crawl, grazing the red slick of algae before the wind can dry it against the warm bark.

Higher up, inside the crowns of the rosettes where the water is never entirely emptied, there is an interior ecology that never touches the stone floor. 

At the base of the dory-sized leaves, where they clasp the green trunk in tight, overlapping hinges, the water is dark, lukewarm, and acidic with decaying pollen blown over the ridge from other valleys. In these permanent sump-pools live colonies of pale, thumb-thick worms—not segmented earthworms, but ribbon-like nemerteans that weave through the drowned fibrous debris. They are translucent pink, blind, and rubbery, living on the micro-debris and the dead carapaces of isopods that lost their grip during the tip. They exude a thick, scentless slime that coats the leaf joints, preventing the massive friction of the cabbage’s tipping hinges from wearing the jade wood down to pulp. Tree and worm exist in a locked barter: the tree provides forty gallons of thermal bath at eight thousand feet; the worm oils the hinge that lets the tree shed its load before the wind snaps its neck.

Then there is the thing the mortar and pestle were brought for.

It does not grow on the pavement, but in the lips of the drainage cracks—the steam-vents where the basalt is hottest. It is a fungus-lichen chimera that looks like thick, rolled tongues of boiled leather, known only to those few who have ever climbed this shoulder of the range. The locals down in the wind-scourged scrub call it *grease-leather* or *tallow-tongue*. It has no green to it. It feeds directly on the iron precipitate in the runoff and the sulfur gases leaking from the fault. 

When young, it is gray and wet like an oyster, but as it matures over thirty or forty seasons, its center swells into a dense, oily knot the size of a fist. If bruised by hand, it yields nothing, but when crushed under a heavy river cobble against the warm basalt of the trough, its cellular structure ruptures into a golden, heavy lipid—a grease that does not spoil, does not freeze at twenty below zero, and smells faintly of wet slate and wild turnip. 

Beside the trough, sitting on the warm stone where the heat keeps the joints loose, sits the one who works it.

Her presence does not interrupt the terrace because she has the same patience as the cabbage trees. She is old, though her age is difficult to read because her skin has been pickled by sixty years of high-altitude sun, continuous steam, and the iron spray that drifts off the waterfalls. Her hair is short, stiff, and dyed the color of dried liver by the red algae she uses to scrub her scalp in the runoff basins. 

She is stone deaf. She was not born that way; the terrace took her hearing forty years ago, one percussive crash at a time, until the hammer-bones of her ears fused together in self-defense. She lives now entirely through bone conduction. 

When a leaf tips, she does not hear the *thump*; she feels the basalt beneath her heels kick upward with a faint, familiar jolt, like the kick of an oar against a gunwale. She knows which tree has dropped by the direction of the shudder through her pelvis. Tree four—the one by the western cliff—hits with a sharp, high ring because its stone basin has a fissure that rings like bronze. Tree seven, near the steps, hits with a dull, wet wallop like a sack of tallow falling on mud.

She lives under the overhang of the weeping basalt, where the cliff curves outward to make a dry shelf three paces deep. Her bed is a layer of dried cabbage-leaf ribs, peeled into long, fibrous boards that soften under body heat and mold to her hips. She does not need a blanket; the rock beneath her mattress is ninety degrees day and night, an immense subterranean hearth that never goes out.

Her routine is small, clean, and measured by the rosettes. 

Every morning—or what passes for morning when the white sea of clouds below turns pink—she walks the drainage channels with a stiff broom made of split bamboo, sweeping away the gravel that the mountain wind tosses over the rim. If the channels choke, the basins back up; if the basins back up, the stone cools, and the tallow-tongue rots instead of swelling. 

With a curved knife of river-steel, she harvests three or four mature knots of the lichen, no more. She carries them to the six chipped steps, sets them in the trough, and takes up the river stone. Her arms are corded, knotty with muscle that has done the same downward roll for decades. The pestle grinds; the grease pools in the shallow lip of the trough; she tilts the calcified gourd to catch the slow, clear trickle.

One gourd takes three weeks to fill. When three gourds are full, she stoppers them with plugs of dried moss dipped in tallow, binds them into a harness of braided grass, and leaves them at the head of the descending chimney path that leads down into the cold scrub country. 

Two days later, the gourds are gone, replaced by a sack of parched barley, three bricks of salt, and a bundle of dried mutton that she will soak in the warm water of the third tree's catch-basin until it softens into meat again. She does not see the men who bring the barley. They are young men from the valleys, and they do not like the terrace. They say the terrace is an engine that was built before God had words, and the noise of the dropping leaves turns a man’s bowels to water within an hour. They drop the grain, take the grease—which their smiths use to quench fine blades and their herders use to keep frost from killing their children’s lungs—and they flee back down into the silence of the grass.

She stays. 

In the late afternoon, when the wind from the eastern ocean meets the wind from the western ocean directly over the ridge, the mist rises straight up like the breath of a furnace, leaving the stone floor dry and polished under a sky so dark blue it is nearly black. 

She sits on the sixth step, her bare feet resting in the warm iron mud of the trough, her hands oily with tallow, and watches the sun hang above the edge of the world. Behind her, fifty gallons of water hit the basalt with the weight of an iron anchor. The stone jumps beneath her heels. She does not flinch. She leans back against the warm rock, closes her eyes, and lets the mountain count its minutes through the soles of her feet.
