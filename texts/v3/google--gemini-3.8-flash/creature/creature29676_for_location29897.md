To move through this water without shedding blood is the first discipline of anything that survives here. 

The stone will not allow a mistake. A stray heel kicked out in an idle glide, a knee dropped against a ledge during rest, and the fresh volcanic glass takes its tax—a slice so clean and painless in the sulfur-warm brine that an animal might bleed into the tea-brown drift for yards before the salt finds the cut. 

Because of this, the people who inhabit the shelves are long-limbed, deliberate, and extraordinarily cautious in the joints. They are not swimmers in the pelagic sense; they do not have the broad flukes or the torpedo-shaped mass of creatures that chase down prey in the open void. Instead, their bodies are constructed for suspension, for hanging and bracing. They have four long, double-elbowed limbs ending in five-toed graspers that are identical at both ends of the torso—there is little difference between foot and hand save that the lower digits are webbed slightly thicker with tough, leathery cartilage.

Their skin is thick, grayish-ochre, like the boiled hide of an eel, but it lacks scales. Instead, it is protected by a continuous, cool-smelling slime that they scrape off and replace every few cycles: a thick lipid secretion that repels the caustic sulfur-salts and causes glancing blows against the razor edges of the pillow-lava to slide off without biting. Where their skin touches stone constantly—the palms, the inner curves of the thighs, the pads of the ribs—the flesh has hardened into pale, fibrous pads like sole-leather, cross-hatched by old, healed white lines where the obsidian won out regardless.

They have eyes, but they are flat, lidless lenses the color of clouded amber, set flush with the skull behind transparent shields of thickened skin. In this bruised, chartreuse twilight, these eyes do not look for form; they read density and warmth. To them, the vents of boiling mineral brine are blinding white spires of light, and a body drifting overhead is a blue shadow cooling against the olive ceiling. 

They breathe with a throat that is almost entirely gill. Two vertical slits, edged with stiff, overlapping plates of charcoal-colored bone, open behind the jaw and rhythmically flare with the convective tide. They do not gulp or pump; they merely hang in the current, head-down or tethered, and let the long, slow breathing of the ocean pass through them. Their blood is rich in heavy metals, smelling of iron filings and vinegar, which allows them to bind oxygen from water that would drown an air-breathing whale in three spasms.

They call themselves nothing that translates into sound, for the vocal cords of surface-creatures would tear here under the hydrostatic load. When they speak to one another across the mesh avenues, they speak in *twang* and *thrum*. 

A person perched on an anchor-crag will reach out with a blunt bone stylus and pluck one of the braided kelp tethers. The sound travels along the cordage with electric speed—a deep, resonant *zung-ng* that vibrates not through the ears, but through the hollow, oil-filled cavities within the breastplate. The whole settlement is an instrument; by changing the tension of a rope or the weight of a greenstone counterweight, a household can signal its waking, its hunger, or the arrival of the cold, deep-ocean drift. In the crowded tiers beneath the pumice domes, conversation is a constant, quiet polyphony of plucks, dull taps on hollow basalt, and the click of bone against pumice.

Domestic life is lived largely inverted. Because the pumice blocks pull upward with astonishing buoyancy, the floors of their homes are actually ceilings: you enter a cell from below, pulling yourself up into a pocket of captured, still water where the temperature is carefully maintained by small, vented hearths of volcanic slag. 

In these upside-down cups, they sleep wedged between pads of dried felt-algae, anchored by broad belly-straps so that an involuntary twitch will not drift them down into the drop. They eat what the mountain gives: the bitter, starchy marrow of the tuberous sea-succulents, which they split open with wedges of chipped greenstone, and the sulfur-matting itself, which they peel from the rock like wet blankets and ferment in sunken jars until it softens into an oily, salty curd. 

They do not hunt the great, pale, cartilaginous drifters that pass along the shelf's edge. To them, the drifters are weather. To kill one would be like trying to butcher a cloud, and besides, the flesh is laden with ammonia and urea. Instead, they wait for the drifters to die, or follow the long, descending wakes of the dead ones toward the abyssal plains—expeditions that take weeks of careful drifting along descent-lines, returning with great slabs of flexible, ribless gristle that serve as roof-plates and door-flaps.

Around them, occupying the small gaps between cord and pumice, lives a peculiar micro-fauna that belongs neither to the open water nor the seabed:

* **Tether-lice:** Flattened, disc-shaped isopods the size of a human palm, their shells made of paper-thin, calcified kelp-ash. They live entirely on the braided ropes, crawling along them upside down, cleaning away the slime-rot and dead weed with rasping mouthparts. The settlers treat them with great care; a line infested with lice lasts three times as long as a clean one, for the lice consume the micro-fungi that chew through kelp-fiber in the warm brine.
* **The Sump-Graft:** A communal, shapeless siphonophore that anchors inside the grease-lanterns. Fed by the rendering of boiled succulent-oil, it glows with a cold, greenish-white luminescence that burns without smoke, its trailing, hair-like tentacles acting as wicks that draw the fat through porous pumice. A well-tended graft will live for four generations, passed down from an elder's tether to an offspring’s.
* **Vane-worms:** Thin as needles and six feet long, rooted by their tails into the crevices of the fresh pillow-lava. They are completely transparent, save for a single black nerve that runs the length of their spine. When the three-hour convective swell shifts direction, the vane-worms all bend in unison, a silent forest of glass needles pivoting forty degrees to the east. By watching them, the cord-weavers know precisely when the tension on the suspension bridges will slacken, allowing them an hour to splice new line before the drift draws tight again.

Death in the settlement is simple and mechanical. 

A body that dies does not float; the muscle of these beings is dense, heavy with sulfur-salts and basalt dust. When an elder ceases to gill, the family does not carry them out to the void. They strip the body of its lipid skin-coverings, wrap it in fresh, unworked fronds of amber kelp, and carry it down—step by dangerous step along the lower moorings—to the very edge of the blackest fissures, where the thermal brine wobbles upward from the core.

They slip the body into the crevice. 

Down there, in the superheated, oxygenless womb of the ridge, the flesh does not rot. It cooks, slowly mineralizing over centuries until it becomes part of the tectonic floor: a black, dense core of bone-turned-jasper that will support the greenstone ballasts of children who will never know the name of the sound the rope made when it was first dropped. 

And high above, in the suspended domes, the ropes continue to stretch. The sound of the city never ceases—the low, wet cello-drone of six hundred pumice houses fighting the planet for the right to rise, and the quiet, clicking reply of the glass below, cooling one grain at a time in the dark.
