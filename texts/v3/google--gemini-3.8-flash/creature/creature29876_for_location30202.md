To find what lives here, one has to unlearn the signs of life that belong to earth and leaf. There are no burrows, no droppings, no scattered plumage, and above all, no rot. Whatever exists across these thousands of paved tiers must endure an existence stripped down to mineral, glare, and caustic wash; it must be comfortable with the chemistry of wet cement and the blinding violence of an unshaded sky.

At first, the eye insists the basins are empty. But if you watch the millimeter of water sliding over a pink earthenware lip, you will eventually see that the water does not always break according to gravity alone.

There are things shaped like thin sheets of clear mica—no thicker than a heavy vellum, entirely flat, and roughly the diameter of a coin. They live pressed tight against the unglazed tile, held there by surface tension and their own suction, completely submerged in the perpetual film. They have no color; they are as transparent as the mountain’s sweat, save for a faint, opalescent sheen along their perimeter that only catches when the sun hits at a raking angle. They are scavengers of the sky. Because the upland air is so thin, cosmic radiation, airborne salts from dry salt-flats leagues away, and microscopic flakes of quartz dust drift continually down, settling onto the wet masonry. These flat discs—neither mollusk nor insect, but something akin to a soft, living lens—slide imperceptibly across the combs and thumb-scratches of the clay, scraping the sediment clean before it can form silt. They are the reason the basins never cloud. They digest the wind-blown grit and exude a fine, dense silica that seals the earthenware, polishing the floor of every basin until it is slick as oilcloth. When they reach the edge of a weir, they do not drop; they fold themselves smoothly over the knife-sharp lip and let the flow ease them down into the next level, rippling once, like a falling pane of cellophane.

Deeper in the holding pans, in the dead corners where the water sits three inches deep, the shadows beneath the rims are occupied by something thicker. 

Tucked into the joints—into the very angles where the pale lime mortar meets the slate—are bodies that mimic the mortar so perfectly that the hand could brush across them and register only the chalky, cold drag of damp stone. They are jointless, slow-pulsing cords of pale, curd-like flesh, perhaps the length of an arm, though they wedge themselves into the seams so tightly that their true anatomy is obscured. They have no eyes; in this blinding white expanse, eyes would be seared blind within a single noon. Instead, their hides are coated in a calcified paste, a chalky rind they sweat out from their pores. 

It is these creatures that maintain the impossible youth of the masonry. They are lithovores, drinking the heavy, alkaline runoff that leaches from the mountain's upper reaches. As they pass the water through their simple bodies, they precipitate calcium carbonate back into the fractures. Where a winter frost might have cracked a seam, or where the water’s slide might have rounded an edge, these pale ribbons creep into the fissure, settling themselves into the gap. Their secretions dry instantly in the thin air, forming those razor-sharp mortar fins that look as though a mason’s trowel had pressed them only moments ago. If you look closely at the "thumb-prints" along the joins, many of them are not the marks of ancient human hands at all, but the blunt, rounded resting-scars of these creatures, pressed into the lime while it was still soft enough to yield.

Beneath the paved skin, within the darkness of the ceramic tubes where the air bubbles make their hollow *gluck*, lives the source of that sound. 

They are long, segmented creatures, hollow-bodied and stiff as dried reeds, anchored by their tails to the rough inner walls of the flumes. They have no blood to freeze; their internal fluids are almost identical to the mineral broth sliding over them. They act as living valves. As the water level rises with the condensation of the afternoon fronts, their hollow segments swell, flexing like ribbed leather bellows, throttling the flow or opening it, directing the cold sheet-water through the thousands of alternating channels so that no single tier ever overflows or dries out. They feed on the vibrations of the current, shuddering rhythmically in the dark pipes, their dry, horn-like segments clicking together—a sound that travels through the terracotta tiles and emerges into the high air as that trillion-voiced, clockwork whispering.

And occasionally, moving with an excruciating, deliberate calm across the blinding glare, there are the walkers.

They are tall, brittle-looking things, built entirely of long, spindly stilt-legs that look like peeled willow or sun-bleached bone, standing twice the height of a man. They have no feathers, no hair, no hide; their bodies are encased in an armor of overlapping plates made of the same gray slate as the shingles, bound together with tough, gray tendon. Their feet do not have claws or toes; they end in broad, circular pads of porous, leathery tissue that fit the span of the rectangular basins precisely. 

They walk without ever touching the knife-sharp rims. They step from the center of one basin to the center of the next, their joints moving with the dry, rasping scrape of unlubricated stone. Because of the vastness of the plateau, they are solitary, separated by leagues of empty, reflective masonry. They carry their small, heavy-jawed heads low, skimming their muzzles through the water as they stride, filtering out the lens-creatures and the mineral scum that gathers in the corners. 

When the walkers move, they cast thin, ink-black shadows across the pink tiles, the only shadows on the mountain that move on their own. They never descend to the valleys, where the earth is soft and muddy; their feet require the unyielding resistance of fired clay, and their respiration depends on the cold, chalk-scented draft of the high altitude. If one of them dies, it does not decompose. It simply locks its joints, standing like an abandoned tripod in the middle of a cistern. Over the course of a few months, the water-film weeps down its limbs, the lime-sweating cords creep up its stilts, and the basin-dwellers gradually plaster it into the landscape, cementing its bones into a new weir, another sharp-edged riser over which the water can smoothly break.

The whole mountain is not an abandoned machine, nor is it a cemetery. It is a slow, calcified organism whose skin is clay and slate, whose blood is the condensed breath of the sky, and whose organs are these pale, blind things that scrub, patch, and balance it—living out their cold, silent lifespans within the millimeter between the tile and the glare, keeping the stone perpetual, waiting for a rain that never comes.
