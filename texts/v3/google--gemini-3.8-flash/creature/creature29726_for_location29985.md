In the dark beneath the streets, where the city’s foundations plunge into the mountain’s chilled marrow, life begins with what the water carries.

The subterranean channels are not empty ducts; they are veins running with a very old, pale biology. In the black cisterns—vaults carved so deep that the surface weather never reaches them—there are creatures that have never seen the thinned blue of the plateau. They are ribbon-thin, blind, and milky-white: blind char and long-bodied troglobitic crayfish whose shells are as soft and translucent as wet vellum. They graze on the "rock-silt," a microscopic drift of decayed algae, pulverized mineral dust, and windblown pollen washed down from the heights through thousands of hairline vents. They drift with the smooth, continuous laminar flow of the flumes, anchored to the joints between basalt blocks by hooked dactyls, swaying like pale weed in the subterranean draft.

Higher up, where the light begins to filter through the drainage grates and notched spillways, this blind life yields to things built for the cold edges.

Along the wet chutes, in the perpetual twilight beneath the four-story lintels, lives a species of heavy-bodied, wingless stonefly. They are nearly the size of a man’s thumb, their carapaces the matte black of wet granodiorite, dusted with microscopic bristles that trap a sheen of air around them, turning them silver whenever they crawl submerged through the torrents. They do not fly; they crawl with absolute, deliberate traction against the sheer drop of the run-off gutters, anchoring themselves with claws so hard they have pitted the stone over uncounted generations. They feed on the gelatinous algae, scraping it down to the bare rock with mandibles like slate-cutters, keeping the lip of every weir and flume cleanly sheared. Without them, the drains would choke with slime within a few seasons; they are the unseen scourers of the city’s hydraulic throat.

Then there are the dwellers of the dry seams.

Where the great cyclopean blocks join without mortar, there are pockets where the water does not penetrate—dry, wedge-shaped voids left intentionally by the masons. Here, the blunt-headed lizards do not merely hide; they nest in communal tiers. They are slow-metabolizing creatures, cold-blooded in the truest sense, their blood thick with natural sugars that prevent ice crystals from forming in their tissues during the nights when the puddles freeze to glass. By morning, they are stiff as the stone itself, indistinguishable from the chipped corners of basalt. By noon, as the rock warms, they loosen, creeping out in hundreds onto the east-facing terraces, drinking the condensation that beads on the dark slabs before the wind licks it away. They do not compete; they lie in overlapping mats, their slate-colored skin darkening to absorb the hard, thin sunlight, their throats pulsing with an almost imperceptible flutter.

And inside the windowless blocks themselves—inside the tight stone cells behind the massive socketed doors—there are those who move the doors.

They are not many, and they are not fast. To call them men would be to misunderstand the degree to which an organism can be worn down and rebuilt by an altitude that allows no softness. 

They are squat, heavy-ribbed beings, their torsos like salt-casks, built around lungs that occupy nearly the entire volume of their chests. Their breath is not a series of gasps, but a long, slow tidal motion—four, perhaps five breaths a minute, taken through wide, low-bridged nostrils lined with thick down to temper the icy air. Their skin is thick, hairless, and entirely lacking in the ruddy flush of valley folk; it has the dull, greyish-olive tone of dry river stone, greased perpetually by an endogenous tallow that repels the mist and keeps their core heat sealed against the perpetual draft.

They do not wear woven cloth, which would rot or sodden in hours under the spray, but garments sewn from the leathery skins of the deep-cistern fish, stiffened with mineral wax, or heavy kilts made of braided liverwort fiber that dry quickly in the wind.

Their relation to the silence is absolute. They do not shout, nor do they sing; their vocal cords are thick, adapted only for low, rumbling hums that travel through the basalt beneath their feet rather than through the thin air. When they communicate, it is often by impact: a rhythmic heel-strike against a five-foot flagstone, or the flat palm slapped against a door-lintel. The stone carries the vibration for miles, through the continuum of interlocking blocks, long after the sound has died in the air. A worker at the lip of the easternmost terrace can feel, through the soles of his broad, calloused feet, the shudder of a sluice-gate being dropped three miles away in the upper city.

Their lives are an unbroken liturgy of maintenance. They are hydraulic caretakers, not by decree, but by biological necessity. If the gutters silt up, the water pools, freezes in the winter months, and the frost-shatter—the only enemy the city has—will heave the great blocks apart. And so, they move through the alleys with long poles of fossilized slate, clearing the weirs, nudging dislodged stones back into their gravity-locked channels, checking the drop of the cisterns by lowering plumb-lines of braided swift-gut weighted with polished river-pebbles.

They eat sparingly, their stomachs adjusted to the lean bounty of the heights: dried cakes of compressed stonefly pupae, salt-cured cistern-crawlers, and the bitter, starchy tubers of an alpine sedge that grows only in the spray-zones of the outer abysses, where the water tears itself into mist. 

When they sleep, they do not lie down on frames or bedding. They wedge themselves into the corners of the inner chambers, sitting upright with their knees drawn to their broad chins, their backs pressed hard into the right angles of the masonry. In this position, the thermal mass of the granodiorite—which absorbs the sun’s fury through the day and bleeds it slowly through the night—keeps them from freezing. They sleep with their eyes partially open, milky third eyelids drawn across the pupils to keep out the dust and the glare of the morning fog.

To watch one of them step out from a socketed doorway at dawn is to watch a feature of the terrace detach itself from the wall. He stands on the wet flagstones, his toes splayed wide to grip the slick basalt, facing into the localized rain falling miles to the east. He does not wipe the water from his brow; he lets it track down the deep, vertical grooves of his face, following the natural channels of his skin down to the chin, where it drips into the stone gutter at his feet, joining the rest of the mountain on its way to the edge.
