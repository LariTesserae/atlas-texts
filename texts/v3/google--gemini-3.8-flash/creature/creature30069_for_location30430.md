### The Floor-Feeders and the Brittle-Legs

At the lowest register, where the heat bends the grain of the floor into hair-thin checks and splits, life belongs to creatures that have abandoned soft tissue entirely. They are little more than animated splinters and dry plates. 

Among the shallowest furrows run the *spindle-crabs*—not crustaceans of the water, but flat, wide-tarsused arthropods whose legs are jointed like dried reeds. Their bellies never touch the timber; they move on twelve stilt-like needles tipped with dense, silica-heavy spurs that strike the grain with a faint, ceaseless *snick-snick-snick*, like rain on dry parchment. They do not drink the morning dew; they wait for it to thicken into the noon-day tack, then scrape the gummy film off the wood with horizontal, chisel-shaped mouthparts. Their carapaces are identical in color and grain to the lignite beneath them—reddish-dun, scored with parallel lines that follow the growth rings—so that when a thermal gust passes, hundreds of them simply freeze in place, aligning their bodies with the grain, vanishing entirely into the floor.

Deeper down, in the structural cracks where two continental-scale roots have ground against one another for centuries, live the *tallow-weavers*. They are pale, blind, and soft-bellied, the only creatures on the plateau that would perish within seconds under the unshielded sun. They spin no silk; instead, they exude a bitter, yellowish wax from their abdominal glands, with which they line the walls of the vertical fissures. This wax keeps the timber from weeping its precious moisture into the dry wind. In these sealed, cool flues—some plunging two hundred paces into the cool darkness of the heartwood—the weavers herd colonies of slow, wood-eating aphid-analogues that suck the dead sap from the dormant veins. 

When a tallow-weaver dies, its fellows do not consume it; they plaster its body into the wall with more wax, turning the deep clefts into mosaic tunnels of translucent insect-fat and dried carapaces, which the human inhabitants sometimes locate by smell, cracking them open with wedges to harvest fuel for their smoldering hearths.

### The Pillar-Haunters and Gliders

The three-hundred-fathom cork pillars are not dead monuments; they are vertical savannahs, home to beasts that have never touched the horizontal plains below.

The sides of these spires are deeply channeled, with fissures wide enough to swallow a horse, formed by the expansion and drying of the spongy, fire-resistant cork. In these channels dwell the *burl-hawks*—creatures that are neither bird nor bat, but broad, flat reptilian gliders whose ribs extend outward into stiff, leathery fans. Their skin is dry, rough as sharkskin, and stained with the pale greens and ochres of dry lichens that grow nowhere else. 

A burl-hawk does not flap. It lacks the deep breastbone and heavy musculature for powered flight, which the thin, sun-cooked air could scarcely support anyway. Instead, it climbs the shady side of a cork tower using hooked wing-claws that sink deep into the cork with a dry *thunk*, ascending through the cool hours of the early morning. When it reaches the sunlit crown, it waits. It watches for the dust-devils that spin across the floor leagues away, or for the sudden scatter of locustids signaling an emergence from below.

When it drops, it does so with a hollow, groaning rattle as its rib-fans lock into place. It can ride the thermal fronts for hours without dropping ten fathoms, banking across the immense open reaches with its head hung low, scanning the gray pavement. Its jaw is a spade-like scoop of dense bone; when it hunts, it dives along the surface of the floor, skimming barely an inch above the wood, and violently shears off whatever is too slow to drop into a fissure—spindle-crabs, molting locustids, or the wicker traps set by men. 

The sound of an approaching burl-hawk is notorious among those who walk the grain: a rising, tearing hiss, like an iron plow being dragged through dry shingles, caused by the air splitting over its hard, resin-caked leading edges.

### The Great Stridulators

The wolfhound-sized locustids that line the ridges are the true masters of the plateau’s acoustic space. To call them insects is to miss their scale and mechanics; they are heavy, long-muscled beasts with rib cages of black, vulcanized chitin that have the density of cast bronze. 

They do not gather in swarms. Swarming requires an abundance that this desiccated plateau cannot sustain. Instead, they are solitary, territorial, and strictly spaced. A single adult will claim a furrow—perhaps a quarter-mile wide and three miles long—defending it against rivals with its deafening song. On their hind legs, along the inner femur, runs a file of black, glass-hard teeth; when drawn across the raised, hollow resonators on their flanks, the resulting vibration does not travel merely through the air. It punches directly into the heartwood floor. 

A traveler can feel a locustid’s challenge five miles away through the bones of their knees: a rhythmic, jarring shock-wave that knocks dust out of the grain and makes the small stones of dried sap dance on the surface. These songs are territorial disputes, but they are also maps. By listening to the pitch and return-echo of a neighbor’s rasp, a locustid gauges the density of the wood ahead, the presence of subterranean hollows, and the moisture level of the morning air.

They eat the cork pillars. At dusk, when the wind settles and the plateau radiates its stored heat back into the purple sky, the locustids descend from their ridges and gather at the bases of the great spires. Their mandibles are massive, self-sharpening wedges that work with a slow, hydraulic rhythm, shearing out chunks of fibrous cork the size of a man’s fist. They chew for hours, their digestive tracts grinding the dry fiber into a dense, combustible meal that they store in internal throat-sacs, converting the cellulose into high-energy fats through the action of symbiotic, heat-loving gut microbes.

When they die, their carcasses are prized above all else by the nomadic hunters. Their leg-files make saws that can notch the hardest heartwood; their abdominal plates, boiled in resin to remove the stink, are turned into light, impenetrable bucklers and roof-scales for the burl-caves; and their dried tendons, stretched between two cork pins and dried under the noon glare, provide the cordage that holds the provisional human world together.

### The Capillary Grinders

Beneath everything—beneath the scurrying crabs, the screaming locustids, and the hunting gliders—move the things that make the floor unsafe. 

They are known simply as *the augers* or *the white-heads*. They inhabit the prehistoric taproots: ancient, fossilized conduits that carried sap during the world's dawn and have since dried into cavernous, horizontal pipes miles beneath the surface. The augers are segmented, cylindrical worms, some as thick as a tall tree and sixty paces long. They have no eyes, no appendages, and no lungs. They breathe through porous spiracles that open along their flanks, drinking the stagnant, resinous air of the deep wood.

Their heads are blunt, hemispherical caps of rotating, tooth-studded enamel that turn constantly, slowly, driven by an engine of massive ring-muscles. They do not burrow haphazardly; they follow the grain, grinding the petrified heartwood into an ultra-fine, bone-dry flour that fills the chambers behind them. This flour is under enormous pressure; as the auger pushes forward, it compacts the wood-dust into a dense, sterile plug that seals the tunnel forever.

The only sign of an auger’s presence near the surface is the *hum*. It begins as an itch in the arches of the feet—a high-frequency, oily vibration that makes the resin-beads in the grain shiver and melt. Within an hour, the wood directly above the worm becomes strangely warm, then scorching hot to the touch, heated by the immense friction of the grinding head twenty fathoms below. 

The people who live here know that when the wood turns hot, the floor has been compromised. The auger’s passage leaves the upper crust brittle, a hollow veneer of heartwood barely a foot thick spanning an empty chasm. A man or a locustid that steps onto such a place will hear a dry, thunderous *whuff*, and the floor will simply drop out from under them, plunging them into a suffocating drift of wood-meal that never settles, where the heat is high enough to roast the flesh from bone.

### The Shadow-Walkers

The humans of this high wooden world do not call themselves men; they call themselves the *Tally-folk*, after the notches they cut into the cork spires to measure their lives, or simply *Those Who Follow the Lean*.

Physically, they have been shaped by the glare and the roar. Their skin is not brown, but a deep, burnished copper-black, stained by the constant application of insect tallow and powdered charcoal, which protects them from the killing UV of the thin air. Their eyelids are narrow, swollen folds of tissue that leave only a slit for vision, and their irises are pale gray, almost silver, reflecting the harsh light like mica flakes.

Because the stridulation of the locustids and the roar of the dry wind are continuous, the Tally-folk have nearly abandoned the spoken voice. Their vocal cords are used only for harsh, barking signals that cut through the middle frequencies of the insect choirs. Among themselves, in the shelter of the burl-caves, they communicate with a lightning-fast manual language, their fingers tapping out complex rhythms on the soles of their feet or on the timber floor—a tactile speech that can be felt through the wood by someone lying three rooms away in the heart of a root.

Their lives are strictly dictated by the long, sweeping shadows cast by the 300-fathom cork pillars. 

These shadows are their countries. In the open plains, where the sun at midday will blister the skin through oil-softened hide in fifteen minutes, no man can survive outside. The shadow of a great pillar is not just a dark patch; it is an oasis of cool air, sometimes five miles long and a hundred yards wide, that sweeps across the plateau like the spoke of a planetary wheel. 

The Tally-folk live within this moving band of gray. They pack their light wicker frames and their skin bladders at sunrise, standing at the western edge of the shadow, and as the sun climbs and the shadow shortens and pivots toward the east, they walk with it. They walk at the precise speed of the earth’s rotation, never leaving the cool, blue-tinted corridor. Within the shadow, the air is twenty degrees cooler; the tacky resin on the ground stays firm underfoot; and the predatory gliders, who rely on the radiant thermals of the sun-baked plains, cannot easily drop down into the downdraft without tumbling.

In the late afternoon, as the shadow lengthens once more, stretching leagues toward the eastern horizon, the Tally-folk make their brief camps. They stake their wicker retaining walls around the condensation basins that are already beginning to cool in the grain. They work with an urgent, mechanical efficiency: scraping the pale grease of the spindle-crabs for cooking fat, splitting locustid femurs into arrowheads and scraping-irons, and setting the smoldering dung-coals into deep-drilled cork hearths that give off no spark that could ignite the plateau.

They have no concept of soil. In their mythology, the universe began with a seed that never sprouted, because there was no wetness to rot it; instead, it simply hardened, grew rings until it filled the sky, and left its children to live upon the rind. 

When a Tally-man knows he is dying—when his joints lock from the dry-rot fever or his lungs clog with the flour of the augers—he does not wait for the band to leave him. He walks out into the glare at midday, stripped of his oil and his skins. He finds an open furrow where the grain runs straight and clean to the horizon, lies down with his spine pressed firmly into one of the growth checks, and spreads his limbs wide. 

The heat does its work with astonishing swiftness. Within an hour, his fluids are drawn down into the porous heartwood beneath him; by sunset, his skin has tightened over his ribs like lacquered cork, his hair has blown away like thistle-down in the high wind shears, and his eyes have turned to two dry pebbles of resin. 

The scavengers come, but they take only the soft parts—the tongue, the entrails. The rest stays. The wind blows its fine red wood-dust over the form until the joints are packed tight and the hollows fill. In forty years, the body is not gone; it has merely become a knot in the floor, an amber-colored swelling in the ancient grain that the young shadow-walkers will step around as they follow the tall pillars across the plain, feeling the faint, stubborn bump beneath the soles of their sandals.
