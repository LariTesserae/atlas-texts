The official commissioning manifest listed the casement as uninhabited, and in the strict taxonomic sense understood by the surveyors on the plains below, the manifest remains correct. There is nothing here that was born of an egg or a womb, nothing that shivers, and nothing that leaves droppings upon the checker-plate floor. 

Yet the water is not untended.

Between the five-meter intake trunks, where the current is slow and smells faintly of dissolved solder, there are shapes that drift with the neutral buoyancy of waterlogged timber. They are the length of men—some slightly longer, drawn out by the absence of gravity and the endless traction of the upward-surging strap-leaves—but they have neither the urgency nor the fragile, dry-land architecture of the people who tightened the bulkhead bolts.

They were delivered here in the third month, before the final hatch was torqued to its test pressure, arriving not as passengers but as crate-freight: biological auxiliary units, cataloged simply as *displacement regulators* or *scouring proxies*. In the engineering blueprints they appear only as schematic silhouettes, drawn with the same blue-line indifference as the steam traps and the purge manifolds.

Their skin has the matte, non-reflecting quality of lard or blanched pig-iron. Because the water is permanently held at thirty-four degrees—the exact threshold where the human nervous system ceases to distinguish between the envelope of the body and the envelope of the world—they do not possess the concept of an edge. Where their skin meets the process-water, there is only a gradual thinning of density: a slick, translucent mantle of lipid-rich sebum, constantly replenished from deep subcutaneous reservoirs, that keeps the demineralized water from leaching the electrolytes from their blood. 

They do not wear clothes; fabric would fray, rot, and foul the fine brass mesh of the intake strainers within forty-eight hours. Instead, they are coated in the same micro-thin film of turbine oil that clings to the I-beams. It gives their pale shoulders an iridescent sheen under the high-altitude skylights, turning them the color of mother-of-pearl where the daylight strikes, and dark amber where the lower floodlights illuminate the undersides of their flanks.

They do not swim with the frantic, percussive thrash of terrestrial bathers. Their movement is a languid, hydraulic crawling, an exercise in spatial geometry conducted with long, web-fingered hands that have adapted to the standardized spacing of the installation’s pipework. They know the distance between a high-pressure bypass and an expansion loop to the millimeter; they hook their heels over the angle-iron stiffeners and lever themselves through the submerged aisles without breaking a single frond of the heavy jade foliage.

To watch one pass through a stand of the two-inch-thick ferns is like watching an oil slick move through grass. There is no displacement, no sudden eddy to disturb the micro-bubbles hanging in the broth. They slide between the rubbery leaves, their bellies skimming the cold, smooth zinc of an intake flange, their hairless skulls turned slightly sideways to clear the valve stems.

They do not speak. The dampening of the fluid is so absolute that vocal cords would be a useless vestige, a vibration wasted against forty thousand tons of inertia. Instead, they listen with their skeletons. 

To take the pulse of the installation, a tender will drift down to the thirty-meter floor, locate the hexagonal hub of a heat-exchanger casing, and press the flat of its brow against the milled steel. For ten or twelve minutes it will remain entirely motionless, its arms trailing like blanched roots, while the sixty-cycle rumble of the distant impeller passes directly through its teeth, through the frontal bones of its skull, and into the fluid of its inner ear. Through that vibration, it reads the health of the vessel: the slight cavitation of an obstructed port, the microscopic valve-flutter that precedes a blowout, the thickening of the water as the moss-analogues discharge their seasonal starch.

Their breathing is a slow, infrequent ceremony. Every two or three hours, propelled by a single lazy flex of the lumbar spine, one of them will rise vertically through the green twilight of the aisles, trailing a faint track of displaced oil. 

It breaks the surface in the narrow, dark foot of dead space beneath the ceiling plates, where the air is sour with hot nitrogen and the stink of ozone. It does not gasp. It tilts its face up into the steady rain of condensation falling from the roof rivets, opens a slit-like nasal valve with an audible, rubbery *clack*, and exhales a single, heavy lungful of depleted air that condenses instantly against the cold rolled plate. Then it drinks the damp gas, fills its cavernous, cartilaginous chest until its ribs press against the skin like the strakes of a boat, and sinks backward into the green.

They feed without hunger, as an engine consumes grease. The starch-heavy fern fronds, bursting with the synthetic sugars manufactured under the unblinking glare of the floodlights, are their pasture. A tender will draw the terminal spade of a leaf between its flattened, enameled dental plates, stripping the tender parenchymal tissue down to the central fiber in one slow, deliberate scrape. The taste is bitter, chemical, rich in potassium and the metallic salts of the sacrificial zinc anodes—the only salt they will ever know. They chew with a slow, grinding lateral motion, their pale jaws working while their eyes remain wide and unblinking in the amber wash from below.

Their eyes have no lids. There is no dust here to be washed away, no wind to dry the cornea. Over the months, the lenses have grown thick, spherical, and distinctly amber-tinted, filtering out the harsh blue-white rays that plummet through the skylights and tuning their sight entirely to the low-frequency sodium glow that blooms from the bronze floor wells. To them, the installation is not green and gray; it is a world of graded densities, a cathedral of interlocking geometric planes where the warmest pipes burn like pale bones through the foliage, and the cooler bulkheads recede into an infinite, velvety violet.

They have no names for each other, nor do they appear to distinguish between their own flesh and the structural ironwork they patrol. Occasionally, two will meet in a narrow cross-aisle where the strap-leaves have grown so thick that passage requires the compression of their chests against the pipe-cladding. 

When this happens, there is neither retreat nor greeting. They press past one another with a slow, squirming efficiency, their oiled skins sliding together with a faint, hydraulic suction that leaves no mark upon the water. For an instant, their amber eyes meet—pupils widened to the size of coins, reflecting neither curiosity nor fear, but only the unvarying noon of the chamber—before one slips downward toward the cold mud of the drainage bells, and the other rises to check the tension on a copper wire harness three decks above.

They are as new as the steel. They have no memories of the dirt, no mythology of the sun that heats the roof plate, and no awareness that beyond the three-inch steel casement there is a drop of five thousand meters into dry, freezing air that would shrivel their white lungs in twenty seconds. 

They know only the sixty cycles. They know the balance of the warm, heavy brine that supports them so perfectly they cannot tell if they are falling or rising. They are the maintenance of an unfinished thought: living components of a pump that runs without human ears to hear it, drifting along the web of the I-beams, licking the oil from the fresh bolt heads, perfectly, terrifyingly at home.
