The life here does not understand metal as something distinct from stone. To the things that crawl, graze, and drift across the plateau, cast bronze and chilled hematite are merely strata of an unusually dense geology—strata that happen to sweat food and generate their own weather.

At the lowest level of this ecology are the tallow-crusts and the graphite-feeders. Where the long connecting rods plunge into the rift floors, they leave a wake of warm, emulsified grease—a pale, mineral lard distilled from deep-earth synthetics that have circulated through the bearings for epochs. On this grease lives a rubbery, slate-colored turf that is neither fungus nor plant. It forms thick, wrinkled mats in the splash-zones, smelling faintly of heated linseed and wet slate. When the adiabatic drizzle sweeps through, this turf swells, its pores drinking the warm water to hydrate the thick hydrocarbon gel that fills its tissues. 

Embedded in these mats are the spindle-slugs: flat, armless mollusks the size of spades, their backs armored with overlapping plates of iron-rich chitin that mimic the slag gravel around them. They possess no eyes, but their undersides are a carpet of fine, muscular cilia that act as rasps, stripping the grease-turf down to the bare, polished steel beneath. A large crank-pin, three hundred paces long, might carry several hundred of these creatures on its underside, riding the slow, rhythmic arc through the damp air, feeding continuously while the motion swings them back and forth between the rift floor and the black vault above.

Higher on the basalt swells, where the ground is drier and the hum of the subterranean armatures vibrates with a dry, buzzing frequency, the herds move. 

They are known only by the sound of their transit—a dry, clicking scuttle like thousands of wooden pegs shaken in a leather sack. These are the *striders*, twelve-legged ungulates whose segmented limbs are tipped with hard, calcified crescents. Their bodies are long, barrel-shaped, and low-slung, clad in a hide like vulcanized rubber that sheds both the oily drizzle and the static sparks that drift from the transmission pinnacles. 

The striders have no heads in the conventional sense. Instead, their forward segments taper into a blunt sensory cowl rimmed with thousands of vibrating whiskers that register the exact cadence of the earth beneath them. They do not sleep lying down; they lean against the warm, humming flanges of the pillow-blocks, synchronizing their slow heartbeats to the stroke of the nearest deep-earth pump. If a herd is startled, they do not scatter; they align themselves parallel to the lines of magnetic flux that leak from the transmission cables overhead and run in dead-straight files across the dark shale, their crescent hooves striking the stone in an intricate, polyrhythmic fugue that allows them to read the contours of the ground ahead by echo-location.

They feed by trailing flexible, rasping proboscises through the gravel, hoovering up the grease-moths that have died and fallen during the pressure-shifts, as well as the thick, salty sweat that crystalizes on the iron bolts. Their meat, if something could catch one, would taste overwhelmingly of machine oil and bitter sulfur, their blood a thick, pale lymph rich in dissolved copper.

Where there are herds, there are those that track them, though hunting in this total, rhythmic darkness requires a strange patience.

The apex hunters of the plateau are the *vent-stalkers*. They have the long, low proportions of weasels, but are scaled like pangolins, their plates formed from compressed keratin impregnated with magnetite. This armor dampens the static charges of the high air, allowing them to move without producing the telltale corona-glow that would give them away to sensitive whiskers. 

The stalker does not stalk by stealth of silence—silence is impossible on stone that trembles like a struck bell. Instead, it stalks by *phase-cancellation*. It moves only during the microsecond of peak compression in the nearest piston stroke—the exact moment when the bedrock is momentarily overloaded with vibration, masking the sound of its own claws. It will spend three days stalking a strider across fifty paces of open basalt, taking one lunging step every seventy seconds, freezing in place while the piston returns, its cold, blind snout tasting the air for the warm, greasy plume of the herd’s breath.

When it strikes, it does not aim for the throat; it shears through the vulnerable joint-caps of the strider’s middle legs, grounding the creature. Then it waits. The strider does not cry out; it only beats its remaining hooves against the stone in a frantic, out-of-tempo rhythm that brings other scavengers running from miles away.

Above all this, in the sunless cavern of the upper air, exists a separate, suspended kingdom. 

The thermal columns that rise from the unhoused cooling vents are massive—invisible geysers of hot, damp air fifty yards across, surging miles into the blackness. Riding these columns are the *kites*: broad, sheet-like organisms twenty or thirty paces across, made of semi-translucent skin so thin that the red friction-glow from distant gears can be seen through their ribs. They have no flapping wings; they are living sails, their perimeters trimmed by long, cartilaginous stays that flex to catch the drafts. 

Trailing beneath each kite is a veil of filamentous tendrils, fifty feet long and coated in a sticky, tallow-soluble mucus. As the kite circles lazily in the blind updrafts, these tendrils drift through the swarms of grease-moths—soft, thumb-sized insects with downy, oil-soaked wings that breed by the billions in the warm sludge of the piston-sumps. The moths become stuck in the drift-lines; every few hours, the kite uses long, jointed claspers to draw the tendrils up toward its central mouth-pore, cleaning them with a slow, methodical rasping sound like dry paper sliding across iron.

Sometimes, a kite will drift too near one of the high-voltage transmission lines slung between the peaks. When this happens, there is no sound of impact—only a blinding, violet discharge that splits the darkness for three leagues around, casting hard, stark shadows of the ancient machinery against the black sky for a fraction of a second. The kite is vaporized instantly, transformed into an oily, ozone-scented ash that settles slowly back down onto the basalt, where the spindle-slugs and the blind lizards feed on the mineral fallout.

And yet, there are things here that suggest a dimmer, more troubling presence than simple beasts.

In the dead spaces—the wide, windless shelves between the major gear-trains where the ground is covered in deep, sifted banks of graphite dust—there are tracks that are not hooves or claws. They are broad, three-toed impressions, deeply sunk, trailing the drag-marks of heavy, low-slung bellies.

These are the *tallow-monks*, though they are not men and they possess no religion. They are bulky, hairless bipeds, their skin the color of lard, standing twice the height of a man with thick, pillar-like legs that end in splayed, padded cushions. Their faces are perfectly smooth—no eyes, no nostrils, only a broad, vertical fissure lined with sensory lobes that can taste the atmospheric pressure down to the millibar. 

They do not hunt, and they do not graze on the moss. Instead, they carry tools—not tools they have made, but shaped scraps of heavy iron dropped from overhead gantry-cranes thousands of years ago, smoothed and rounded by generations of leathery palms. 

A tallow-monk will spend its entire two-hundred-year life attending to a single bearing. It lives in the pocket of warm air beneath the bearing’s massive casting, sleeping in the grease-troughs, waking to the three-day cycle of the flywheel. When the pressure-drizzle comes, it climbs the bronze ribbing with slow, terrifying agility, using its scrapers of salvaged iron to clear the hardened slag and dried carbon from the lubricant grooves, ensuring the synthetic grease continues to flow freely into the journal. 

They do not speak, but they communicate across leagues by striking the great drive-rods with rounded stones. A single strike rings through thousands of tons of metal, traveling through the continental network of shafts and cables to be felt through the soles of another monk forty miles away. They exchange no histories, no warnings, no names. The signals are always the same: a steady, rhythmic confirmation of the stroke, the temperature of the oil, the clearance of the bronze, the continuation of the torque.

They believe, if the slow chemical currents in their broad brains can be called belief, that if the iron stops turning, the radiant heat of the stone will die, the warm drizzle will turn to glass, and the sunless black overhead will fall like a stone roof, crushing everything into the silent, frozen dark. And so they scrape, and they listen, and the machines knead the marrow of the world beneath them, indifferent, eternal, and perfectly fed.
