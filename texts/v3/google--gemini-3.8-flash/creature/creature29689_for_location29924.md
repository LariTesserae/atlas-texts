To endure this place, life cannot possess an edge that the water can catch, nor a belly that cavitation can hollow out. Anything with fins or broad sails of flesh is turned into broth against the flint-hard walls within three turns of the gyre.

What truly lives here does not swim. It wedges.

Deep inside the cleavages of the basalt—where the rock split only weeks ago and the fissures are as narrow as a folded knife—are the *pavements*. At first glance they appear to be runs of lead solder poured into the cracks, gray-white and dull, sealing seam after seam. But under the concussive shocks of the water, they shiver. They are colonial siphon-worms, their bodies flattened into ribbons no thicker than parchment, bound to one another by a curdled, copper-rich mucus that hardens on contact with the brine into something resembling vulcanized rubber. They do not feed on organic drift; there is none to be had. Instead, their exposed margins are lined with microscopic, comb-like cilia made of spun silica that rasp endlessly against the stone, drinking the fresh, un-weathered mineral powders cast off by the basin’s grinding floor. They have no eyes, no nervous centers larger than a bead of resin, but they possess a terrible, hydraulic grip. If a fissure begins to widen under the hydraulic hammering of an implosion, the colony swells, pumping dense, salty fluid into its interior vessels until it locks itself into the gap like an expanding masonry bolt.

Higher up, along the submerged ledges where the dry-stacked shelters were built, the builders themselves are still present. 

They did not drown, because they were never purely creatures of the air, though they were not made for this violence either. They are things of long, jointed proportion, pale as lard, with limbs that fold in three places like the legs of a surveyor’s tripod. When the floor tore open and the brine welled up from the mountain’s gut to swallow the cirque, they did not flee into the freezing sky—perhaps because the air of this altitude kills them faster than the heat, or perhaps because their joints are too brittle to support their own weight without the thick, syrupy lift of the brine. 

They have retreated into the collapsed interiors of their own huts. 

There, tucked beneath tilted slabs of basalt that have not yet drifted loose, they sit folded in upon themselves, four or five to a shelter, knees pressed against their narrow breastbones. They have bound themselves to the flagstones with braided hanks of the rust-colored moss, looping the fibers around their wrists and ankles until they are tethered like sunken anchors. They do not move with the rhythm of the water; they let the water move *around* them, their gray, leathery skin shivering with the skull-vibrating hum of the basin. 

Their heads are broad, hairless shovels of cartilage, entirely devoid of mouths. Where a jaw should be, there is only a horizontal slit protected by a series of overlapping, calcified plates—bony louvers that open only when an imploding vacuum bell passes nearby, venting their internal pressure so their chests do not cave in. They breathe the sulfur and the raw copper through four spongy pits set between their shoulder blades, pits that flare and choke rhythmically, staining the stone behind them with black rings of oxidized iron. 

They are patient with the patience of things that know a catastrophe is only hours old. Their chalk marks are still intact on the stones they sit upon. Between their tucked knees, their long, spade-fingered hands are constantly working: not carving, but feeling. They trace the fractures running through the slabs. With blunt, horn-tipped thumbs, they grind away the razor edges of the rock, smoothing the stone beneath their own bodies grain by grain, as though they intend to remain until the cathedral-wide churn has polished the entire mountain down to a pebble.

And then there are the scavengers of the skin. 

Right at the rim, where the brine shears against the open void and the water bulges out toward the empty valley thousands of feet below, the violent gyre thins into a laminar sheet. Here, where the water is only inches deep before it curves back into the roaring center, lives something entirely different: thin, disk-like animals no larger than a coin, clear as mica, skating by the thousands along the inside of that trembling surface tension. 

They live upside-down, their dorsal surfaces glued to the boundary between the brine and the atmosphere. Through the lens of the water, their undersides drink the thin, cold sunlight of the peak, while their fine, wire-like legs dangle down into the boiling froth below, snagging the shredded tatters of the soft-shelled fissure-worms that are pulverized in the depths. When a cavitation bubble breaks the rim and the liquid skin flexes wildly outward into the sky, these disks do not wash away; they contract their margins, cupping the water like thousands of tiny suction bells, holding the very threshold together with their bodies until the wave retreats.

They are the keepers of the wall. Without them, the trembling skin would have torn days ago, and the sea would have tipped its boiling heart down the scree slopes into the lower world. They cling to the edge of nothingness, half in the boiling copper murk, half facing the spinning, inverted heavens, fed on pulp, holding the sky out and the drowned stone in.
