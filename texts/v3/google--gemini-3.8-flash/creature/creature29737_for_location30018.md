To understand what lives on the black gabbro, one must look first at their feet.

Nothing with soft pads survives more than a single scramble across these benches. The rock here is not worn; it is cleft. A slip against a fresh fracture of argillite does not bruise—it flenses. The voles that scurry through the condensation gutters have developed under-paws like vulcanized rubber, pebbled and hard, the skin calloused into a split-resistant tread that smells of wet slate and animal fat. They do not run so much as wedge themselves, jamming their splayed digits into micro-fractures, moving in flat, nervous spasms close to the wet stone. 

Their fur is extraordinary: double-layered, ridiculously dense, and completely hydrophobic. It does not absorb the aerosolized steam that blows up from the chimney zones below; instead, the droplets bead instantly upon the needle-fine guard hairs, forming a shivering coat of quicksilver beads that rolls off with every shudder of their flanks. If you were to catch one—though they dive into fissures narrow as a knife-blade at the slightest shadow—it would feel dry and feverishly hot in the palm. Their metabolisms run at a screaming pitch, fueled entirely by the protein drift: millions of dead and stunned midges skimmed from the surface of the weeping pools, and the thick, crustaceous rings of yolk-yellow lichen that peel from the wet rock like wet rind.

They share the fracture networks with the marmots, though the marmots are of an older, heavy-boned temperament. These are broad, low-slung brutes, their pelts the color of wet cinder, so thoroughly impregnated with carbon dust and precipitated lime that their coats have a stiff, felted texture, like the lagging wrapped around industrial steam pipes. 

The marmots do not hibernate. In this altitude, where the seasons are muffled beneath a perpetual ceiling of factory-warmed fog, time does not pass in winters and summers, but in the throttling up and down of the works below. When the continental engines enter their weekend or cycle troughs—when the sub-audible hum drops half an octave and the thermal updrafts slacken—the marmots retreat into the deep joint-planes where the tectonic strain keeps the rock slightly warmer than the air. They sleep piled against one another, their heavy skulls pressed directly against the gabbro. They are addicted to the vibration. Generations have been whelped to that 60-cycle bone-thrum; its cadence regulates their heartbeats. If the valley below were ever to go cold and quiet, the silence would likely kill them of pure disorientation.

Above the shelves, where the verticality becomes absolute, the soot-prions and cliff-swallows own the margins. 

Their nests are not made of mud and twigs—there is no mud here, only stone flour, and not a twig for forty miles. Instead, the swallows have engineered a masonry of necessity. They catch the airborne fluff that rises in the hot plumes: shredded fiberglass drifting from distant filtration plants, synthetic fibers, carbon particulate, and silica moss, binding it all together into tough, bracket-shaped cups glued to the overhanging lips of the gabbro with an exceptionally viscous, chemical-resistant saliva. The nests look like carbuncles of burnt sugar clinging to the cliffs. 

The birds themselves are wedge-winged, their eyes hooded by heavy, nictitating membranes that guard against the stinging bite of the copper-rich moisture. They fly like thrown blades. Because the updrafts are ferocious and constant, rising out of the white inversion like hot air through a furnace flue, the birds rarely need to flap. They hang motionless on the edges of the abyss, cocking a single primary feather to shift thirty feet up a vertical line, scooping up clouds of pale, blind midges that breed in the warm trickle of the drainage tracks. The prions are larger, darker, with greasy bills that smell sharply of sulfur and machine oil; they nest deeper in the twilight clefts, their calls sounding like wet pebbles shaken in a gourd, swallowed instantly by the sheer scale of the drop.

And then there are the watchers on the handspan ledges.

Seen from below, through the shifting rents in the steam, they appear less like living animals and more like seams of quartz or pockets of unmelted rime. They are ungulates, but distorted by the extremity of their home into something lean, architectural, and spare. Their legs are disproportionately long and corded with tendons as thick as ship’s hawser; their ribs are laterally compressed, allowing them to press their entire mass flush against a sheer wall to let a falling slab clear their shoulders.

Their hooves are miraculous things. Split deep up the middle, the outer horn is hard as the iron-banded stone it treads, chipped into jagged, self-sharpening cusps that bite into microscopic tool marks on the fracture faces. The inner pad is a bulbous, greasy cushion of elastic tissue that adheres to wet gabbro almost by suction. They do not jump. They pour themselves upward from notch to notch, moving with a deliberate, agonizing slowness that resembles liquid creeping up a crack.

They are chalk-white, but stained along their bellies and briskets with the orange rust of the argillite they scrape against. Their eyes are flat, oblong bars of pale amber, set high and sideways, entirely focused on the fog. They stand for hours without shifting a muscle, their jaws grinding in a slow, rotary circuit. They are eating the rock itself—or rather, the dense, silica-stiff moss and the crystalline mineral salts left behind where the steam sheets dry out against the freezing stone. Their teeth are broad, flat plates of enameled iron, yellowed and grooved, constantly worn down by the abrasive dust and constantly growing from the root.

They have learned the language of the mountain's birth. When an internal pressure builds within a buttress—hours before the stone actually shears with that dry, gunshot snap—a change occurs in the sub-audible drone traveling up from the valleys. The pitch sharpens; the rock begins to sing at a frequency that humans could only feel as a nausea in the inner ear. The pale beasts hear it. Long before the slab drops into the mist, they step delicately, without haste, across the face to an older, deadened anchor of stone, and wait.

There is no grass here for them, no seasonal rut, no sweet water. They drink the weeping faces, licking the cold, coppery ribbons as they slide over the black stone. They have no natural predators, for nothing with the appetite for meat could navigate the sheer terror of these faces; their only enemy is the structural integrity of the crust itself, and the occasional blast of an over-pressurized thermal plume that turns the air around them to scalding vapor.

They stand like gargoyles on the lip of the modern world, entirely wild, entirely unaware of what lies beneath the sea of white-gray inversion. They do not know of the furnaces, the boilers, the sweating men, the great spinning turbines that sustain the air they breathe. They simply accept the mountain as it is: a hard, vertical cradle that never stops trembling, fed by the warm breath of an invisible god that lives in the dark at the bottom of everything.
