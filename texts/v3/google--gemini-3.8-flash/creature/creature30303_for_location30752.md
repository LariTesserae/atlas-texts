To look closely at the workers who dismantle and rebuild the terraces is to find oneself staring at an anatomy that has forgotten the sky, the night, and the use of the tongue.

They are no taller than four feet, but their torsos are broad, barrel-shaped, and flattened from chest to spine, like river boulders wedged between logs. Their clavicles are thick as ox bones, fused almost directly to the base of their skulls without any visible throat. Under the oiled eel-hide smocks—greasy, lead-grey skins stripped from blind morays that dwell in the deep drain-holes—their musculature is not banded like an athlete’s, but continuous, dense, and lard-pale, lacking definition except where the heavy tendons of the shoulders knot into the neck.

Their faces have no brows. Where an eyebrow would ridge, there is only a smooth, overhanging plate of tallow-colored skin, grooved with drainage channels that steer condensation away from the eyes and down across the temples. The eyes themselves are shocking: they have no irises, no whites, and no lids. Instead, each socket is filled by an oval disc of dark, horn-hard amber, flat and unblinking, behind which a tiny, pinprick pupil shifts like a bead of mercury. They do not close these discs against the glare of the ceiling because they cannot. The glare is the only condition their tissue has ever known; to shut it out would be, to them, a cessation of orientation akin to falling off the earth.

When they breathe, their nostrils do not flare. Instead, a pair of slit-like spiracles beneath the hinge of the jaw pulse with a wet, bellows-like regularity, venting small puffs of vapor into the cold air. They do not talk. Their vocal tracts are reduced to vestigial cartilages, dry whistles that only rattle when an elder’s lungs fill with limestone dust at the end of life. All coordination is seismic. 

When a gang of sixty heaves a two-ton foundation flag from its bed, they do not chant. They place the flat pads of their calloused bare feet—soles as thick as boot-leather, pale and fissured like dried turnips—against the bedrock and strike the stone with the butts of their bone mallets in a staggered, galloping cadence. The frequency runs through the tonalite, travels up through the marrow of the others, and produces an instant, synchronized contraction of their legs. They haul in silence, their lips drawn back over yellowed, blunt tooth-bars that are not individual teeth at all, but solid plates of dentin used for crushing amphipod carapaces and breaking soft slate.

Their domestic life takes place entirely within the chest-high walls they never finish. 

Because the light never tilts, they have no concept of a room with a roof. To cover oneself from above is, in their instinct, to invite suffocation or to court the weight of the mountain dropping down. They live open to the halite sky. Their "dwellings" are merely stone-walled corrals twenty feet across, floored with polished river-shale that is constantly wiped free of condensation by the drag of their leather aprons. 

Inside these pens, there are no beds, no hearths, and no fire. They have no fuel and no word for heat; warmth is an aberration found only in the gullets of freshly caught eels or in the steaming, sulfurous bile of the deepest sump-cracks. They rest by squatting together in tight, hexagonal clumps—ten or twelve bodies wedged hip-to-hip, backs outward, heads tucked low between their knees. While the outer layer of the cluster dozes in four-minute micro-sleeps, their amber eye-discs staring unseeingly at the flags, their thick hands continue to twitch, fingers endlessly knotting and unknotting lengths of dry gut or feeling the seam of the nearest stone for grit.

The walls must be moved because the stone is alive with mineral rot.

In this damp, salt-saturated deep, the limestone is not static; it "grows" at a rate perceptible across weeks. Cold mineral sweat leaches out of the pores, depositing crusts of brittle, needle-sharp aragonite that weld loose stones into solid, choking masses within forty days. If a terrace is allowed to calcify into a single piece, it blocks the hairline rills through which the basin drains its moisture. The water pools, stagnates, and becomes foul with an anaerobic grease that rots the feet of the scarabs and kills the amphipods. 

The people are not building monuments; they are operating a gigantic, manual sieve. By prying the stones apart, scraping the white aragonite fur from the joints with their horn chisels, and restacking them three hundred yards away, they keep the floor porous. They are the kidney of the basin. The entire city is an engine of dry-laid gravel that must be stirred constantly, stone by stone, to keep the rift from sealing itself into a glassy, flooded tomb of lime.

Behind the work-gangs follow the cleaners: the younger ones, lean and spider-limbed, who carry wide scoops made from the split jaws of shovel-nosed lungfish. They scrape up the thousands of dead crabs that have been crushed under the sledges, tossing them into tall wicker panniers strapped to their backs. Nothing lies on the stone for more than twenty heartbeats. 

If an amphipod falls from a net, three pale crabs have its limbs before it stops twitching; if a crab is crushed by a mallet, a lard-colored flyer drops like a thrown plumb-line, hits the cobbles with a leathery slap, impales the pulp on its needle-beak, and is dragged back into the air by its frantic, four-winged sculling before the hammer has even risen again.

These winged things—the *lards*, as their flesh resembles nothing so much as cold suet—are neither birds nor insects. They have no feathers and no chitin. Their bodies are tubular, wrapped in a smooth, translucent skin through which one can see the black knot of their digestive sacs and the frantic, three-chambered pumping of a colorless heart. Their four wings are not hinged with bone, but supported by flexible rays of gristle that bend and whip with an ugly, mechanical snapping. They have no eyes; their heads taper to a blunt, pig-like snout pierced by thousands of microscopic pits that sense the tiny electrostatic charges given off by the twitching legs of the scarabs below. 

They breed on the wing. They never land except to strike, and when they drop, their impact is careless, often breaking their own gristle-spurs against the tonalite. They die by the hundreds, crashing into one another in the air-pockets that plummet from the ceiling, their heavy, grease-filled bodies tumbling into the plazas where the human scavengers gather them without looking up, snapping off their wings and dropping the fatty carcasses into brine-vats to be rendered into the lubricant that keeps their sledges sliding over the slate.

At the edge of the skin-deep lake, the work is even more desperate. 

The lake is not deep enough to drown a child, but it is six miles broad—a shivering, grey skin of water that stays at precisely thirty-six degrees. The bottom is an unbroken pavement of smooth, water-worn marble, covered by a three-inch layer of living grease: a carpet of sulfur-metabolizing microbes so dense it feels like wet velvet underfoot. 

Feeding on this carpet are the amphipods—creatures the length of a man’s forearm, but flat as ribbons and completely clear. When lifted from the water, they look like strips of cut cellophane, their hundreds of fringed swimming-legs thrashing with a sound like wind in dry grass.

The humans who work the sluices do not wear the eel-hide. They work naked from the waist down, their thighs and calves caked in a thick, grey tallow made from crushed lard-wings and chalk-dust to protect their skin from the mineral brine, which would otherwise pickle a man’s flesh until it peeled in sheets. They wade in long, diagonal ranks, pushing weir-hurdles made of split rib-bones through the churn. 

They do not look at each other. They work by the pull of the current against their knees. When a weir fills, it is hoisted by four people using bone tripods, their amber eyes turned downward into the sloshing pulp. They plunge their forearms into the mass, feeling for the heavy, bloated "mothers"—the five-pound breeders whose bellies are yellow with fat-globules—which are bitten through behind the head to kill them instantly, then tossed into the carrying-urns. The smaller ones are dumped into the stone sluices, washed down into the residential quarters where the old ones and the infants wait behind the low walls, scoops in hand, to skim their daily ration from the rushing ditch.

There is no ceremony for the dead here. When a builder drops—his heart bursting under the weight of a slate block or his spine failing after fifty years of the stone-sieve—the reaction of the work-gang is as swift as it is indifferent. They do not drop the rope. The sledge does not stop. 

Two of the younger runners peel off from the rear, roll the dead one onto his back, and strip him of his eel-skin in four practiced strokes of a flake-knife. His bone mallet and his horn wedges are divided between the runners’ own pouches before his skin has even lost its slickness. The body itself is not buried; there is no earth to dig, and to leave it on the flags would allow the lime-sweat to cement it to the tonalite within days, creating an obstruction that would have to be chiseled away later.

They drag the carcass to the edge of the sluices and tip it into the current. 

By the time the body reaches the skin-deep lake, the crabs have already removed the face. By the time it drifts half a mile into the white froth where the amphipods churn, it is a clean, yellow-white cage of ribs, rolling over the smooth marble bottom like a tumbleweed. In three weeks, the bones will be worn round by the sand-scour, washed up onto the southern terraces, and gathered by the work-gangs to be split into trowels, wedges, and net-hurdles.

Nothing leaves the rift. Nothing enters it. 

Above, through the skylight of halite, the outside world occasionally changes its tint: a red dusk that the inhabitants cannot see, a grey cloud-cover that dulls the glare by a fraction of a candle, an eclipse that turns the salt-cracks to violet for twenty minutes. But to the things on the floor, these are not events; they are merely minor fluctuations in the weight of the glare, no more significant than the pulse of water through a gill.

The city has no history because it has no nouns. It has only verbs: *to haul, to scrape, to unjoint, to wedge, to crush.* Under the flat, shadowless white, in the cellar-cold air that swallows the cry of the newborn and the cracking of stone alike, the work-gangs run their eighty-stride boulevards. They carry the city three hundred paces to the east, stone by polished stone, and when they have laid the last slate flag and the aragonite begins to needle its edges once more, they turn about, raise their horn chisels, and begin to carry it back.
