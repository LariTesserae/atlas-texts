To live here is to be composed of the same grease and lime as the sumps.

At the lowest tier of existence, where the water is thickest with pulverized slate and the tallowy byproducts of the cylinder heads, there are the incrustators—creatures that are barely more than calcified scabs clinging to the warm metal flanges. They are not barnacles, though they share their stubborn geometry; they are colonial worms that secrete an armor not of pure aragonite, but of iron sulfide and mineral grease, dark as wet anthracite. They grow in long, low ridges that align themselves with the directional sheer of the current, like living rifling inside the discharge flumes. When a discharge valve slams shut, sending a water-hammer shock down through sixty yards of riveted plate, these beds of tubes do not retract. They have no soft parts that fear concussive force; their flesh is a grey, curd-like marrow that thrives on the hydrostatic pulse, pumping sulfurous liquor through their bodies by the sheer external compression of the machine’s stroke.

Moving over them are the pickers. These are pale, flattened things, shaped like spade heads or wide wooden wedges, neither crab nor woodlouse, with nine pairs of unjointed, paddle-like limbs tipped with black, chitinous chisels. They are completely blind, lacking even the pitted sensory depressions where eyes might once have sat. Instead, they navigate entirely by the rhythmic displacement of the water. They know the geography of the basin not as rock and iron, but as zones of pressure: the dead, quiet suck behind an intake cowl; the shearing, skinning rip of a cracked high-pressure flange; the slow, heavy heave where a thousand gallons a minute spill over a basalt sill. They graze the bacterial mats and the dead tubificids that accumulate in the lee of the pipe saddles, their mouthparts making a dry, rhythmic clicking—a tiny percussion that is utterly drowned out by the roar of the falls, felt only if one presses a gloved palm directly against the plate they are clearing.

Higher up, where the steelwork climbs out of the brown soup into the warm, yellowed mist, the life becomes vertebrate, though scarcely more recognizable.

There are the lung-eels—thick around as a man’s thigh, grey-pink, with loose, folded skin that looks as though it were tailored for an animal twice their bulk. They do not stay in the basins. Because the rock is slicked with an inch of warm, moving film, the world is three-dimensional for them; they crawl up the vertical cleavage faces of the dolomite, their bellies pressed into the falling sheets of zinc-heavy water, using broad, muscular pectoral pads tipped with single, curved hooks of bone to hoist themselves from one weeping ledge to the next. They nest behind the hot-water conduits where the ambient temperature approaches eighty degrees Celsius, coiled together in knots of five or six, their broad, blunt snouts thrust into the exhaust jets of the auxiliary valves. They have abandoned gills almost entirely; their throats are lined with highly vascularized, spongy red tissues that absorb oxygen directly from the saturated aerosol mist. When the low-RPM pumps drop a stroke—when the five-second beat falters and the stone shudders with an irregular hitch—the lung-eels stir within their crevices, twisting against one another with the heavy, wet sound of dropped meat, disturbed not by the sound, but by the sudden absence of the rhythm that keeps their blood moving through their sluggish hearts.

And then there are the screen-scrapers. 

They are men, or they were sent down as men, but the basin does not preserve the human form in its dry-land proportions. The human body here cannot maintain its boundaries. In an atmosphere that is permanently eighty-five degrees, saturated with zinc salts, engine grease, and pulverized calcite, the skin ceases to act as a barrier and becomes a porous, living transition between the flesh and the slurry. 

Their skin is not pale in the way a cave salamander is pale; it is the color of lard that has been melted and re-solidified with machine oil, yellowish-white and mapped with blue, sprawling veins that lie close to the surface to dump heat. They have lost their hair entirely—not through sickness, but through the continuous, scouring slip of the water that washes over them whenever they move along the catwalks. They do not wear clothes, for cloth rots into a sour, black cheese within seventy-two hours of immersion; instead, they coat their loins and torsos in thick slabs of tallow and boiled bitumen, an artificial hide that must be scraped off and reapplied with wooden laths as it fouls with sediment.

Their ears are long dead. The acoustic compressor of the vault broke their drums in their first week below the headworks; their auditory canals are plugged with plugs of greased hemp or have simply swollen shut with thickened, waterlogged scar tissue. They communicate entirely through iron. They carry short, heavy drifts—lengths of hex-stock steel about three feet long—which they use to hook themselves to the safety cables when the footings grease over, but which serve primarily as their ears and tongues. To know what the pump house five miles to the north is doing, a scraper drops to his knees on a wet stringer, sets the cold chisel-end of his steel drift against the web of an I-beam, and clenches his back molars directly around the top of the hex-stock. 

Through the jaw, through the zygomatic arch and the temporal bone, the machine speaks to them with immense clarity. They can tell by the vibration in their dental enamel if the number-four intake in the northern sump has choked on a drift of amphipods, or if the packing glands on the main lift shaft have dried out and are scoring the five-ton crank. When they speak to one another, they do not open their mouths; they strike their drifts against the flanges in short, brutal couplets that travel through the iron bridges faster than sound ever could through the wet, greasy air.

They live in the "dry pockets"—though nothing is ever dry, only less drowning. These are the dead steam chests and the rusted-out casings of seventeenth-century boiler shells that were abandoned when the deeper levels were blasted out fifty years ago. They sleep jammed into these steel barrels like salt herring, their limbs intertwined to keep from rolling into the churning drains if a tie-rod snaps in the night. 

They eat what the intake screens catch. Every six hours, according to the slow, heavy drop of the pressure gauges, two scrapers must wade out onto the cantilevered grating that guards the intake of the primary silt pumps. The water there reaches their armpits, dragging at them with a muscular, idiot force that would roll a draft horse over the brink into the grinding screws. They work with long-handled scoops made of perforated iron sheet, dredging the drifts of porcelain-white amphipods that have wedged themselves into the mesh. They heap the crustaceans into galvanized buckets. Eaten raw, they taste of wet copper and cold lard, but the scrapers crush them in stone mortars with lumps of sulfur-fungus scraped from the blasting timbers, pounding it into an oily, grey paste that does not spoil in the steam.

There is no generational memory among them of the sky. If you could clear the scar tissue from their throats and quiet the pumps long enough for a voice to carry, they would not understand the concept of an edge to the world, or of air that does not possess weight and fluid mass. To them, the rift is the universe: an infinite vertical drop of broken basalt down which all the water of the world is falling, and a floor of iron machines that must be tended with grease, lead, and tallow, lest the warm sludge rise over their lips and fill the quiet hollow of their lungs.

And between the scrapers and the machines, there is a third thing—the "packers."

Whether they are men who crawled too far into the drainage culverts during the great subsidence twenty years ago and remained there, or something that was already dormant in the dolomitic fissures before the first borehole breached the vault, no one can say. They are seen only as bulges in the lagging of the long steam pipes. They have worked their way between the asbestos mattresses and the hot steel casing, where the temperature is high enough to blister ordinary flesh. There, they have become long, narrow, and ribless, their bones having softened into tough, cartilage-like thongs that can flatten into gaps three inches wide. 

They do not use tools. They tend the joints with their mouths and fingers, licking the weeping threads of the high-pressure couplings, their saliva apparently rich in silica or some mineral binder that reacts with the iron dust to form a hard, black mastic that stops leaks better than red lead. The scrapers do not kill them; they leave small heaps of crushed amphipod meal on the valve bonnets for them. In return, the packers do not drop from the roof into the churning sluices where the men work, and when a pipe is about to burst from steam-erosion, they drum upon the casing with their soft, flat knuckles—three long rolls and a stop—giving the scrapers time to drop their bars, wedge their heels into the rivet rows, and hold their breath against the killing wave of boiled iron.
