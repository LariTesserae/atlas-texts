To understand who can live here, you have to look first at their feet. 

The people of the Rung do not have the long, arching insteps of valley travelers. Their feet are broad, almost spade-like, with short, thick toes that have developed an unusual prehensile splay. The outer edge of the foot is rimmed with a yellow, rubbery horn—callus built not by dry rock, but by generations of gripping peeled birch-poles, slimy nettle-withes, and the wet, corrugated bark of the cypress trunks. They do not wear boots; leather rots off the ankle within a month in the spray. Instead, they bind their arches with strips of beaten willow-bast soaked in tallow, leaving the pads of the heel and toes bare to the cold slime. They slip, of course, but they slip predictably, with a low center of gravity and a strange, rolling shuffle that drops their hips toward the timbers at the first shudder of the walkway.

They are short, thick-chested folk, their lungs wide-belled to drag nourishment from the thin, mist-swollen air. Their skin is rarely seen bare; it is coated from childhood with a paste of rendered river-weasel fat, crushed cypress gall, and soot from the braziers, which turns them the color of a wet slate shingle and sheds water like an oiled skin. Beneath the grease, their flesh is subject to the *moss-bloom*—a pale, harmless dermatomycosis that settles in the creases of their elbows, between their shoulder blades, and under their nails, smelling distinctly of damp malt. A clean, pink-skinned man in the Rung would be considered either an invalid or an exile; to be grey, greasy, and faintly scented of mold is to be sealed against the marrow-bite of the mist.

They are essentially deaf to middle frequencies. Three centuries of living inside the sounding board of a granite cleft filled with two plunging cataracts has ground down their hearing until ordinary speech is useless. They communicate across the tiers with a vocabulary of high, piercing whistling—drawn through the teeth and shaped by cupped palms—that cuts cleanly through the bass vibration of the falls. In close quarters, inside the huts, they rely on a rapid, thrumming sign-language made against the ribcage or the thigh, their voices reserved for a low, gravelly hum that vibrates through the jawbone rather than traveling through the air.

Their social life is dictated by the depth of the wood. The lowest tiers, where the huts have been half-digested by the secondary cambium of the fig-cypress, belong to the *Crawl-folk*—the oldest lineages, who tend the root-casings and harvest the deep-well mollusks. These people live in a state of green twilight, their eyes adapted to the sulfurous smolder of birch-punk braziers and the cold phosphorescence of a bioluminescent shelf-fungus that they cultivate on the wet timber walls. They are quiet, slow-moving, and their joints are often warped by the gravel-ache, a severe form of high-altitude rheumatism that turns their fingers into gnarled, bark-like hooks. Yet it is they who hold the city together: they know which roots are drinking too fast, which anchors are rotting from heart-rot, and where to drive the hemlock wedges to divert a new runoff stream before it can scour a tier of huts out over the void.

Above them, where the tree branches into light and air, live the younger families and the trappers. Here, the dwellings are lighter, little more than domed baskets lashed with rawhide that stretch and groan in the wind. The people here are faster, leaner, and perpetually bruised from the recoil of their bird-traps. 

Between the people and the forest, there is no hard boundary of domestication; there are only varying degrees of truce. 

The mountain weasels, for instance, are not pets. They are longer than lowland weasels, pale-bellied with dense, oily pelts of smoky brown, their ears small and flattened against wedge-shaped skulls. They live inside the double-woven thatch of the huts, moving in the dry space between the inner rush-liner and the outer hide. The humans tolerate their musk because the weasels are the only defense against the *pucu*—a blunt-headed, blind rodent the size of an otter that burrows through living bark and can saw through a six-inch support vine in an afternoon. A woman nursing an infant will often have a weasel curled across her collarbones, asleep in the hollow of her throat; its body temperature is higher than a human’s, a living stove that stays dry by stealing the grease of the household. When a weasel grows old and its teeth blunt, the people slit its throat with a bone sliver, dress its pelt into sleeve-cuffs, and render its body into the tallow that keeps the cold river water from cracking their own skins.

In the mid-canopy, where the bromeliad gardens hold barrels of black, stagnant rainwater fifty feet above the bridges, lives an entire fauna that never touches the rock or the wood. 

There is the *gourd-frog*, an amphibian with a belly like a bladder of cold oil, whose skin exudes an astringent mucus the Rung-folk scrape off with wooden spatulas to treat split heels and fungal sores. These frogs lay their eggs in the leaf-wells of the bromeliads; their tadpoles are carnivorous, preying on the wriggling swarms of midge-larvae that would otherwise make the settlement uninhabitable. When the downdrafts hit the canyon and the mist clears, the frogs drop into a torpor, their throats puffing rhythmically, waiting for the return of the white wet.

Below them, the guans—turkey-sized, blue-wattled, with tails like black shingle-slabs—are the great nuisance and the primary meat of the tiers. They have no fear of the inhabitants. They are heavy, clumsy fliers that run along the flumes, pecking at the discarded mollusk rinds and vegetable scrapings that drop from the kitchens. A guan will walk boldly through the low, oval door of a dwelling to steal meal from a mortar, only to be struck behind the skull with a short throwing-stick by a child who has been waiting motionless behind a hanging sheepskin for three hours. The feathers are not discarded; they are split, boiled in lye made of wood-ash, and twisted into the nettle-twine to make water-shedding capes that rattle when the wind rises.

At the lowest margins, beneath the suspension bridges where the roots trail off into the gray drop of the gorge, there is life that the Rung-folk treat with a superstitious practical caution. 

Here, in the cold downdraft where the spray turns to needle-fine sleet, cling the *hangers*—large, solitary, leaf-nosed bats with six-foot spans of leathery, translucent skin. They do not hunt midges; they drop from the roots into the empty canyon air to snatch the fast-flying swifts and parakeets out of the mist, climbing back up the braided anchor lines hand-over-hand with their wing-claws like deformed mountaineers. The people do not hunt them. They believe the hangers are the discarded breath of the mountain, and that if a hunter cuts one down from its root, the wind that held the basin clear will fail, and the canyon will fill with "the red rot"—a smothering, sulfur-choked cloud that occasionally burps up from the volcanic cracks ten leagues down the range.

The children are perhaps the most completely adapted organisms in the Rung. Small, round-bellied from a diet of dried parakeet meat, moss-paste, and cold-water snails, they run along the bamboo hurdles before they can sign their own names. They have a game they play in the spray of the twin falls: they lower themselves by braided nettle lines into the plunge-wells beneath the water-hammers, where the water is whipped into a white, thundering froth that would drown an ox in two minutes. There, in the foam-holes where the granite has been drilled smooth by centuries of gravel-spin, they reach into the crevices with bare fingers to feel for the blind crayfish. They can tell by the vibration of the rock through their knees whether the trip-hammer is about to drop, rolling out of its path with a wet, seal-like flip of their hips a split second before three hundred pounds of soaked hemlock crashes into the trough.

They do not dream of open horizons; the concept of a level plain where the eye can see further than forty paces without hitting a wall of moss or a bank of cloud is an abstraction they find slightly sickening. When a Rung youth is taken down into the foothills to trade river-salt and cured weasel pelts for high-altitude maize, they are notoriously subject to *sky-vertigo*. Deprived of the overhead shelter of the limbs and the solid granite walls that cradle their world, they crawl on hands and knees, clutching at the bunchgrass, their eyes watering in the dry, unmoistened light, begging to be put back under the dripping wood, where the air has weight, the floor breathes, and the mountain is always speaking.
