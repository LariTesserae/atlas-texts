In the shallowest rock pans, where the water lies so flat it seems like an extra layer of polish on the stone, life exists first as a property of surface tension. 

If you watch the water long enough—through several of the falling drops—what appear to be pale grains of silt reveal themselves to be alive. They are minute, flattened crustacea, no larger than the seed of a rush, their carapaces the exact amber-horn color of the pool bottoms. They have no visible limbs when viewed from above; they drift without displacing the water, steered by cilia so fine they leave no wake. They do not swim through the depth, for there is almost no depth to swim through; they graze the microscopic biofilm that coats the submerged limestone, licking away the thin grease of bacteria and iron that the sweating rock produces. When a drop falls and the sharp *clink* shivers the water, they do not scatter. They flatten themselves against the stone, clamping down like microscopic limpets until the ring of the ripple passes over them, and then resume their slow, unhurried drift.

Behind them, where the northern wall sweats and feeds the pelt of liverwort, the inhabitants are slower still. In the hairline fractures behind the green film live pale, blind snails. Their shells are not coiled into cones, but are flat, planorbic spirals, wafer-thin, translucent as fingernail parings. They move perhaps an inch in a cycle of days, rasping the damp underside of the liverwort with radulae so small they leave only microscopic tracks in the algae. When the moisture recedes by even a fraction of a millimeter, they do not retreat into deep crevices; they simply secrete a clear, calcareous ring, cement their shells to the rock face, and sleep. They can remain that way for years, indistinguishable from small, round flakes of peeling lime, waiting for the humidity to rise again to the exact degree that softens their glue.

Higher up, in the dark joints where the bluff’s overhang cuts off the sky, there are things that do not need water at all. In the dry powder of the bedding planes, perfectly sheltered from drafts, are the harvestmen. They are not the spindly, skittering creatures of the woods; their legs are short, thick, and banded in chalk-grey and rust. They do not build webs. They sit motionless in the niches, legs folded over their bodies like closed umbrellas, their grey bodies pressed into the soft limestone flour. Their metabolisms are reduced to an imperceptible simmer. A single harvestman will occupy the same hollowed scallop of stone from one season to the next, never shifting its weight, feeding only when some wandering midge, lost from the upper world and exhausted by the dead air, falls into the depression and ceases to struggle.

Near the center, however, the life of the hollow gathers into something heavier, something that partakes directly of the scale of the revetment.

Beneath the broad slab propped by the two cobbles, where the earth is damp and cool and smells of red clay, there is a hollowed depression. It was not dug by claws; it was formed by long, repeated pressure, the way a boulder settles into silt over centuries.

Living there, tucked into the shadow of the ledge, is a creature that belongs entirely to the mineral logic of the place. 

It is low-slung, broad, and oblong, perhaps four feet in length, its back a solid, rounded ridge like an overturned boat or one of the convex flagstones that pave the floor. At first glance, it appears to be carved from the buff limestone itself: its skin is thick, dry, and granular, mapped with a network of fine fissures like dry river mud, and frosted along the spine with a pale crust of calcium carbonate that has accumulated there from resting beneath the sweating rock. It has no fur, no scales, and no feathers. Its hide is the hide of an elephant or a giant tortoise, but softer, like leather that has been steeped in lime-water until its oils are gone.

Its limbs are short, pillar-like, and tucked close beneath the margins of its body. The feet end not in sharp claws, but in broad, blunt pads of horn, worn smooth from shifting across the bare flagstones. It has no tail to speak of—merely a blunt, fleshy terminus that seals the rear of its shell-like fold of skin.

Its head is pulled back almost entirely into the deep, wrinkled hood of its neck. Two nostrils, round and dark as drill-holes, open and close with a rhythm so sluggish that minutes elapse between breaths. Above them, its eyes are small, dark beads set deep under heavy, pebble-textured brows; they are covered by milky, semi-translucent nictitating membranes that filter the shadowless amber-grey light, softening it even further. It has no need for sharp vision in a room fifty paces wide where nothing moves.

It is awake, but its wakefulness is identical to the rest of the hollow: an enduring, unblinking presence.

When the falling drop hits the stone pan with its sharp, musical note, the creature does not startle. Its throat pulses once—a slow, leathery swallow. Then, with a sound like two stones sliding across wet sand, it shifts.

It does not stand up; it merely straightens its thick legs enough to lift its belly from the cool depression in the clay. It moves forward an inch at a time, its broad pads finding the scallops in the flagstones that its body has smoothed over decades. It crawls with an ancient, ponderous equilibrium, making no more sound than the dust that settles behind it.

It approaches the revetment, reaches the low wall of unmortared stones, and pauses. The wall was built to its measure. Perhaps it was made by men who kept these creatures, or who revered them; or perhaps the hands that piled the stones were simply older versions of the same blunt, patient limbs, clearing a space to turn. 

The creature extends its neck. The skin of its throat is paler, the color of wet chalk. It lowers its heavy, blunt snout to the nearest rock pan, where the clear skiff of water sits undisturbed. 

It does not lap. It immerses its broad, lip-less mouth into the surface, its nostrils remaining just above the meniscus. The water rises slightly against the stone margins, held back only by surface tension. Slowly, with a deep, internal suction that causes its leathery flanks to pulse at wide intervals, it draws the mineral-heavy water into itself. As it drinks, the tiny amber crustacea drift past its jaws, unbothered, grazing upon the skin of its face as if it were merely another shelf of submerged rock.

When it has finished, it lifts its head. A single bead of water clings to the dry fold beneath its chin, trembles, and falls back into the pan. 

*Clink.*

The ripple spreads, touches the limestone rim, and vanishes. The creature turns slowly, its heavy flank scraping the curved revetment, and begins the long, deliberate journey back into the cool shadow beneath the slab, there to wait until the stone breathes out its warmth and the hollow settles once more into its unbroken, centuries-long afternoon.
