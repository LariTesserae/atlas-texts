Under the mats of liverwort, where the black stone is softened by a half-inch of grease and rotted grit, there is a kingdom of ribs and suckers. 

The sheet-flow is too rapid for anything with legs that lift. What moves upon the wet face creeps like wet parchment or wedges itself into the microscopic steps of the hornblende. Flat, liver-colored leeches, no longer than a thumbnail and tough as wet boot-leather, lie along the veins of the saxifrages, waiting for the birds to settle. They do not swim; they hitch themselves along on two disk-like mouths, their bodies ridged to break the surface tension of the water so the torrential draft cannot peel them into the void. Beneath them are the pale things the prions dive for: blind, six-legged scuttlers with chalk-white carapaces, cousins to the cave-crickets, but compressed until they are as thin as copper coins. They graze on the slime that coats the roots, their long, whip-like antennae perpetually snapped back flat against their flanks by the gale, tasting the spray for the alkaline tang of fresh droppings.

In the deepest sockets behind the hanging beards of moss—where the rock stays marginally drier, smelling of ammonia and iron—dwell the spiders. They are grey, the exact hue of the fog-bank below, with short, bowed limbs tipped with twin curved talons that hook directly into the grain of the hornblende. They do not spin orbs; the air moves too hard for geometry. Instead, they paste down dense, felted trip-lines across the mouth of each prion-burrow. When an adult bird, heavy with oil and blinded by the change from sunlight to slit-dark, blunders into the burrow mouth, the spiders dart out not to kill the bird, but to harvest the biting mites that swarm in its primary feathers, shearing them away with heavy, spade-shaped mouthparts before retreating into the stone’s seams.

Then there is the smell of the shelter, which cuts through the ozone and water-mist with a greasy, domestic yellow. 

The one who lives in the swallowed tooth of stone is built wide and low, as if the updraft had spent fifty winters planing away whatever was proud or upright in their frame. She wears three layers of split goat-hide, the hair turned inward, but the outer leather is not brown or white; it has been rubbed black with hot tallow and soot until it shines like the rock it leans against. It is stiff as armor until the warmth of her blood softens it, and it does not absorb water. The horizontal spray hits her back and simply sheets off, joining the run of the cliff.

Her hands are the most remarkable thing about her. The fingers are thick at the base and spatulate at the tips, the nails worn down to pale crescent ridges no thicker than birch bark from decades of feeling for blind purchase in wet joints. The skin of her palms is yellow-white and permanently furrowed like waterlogged wood, but tough enough that she can pinch out a burning twist of moss-wick without flinching. 

She is not alone, though she does not speak. In the low shadows of the shelter, where the roof is so tight a man could not sit upright without scraping his crown, sleeps an old dog—or something shaped like one, barrel-chested, short-muzzled, with the splayed, six-toed feet of an animal that has never walked on level ground. Its coat is an impenetrable, oily wool that smells of dried blood and salt. When the kites scream, it does not raise its head; its ears are thick, folded gristle, almost entirely sealed against the continuous bass rumble of the chimney. It communicates through the stone, feeling her approach by the slight, sharp shift in the hornblende’s vibration when her heels drop into the pecked cups.

Their fire is an odd, quiet thing. Wood is too rare to burn—the birch withes of the racks are hoarded like metal, brought up years ago from the timberline three thousand feet down the eastern couloir, where the world is still horizontal. Instead, she burns the oily keels of the prions after the meat has been stripped and pressed. 

She keeps a low stone hearth hollowed into the rear wall, where a natural draw-hole pulls the smoke deep into the mountain’s cracked interior. Over the small, steady flame of prion-bones and tallow, a shallow slate pan sits simmering. It holds a mash of pulverized saxifrage roots—which taste of raw iron and turnip—and the small, fatty hearts of the birds, poached in their own rendered oil. This grease is her wealth. It fills half a dozen bladder-skins hung from the slate roof, each plugged with a peg of bone and sealed with cold mutton-fat. In the thin, biting air, her lips would crack to the bone within three days without it; she smears it across her cheekbones, into her eyelids, and down the seams of her hide sleeves until she carries the same gamey, oceanic musk as the chimney itself.

Her day has no morning, only the swing of the light. When the sun clears the eastern horn of the cleft, the shadow falls away like a dropped sheet, and the vertical pasture turns white-gold. 

This is when she works the racks. She does not use the goat-hide rope to descend; that is only for hauling the willow-baskets of dried meat up when she returns from the lower ledges. To reach the drying platforms, she simply leans out out of the shelter’s threshold, her left hand knotted into the hammered copper ring, her bare feet finding the slick, greasy notches she knows better than her own teeth. 

Her balance is not heroic; it is mundane. She does not look down into the three thousand feet of sunlight, nor does she look up at the kites wheeling like iron leaves. She looks only at the five inches of rock directly in front of her mouth. Her forehead often touches the wet hornblende as she shifts her weight, her brow absorbing the chill of the weeping stone. With her right hand, she works the willow withes, turning the split birds on the thorns. The skin has gone translucent, dry and stiff as horn, while the red meat beneath has shrunk into dark, fibrous strings that will keep for three seasons in the frost-bins behind the hearth.

Sometimes a prion hits the stone alive. The updraft is an engine of freakish power; occasionally, a sudden gust from the valley will twist a bird's scissor-wing mid-snap, snapping the delicate hollow humerus with a dry *pop* that is lost in the roar. The bird will tumble, fluttering wildly like a torn leaf, until it is plastered against the weeping mats by the sheer force of the wind. 

When this happens near the racks, she does not rush. She waits until her current hitch is secure, then reaches out with a willow rod tipped with a curved iron barb. She does not kill them out of cruelty or pity; she simply pins the fluttering slate-colored thing against the liverwort, reaches down the shaft with two fingers, and snaps the neck with a dry, practiced twist of the thumb. She slips the body into the deep belly-pocket of her hide coat while it is still hot, using the small warmth of it against her ribs as she turns back to the rack.

In the late afternoon, the cloud-tier rises. 

It does not come up slowly. The air in the cleft darkens from gold to blue-grey in the space of fifty heartbeats. The roar of the chimney deepens by half an octave as the heavier, wetter air from the spruce-valleys below plugs the narrow throat. The wind begins to spit sleet—pellets like coarse sand that sting the eyes and rattle against the dry-stacked slate of her hut.

She retreats into the horizontal tooth. She pulls a slab of schist across the narrow opening, leaving only a palm-width gap at the base for the draft. Inside, the roar becomes a thick, muffled drumming that lives inside the marrow of the jaw. She sits with her heels tucked up under her buttocks, her back pressed against the living stone of the chimney’s core, which holds a faint, sluggish geothermal heat from some ancient fire buried miles beneath the root of the range.

The dog lays its heavy chin across her arches, pinning her feet to the stone. By the light of a single tallow-pot, she takes a splinter of prion-bone and a scrap of hard gritstone and begins to scrape. She is making needles—thin, slightly curved, with the eye bored through by patient twisting with a copper awl. 

Outside, five feet away, the kites have gone to their roosts in the flutings, tucked into their own feathers like fists. The needle-tailed prions are screaming their night-alarms, their thousands of voices blended by the wind into a high, tearing whistle that sounds like steam escaping an iron boiler. The hornblende runs with water that is already starting to form needles of black ice at the lip of the abyss. But inside the seam, under the weight of three hides and the grease of two thousand birds, it is fifteen paces wide, warm as a belly, and utterly motionless.
