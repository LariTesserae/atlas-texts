The white shells do not have eyes, but they have ears in their knees—small, recessed pits of taut, wet parchment that register the frequency of fracturing basalt long before the human ears above can separate it from the roar. When a slab begins its shear a thousand feet up, the beasts do not look up; they squat. Their many yellowed foot-pads, thick as saddle leather and cupped to form vacuum seals against the greasy biofilm, clamp hard to the stone. Those caught beneath the strike simply burst. The shell is thick—an inch or more of layered, chalky aragonite interleaved with an oily chitin that never quite hardens—but it has no arch strength against a flat drop. It splinters like parched slate. 

Their flesh inside is gray, cold, and smells heavily of wood vinegar. It does not bleed red; their circulatory fluid is a thin, zinc-tinted whey that pools in the cracks of the rock and foams as the acid damp hits it. This whey is what draws the *skimmers*, the long-legged things that stand eight feet tall on three or four jointed, needle-tipped stilts. 

These skimmers are not hunters in any clean sense; they are balance-artists. Their bodies are no bigger than a mastiff’s, but compressed laterally into a keel so thin they seem to vanish when they turn head-on. Their legs are nearly pure bone, pitted like volcanic pumice, ending in hardened, iron-stained spurs that they drive directly into the gaps between the crawling shells. They walk *over* the herd, stepping into the violent, jostling seams where the swine-things grind against one another, their long, feather-fringed balance-whips twitching constantly in the rising heat. When a rockfall pulps a cluster of shells, the skimmers do not plunge into the meat. They wait on their stilts at the perimeter of the crush, swaying with the seismic shudder, and drop long, segmented, prehensile probosces—fleshy tubes lined with inward-facing horn teeth—down between the thrashing limbs of the survivors to siphon the warm, curdled mash of broken organs before the mass closes over it.

Underneath the great crush, directly against the bedrock in the dark where the sun-slabs never reach, lives the true baseline of the rift’s meat: the *casing-worms*. They are white, flat, and rubbery, some forty feet long, moving not by muscle contraction but by thousands of microscopic cilia that row through the layer of crushed biofilm and liquid dung left in the wake of the swine-herds. They have no skeleton, no jaws, and no head; they are essentially gut-tubes turned inside out, absorbing the greasy wash through their skin. Where a swine dies without being crushed—choked on stone-flour or spent from breeding—the casing-worms find the vent-seams of the carapace from below. They slide inside while the shell is still warm, filling the cavity until the dead beast appears to swell, then liquefying the interior with enzymes that smell of turnip rot until the shell is nothing more than an empty, ivory box resting on the slate.

Human life here is lived entirely in the intermediate tension between the rope and the stone. 

The people who work the Hangs are physically distinct from any surface stock, though they were likely driven down the rifts only three or four generations ago. The constant sulfur mist and the quartz-dust have destroyed their tear ducts; their eyes are permanently dry, protected by a second, thickened lid that they blink horizontally—a thick, milky conjunctiva that turns the white glare of the noon shafts into a bearable, yellow murk. They have no body hair; the acidic steam sloughs it away in childhood, leaving their skin pale, slick, and permanently scarred with the gray "scab-tallow" they brew from crawler-fat and smeared over their bellies to keep the sulfur from ulcerating their flesh.

They do not speak with the throat. The noise of the rift—the continuous, hollow *clack-whir* of the shells and the thrum of falling rock—is too vast and low-pitched for the human larynx to reliably pierce. Instead, they communicate through the rigging. Their platforms are connected by "twitch-cords," twisted lengths of dried intestinal wall pulled so taut that a flick of an elbow or a strike with a bone-knocker carries five hundred yards along the cliff face. A three-beat slap means a rock-cleave is imminent higher up; a rolling vibration means the herd below is "heaping"—a fatal condition where the detritivores, driven by some sudden pocket of warm gas or rich mineral slick, begin to climb one another until the writhing pile reaches forty feet high, threatening to swamp the suspended camps.

A platform is never still. It is a basket of crawler-ribs, bound with green sinew that groans and tightens as the humidity swings, floor-boarded with slabs of split shell that have been scraped clean of grease. At night—or what passes for night, when the planet’s rim rolls away from the rift and the glare turns to a deep, liver-red twilight lit only by the sulfur-glow from the deeper vents—the families sleep strapped to these bones. A mother will lace her infant into the hollow of an inverted dorsal plate, hung from three separate copper pegs driven into the basalt. If one peg blows out from a seismic twitch, the child swings; if two blow, it hangs; only the failure of the stone itself brings the drop.

Their water is their greatest labor. The moisture on the walls is toxic, laden with copper vitriol and dissolved iron that blackens the gums and drops the teeth in months. They must catch the breath of the stone rather than its sweat. They rig "cool-hoods"—vast, triangular sails made of the flyers’ thin, veined wing-skin, stretched on flexible ribs of split horn. These hoods are hauled up into the cooler air-currents near the lip of the trench by counterweights made of stone-filled carapaces. When the warm, greasy steam rising from the bottom hits the cold vellum of the hood, it condenses into tiny, potable beads that run down the sewn veins into bladder-bags swinging below. It tastes of wet feathers and tin, but it does not rot the belly.

The flyers themselves are the only things that ever leave the rift, though they never go far. They are not birds; their flight has the heavy, rowing, frantic quality of flying fish or lungless water-beetles. They have four wings, each consisting of a single, leathery skin-fold held taut by three elongated, hollow fingers that click like castanets as they bank. They live on the upper ledges, where the stone is somewhat drier, roosting in their thousands like gray, leathery leaves hung in rows. 

Their relationship with the platform-dwellers is one of vicious, mutual theft. The flyers will drop from the glare to snatch children from the baskets if the bone-screens are left unlatched, their three-toed feet tipped with curved, black glass claws that can sever a femoral artery with a twitch. In return, the humans hunt them with *spring-gits*—short staves of native copper bent under tension that release a cluster of barbed horn needles when a trip-cord is brushed. 

A killed flyer is entirely used. The wing-membrane is the only material light and oil-resistant enough to make water-hoods and steam-curtains; their hollow bones are snapped into lengths to make needles, fish-tail scrapers, and the small, rhythmic clappers the hunters use to mimic the death-shudder of a crushed shell.

Down on the floor, the butchers—the *tallow-men*—are the shortest-lived of all. They are young men, mostly, whose limbs are still fast enough to dance the "crush." They do not wear ropes; ropes tangle in the mass and drag a man down faster than his legs can carry him. They wear only split-shell greaves bound to their shins with wet gut, and their chests are bare, caked in crawler-bile to hide the salt smell of their sweat, which the flyers can detect from three hundred feet above.

A butcher’s entire life is measured in the space between tremors. When the cliff shakes and the white mass folds into the fresh kill-zone, the tallow-man drops from an overhanging ladder, landing on the back of an uninjured crawler. The animal does not feel him; its shell is devoid of sensation except at the extreme margins, and it is intent only on the iron-slime. The tallow-man leaps from shell to shell, his greaves clattering against the ivory, until he reaches the pulped center. 

He has perhaps three minutes before the peripheral herd, shoved by forty thousand tons of blind, mindless appetite behind it, compresses the site into a solid, grinding mortar. He uses a crescent-bladed adze made of copper-headed basalt, hacking off the soft, muscular basal stalks of the crawler's six anterior arms—the "sweet-root," the only part of the detritivore that is not saturated with urea. He tosses the pale, twitching ropes of muscle into a net slung behind his shoulders. 

When the herd begins to rise around his knees—the white plates sliding over one another like ice floes in a spring thaw—he does not run for the cliff. He cannot; the walls are sheer and greased with biofilm. Instead, he looks for a *herder*.

He drives his adze-hook into the fibrous joint of an eight-foot skimmer-stilt, five feet up from the crush. The skimmer, startled by the drag, lifts that leg high, snapping it up out of the ruck to clear the annoyance. The tallow-man rides the leg upward, swinging his body like a pendulum, until his free hand catches the hair-fringed belly-rail of the beast. For sixty seconds, eighty seconds, he hangs there in the sulfur steam, dangling over the white, gnashing mill of plates below, while the skimmer staggers and balances on its remaining needles, screeching a dry, lungless hiss into the sun-shafts. 

Then the rope comes down from the platform. It does not drop to him; he must catch the leaden ball swinging at its tip with his teeth or the crook of his elbow as it sweeps past through the haze. If he misses, the skimmer will eventually tire of his weight, drive its own needle-foot through his thigh to scrape him loose, and drop him back into the cream-colored churn, which has already forgotten the stone that fell, the meat it took, and the men who live upon the edge of its teeth.
