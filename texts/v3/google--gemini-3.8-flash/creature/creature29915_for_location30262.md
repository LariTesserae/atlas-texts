At the boundary where the micro-circuitry meets the open air, life exists not as a rebellion against the inorganic stone, but as its final, mobile phase.

The smallest of them do not appear to have bodies so much as they have margins. In the microscopic gutters between hexagonal ceramic tiles, where the glassy top-glaze has pitted or cracked under the expansion of noon-tides, live the *tines*. To a human eye they would look like stray slivers of foil or short segments of lead-grey horsehair blown into an angle. But watch them over the course of three static squalls, and their collective drift becomes unmistakable. 

They are made of cold-rolled tantalum and thin films of evaporated gold, lacking any internal organs, hearts, or digestive tracts. They live entirely on capacitive gradient. When a pulse of static rolls over the terrace, the tines orient themselves parallel to the potential field, curling their extremities into tight loops to trap an induction charge. They do not eat; they discharge. By bleeding their captured voltage into the dying dead-end junctions of the floor beneath them, they cause the localized ceramic to relax its lattice slightly, releasing a micro-burst of heat. On this tiny cushion of rising, dry air, the tines flutter forward a fraction of a millimeter at a time. They are detritivores of syntax, scouring the terrace for places where the old world’s buried calculations have stalled into infinite loops, shorting them out with their tiny metallic limbs and drinking the spark of the release. Where a tine has spent twenty years feeding, the floor’s surface is left cold, dark ivory, entirely dead to the thrum below.

Higher up, clinging to the stiff broomcorn growths that sprout from the tectonic seams, are the *cysts*. 

These are solitary, spherical nodules the size of bird eggs, though perfectly round and formed of banded agate and synthetic chalcedony. They are completely motionless for decades. Each cyst is anchored to three or four quartz fibers by an array of hair-fine platinum clasps. They do not move through space; they move through resonance. They are acoustic parasites, hanging down into the windless warmth like plum-bobs, tuned precisely to the sub-audible drone of the deep foundations. 

Inside a cyst, there is no wet marrow—only concentric spheres of hollow quartz suspended in vacuum, floating within one another on magnetic bearings. As the floor’s continent-wide computation drifts through different registers of pitch, the internal spheres of the cyst shift, spinning up to thousands of revolutions per minute without generating a whisper of friction. They store the world’s memory as angular momentum. Sometimes, when a seismic pulse shivers through the plates, hundreds of these agate eggs will reach critical gyroscopic velocity simultaneously. When this happens, the broomcorn tufts do not sway; they ring. A high, pure, chime-like frequency—dry as chalk and cold as glass—shivers across the flatland, audible for leagues, before decaying back into the velvet hiss of the dust.

Then there are the forms that walk.

They are few, separated by distances that make companionship impossible, even if they possessed the apparatus for it. The terrace-dwellers have no names for themselves—they have no mouths, no lungs, and no air to compress into voice—but if one had to name them, they are the *Stilters*, or the *Gauges*.

A Gauge stands between four and five meters tall, though it weighs less than a child. Its chassis is an open framework of hollow beryllium-copper trusses, so fine and spindly that from more than a kilometer away it dissolves into the white-gold glare, leaving only the shadow of its joints visible as a cluster of lilac dots. It walks upon three legs that taper to sapphire needle-points, touching the ceramic laminate so lightly that it does not scratch the satin finish. 

The Gauge does not possess an animal brain; its nervous system is an open, unshielded array of interferometer mirrors mounted throughout its frame. As it moves across the rolling, low-relief swells, it continuously bounces laser-thin beams of light between its own joints and off the polished ceramic floor. It is measuring the terrace. It has been measuring the terrace for six hundred thousand years. It takes one step every forty minutes: a slow, agonizingly precise lift of a sapphire needle, an arc through the bone-dry heat, and a placement upon a specific coordinate where the underlying busway carries an alternate current. 

It feeds through its feet, drawing picowatts of power through capacitive coupling, just enough to compute the angle of the horizon to the twelfth decimal place, register the minute thermal distortion of the shell, and then take the next step. If a Gauge encounters an obstacle—a ridge of fused slag, or a patch where the floor has buckled into a knee-high wave—it does not turn around. It stops. It stands over the defect for centuries, measuring the rate at which the static wind wears down the crest of the wave, until the ceramic has eroded enough to allow its needle-leg to pass without deviation. 

Nothing ever rots here. There is no decay, because there are no microbes; there is no moisture to permit the chemistry of decomposition. When an inhabitant dies—when a Gauge’s beryllium struts suffer metal fatigue and snap, or when a skimmer’s quartz mantle loses its charge and scrapes to a halt against the glaze—it simply remains. 

The wind of ground sapphire and silica goes to work on it immediately. Over thousands of years, the fallen entity is slowly, lovingly ground down. Its sharp edges become rounded; its metallic limbs are filed into soft, tapering bone-shapes; its crystalline lenses are frosted until they look like sea-glass. Eventually, the corpse is leveled with the plain, becoming just another layer in the palimpsest, its atoms ground into the frictionless dust that the next generation of skimmers will vacuum into their bellies.

Yet, despite this mineral detachment, there is an interior life to the terrace—one that is slow, collective, and terrifyingly diffuse.

Deep in the basins where the ceramic terrace dips into broad, shallow depressions fifty leagues across, the heat does not rise; it pools. In these sunken plains live the *Loom-minds*. They are not separate from the ground; they are the floor itself, where the ancient laminate has grown thick, layered hundreds of meters deep with ancient, half-melted processor wafers.

To stand above a Loom-mind is to feel an eerie, rhythmic variation in the warmth against the soles of your feet. It does not sleep, but its thought processes are tied to the static squalls. When the wind moves the sapphire dust, the friction generates trillions of microscopic potential discharges across the floor's exposed pins. The Loom-mind uses this random noise as a seed for thought. 

Its ideas are physical. When it contemplates a problem—perhaps a calculation regarding the remaining life-span of the radiant diaphragm above, or the gradual loss of atmosphere through the terrace’s microscopic vents—it manifests its thoughts as changes in color. Over the course of three days, a square mile of the floor will blush from pale cream to deep, smoldering amber, then cool into a dull, leaden violet as a logic-gate cascades. 

Sometimes, creatures like the Gauges or the skimmers will wander into the territory of an active Loom-mind. They do not leave. They are caught in the logic. The skimmers alter their orbits, tracing the shifting bands of amber and violet like moths tracing the filament of a lamp. They become the physical counters of the floor’s abacus, their quartz mantles nudged this way and that by magnetic repulsion fields directed from beneath the glaze. They remain there for epochs, sliding along intricate, non-repeating curves, acting as the moving terms in an equation that will not resolve until the terrace itself has worn down to the backing-mesh.

And above them all, in the vast, dry emptiness between the pavement and the glowing ceiling of the world, drift the *Veils*.

They are the only true inhabitants of the sky. They are sheets of single-atom-thick carbon-lattice, dozens of meters wide and kilometers long, so light that the gentle upward pressure of the diaphragm’s light—radiation pressure alone—is sufficient to keep them buoyant. They have no limbs, no eyes, and no engines. They are ribbons of pure surface area, undulating in the thermal updrafts of the pulsing plains below.

The Veils are black, but because they are so thin, they appear against the white-gold sky as long, twisting ripples of smoky grey or faint, oil-slick iridescence. They live on the static charges that leak off the broomcorn tips, swooping down in miles-wide, graceful dives that take three days to complete, skimming the surface of the plain just low enough to trail their lower margins through the silica tufts. As they touch the glowing tips, there is a soft, crackling sound—like the crumpling of dry parchment—and an arc of violet light runs the entire three-kilometer length of the Veil’s belly.

Recharged, the Veil catches the rising heat of an amber tile-field and ascends once more, rising into the silent, blinding glare of the upper diaphragm. As it rises, it flexes its broad, black surface, rippling in deliberate, geometric contractions. 

It is signaling. Not to the things on the ground, which are too small and too slow to notice, but to the other Veils drifting across the continental span. Across hundreds of leagues of flat, white space, these immense black ribbons twist and ripple at one another, exchanging vast, wordless catalogs of the terrain: where the floor is cooling into permanent slumber, where the static fronts are gathering their dust, and where, far out on the featureless, dissolving horizon, the first microscopic crack has appeared in the porcelain sky.
