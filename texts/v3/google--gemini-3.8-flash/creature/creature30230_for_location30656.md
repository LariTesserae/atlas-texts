To watch them for any length of time is to realize that the individual does not truly end where its skin meets the air. Because the seam is so narrow and the atmosphere so thick with atomized tallow and boiling spray, their respiration is nearly shared: each creature draws in the vapor exhaled by the three pressed against its flanks, filtering the scalding steam through a paired set of ventral slits that pulse like puckered gills beneath their collarbones.

They have no teeth. Their mouths are small, circular rings of hard, fibrous cartilage, like the cut end of a tendon, wet and permanently parted. They do not chew the fat they harvest. Instead, they press these rings directly to the freshly flayed ribbons of their kin, working their gullets in a steady, peristaltic pump until the white grease liquefies under their high body heat and slides down their throats. When they gorge, their heat-pits flare from a dull, bruised violet to a raw, luminous pink, the blood beneath the translucent tissue surging so violently that a faint, thrumming hum can be felt through the dacite walls—a collective vibration that registers not as sound, but as an itch in the soles of the feet.

Beneath the working tiers, where the fissure narrows to the thickness of a thumb before widening into impossible depths, lies the brood-sludge. 

Reproduction here is not seasonal, nor does it involve pairing. It is an ambient, continuous extrusion. Every adult, regardless of its position in the mass, carries along its lower belly an open furrow of soft, unkeratinized flesh. As they scramble over one another in their work, this furrow sloughs off dense, walnut-sized nodules of yellow yolk wrapped in a membrane as tough and clear as oiled parchment. 

These eggs do not fall downward; the strange, sideways drift of gravity in the deeper angles draws them horizontally toward the back wall. There, in the grooves chiseled out by the glass-borers, the nodules collect in quivering, gelatinous dams. The boiling mineral brine, rich in dissolved silica and iron, washes over them continuously. Within forty minutes of being dropped, dark, thumb-sized forms can be seen thrashing inside the envelopes, using their nascent, glassy nails to shred the parchment from the inside. They emerge hungry, fully limb-jointed, and slick with an amniotic grease that smells sharply of scorched vinegar. They immediately begin scraping the shell-fragments of their own casings, testing their edges against the black rock, joining the climb upward before their thermal pits have fully cleared of embryotic film.

Yet they are not the only things sustained by this pressure cooker.

Between the writhing bodies, clinging to the razor margins of the glass where even the liver-skinned things cannot find purchase, lives an intermediate form—neither tool nor vermin, but an outgrowth of the slaughter itself. These are pale, hair-like nematodes, fine as split thread and three or four inches long, that drink the atomized blood hanging in the heavy air. They do not swim through water; they swim through the steam itself, their micro-fissured bodies light enough to be buoyed by the rising convection. They drift like drifting horsehair between the tiers, settling in dense, shuddering fringes upon the scrapers’ ears and the joints of their limbs. The scrapers do not brush them off. In fact, as the worms engorge themselves on the hot grease that coats the creatures' hides, they harden, their outer cuticles mineralizing from the high silica content of the steam until they snap off like brittle glass needles, dropping into the cracks to supply the working adults with fresh, splinter-sharp points for their drilling.

Further back, where the cleft defies its own measurements, the inhabitants change.

Those who work the impossible shelves—the tiers that stretch back yards beyond the mountain's outer stone—are noticeably different from the stocky, frantic scrapers near the light. Their limbs are longer, their joints double-hinged, their skin stretched so thin over their ribcages that the dark, knot-like churning of their intestines is visible through their flanks. They do not chip at the rock; they hold it. 

These deep-dwellers wedge themselves into the corners where space slips out of register. They press their flat, spade-like skulls into the junctions where two surfaces meet at an angle that should not exist, their bodies acting as living gaskets against the mountain’s folded interior. The steam that issues from around their pinned forms is different from the vent-brine near the mouth: it is colder, dry, and smells of ancient dust and iron fillings. When one of these anchor-things dies, it does not collapse. It remains locked in the seam, its tallow drying not into grease, but into a tough, amber-colored resin that seals the geometry shut, keeping the pocket from collapsing in on itself.

And at the very mouth, where the seam meets the freezing blue sky, is the waste-edge.

Here, the dynamic is reversed. The warmth fails abruptly, within the span of two centimeters. The creatures that are crowded to this outer perimeter by the sheer volume of new births do not fight to return to the center; the mechanical drive within them does not possess a mechanism for retreat. They continue to scrape at the boundary rock until the cold takes their fingers. 

The transition is instantaneous: a hand thrust an inch too far into the alpine draft freezes solid, the moisture on the skin vitrifying into a white frost that shatters the brittle nails. The creature pulls back, uncomprehending, its heat-pits blind in the cold air, and begins to peel the frozen skin from its own wrist with its good hand, feeding the crystallized strips back into the humid dark of the cleft for those behind it to consume. 

Eventually, pushed outward by the surge of bodies from the breeding shelves, the spent workers are nudged into the mountain's wind. They do not drop down the cliffs as soft meat. Before they have fallen ten feet through the thin, sub-zero air, the scalding moisture that saturated their tissues flashes into frost, freezing them into dense, black-red stones. They strike the scree slopes far below with the sharp, ring-like clatter of fired brick, rolling down into the snowfields—dry, hollow, and light, already stripped of everything they were made to hold.
