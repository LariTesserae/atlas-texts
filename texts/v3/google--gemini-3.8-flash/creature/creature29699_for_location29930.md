In a place where water sweats out of the sky and the ground eats itself in days, the things that live cannot afford the slow, wandering evolutionary routes of the lowlands. They are either refugees that have been re-tooled by the pressure, or the lingering components of whatever mechanism dropped the pale spar into the stone.

The silica-backed crawlers—the chitons—are more than grazers; they are masonry tools with guts. Under their overlapping glass plates, which are thick enough to turn a dropped chisel and clear as quartz, beats no red blood, but a cold, pale emulsion rich in silicates. They do not have mouths in the usual sense, but wide, rasping pads of synthetic diamond grit that scour the pink slime down to the basalt’s grain. As they feed, they plane the rock flat, polishing the shear faces until they reflect the flat sky like mercury. The trails of salt they leave are not waste, but engineering: the salt crusts instantly in the wet heat, forming tiny, millimeter-high levees that divert the trickling condensate away from their breathing-vents and direct it into the deep clefts where the ferns drink. If you lift one from the stone—which requires prying with a knife, so fierce is their hydraulic seal—the underside is a single, ribbed tongue of gray muscle, completely dry, smelling of solder and vinegar.

In the steaming cisterns, where the water collects at near-boiling temperatures, life takes on a liquidity so absolute it is difficult to see where the puddle ends and the creature begins. 

These are the *curtain-leeches*, though they are not vermin. They look like sheets of wet, gray greasepaper, two feet across and no thicker than a fingernail, lying perfectly flat on the cistern floors beneath six inches of scald. They do not swim; they ripple with the convection of the hot water. When the plum-colored fronds above rot and slough off their daily load of black, porridge-thick pulp, it sinks into the cisterns. The paper-beasts rise to meet it, wrapping around the descending muck like hot wrappers, dissolving it with enzymes so aggressive that the water briefly froths with carbonic acid. Within twenty minutes, the rot is gone, the sheets settle back to the basalt floor, and the water is left unnaturally clear again, stripped of everything except dissolved lime and the faint, sweet grease of their skin.

Higher up, where the invisible edge of the field shears against the seventy-mile-an-hour gales of the upper troposphere, the gas-bladders live out a violent, rhythmic life. 

They are the size of bowling balls, semi-translucent, their skins made of an oily parchment that turns opalescent when stretched tight. They are neither flora nor fauna, but floating metabolic valves. Generated in the moist rot of the fern-bases as thumbnail-sized blisters of swamp gas, they detach when mature and bob lazily upward through the tallow-thick heat until they bump against the shear-boundary. There, where thirty-degree steam hits minus-forty-degree gale in a margin no wider than a thumb, the gas within them contracts violently. The cold freezes their outer membranes to brittle parchment, and they plummet back into the warmth of the notch. 

In the heat below, the gas expands again with an audible *pop*, like a wet leather glove slapped against stone, and they rise anew. They spend their three-day lives in this perpetual, bouncing loop: down into the scald to absorb moisture through their skin, up into the freeze to crack it off as frost, dropping high-altitude ice pellets down into the cisterns like hail. When their skins finally fail from thermal fatigue, they burst in mid-air with a sharp, flat crack, scattering a fine mist of lime-scented spores across the obsidian courtyard before their husks are consumed by the crawlers before they even finish cooling.

Between these fast-burning things, however, something much older and slower keeps house.

In the chimney behind the black pylon, suspended in the dry void where the sheeting water curves out into mid-air, lives an organism that has no counterpart on the world below. It clings to the basalt with four jointed limbs made of the same soapy, lukewarm jade that formed where the pale rib fused with the mountain. 

It has the bulk of a starved hound, but its torso is flattened, shaped like an asymmetrical wedge to fit the chimney’s draft. Its skin is not hide, but a dense, velvet pelt of hair-fine glass needles that shiver constantly in the pylon’s hum. It has no eyes, no nostrils; its head is a smooth, blunt shovel of ivory bone, split down the center by a vertical seam that opens only when the pylon’s field shifts during atmospheric storms.

It does not feed on the ferns or the crawlers. Instead, it lives entirely off the gradient. It keeps its lower limbs anchored in the wet, ozone-rich moss at the pylon’s base, while its upper limbs reach into the dry, vibrating vacuum of the diverted water curtain. It is an animal battery, charging itself on the difference in potential between the artifact’s boundary and the mountain’s wet rock. 

For weeks it does not move, looking like nothing more than an irregular spur of mineral or a discarded mold. But when the great gales outside drive a wedge of true sub-zero air deep into the notch, threatening to collapse the bubble, the thing acts. It drops from the chimney with a brittle, dry clatter, scurrying on its jade-tipped legs across the wet glass rock toward the central white rib. 

Its movements are terrifyingly swift, entirely silent, and utterly devoid of hesitation. Arriving at the spar, it presses its needle-covered chest against the pale, non-metallic curve, locking its joints with a dull *thunk*. For an hour, its glass fur glows with a dull, sour orange phosphorescence, discharging the static it has skimmed from the pylon directly into the rib's root. The mirage around the bone violent swells; the air pressure spikes until the ears of any creature would pop; the frost creeping in from the lip of the ridge hisses into gray steam and retreats. 

Its task done, the creature detaches itself, leaving behind a faint smudge of scorched oil on the white material, and limps back to the chimney on joints that have grown stiff and white with salt, ready to sit another three months without breathing.

And below all of them, inside the rock itself, something else stirs that is not quite life yet. In the fractures where the milky sap of the vines leaks into the microscopic pores of the obsidian, the rock is growing soft. It does not crumble; it yields like cold cheese under the fingernail. If you were to peel back a layer of this softened basalt, you would find that the dark stone is being replaced, grain by grain, by a lattice of microscopic hollow tubes that hum at the same low frequency as the pylon. The mountain is being hollowed out from the inside, converted into an organ of stone and tallow-air, waiting for the terrace to grow wide enough to hold whatever is still buried under the shale.
