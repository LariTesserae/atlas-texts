Under the green light and the constant, mechanical roar, the floor of the basin belongs to the stone-skinned runners. 

They are small—scarcely longer than a man’s forearm from blunt snout to vestigial, paddle-shaped tail—and their weight is surprising when they move, heavy and low-slung, as though their bellies were ballasted with lead shot. Their skin has no fur, no scale, no grain; it is an uninterrupted sleeve of charcoal-colored epidermis, slick with a clear, odorless mucus that protects them from the alkaline dew dripping from the fern tips. Where an animal’s ears should be, there are only tight, muscular folds—slits lined with rows of microscopic, vibrating hairs that feel the clatter of the cicadas rather than hear it. 

To watch them hunt is to watch an equation being solved by meat. Because the cicadas form a vibrating acoustic ceiling that bounces off the curved quartzite walls of the basin, the runners do not look for their prey. They track the sound-shadows. When a flyer or a sudden downdraft disturbs a cluster of cicadas on a pylon, the rattle changes pitch by a quarter-tone; instantly, three or four runners will wheel on their short, splayed legs and strike toward the silence, their bifurcated tongues whipping out like whips of wet gristle to snatch the insects straight from the curdled stone. 

When the twenty-minute pulse arrives from the deep core, their world is annihilated for two seconds. 

In that sudden, yawning silence, the runners do not keep moving. They cannot. Without the acoustic geometry provided by the millions of vibrating carapaces, their nervous systems stall. They drop flat to the wet lime moss, their slick chins pressed hard into the basalt loam, limbs locked. For two seconds they are merely smooth, river-worn cobbles scattered across the turf. Only when the first erratic *clack* sounds from the jade stone do their ribs heave again, taking in a sharp, shuddering breath before they resume their manic scuttle.

Their nests are not burrows, for the basalt under the moss is too hard to dig. Instead, they breed inside the emerald pipes of the older, thicker ferns. 

These plants—the solitary, ribbed cylinders that stand like fluted pillars across the garden—are hollow. Their tops are flared open to receive the warm condensation, which collects inside their shafts in long, stagnant columns of pale, mineralized liquor. A runner female, heavy with two or three soft-shelled, spherical eggs, will climb the exterior ridges of a fern using clawed, opposable dewclaws, hoist herself over the lip, and drop inside. She stays there for weeks, floating in the warm, alkaline broth, suspended between the ribbed walls of the living pipe, breathing through nostrils that dilate and seal like the valves of a diver’s suit. Her skin leaches nutrients from the plant’s internal tissues; the plant, in turn, drinks the nitrogenous waste she sheds into its interior cistern. When the young hatch, they are completely aquatic, finned and pale, devouring the mosquito-sized larvae of the cicadas that drop into the ferns from above, until their limbs bud and their lungs clear, and they haul themselves up the slick emerald throat into the deafening air of the basin.

Lower down, where the flags of ancient masonry have buckled into shallow gutters, the water runs slow and heavy with dissolved copper. 

The crayfish that inhabit these runnels are ghosts made of thin calcite. Their carapaces are so thin that the blue, hemocyanin-tinted pulse of their hearts is visible through their backs, beating with a sluggish, unhurried cadence that ignores the madness of the insects above. They have no eyes—not even the dark pits where eyes once rested—but their second antennae have elongated into whip-like filaments twice the length of their bodies. These antennae are perpetually sweeping the stone, not for food, but for the imperceptible micro-currents generated by the pylons’ root-structures beneath the flagstones. 

The crayfish feed on the dead: the drowned flyers, the exhausted cicadas whose life cycles end after forty-eight hours of screeching, and the molted skins that drift down like flakes of burnt copper. But they also graze on something rarer. Along the underwater seams where the jade material of the pylons plunges into the rock, an encrusting, sulfur-yellow film grows—a biofilm that is neither algae nor fungus, but an aggregate of extremophile cells that derive their energy directly from the electromagnetic leak of the machine. The crayfish scrape this film away with modified, spoon-shaped third maxillipeds. Their shells become impregnated with the metals they consume; when an old crayfish dies, its hollow husk does not decay, but remains in the pool for decades, slowly turning green with verdigris, an empty bronze monument sitting in an inch of warm water.

Above them all, cutting the saturated air with a dry, papery *whir-whir-whir*, are the flyers. 

They are the most frantic things in the depression. They are larger than the hummingbirds they mimic, their bodies thick and cylindrical, clad in segmented plates of an intense, oily indigo that shifts to bronze in the light. They have no feathers; their flight is powered by two pairs of overlapping, chitinous wings that move with such violent velocity that they create miniature wind-shears, knocking moisture beads from the ferns as they pass. 

They are addicts of the machine. The southern face of each pylon weeps a thin, viscous exude—a synthetic resin that the ancient mechanisms use to purge their internal heat-sinks of static charge. It tastes of bitter petroleum and concentrated salts. The flyers hover inches from the seamless milk-stone, their long, needle-like proboscises stabbing repeatedly into the microscopic pores of the material, lapping at the oily dew. 

The oil burns through them with terrifying efficiency. Their internal body temperatures run at forty-two degrees; if a flyer stops moving for more than a few minutes, the heat generated by its flight muscles and the chemical fire of the oil will cook its own nervous system. When they mate, they do so in midair, locked together in an erratic, tumbling dive that ends barely an inch above the moss, parting at the last possible fraction of a second before they smash into the loam. 

They do not dare sleep in the warm heart of the bowl. At night, or when their strength flags, they rise on stiff wings toward the perimeter, climbing the vertical fifty-foot cliff of ancient quartzite until they reach the boundary layer—the knife-edge where the twenty-nine-degree steam meets the whistling, sub-zero air of Mount Vraun’s plateau. 

There, in the narrow horizontal band where frost and mist fight for possession of the rock, the flyers wedge themselves into hairline fissures. They hang upside down like bats, their indigo armor coated in a crust of rime, their metabolisms plummeting instantly into a frozen, death-like torpor. They use the mountain’s arctic breath as a brake to stop their engines from consuming them. At dawn, when the thermal plume expands slightly and washes over their crevices with the scent of wet moss and warm copper, their hearts seize, flutter, and roar back to life, and they drop out of the frost into the green noise below.

There is nothing here that understands what the pylons are, yet everything here is shaped to their measurements. 

The cicadas lay their eggs in cycles that match the minor fluctuations in the pylons' static output. The runners’ claws are shaped to grip the precise circumference of the fern pipes, which themselves grow only at the intervals where the buried power lines leak warmth into the loam. 

Even the silence has been colonized. In that two-second pause every twenty minutes, when the pulse shakes the mountain’s roots and the roaring clatter dies, a microscopic thread-worm—thousands of which live on the tips of the moss frills—reaches up into the empty air. For those two seconds of stillness, and *only* those two seconds, it shoots a plume of invisible, microscopic spores into the air column. It cannot do this while the cicadas rattle, because the acoustic pressure waves are so intense within the bowl that they shred the spores’ delicate envelopes before they can disperse. The worm has no eyes, no brain, no ears, but it knows the pulse. It waits for the world to drop dead, throws its seed into the quiet, and curls back down into the lime-green wet before the mountain begins to scream again.
