Because weight in this chamber rolls toward the perimeter, whoever lives here does not sleep on the floor. 

To rest, they give themselves to the wall. 

High up on the southern curve, where the cambium has peeled back into a thick, rubbery flap like the lip of a horse, there is a hollow just deep enough to cradle a spine. It is occupied. What rests there looks at first like an older structural rib that has failed to take root, or a discarded casing of fibrous packing material, greyed by sap and matted with dry cellulose lint. But it breathes with the deep, slow rhythm of something whose pulse has been forced to match the hydraulic tempo of the root.

It is a woman, or the remainder of one, though the categories that define a person on the surface have gone slack down here, like wet twine. 

She is long-limbed and thin to the point of transparency, her skin pickled by alkaline starch into the pale, tough condition of soaked rawhide. Where the sap has repeatedly scalded her forearms and thighs, her flesh has developed a calloused, yellow-white crust that does not blister anymore; it simply sloughs off in dry, translucent wafers when the room expands, falling like broad flakes of parsnip into the gaps below. 

She has no hair. The aggressive chemical wash of the air—the constant, reeking bath of freshly sheared cell-sap and sulfurous oxygen—has dissolved it down to the follicle, leaving her scalp as smooth and greenish-white as an unpeeled turnip. Her ears are stopped permanently with plugs of rolled, mucilage-soaked bast, because the first cavitation snap of a xylem column forty yards away would split an unprotected eardrum like a dry pod. She hears entirely through her skeleton. Her cheek is pressed flat against the naked cambium, her jaw clamped lightly onto a ridge of lignified tissue; through her teeth, she registers the hum of the deep water-trains, the rising hydrostatic pressure in the lower quadrants, and the micro-fractures that precede the tearing of a new room.

She does not live *in* this room; she follows it. 

The chamber is a fleeting accident—a temporary bubble blown in the meat of the root by its own frantic metabolism. In forty hours, perhaps fifty, the ribs of new tissue will have latticed together, the walls will have swollen shut with the brutal force of hydraulic pistons, and this quiet attic will be solid, crushed vegetable flesh again, wet and lightless. 

When that happens, she will already have moved. She knows the sound of the root tearing before the tear appears. She knows the distinctive, high-register whine that runs through the xylem when the meat is about to split open under its own sap-strain. When the rupture comes, she will take down the tripod, tuck the stone into her belly-wrap, and wriggle through the shearing fibers into the fresh, scalding void while the air is still roaring with the shock of its creation.

She is dressed in what remains of linen work-clothes, but the linen has long since been colonized by the plant’s own fungal associates. The threads have been replaced, stitch by stitch, by pale mycorrhizal hyphae that have knitted her shirt directly into her skin. Where her back meets the wall, she does not sit upon the plant; she is lightly adhered to it by a sticky, sugar-rich exudate that she must peel herself away from with a wet, sucking sound whenever she moves.

To cross the floor toward the tripod, she must fight. Because the massive vegetative bulk behind the walls exerts its own lateral attraction, walking toward the center feels like wading out of a steep stone basin. She leans out over the empty space at an impossible angle, her knuckles—broad, scarred, and stained the permanent dark green of crushed dock-leaves—grazing the floor for balance.

She comes down only for the light and the slate.

The noon-light that pours through the ceiling lens is merciless, but down here it is the only pure thing. It does not heat the air—the plant’s transpiration keeps the temperature locked at the fever-pitch of an animal’s throat—but it bleaches. Her eyes, when she opens them in the geometrical pools of glare, are startlingly clear: the pupils reduced to microscopic black grains, the irises washed out to the pale, watery blue of skimmed milk. She does not look up into the lens. To look up would burn a hole through the back of her head. Instead, she sits at the margin of the shadow-gouges, where the line between noon and night is as thin as a razor, and holds her hands out into the beam until the starch on her fingers bakes into a sweet, dry crust that she licks clean with a grey tongue.

Her food is the room’s blood. She wedges small chocks of dry pith into the xylem tears, letting them soak up the high-pressure sap until they swell like fat sausages, then chews them for hours to extract the cold, sweet starch and the bitter mineral salts drawn up from the deep bedrock. It is a diet of raw vigor and no fat; her ribs show through her flanks like the staves of an empty barrel.

Her work is the stone. 

When the lateral pull allows her to reach the center, she crouches by the tripod. The wedge of river-slate is her only horizon. In this place, where the earth’s mass is contradicted by the living mountain of starch around her, the slate is the only object that remembers where the center of the world actually is. It hangs dead vertical, pointing toward the deep iron of the planet, entirely indifferent to the muscular swelling of the root.

She touches the chipped edge with the ball of her thumb. She does not alter it. She uses it to measure the lean. 

Every three hours, she takes a splinter of dried bone-wood and scratches a tiny line into the floor directly beneath the slate’s point. The floor is covered in these marks—overlapping clusters of fine, nervous scratches that track the root’s slow, staggering crawl against the bedrock. By the drift of the slate, she knows which way the monster is turning, which buried boulder it is currently shouldering aside, and how many hours remain before the wall behind her folds inward to snuff the room out.

She does not speak, because there is no air-space large enough to make a voice worth having, and the scream of the water-pipes would tear words to shreds anyway. But sometimes, when the light shifts from sulfur-yellow to salt-white as an overhead cloud passes across the unseen meadow eighty yards above, she will lay her palm against the grey slate.

It is cold. It is the only cold thing in eighty fathoms of boiling, growing green. She holds it until her palm aches with the chill of a real winter she has forgotten how to believe in, while the floor beneath her knees leaps and shudders like the skin of a horse bitten by flies, and the room goes on screaming its silent, noon-bright, impossible life into the dark.
