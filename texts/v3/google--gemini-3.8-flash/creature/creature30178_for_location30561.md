At the smallest scale, the life here is chemical and heavy.

In the conduit troughs, where water-resistant rubber cable-sheathing was laid down eighty years or eight hundred years ago, there are the borers. They are not insects in the fragile, papery sense; they are broad-backed beetles the color of wet slag, three inches long and flattened like wedges, with mandibles that can shear lead. They do not eat the copper cores—they leave behind bright, combed braids of bare wire that catch the sun like fishing lines—but they chew the black vulcanized rubber and the tarred horsehair packing until their guts are full of bitter hydrocarbons. Their frass is a dry, gray graphite dust that piles up in the cable runs, forming drifts that small, blind millipedes burrow through. These beetles are so dense that when a hawk strikes one off an exposed flange, the dropped shell hits a corrugated roof sixty feet below with the unmistakable *tink* of an iron washer.

Beside them, in the endless drip-pans beneath the steam valves, are the raspers. They are freshwater limpets the size of plum stones, but their shells are not pearl; they are layered with rust, lime, and copper carbonate, banded in rings of bile-yellow, teal, and oxidized red. They creep across the submerged plates at the rate of an inch a day, grazing the dense, hair-like mats of iron-fixing bacteria that form rust-colored carpets over the rivets. Where a limpet has passed, the steel is left scoured, silver-bright and weeping raw orange tears in the damp air within hours.

The flumes themselves—those warm, knee-deep aqueducts marching along the shelf edges—are their own riverine world.

In the weed-lines of wild ginger and trailing fern that choke the shallows, there are lung-eels. They are thick as a man’s forearm, slate-colored, slick as grease, with small, pale eyes that have filmed over from generations in the zinc-poisoned runoff. They live on the larvae of the condensation gnats and the fat, dead beetles that tumble from the overhead girders. When a flume rusts through and the water level drops into a trickling sheet, the lung-eels do not die; they haul themselves out onto the warm, pitted deck-plates by their muscular pectoral fins, dragging their bellies through the hot mulch until they find an overflow pipe or a lower siphon. In the rainy season—when high-altitude cloud-bursts wash over the upper tiers—they can be seen crossing the open promenade decks in great, glistening lines, sliding across five-acre expanses of sun-bleached primer between one gutter and the next.

Above the flumes, on the vertical faces where the plates meet in rows of swollen rivets, the pale skinks hold territory. 

They are lizards that have forgotten stone. Their bodies are wide and flattened, their scales soft and velvety to the touch, patterned in irregular, oily rings of cream and engine-varnish brown that make them vanish against stained steel. Their toes do not have simple claws; the pads are splayed and ridged, weeping a thin, sticky lymph that allows them to run upside down along the rust-pitted bellies of forty-ton crane booms without breaking stride. They live on the heat of the structure. If you lay a hand on an exposed box-beam at midday, it is almost too hot to keep your palm down; the skinks press their white underbellies flat against this metal, absorbing the dynamo-fever from below and the raw ultraviolet from above until their blood runs thin and fast. They fight constantly, snapping at each other with broad, blunt jaws, their squabbles sounding like wet leather straps slapping against timber.

The birds rule the middle air, between the catwalks and the overhanging flanges.

They are corvids of some forgotten branch, soot-black, but their plumage is never dry. Because of the constant, lukewarm mist and the oily atmosphere, their feathers are permanently slicked back, giving them the slender, serpentine look of cormorants. They build their nests in the deep angle-brackets where stanchions cross, using the only building materials available: frayed ends of hemp mooring line, dried runners of brake fern, and thin, ductile strips of electrical grounding ribbon stolen from the open junction panels. They weave these with copper wire into baskets so tough that the high winds of the outer decks cannot dislodge them.

These birds have developed a harsh, percussive vocabulary that carries over the machinery’s hum. They do not whistle; they click, clatter, and produce hollow, metallic *clongs* deep in their throats that sound uncannily like a dropped spanner hitting an empty boiler. They follow the human tracks—or what passes for human here—waiting for the black peat in the gutters to be overturned, or for the balance-pipes to vent. When a vent opens, spewing gallons of half-scalded, stunned grubs and boiler-crust into the concrete-lined traps, the birds descend in a screaming, tumbling cloud, fighting the skinks for the offal.

And then there are the walkers.

They are men and women, though their proportions have shifted toward the architecture. They are narrow-shouldered and long-limbed, with ribs that can compress to slip between standard ladder cages and through eighteen-inch access scuttles. Their skin is the color of tea, stained permanently around the knuckles, heels, and neck with the yellow-brown grease of the long-dead lubrication reservoirs.

They do not wear shoes. The soles of their feet are three-quarters of an inch thick—hard, yellow, horn-like calluses that have formed around the shape of rivet-heads and the edges of angle iron. The great toe is separated from the second by a wide, muscular cleft, capable of gripping a wet one-inch pipe with enough force to hold their body weight while their hands are busy with a spade or a basket.

They live in the "dry cells"—the hollow interiors of the great cellular box-beams that make up the backbone of each shelf. Inside these beams, the air is not steamy, but dry and warm like an oven, draft-fed by the intake chimneys below. They sleep in canvas hammocks slung between internal cross-struts, their heads resting on bundles of dried ginger leaves, their ears so accustomed to the three-cycle heartbeat of the deep dynamos that in the rare moments when a shaft seizes and the vibration dies, infants wake up screaming from the silence.

They do not mine the iron—they garden it. They understand the truce.

A family might tend six hundred yards of flume. They keep the channels clear of the heavy iron-kelp that would dam the flow and cause a deck-collapse; they fork the rotting fern-mulch out of the gutters and pack it into the troughs of the hollow crane-tracks, where they grow short, fibrous squash and sweet, pale tubers that taste of sulfur and rain. They know which pipes are "sweet"—venting clean, warm distilled water from some deep condenser—and which are "sour," spitting green water loaded with copper salts that will knot a man’s bowels until he dies.

Their tools are iron, beaten cold from old hatch-dogs and bracket-pins, heavy and simple. They carry balance-poles made of light, hollow aluminum tubing salvaged from the higher, more modern tiers, using them to walk the eighteen-inch flanges over three-thousand-foot drops with the bored, swinging gait of plow-horses on a ridge.

They do not look down often. The blue void below, where the iron pillars fade into gray distance and finally disappear into the cloud floor, is of no use to them. Their world is horizontal and stepped: two miles of sunlit iron behind them, two miles of hanging green ahead, and beneath their calloused feet, the deep, warm pulse of the engine that has kept the frost from their skin for as long as their songs remember.
