To find what lives here, one must first discard the notion of a pulse.

In an ecology where time does not circulate—where the light does not tip toward dusk, where the frost neither deepens into rime nor yields to melting, and where the water flow is an unswerving mathematical constant—the metabolic cycle has had no reason to learn the rhythms of sleep, digestion, or decay. The life that occupies this roaring chasm does not breathe because the air does not require breathing; it is already hyper-oxygenated, saturated to the threshold of liquid, and sheared into an electric aerosol by the turbines. 

Life here does not fight the current; it is a deposit of it.

***

In the narrow, wet gaps between the copper buss-bars and the slate cliffs, where the verdigris thickens into an oily, jade-colored paste, the first true inhabitants make their home. They are not insects, though they possess a jointed, segmented neatness that suggests them. 

They are **galvanic lichens**, though to call them lichens is to understate their architectural density. They grow as thin, flexible ribbon-lattices of cold, metallic cellulose, binding themselves directly to the wet metal. They do not feed on organic rot—for there is none—nor on sunlight, which has no warmth to lend. Instead, they bridge the microscopic air gaps between charged surfaces. Where a high-tension cable anchors into the basalt with an iron flange, these ribbons lay themselves down across the insulator bells, forming dense, fan-shaped mats that sizzle very faintly beneath the louder, drowning roar of the penstocks.

They live on the differential. Their tissues are rich in copper salts and pulverized magnetite torn out of the freshly cleaved slate by the flumes. When the 60-cycle vibration passes through them, their fronds do not sway; they stiffen. They have grown to match the pitch of the dynamos so precisely that their cell walls resonate in harmonic lock with the city’s heart. If the dynamos were to drop by even half a cycle, these mats would shatter like cold glass; but the dynamos do not falter, and so the mats remain flexible, translucent, and cold as wet grease, slowly thickening year by year into the shape of the structural gussets they cling to.

***

Farther out along the catwalks, suspended over the two-league drop, the empty accommodations begin to show what has settled into them.

The city was built for hands, for boots, for torsos that bend at the hip and eyes positioned five and a half feet from the floor. But because no hand ever touched the brass latches, the **saponified masses** have claimed them instead. 

In every sheltered recess—beneath the eaves of the sawtooth roofs, inside the glassed-in control pulpits where the dials all point toward their invariant maximums, and in the dry, dead-air pockets behind the hydraulic cylinders—there are things made of the machine’s own sweat. They are born from the amber emulsion of high-viscosity turbine oil and the pure, mineral-heavy condensation dripping from the basalt. 

Over what must be centuries of unchanged mid-morning light, this sludge has not stayed inert. The mechanical hum has organized it. The constant, high-frequency vibration of the iron floorings has sifted the oil droplets into colloidal lattices, which have then been colonized by the hair-fine hyphae of the cold-tolerant liverworts. 

The result is something pale, dense, and tallow-like, possessing the consistency of cold axle grease but the internal vascular architecture of an alpine fungus. They do not scuttle or stride. They do not possess faces or limbs. Yet they are clearly, unmistakably *settlers*.

One finds them slumped in the pressed-steel operator chairs. They do not mimic human form out of mockery or memory—they simply occupy the gravity well that the chair provides. A mass of this tallow-flesh, three hundred pounds of pale, grayish-amber lipid, sits where a worker was meant to sit, its bottom flattened into the perforated sheet metal, its upper bulk drooping over the armrests until its lower tendrils touch the control toggles.

It does not throw the toggles. It has no will to alter the circuit. Instead, it has grown around the Bakelite knobs, casing them in a translucent, cold rind. The copper currents beneath the floorboards provide a gentle inductive warmth—scarcely two degrees above the freezing air, but enough to keep the lipid core viscous. Inside these masses, tiny, crystalline droplets of copper sulfate circulate along microscopic capillary channels, pumped not by a heart, but by the physical thrum of the 60-cycle vibration passing through the steel frame into their bodies. They are living inductors. They dream, if they dream at all, in low-frequency phase shifts.

***

If you look down through the iron grating of the avenues, into that blue-gray desert of vertical air where the mist is atomized into pale ribbons, there is motion that does not belong to the machines.

Miles out from the cliff faces, strung between the mountain summits by high-tension guy-wires that are miles long and bowed by the wind, live the **Tensioners**.

They are the largest presences in the void, yet they are nearly invisible because they are made almost entirely of the water itself. Where the cold mountain air shears past the high-tensile steel cables, the moisture does not merely collect; it is spun by the wind and the electrostatic field into long, rope-like tubes of coherent ice and gelatinous slime—a biological mucus secreted by aerial saxifrage spores that have hybridized with the river moss.

These ropes have developed a slow, peristaltic tension. They are miles in length, no thicker than a man’s thigh, clinging to the underside of the main bridge cables like wet, translucent bellies. They live by catching the spray thrown up from the flumes thousands of feet below. 

At intervals along these ice-girdles, where the cables cross and the iron trusses form great structural knots over the gulf, the Tensioners thicken into heavy, pear-shaped nodules. These nodules hang over the abyss, perfectly still, counter-weighting the sway of the bridges. Within their clear, frozen sheaths, one can see slow, convective currents of dark green sap moving through coiled intestinal loops. They are plants that have abandoned leaves; they have no use for foliage when there is no season to prepare for and no soil to drop into. They are pure vascularity, living on the mineral dust carried by the wind and the ceaseless galvanic trickle that leaks from the suspension lines.

Sometimes, when the wind hits a cleft in the slate at a particular angle, the acoustic pitch of the chasm rises—not a change in the machine roar, but an overtone, a dry, glassine harmonic that rings through the trusses like a wet finger run around the rim of a crystal bowl. When this happens, the Tensioners along the cables tighten. You can hear them: a wet, creaking groan that travels six miles across the void in a fraction of a second, the sound of living fiber taking up the strain of the steel, stabilizing the empty city against the sheer vacuum of the drop. They are not parasites on the apparatus; they have become part of its structural calculation. The iron holds them up; they hold the iron true.

***

And what of the tenements? What lives behind the rhythmic, frosted wire-glass windows of those multi-tiered brick-and-iron blocks that face the opposing peak?

Open a door—its brass knob cold enough to leave a white patch of dead nerve on the thumb—and step across the unworn threshold into the vestibules. 

Here, away from the direct wind, the roar of the flumes is muted into a deep, hollow pressure that vibrates the teeth inside one’s jaw. The air is stagnant, but it does not smell of mold or dry dust; it smells of distilled water, ozone, and wet slate. 

The rooms are entirely colonized by the **saxifrage lanterns**.

They have grown through the floorboards from the wet foundations anchored in the basalt, their thick, fleshy root-stocks lifting the parquet tiles with hydraulic slowness. In each room, an enormous rosette of pale, jade-green leaves fills the center of the floor, five or six feet across, perfectly symmetrical, its leaves so full of pressurized water that they are rigid as sheet horn.

At the center of each rosette rises a single, unbranching spire of crystalline tissue that reaches the ceiling. It does not flower—a flower is an invitation to an insect that does not exist here. Instead, the spire terminates in a bulbous, translucent node that presses firmly against the glass globe of the ceiling fixture, where an incandescent bulb has burned without dimming since the dynamos first spun up.

The plant does not eat the light. It grounds it.

The roots of the saxifrage have found the iron conduits running through the walls; the spire has found the brass casing of the lamp. The entire organism serves as a biological ballast, drawing off the surplus amperage that would otherwise burn out the carbon filaments in the fixtures. The bulb glows, a dull, orange-yellow thread in the gray mid-morning; the plant drinks the current through its central stalk, transmuting the electrical flux into a faint, pale luminescence that travels downward through its fleshy leaves, illuminating the wet, empty tenement floor with an emerald light that never flickers.

In the corner of such a room, beneath the window that looks out upon twelve miles of blue air, there are often two or three smaller growths: little domes of wet, white liverwort that have formed precisely over the hot-water radiators. The radiators are dry—the steam systems were never filled—but the liverworts have filled them from within, their spongy, harness-leather hides bulging between the cast-iron ribs, drinking the heat that leaks through the metal pipes from the turbine halls leagues away.

They are content. Nothing has ever disturbed them. They have never known a predator, a drought, an autumn, or a footstep.

***

If you wait—though waiting has no meaning where the shadows do not shift by a pencil’s breadth—you realize the ultimate truth of what inhabits this chasm.

The apparatus itself is not dead iron waiting for an engineer. The plants, the greases, the copper-crusted lichens, and the hanging ropes of hydraulic flesh are not accidental infestations. They are the organs of the place. 

The water enters the mountain’s brow as sweat; it descends through the rock-cleavages, guided by the iron pens; it spins the bronze wheels; it throws its static into the buss-bars; it feeds the fat, silent dwellers in the control pulpits; it stiffens the cables that keep the trusses from dropping into the mist; and then it atomizes into the void, rising again as ambient light and pressurized damp to weep once more from the flint-keen basalt.

There are no voices here. There is no language. But the city is inhabited down to its last cold rivet by a single, dispersed, unblinking awareness: an organism whose bone is black slate, whose nerves are drawn copper, whose flesh is alpine moss and machine oil, and whose breath is the invariant, deafening 60-cycle scream of the sky falling through itself forever.
