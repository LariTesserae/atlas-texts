To understand what lives on the plateau, one must first discard the notion of hunting, of chasing, and largely of hunger. In an environment where the bedrock does not yield and the sky offers only a descending grease of moisture, food is not sought; it is processed in place. The creatures do not inhabit the stone so much as they have calcified upon it, like a living stratum laid down between the pink gneiss and the low, thick heaven.

Closest to the granite are the **pavement-discs**, whose underside is an intricate, moist labyrinth of creeping foot-tissue. Their shells are not lime or bone, but a dense, horn-like keratin, layered like the growth rings of boxwood, worn smooth by the mist and the passing bellies of heavier things. Each disc possesses three concentric mouth-rings on its belly, armed with rasping tongues that do not chew the rock—for the rock will not give—but shave away the thin, microscopic blooms of sulfur-eating bacteria and the slick, grease-like condensate that trickles between their rims. 

They are communal in a physiological sense: where their edges meet, their mantles exude a pale, tallow-colored paste that cures in the mist to form a flexible seal. A solitary disc will desiccate in three days if lifted from the granite, its pale edges curling inward like a fried leaf; but fused into sheets of twenty or thirty thousand, they retain an unbroken reservoir of body-water beneath themselves. In this sub-floor crawlspace, which smells faintly of cellar earth and warm suet, the **scavenger-crabs** go about their business. These detritivores are little more than sets of pale, articulated stilts supporting a soft, droplet-shaped abdomen. Their legs are translucent, five inches long and thin as broomstraws, tipped with blunt, horn-shod points that can tap across the bare stone without snagging. They have no eyes, nor any use for them; instead, their joints are fringed with sensitive, silky hairs that read the vibrations of the breathing pavement above them. When a disc dies—its mantle relaxing, its respiration ceasing with a faint, bubbling hiss—the crabs gather at the rim. They do not tear. They soften the deceased with an alkaline saliva, dissolving its flesh into a gray broth which they draw through capillary mouthparts, leaving behind only the translucent horn-round of the shell, which will eventually be crushed and smoothed into the floor by the slow weight of the herd.

Above the discs are the **mounds**, which the local biology seems to have settled upon as the ideal geometry for containing heat. These are creatures that have forgotten limbs entirely, or perhaps never had them. Their skin is the shade of boiled liver or slate soaked in lard, perpetually beaded with water that rolls into specialized, gutter-like creases along their sides, directing the moisture straight to their lower intake vents. 

They have no teeth, no claws, and no distinct head. What serves as their face is simply the leading end of the mass: a blunt, slightly raised dome containing three paired nostrils and an immense, rubbery lip that stays pressed against the disc-floor. They do not feed on the discs—the discs are too hard, and too necessary as an insulator against the damp gneiss. Instead, the mounds feed on the sky. When the warm, oily mist comes rolling across the plain in the afternoons, the herds turn into the drift with an agonized slowness that takes hours to complete. Their intake slits gape wide, revealing pale, convoluted curtains of vascular tissue—not unlike whalebone or the gills of a carp, but thick as felt and dripping with a clear, viscous mucus. The mist passes through these internal radiators, surrendering not only its water, but the heavy atmospheric load of spores, suspended fats, and airborne protein that the high, resting heat of the lower continent lifts into the clouds. The herds are atmospheric filter-feeders, living on the breath of the lower world.

Because their food arrives on the wind, they have no reason to fight. There is no jealousy in an atmosphere. Their movements are so negligible that a single mound may spend forty years in a depression no larger than a farmyard pond, rocking its two tons of weight from side to side in an endless, rhythmic prayer that works the moisture down toward its lower gutters. Their internal skeletons are not bone, but flexible cart-springs of cartilaginous horn that absorb the weight of their neighbors. When they sleep—if the long, dreamless stupor of their midday hours can be called sleep—they settle against each other with a sound like wet bags of grain being dropped onto a dock.

Yet there are hands here.

The stone circles and the polished, upright slabs are the work of the **herders**, though that word implies a mastery that does not exist. These beings are few, and they move among the mounds with the strange, unhurried balance of deep-sea divers. 

They stand perhaps five feet high, but they are nearly as wide as they are tall, their torsos barrel-shaped and supported on two short, pillarlike columns that end in broad, pad-footed heels without distinct toes. Their skin is not naked like the mounds', but covered in a fine, greasy shag of gray, hair-like fibers that do not shed water so much as absorb it until the creature carries its own personal marsh within its pelt. They have small, hooded eyes set deep beneath heavy arches of gristle, shielded from the salt-free, grease-laden damp of the air. 

Their hands are remarkable: broad, shovel-shaped palms tipped with four short, opposable digits that lack fingernails, ending instead in rough, callus-capped pads that can adhere to wet quartz like suckers. They do not use tools because the plateau offers nothing to make a tool from—there is no wood, no flint that will spark, no bone that does not soften in the perpetual humidity. 

Instead, they use their mass. They are the only creatures on the peneplain that possess curiosity, though it is a curiosity slowed down to the tempo of geology. A herder may spend three days levering a single rounded boulder out of a basin using its broad chest and thick, wedge-like knees, rolling it inch by inch toward a ring-wall. They build these low enclosures not to keep the mounds in—for the mounds could crush the dry-stone rings with a single heave of their gray flanks—but to create wind-shadows. Inside a ring of stones, the air swirls in a specific, dead-end vortex; the mist lingers there twice as long, depositing a thicker, sweeter grease upon the rock. The herders bring the youngest discs into these rings, planting them like slate tiles, pressing their edges together with their heavy, spatulate palms to encourage them to fuse. 

The relationship between the herders and the mounds is neither pastoral nor predatory; it is mutualistic and tactile. The herders do not eat the mounds. They live upon the secretions of the great beasts: the amber-colored, tallow-like wax that accumulates around the creatures' intake valves, which the herders scrape away with their calloused thumbs and eat directly. It is rich, dense with concentrated mist-fat, and tastes of wet wool and river silt. In return, the herders perform the only service the mounds require: they clear away the dead-weights. When a two-ton mound finally suffocates in its own bulk or dies of simple antiquity, the herders gather, four or five of them, and lean their backs against the cooling carcass, digging their heels into the spaces between the floor-discs until they find purchase on the bed-gneiss. With rhythmic, grunting surges—accompanied by a low, humming rumble they make deep in their gullets—they roll the dead thing toward the standing stones.

There, upon the slabs braced by white quartz, they hoist the carrion upright or prop it against the lee, where the mist-flies—tiny, translucent insects with no wings, that hop like fleas across the skin of the world—can reach it more easily, reducing the great mass of muscle to a dried, leathery ribbon in a matter of weeks, keeping the living floor below clear of putrefaction.

The standing stones are their history. They are not carved; they are written in grease and rub-marks. A herder can press its cheek against the smooth flank of a quartz slab and tell, by the smell of the sheep-like fat worked into the grain and the height of the polish, which of the great elders rubbed there during the last century, and whether the herd was thick or scattered when the southern wind brought the great mists up from the low valleys.

In the deep hours, when the mist is thickest and the silver dew-mirrors have joined into a single, shallow sheet of water that covers miles of the stone, the herders climb atop the low walls and crouch. They do not sleep lying down; they draw their legs beneath their round bellies, pull their heads into the fatty collars of their shoulders, and let the dew gather in their gray shag until they look identical to the boulders around them. 

Below them, the plain breathes. A million vents open and close. A wave of gray flesh ripples across a swale three leagues away, and forty minutes later, the water around the herders' feet trembles, shifting half an inch to the west, then settling back into the stillness of the stone.
