They do not have names for the stones, but their bodies are entirely patterned after the mason’s work. 

To live here is to be an inhabitant of joints, chamfers, and smoothed mortises. Over ten thousand years, the fauna has not merely adapted to an aquatic cave; it has adapted specifically to *dressed megalithic architecture*. The lineage of every crawling and drifting thing in the chamber has been curated by ninety-degree angles, flat basalt paving, and the gentle, cyclic convective roll between floor and ceiling.

At the lowest trophic tier, upon the very flagstones through which the volcanic warmth bleeds, are the **tallow-worms**. They do not resemble common earthworms or ocean vent tube-dwellers, though they share the blood-red hemoglobin of the latter. They live within the ancient runnels—the geometric drainage grooves chipped out by bronze tools before the ceiling was closed. Millions of them pack these channels flush to the stone’s surface, held together by an amber, sulfurous grease they exude from their lateral pores. They have no digestive tracts; their skin is a porous, folded mantle hosting sulfur-oxidizing archaea that drink directly from the heat-bloom of the basalt. To the touch, they would feel like warm, slick lard. When a fish drifts too close to the floor, the displacement of water causes the tallow-worms to withdraw into the grooves in a single, rolling ripple that travels thirty feet across the hall, leaving behind only the cold square geometry of the channel before they slowly, blindly well back to the surface.

Climbing from these channels are the **carpenters**—the porcelain crawfish. They are utterly pigmentless, their carapaces not clear, but the dense, chalky white of unglazed parian china. Their stalks have lost their lenses entirely, replaced by two blunt, club-like nodes of chemo-sensory flesh that shudder continuously in the current. Their chelae (pincers) are absurdly elongated, three times the length of their bodies, slender as reed needles. They do not fight. Aggression is a waste of heat in a chamber where food is distributed like butter spread thin across stone. Instead, their claws are delicate calipers used to measure the faces of the pillars. They spend years walking the perimeters of single megaliths, using their whip-like second antennae—antennae that can span seven feet—to map the familiar micro-fractures in the rock. They feed upon the moltings of their kin and the ivory sponges, clipping away the dead fibrous sponge-skeletons with surgical precision. When two carpenters meet upon a narrow lintel, they do not strike; they intertwine their impossibly long antennae, tasting each other’s shell-chemistry, determining who has molted more recently and therefore possesses the softer flank, and the firmer of the two carefully steps over the other, their porcelain legs tapping a dry, muffled *pip-pip-pip* into the basalt.

Between the pillars, suspended in the neutral buoyancy of water that matches their internal temperature to within a fraction of a degree, hang the **drowners**. 

These are the great fish. They are degenerate cyprinids or ancestral silurids whose forebears were sealed in the reservoir when the mountain civilization capped the last roof slabs with clay. In the intervening millennia, they have surrendered their eyes, their swim bladders, and almost all their bone density. Their skeletons are soft, gristly cartridges saturated with light lipids. A drowner may measure eight feet from the blunt spade of its snout to the round paddle of its tail, but it weighs almost nothing in the water; it can be displaced by the wake of a crawfish’s sudden kick.

Their skin is naked, thick, and velvety, pale as lard, with an irregular pattern of sensory pits that look like small, rust-colored puncture wounds along their flanks. From their lower jaws hang dozens of fleshy, branched barbels that drag along the stone floors like lace fringes, tasting the silt for dead worms, fallen isopods, and the rich, fatty sloughs of bryozoan mats. The drowners do not truly swim; they scull with tiny, paddle-shaped pectoral fins, turning lazily in the convective plumes. It takes a drowner twenty minutes to complete a single turn about a pillar. Their mouths are vast, toothless, and cavernous, perpetually agape by a finger’s width, taking in the slow, warm drift and expelling it through broad gill slits protected by filters of cartilaginous mesh.

When a drowner dies, it does not rise. It does not drop like a stone. It simply enters the convection plume and stays there for months—a pale, serene monument rotating through the hall, rising on the heat of the floor, drifting along the barrel-vaulted ceiling forty feet above, descending the outer walls where the mountain chills the stone, and sweeping back across the basalt pavement. The carpets of tubeworms and isopods consume it as it passes near them, stripping away ribbons of its blubber and skin while it remains in perpetual, slow-motion orbit, until only the light, cartilaginous hoop of the skull remains, tracing the circuit of the dead airless hall until the stone dissolves even that.

Higher up, near the corbeled ceiling where the water pools at its hottest, lives the hall’s quietest inhabitant: the **sheath-leech**.

These are ribbons of black-purple muscle, broad as a man’s hand and sometimes ten feet long, though they are thin enough to slip into the hairline seams where the roof-stones meet. Unlike their parasitic ancestors, they have become ambush-feeders on the ceiling currents. They cement their posterior suckers to the ceiling megaliths, right where the ancient bitumen was pushed out between the joints like black mortar, and hang downward like heavy ribbons of kelp. They possess no eyes, but their dorsal surfaces are crowded with thermal receptors so sensitive they can detect the temperature shadow of an isopod moving five feet below them. They wait for the convective plumes to carry small, floating carcasses or dying fish toward the masonry vault. When an object drifts into their thermal field, the leech does not strike with violence; it merely drops its head, pours itself through the water with an oily, undulating motion, wraps its muscular skirt entirely around the food, and re-adheres to the ceiling, digesting its meal in an envelope of acid and mucus over the course of three weeks.

There is, too, an old and singular organism that belongs neither to the crawlers nor the swimmers. Deep in the central aisle of the cistern, where the megaliths form a kind of sunken nave, four pillars fell together thousands of years ago, when the volcanic vein first shifted beneath the mountain. In the cramped, triangular sanctuary formed by the collapsed stone lives the **Bell**.

Whether it is a single creature or a colony of hyper-specialized hydroids is impossible to discern. It appears as a dome of thick, gelatinous flesh, six paces across, that has rooted itself over the fallen lintels. Its surface is translucent, veined with a faint, pulsing phosphorescence that waxes and wanes on a thirty-hour cycle—the only clock the cistern possesses, perhaps a forgotten memory of the tidal draw of the moon upon the mountain rock. The Bell does not move. It breathes. Every few minutes, its central mantle contracts with an audible, muffled *whump*—a deep, hydraulic compression that forces a wave of warm water outward across the flagstones, lifting the silt and scattering the white isopods like autumn leaves.

The other animals treat the Bell not as a predator, nor as kin, but as a feature of the masonry. The blind fish use the pressure wave of its contraction to orient themselves in the middle water, where there are no pillars to touch. The porcelain crawfish climb across its gelatinous margins without fear; its skin does not react to their claws, secreting only a thick, sweetish slime that the crawfish scrape into their mouths with their secondary legs. It is the heart of the cistern—a great, pale lung made of simple tissues, anchored to the ruins of an empire it never saw, beating gently in the dark to keep the water moving across the stones that hold it.
