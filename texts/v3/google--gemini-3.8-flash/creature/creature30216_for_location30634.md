They are a thick-set people, short through the femur and broad across the clavicle, as though the weight of the cloud ceiling has kept them pressed down toward the stone. 

To look at their hands is to understand how the settlement stays attached to the wall. Their skin does not prune—it has grown immune to the rot of the wet through generations of tallow-soaking and the constant rubbing of raw wool. The epidermis of their palms is yellow and horn-hard, like the pads of an otter’s foot, scored by deep, pale creases that never fully close. A common deformity among the condenser crews is the flattening of the thumb-joint, worn smooth from clearing grit from the roller gears and tightening the brass wing-nuts on the flume gates with cold fingers.

They dress in what they call *the pelt*: layers of dense, undyed wool that has never been scoured of its lanolin, coarse as doormats and smelling strongly of the sheep-pens of the lower valleys three miles down. Over this, they strap aprons and gaiters of split-ox hide boiled in birch-pitch until it is stiff as boiler iron. The hide does not bend so much as hinge. When an engineer walks along a zinc gangway, the leather creaks with a heavy, dry groan that is often the only warning of their approach, since their boots are soled with slabs of salvaged conveyor-rubber that make no sound at all on the wet metal.

They speak in a flattened, low-register dialect that avoids sibilants. High-pitched sounds simply die in the liverwort rugs, so they have discarded them; their words are all guttural stops and low, humming vowels that carry along the wet granite faces like the vibration of the lead-vat stirrers. Among the drums, where the *ch-clack* is loudest, they do not speak at all, but communicate with a vocabulary of three dozen wrist-flicks and knuckle-taps against the iron uprights.

They live inside the tenements by the light of tallow candles made from sheep-fat traded up from the foot of the massif in exchange for battery-lead and distilled condensate. The interiors are low, smelling of drying kelp-fiber, damp straw, and the sweetish, turnip-like steam of their porridge. 

Because green wood is an impossibility here, their beds are built from discarded shipping crates and condenser frames, hung with felt curtains to trap the sleeper’s breath. Whole families sleep in the same box, three or four deep, dressed in their day-wool, their boots tucked into niches near the floorboards where the ground-heat seeps up through the peat foundations. They do not wash their bodies with water—water is their livelihood, their crop, their master, and they consider soaking the skin in it to be an invitation to the “hollow-rot,” an endemic fungus that gets under the nails of newcomers and turns the flesh to grey paste. Instead, they scrape their limbs with zinc blades and rub themselves with sulfur powder from the battery floors.

Their diet is narrow, heavy, and pale. In the deep basement gullies, where the lagged steam pipes keep the peat at the temperature of a fresh loaf, they maintain the *beds*—vast, low-ceilinged cellars where an edible shelf-fungus, grey-white and shaped like a mule’s tongue, grows on beds of soaked willow-shavings and horse manure. It is rubbery and tastes of river silt, but it produces reliably in the dark. This they boil down with groats and salted fatback into a grey mash that is eaten three times a day, timed to the flushes of the upper valves.

Yet they are not the only things living among the iron.

In the damp spaces between the rear walls of the tenements and the dripping granite curve, where the draft never quite penetrates, there lives a breed of wingless cricket. They are the size of a man’s thumb, stark white, and blind. They live by the millions in the cracks of the mortar and the fibrous lagging of the steam mains, feeding on the grease that weeps from the pipe-joints and the felted dust of the condensers. In the dead of night, when the engines are throttled down to let the cisterns settle, their feeding can be heard through the lath walls: a dry, rustling sound, like thousands of dead leaves being stirred with a wooden spoon. The children catch them in horn bottles, pinch off their legs, and drop them into the tallow pots; their fat is clean and leaves no soot on the glass chimneys of the carbon lamps.

Higher up, in the dark hollows where the timber flumes run under the overhangs, lives the mountain’s only true native parasite: the *sieve-leech*. They are not black like valley leeches, but the pale, translucent pink of an eyelid. They inhabit the seams of the wooden troughs, lying flat as grease-paper against the slimy deal, waiting for the workers who clear the grit from the intake grates. A man will come back from a twelve-hour shift on the upper combs and find three of them stuck between his toes or in the fold of his armpit, bloated to the size of sausages with cold, watery blood that doesn't clot easily in the thin air. The men do not salt them off; they burn them with the glowing ends of their pipe-spills, cursing them with the weary familiarity one shows to an uncle who steals tobacco.

The dead are not buried. To dig into the peat would be to breach the insulation that keeps the foundations above freezing, and the stone of the amphitheater has no soil to give. 

When a drum-tender dies, or an apprentice slips from an icy zinc ladder and breaks against the cobbles, the body is stripped of its wool and pitch-hides, which are too scarce to waste. The corpse is washed once—its only complete bath—in the icy overflow of the central reservoir, then carried up to the high shelves where the wind moves fastest. There, in the narrow clefts between the granite teeth where the cloud trots perpetually eastward, they wedge the body into an iron-slatted rack, like a baker’s cooling tray, bolted directly to the rock face.

The wind does not dry the dead; the air is too wet for mummification. Instead, the steady, cold draft and the perpetual mist slowly macerate the soft tissue, washing it out through the slats in fine, colorless drips that feed the giant liverworts below. Within two seasons, the skeleton is picked clean by the damp itself, scrubbed white by the wind-driven mist until it is as smooth as the granite boulders, ready to be pulled down and ground into mineral paste to seal the joints of the next new cistern. 

They accept this without ceremony. To them, a body is simply another drum that has ceased to turn, returning its moisture to the prevailing cloud so that the pumps below might catch it again by evening.
