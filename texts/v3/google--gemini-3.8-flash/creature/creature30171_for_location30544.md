To understand what lives here, one must begin with the skin, because nothing in this depth possesses the luxury of an edge that is not constantly touched by something else.

The largest among them—the great segmented bulks that choke the turbine pits and slump against the bases of the flywheels—have no names that could be spoken, for there is no air here dry enough to carry vowels without turning them to mush. The workers who might once have labeled them are gone, or perhaps these *are* the workers, settled into an enduring larval middle age. They are known only by their displacement: five tons of tallow, blind muscle, and salt-slick hide folded into rings like the bellows of a forge.

They lack faces in any recognizable sense. Where a head might terminate, their bodies taper into a blunt, spade-like disc of cartilage, pale gray and veined with indigo. This disc is rimmed with fleshy, prehensile lobes that move with the slow, searching precision of sea anemones. These lobes do not seek meat; they seek thermal leakage and the galvanic fur that grows on wet copper. A grub will press its disc against a busbar, sealing itself to the metal with a wet, vacuum sigh, and remain there for four months, drawing into its throat the bitter, alkaline sweat that weeps from the junction where iron meets basalt. 

Their respiration is so slow that a man standing in the dark might mistake their flank for a damp wall until it rises beneath his palm—a three-inch heave that takes four minutes to crest and four to subside. Inside them, there are no lungs, but vast, fluid-filled labyrinths lined with vascular cilia that strain the suspended oil from the dark. Their flesh tastes faintly of battery water and lard; when they die, they do not rot so much as turn to soap, their fats reacting with the mineral alkalis in the brine until their carcasses become hard, white banks of grease that the living slowly wear down through the friction of their passage.

Moving over and through these vast sleepers are the crawlers, the things that own the clicking.

They are chitinous, but it is not the chitin of crabs or beetles. It is soft-tempered and oily, the color of a wet umbrella, flexible enough to bend beneath the weight of a grub without cracking. They are long—some the length of an oar, others no bigger than a split shoe—and their legs are absurdly numerous, needle-fine, and jointed in three directions. These legs do not scratch the iron; they end in soft, cup-shaped pads that produce a distinct *lip-lip-lip* when they cross flat steel, though when they scale the rough faces of the cleaved basalt, little retractable spurs emerge from the pads to find purchase in the mineral grain.

These multi-limbed things are the cleaners, though not by intent. They are driven by an unceasing, neurotic itch that forces them to scrape. They scrape the paraffin crust from the flywheels; they clean the margins where the grubs’ bellies meet the rock; they groom the vertical films of water that hang suspended from the sheer stone faces. 

When a crawler reaches one of those vertical lenses of brine—water that clings to a sheer wall through some broken angle of gravity—it does not alter its stride. It steps off the stone and into the floating water, its movements growing instantly wider, more languid, its dozens of legs rowing through the heavy grease-water like the fringes of a ribbon-worm. Inside the water-lens, it feeds on the film of blind, oil-drinking rotifers that coat the inner surface of the surface tension. One can hear them in the stillness: a dry, rapid vibration, like parchment being torn beneath a pillow, as their mandibles shear through the slime.

And then there are those that walk.

They are rare, spaced perhaps half a league apart, though they move constantly, so one can never be certain of their census. They are bipeds, or they choose to be bipeds when the clearance permits, though they drop to all fours with a fluid, broken-jointed ease whenever the ceiling drops or a mass of grubs blocks the terrace.

They are tall—well over seven feet—and lean with the terrible, knotty strength of ropes soaked in brine. Their skin is neither hairless nor furred, but covered in an ultra-fine, downy moss, slate-colored and cold to the touch, which remains perpetually wet with the condensation that drips from above. They have broad, flat chests that do not expand outward, but compress laterally when they take air.

Their hands are extraordinary: the fingers are five, but the palms are double-jointed, capable of cupping a pipe or an axle with the completeness of a lead sleeve. The pads of these fingers are extraordinarily thick, devoid of ridges or fingerprints, consisting instead of smooth, gray calluses that are sensitive to the micro-vibrations of the floor. A walker does not look—its eye-sockets are narrow, dry horizontal slits sealed behind plugs of fibrous yellow gristle—but it will stand for hours with one hand laid lightly upon a copper rod, its head tilted toward the stone, reading the state of the buried grid through the twitch of the metal.

They do not wear clothes, but their bodies bear the marks of an ancient familiarity with the machinery. Many have patches of their flesh scarred into precise geometric cross-hatchings, the exact negative impression of iron catwalk grating, earned from lifetimes of resting their weight against the floor. Others have worn smooth grooves into their collarbones and hips where they have squeezed, thousands of times, through the same narrow clearance between a turbine housing and a basalt ledge.

The walkers do not prey upon the grubs; indeed, there is no predation here in the conventional sense. The energy of this place is not solar; it is not eaten; it is conducted. When a walker grows weak, it finds a place where a busbar enters the stone—where the basalt looks like frozen tar around the copper—and it wedges itself between the two. It presses its chest to the cool stone and wraps its long, flat hands around the bar. It stays there until the ozone smell rises, until the dry, snapping noise arcs through the dark.

When that arc happens, the walker’s body acts as a shunt. The current passes through the downy hide, down the spine, and into the rock. It is not an execution; it is an inflation. During the communal shudder, when the hundred thousand grubs draw their bellies inward and the dark grows tight with the smell of scorched linoleum, the walker’s throat opens.

It does not cry out—it has no tongue, only a broad, ridged floor of horn in its mouth—but it vents. A long, thin hiss of steam escapes its chest, smelling of hot river-silt and tallow, rising toward that invisible, low ceiling to condense, grow fat and heavy, and fall again as grease upon the backs of the things that wait.
