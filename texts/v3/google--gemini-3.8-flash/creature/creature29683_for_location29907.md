At first, the only presence is the water’s own cargo, arriving in pieces. 

The flume brings whatever the headwaters could not hold against the current: tiny, torn fragments of liverwort; hard, black seeds from some scrub oak miles up the gorge, polished to a gloss by their slide down the conduits; and the dead. Most of the insects that hit the standing water are broken instantly by the plunge-fall, hammered to the floor and sucked toward the overflow slots before they can unfurl. But their carapaces leave an invisible residue, a thin, fatty grease that clings to the travertine right at the waterline, marking the perimeter with a faint, amber tide-line that the lime-wash has already begun to absorb.

Then there is what clings. In the chisel-marks where the sun lingers—the only irregular shelter in fourteen paces of squared wall—the green is not alone. It is a skin, and under that skin the lime is already being worked. Microscopic rotifers, carried as dry dust on the stonemasons’ clothes or blown through the square aperture in the ceiling by a terrace gust, have reconstituted in the bath-warm stillness. They do not swim through the blue volume; they are too fragile for the cataract’s drumming. They huddle in the parallel grooves of the pick-strokes, deep inside the shadow of the stone’s own grain, grazing the emerald velvet down to the white mineral paste. When the midday beam shifts even an inch, their small pasture burns, and they die in crusts of dried salt and starch, only to be replaced by the next drift of cysts tumbling down the flume.

Yet the chamber is too deep and too loud to remain merely a tank of microscopic weed. 

In the blind angle beneath the flume—where the stone lip projects three feet over the pool and casts the only permanent wedge of black shadow in the entire cistern—something larger holds itself. 

It did not grow here; it was drawn in from the subterranean feed-runs, washed through the dark arteries of the mountain before being spat over the drop into the terrifying noon. It is a soft-shelled river turtle, but one bleached by long passage through unlit caverns into the color of wet tallow. Its carapace is the size of an armorer’s buckler, leathery and scarred, its rim fringed with small, ragged tags of dead skin that flutter in the eddy like torn parchment. 

It does not leave the wedge of shade. To venture into the white glare of the travertine floor would be to drown in light; its eyes, small and black as elderberries, are lidless and swollen from the mineral bite of the water, squinting painfully against the upward hammer of the sun. It remains wedged by its hind claws into the dry-laid mortar joint between two ashlar blocks, six feet below the surface. Its forelegs—heavy, spade-like paddles armed with pale nails—hang limp in the back-eddy, tasting the current. 

Every ten minutes, the turtle releases its purchase with a slow, muscular shrug. It does not swim so much as let the counter-surge of the plunge-fall lift it. It rises along the masonry wall like a pale, flat stone cast upward by an explosion, drifting vertically through the roaring air bells until its tubular, pig-like snout breaks the surface skin an inch below the ceiling arches. It takes a single, violent draw of air—air that smells of fresh mortar, wet calcium, and hot cedar from the terrace above—and then, with one stroke of its great gray webs, banks downward, sliding along the shadow line back to its seam. 

It is waiting for the sound to stop, but the sound never stops. The percussion of the flume beats against the flat plate of its plastron with the rhythm of an anvil. It lives in a state of permanent, vibrating suspension, stunned by the sheer geometry of the place. There are no banks to dig into, no roots to crawl beneath, no silt in which to bury its leathery belly. When it tests the bottom with its snout, it finds only the smooth, hard plane of the pale floor, smelling faintly of iron wedges and vinegar, impossible to breach.

And higher up, in the splash-zone where the torrent shatters against the air, there is another tenant, smaller and more transient. 

A pair of cliff swallows, nesting somewhere in the sun-baked cornices of the terrace above, drop down through the ceiling square twenty times an hour. They do not touch the water; they are hunting the froth. The drop churns up an endless mist of sulfur and cool foam that rises through the aperture like chimney smoke. The birds dive through the cobalt square of sky, brake hard against the booming acoustic wall of the cistern with a snap of their sickle wings, and snatch the drowning ants and drowned beetles that the flume has tossed against the vertical stone. 

For a fraction of a second, their blue-black bellies catch the travertine’s upward glare, turning them into white sparks that flash across the water’s skin. Then they are sucked upward again by the thermal chimney, out through the coping into the vast, dry quiet of the upper world, leaving the turtle alone in its corner, the algae knitting silently into the tool-grooves, and the great white box thrumming with its single, boiling heart of water.
