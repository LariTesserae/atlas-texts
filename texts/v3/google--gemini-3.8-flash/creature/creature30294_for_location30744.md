To understand what lives in the basin, one must first discard the distinction between the tenant and the wall. 

In this place, the buildings are not shelters; they are husks, exoskeletons, and nursery beds. The stone does not merely mimic an apartment block or a colonnade through some blind accident of mineralogy—it is extruded by things that require those exact rectilinear dimensions to complete themselves, though “themselves” is a shifting boundary.

Inside the high, perforated tenements, behind those millions of identical rectangular slots, the rooms are not empty. 

If you peel back the chalky veneer of a third-story facade while it is still rising from the dust, you find the chamber packed with an intricate, pleated mass, dry as dried marrow. These are the *in-dwellers*—though they possess no faces, no limbs that could wander, and no distinct organs of sense. They resemble great stacks of pale, brittle parchment, thousands of leaves compressed into a block that fills the rectangular cell to within a fraction of a millimeter. 

They are sessile animals whose metabolism is an infinitely slowed combustion of dust and dry air. Through capillary action that relies on electrostatic charge rather than moisture, they draw the airborne silt into their paper-thin laminations. As the dust passes through them, their inner leaves deposit lime, extruding the flinty foam outward to thicken the very walls that enclose them. They are living formwork. A room exists because a creature of that exact volume has calcified its own skin; when the room is fully hardened, the creature inside withers, its thin laminations flaking away into fine, talc-like meal that drifts out through the window-slots, leaving behind an apparently vacant civic apartment.

Thus, the city’s vacancy is an illusion caused by its life cycles: when a street looks finished, its builders have already turned to dust, and when it is being born, its builders are locked inside its stones.

Yet not all life here is stationary. The hound-sized, jointed articulates—the ones seen patrolling the cornices like blind, chalk-glass whip-scorpions—are neither inhabitants nor builders in the human sense. They are shed fragments of the architecture itself, liberated during the moments of structural shift. 

When an architrave reaches a certain critical density, its crystalline structure does not simply stop growing; it buckles along specific, biologically determined fault lines. The excess stone peels away from the lintel not as rubble, but as a fully articulate creature. Its legs are the former teeth of a dental molding; its pedipalps are the scrolls of an aborted capital. 

These creatures, sometimes called *paling-striders*, carry no digestive tracts. They do not eat. Their bellies are hollow vaults containing a tight, spinning whorl of fibrous mineral hair that generates an electrostatic field, keeping their joints suspended in a state of frictionless levitation—a necessity in a world without tallow or grease, where stone grinding on stone would otherwise bind within hours. 

The striders wander because their charge compels them toward points of structural discordance. Where a wall has leaned too far toward the perpendicular, or where a mineral fissure threatens to drop a floor prematurely, the strider gathers there. It uses its heavy, scissor-like mouthparts to rasp away excess mineral froth, or conversely, it stands perfectly still for six weeks against a bowing column, slowly permitting its own body to be calcified into the structure, sacrificing its mobility to become a flying buttress. One can walk down an avenue and see, half-absorbed into the side of a tenement, the segmented abdomen and tucked limbs of a strider that has successfully converted itself back into architecture.

Below them, on the street level, the floor is not dead ground. 

Under the carpet of impalpable white flour that catches the falling balconies without a sound, there exists a lower fauna that lives entirely within the fluidized dust. These are the *sieve-ribbons*. They are broad, ribbon-like vermin, three to five feet in length, made of overlapping plates of horn-like keratin, flexible and completely devoid of fluid. They swim through the dry dust as flatfish swim through silt, never breaking the surface, undulating with a rhythmic, mechanical flap. 

The ribbons feed on the shed sheets of the in-dwellers and the microscopic scrapings left behind by the striders. Because there is no sound in this basin, the sieve-ribbons hunt and orient through the subtle, freezing thermal drafts that leak out of the foundations as the older arcades subside. 

When a pillar sinks into the earth—retracting with that eerie, piston-smooth grace—it does not simply disappear into bedrock. It is being drawn down into the jaws of the *keystones*: massive, subterranean organisms that anchor the city from below. 

These keystones are the closest thing the basin possesses to a primary brain, though they are scattered, solitary, and largely mineralized. They occupy vast, scooped cisterns in the cold siltite beneath the pavements. A keystone is an immense, radially symmetrical dome of dense, layered calcite, like a gigantic fossilized urchin forty feet in diameter. It possesses no eyes, no heart, and no blood; its flesh is an arid, fibrous paste that exists under pressures so colossal that no fluid could remain liquid within it anyway. 

The keystones consume the retreating architecture. As the old colonnades are driven down into the cellar by the weight of new growth above, the keystones receive the pillars into deep, radial sockets. With the patient, tectonic force of shifting strata, they grind the flint-glass stone down into fine powder, absorbing the mineral charge, filtering out the ambient cold, and re-pumping the raw calcitic vapor back up through the hollow aqueducts to feed the newly sprouting facades at the northern rim.

They are the lungs and stomachs of the streets, and yet they are completely mindless, reacting only to the pull of gravity and the microscopic expansion of crystal lattices.

In the interstitial spaces—in the empty alleys between two tenements that have grown so close their eaves nearly interlock—one finds the aerial life. 

It does not fly; flying requires atmospheres that offer lift and wings that can strike damp air without snapping. Instead, these things are *spindle-weavers*. They resemble clusters of dry needles connected by hair-thin wires of drawn mineral glass. They hang suspended in the dry drafts, anchored between two walls by tension lines so taut they hum at frequencies far above human hearing, though the sound-eating stone smothers even this vibration within an inch of the thread. 

The weavers drift up and down the height of the canyons on thermal cushions, collecting the silver lichens that tear loose from the cornices. If two weavers collide in mid-air, their needles do not tangle; they knit. They interlock with a clean, dry *snick*, combining into a larger, multi-pointed polygon that hangs more heavily in the draft, sinking slowly down to the street like a dead snowflake, where the bellows-weeds ingest it with a silent, paper-lunged gasp.

Even the silver lichens are not entirely plant-like. Under magnification, their "filings" are revealed to be minute, jointed claws that cling to the microscopic pits in the stone. They are migratory colonies. Over the course of three days, a silvery tuft will creep four inches to the west, pursuing the faint electrostatic charge generated by the newly rising lintel above it, leaving behind a faint, polished track on the stone like the path of a snail, but dry as diamond-dust.

Nothing here dies in a way that leaves a corpse. There is no rot, for there are no bacteria; bacteria require moisture to split their cells, and here, a drop of water would be as destructive and corrosive as a splash of boiling vitriol. When a creature here reaches the end of its cycle, it simply ceases to discharge its electrostatic field. 

Without that field, its joints lose their suspension. The mineral-hair whorls inside its belly stop spinning. The creature instantly stiffens, becoming inert, glassy pumice. 

Within minutes, the wandering striders will find it. They will not mourn it, nor will they feed on it. They will approach it with their long, hair-thin antennae extended, feel the cold lack of vibration in its plates, and begin, with rhythmic, surgical clicks of their mandibles, to carve it into six or eight neat, rectangular bricks. 

These bricks are picked up, carried three streets over, and placed with blind, architectural perfection into the rising wall of a new colonnade, where they will wait for the next generation of in-dwellers to settle between them, fold their dry leaves, and begin the work of turning stone into town once again.
