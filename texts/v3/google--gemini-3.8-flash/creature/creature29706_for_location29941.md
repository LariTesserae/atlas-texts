At first, to search for an inhabitant is to look for something that moves against the stillness, and therefore to find nothing at all. The hollow rejects the usual currencies of animal life: there is no scurry, no flutter of wings against stone, no pair of eyes catching a sliver of light from the entrance. 

If there is life here, it must agree with the terms of the stone.

The first presence is so continuous with the shrubs that it appears to be an exudate of their wood. Clustered along the undersides of the green-black twigs, just below the blunt thorns, are small, oval encrustations, no larger than lentil seeds. They look like dried droplets of dark sap or swellings of the bark, but they are shelled, legless scale-feeders. They do not possess eyes, nor do they possess limbs once they have settled. In the first year of their lives—an interval that may last a human decade—they creep out from beneath their dead parent’s casing like microscopic grains of soot, navigate the dry grain of the branch, find a fissure where the bitter sap runs closest to the surface, and drive a needle-thin stylet into the wood. 

Once anchored, they never shift again. They absorb the resinous, peppery fluids drawn up from the deep joints of the bedrock, transforming that dense hydrocarbon into a hard, waxy shell the color of dried liver. Their metabolism is so sluggish that their respiration cannot be measured; they produce no heat that exceeds the isothermal stone beneath them. They do not excrete honey-dew; every molecule of moisture is retained, bound into their dense, resinous tissues until they die and dry into brittle husks, leaving their hollow carapaces behind to be slowly filled by mineral dust.

Beneath the shrubs, within the floury, uncompacted silt, exists the second order of this place.

The surface of the dust appears unbroken, but two inches down, the silt possesses a loose, particulate grain that behaves more like a cold fluid than earth. Here live creatures that do not crawl across the floor, but swim through it. They are pale, elongated arthropods, related distantly to silverfish or bristletails, but flattened until their bodies have the thickness of heavy parchment. They are entirely blind, their heads smooth and conical, designed to wedge through the powder without resistance. Their carapaces are not hard chitin, but a flexible, velvet-textured cuticle that sheds dust effortlessly, coated in a microscopic array of hairs that trap a layer of dead air around their bodies.

They feed on the dust itself—or rather, on what the dust collects. Every few months, an imperceptible draft at the throat of the blind drops a microscopic shower of spores, dried pollen grains from the plateau miles above, and the sloughed-off skin flakes of the scale-feeders. The parchment-tails sieve this flour through delicate, comb-like mouthparts. When they displace the silt, they do it beneath the surface; the dust settles behind their passage like water behind an oar, leaving the level grade above entirely flat, innocent of trails.

And yet, this chamber is not solely inhabited by things the size of a seed.

In the deepest recess of the hollow, where the floor rises into the apron of settled talus, there is an animal that matches the scale of the room. It does not hide; it simply belongs so completely to the tonal values of the sandstone that the human eye might scan across it three times and register only the curved shelf of the bedrock.

It is a reptile, though one whose physiology has decoupled itself from the bright, sun-baked violence of the world outside. It is broad, low, and long-bodied—perhaps the length of a man’s forearm from blunt snout to tapered tail. Its limbs are splayed and thick, ending in broad, fleshy pads that distribute its weight so widely that its belly rests on the powder without sinking. 

Its skin is the gray of wet slate dried under an ash-cloud, entirely matte, absorbing what little scattered light reaches the rear wall. It has no pattern, only the granular texture of fine sandpaper, mirroring the surface of the wind-smoothed sandstone against which it lies. Over its back and along the ridges of its spine, the scales have grown into small, calcified nodules that gather a faint drift of ambient dust, so that it wears the very floor as a second, mineral hide.

Its eyes are open, but they are not the bright, slit-pupiled eyes of a desert hunter. They are round, milk-filmed globes, clouded over with a dense, protective nictitating membrane that does not retract. It has no use for sharp focus in this perpetual charcoal dusk. Instead, its vision is sensitive only to the geometry of the chamber's warmth: two large, depression-like pits between its nostrils and its eyes read the thermal landscape with exquisite precision. It can feel the faint coolness of the entrance sixty feet away; it can feel the radiant pulse of the deep stone beneath its belly; it can sense the tiny, warm ember of a living scale-insect on a twig above its head.

It breathes once every three or four minutes. The flank rises—a fraction of an inch, lifting the dust that rests upon it—and then sinks without a sound. The air is warm enough that it loses no heat in the exchange, dry enough that it gives off no visible moisture.

This creature’s food is not the product of a chase. Perhaps twice in a year, it moves. Its movement is an event without urgency: a slow, dragging slide of pale feet across the powder, timed to the drift of an errant draft or simply to some internal, glandular clock. It approaches a low-hanging thorn, raises its blunt head, and with a tongue that is broad, dry, and tipped with a sticky, viscous mucus, it strips three or four of the waxy scale-insects from their twig. The wood vibrates once; a single thorn scratches against stone; then the silence re-asserts itself. It chews with a faint, brittle crunch—the only sharp sound the hollow has known in a decade—and swallows. The fuel from those few dried casings will sustain its slow heart through the turning of an entire season on the world above.

Between the creature’s resting place and the apron of talus lie the dark chert flakes left behind by human hands. 

The animal does not avoid them. In fact, its rear claws rest against the rounded river-cobble, the hammerstone that holds the thumbprint of the knapper. The stone is cooler than the floor, having retained a denser core of mountain-chill, and the creature uses it as an anchor, a firm counterweight against which to brace its hip.

The hands that chipped those flakes departed thousands of years ago, but to this dweller, that departure is not ancient history. The chamber does not record time in years; it records it in the slow dissipation of heat, the microscopic rain of dust, the growth of a shrub’s root through an eighth of an inch of shale. The thumbprint in the hammerstone and the body of the gray reptile are peers in this space. They have both found their angle of repose. 

When the light at the mouth of the gorge thins toward evening, the creature does not retreat into a burrow. There is nowhere safer than this open floor; nothing comes in from the alley outside, because there is nothing here that an animal from the sunlit upper rim could recognize as food or sport. It simply lies where it is, its head resting against the flat dust, its filmed eyes reflecting the total absence of change, breathing the resin-tinted air of an earth that has forgotten how to move.
