The first thing you hear of them is not a voice, but the sound of skin unsticking from wet river-silt. It is a slow, wet *shluck*, like a boot pulled from an estuary flat, repeated three feet above your ear, then behind your collarbone, then somewhere down near your shins.

They do not have the carriage of people who walk. Their collarbones have folded inward, the tips nearly meeting under the throat, narrowing their chests until they can turn entirely around inside a flue no broader than a wine firkin. Because they have never stood against a level resistance, their heels have softened into round, cushiony bulbs of pink gristle, while their toes have lengthened, the big digit diverging from the rest like the thumb of a potto. They use these to crimp hazel laths into place while their hands are busy with the mud.

Their skin is the pale, grey-green of a turnip left too long in a cellar, permanently macerated by ninety-eight-degree moisture. The epidermis on their forearms and shins is not smooth; it has the pebbled, cross-hatched texture of the coarse hemp netting they sleep against, an imprint so deep and permanent that their hair follicles grow in little diagonal rows, tracking the twist of the cord. There is no body hair to speak of anyway—the alkaline lime in the mortar slurry strips it away, leaving their thighs and forearms slick as peeled plums, smelling perpetually of river-bottom and iron.

They are small, but it is an elastic, sinewy smallness, like an otter’s carcass stripped of fat. A full-grown one can squeeze through an aperture that would trap an eight-year-old child from the plains, provided they exhale first and let their ribs overlap.

They do not speak with the open throat. The gale outside is too shearing, and the interior steam is too thick for vowels—they choke on their own spit if they try to hold an *ah* or an *oh*. Instead, they converse through their teeth in an alphabet of wet, clicking consonants and palate-pops that sound precisely like hazel rods snapping under strain. When they must send word across three knurled joints to the mud-gangs below, they take the flat of a bone spatula and slap the taut cow-hide skin of the outer hull. The whole tower is a drum; they listen with their temples pressed against the wicker, reading the thrum of the spire’s ribs through their jawbones.

They eat the joinery.

Not the timber itself, but the life that erupts from its agony. When the green hazel laths bleed their white, almond-scented sap under the bite of the gut-lashings, the women and the half-grown young scrape the curd with bone spatulas before it can sour into vinegar. It has the consistency of warm clotted grease and coats their tongues with an oily, bitter film that staves off the water-bloat. Behind them come the blind, hairless mites—fat as lentils and soft as grease-balls—that breed where the cow-hide overlaps. The people scoop these off the damp hair with their nails and mash them against the roof of the mouth, swallowing them whole for the salt.

There are no elders among them because there is no time for an elder to happen. A worker lasts perhaps twelve hundred *slips*—the vertical measure they use instead of hours, marked by how many times a basket of wet grey mud must be hauled through the groin-holes before the floor below begins to bow. By the time their teeth are ground down to the pink by the silt that hangs in every breath of steam, their finger joints have calcified into hard, hooked galls that cannot be pried open. A person who can no longer knot cannot stay in the pockets; there is nowhere to put them where their weight would not pull a willow strut out of plumb. They are worked into the core. Their wrists are crossed over their knees, bound with the same green withy that holds the outer hurdles, and plastered into the thickest part of the knurling where the wind-shear is most brutal. The mud covers their eyes first, then the bridge of the nose, their breathing tubes kept open with hollow reeds until the silt sets enough to carry the next course of wicker. Their calcium becomes part of the damp compression that keeps the spire from twisting off into the blue.

Between their bellies and the walls, other things move that have never known the sun at all.

There is a breed of small, blunt-headed vole, no longer than a thumb, with pink, shovel-shaped forelimbs and no eyes whatever—only two bald, puckered pits behind the snout. They do not burrow in dirt; they swim through the fresh mud-courses while the silt is still liquid, hunting the fat, white maggots of the tower-wasps that nest in the dry centers of the hemp knots. The people do not kill the voles; they listen for their wet tunneling inside the wet partitions. If a vole stops moving, it means the mortar in that sector has taken an unnatural frost from some needle-leak of the upper sky, and the whole crew must abandon the pocket before the willow turns brittle and shatters under the weight of the basket-chains.

In the lower tiers of the joint, where the air is oldest and tastes of boiled tripe and hair, lives the Weaver.

Whether it is one creature or a family that has lost the memory of individual ribs, no one who works the trowels can say. It remains wedged in the blind angle where four major weight-struts cross—a space no larger than a bread-trough, where the water collects in a deep, stagnant trough of tallow-colored slime. It has six fingers on each hand, all of them double-jointed, with long, horny nails that it uses to split river-cane into threads no thicker than horsehair. It does not lay mud. It does nothing but repair the internal tension-cords that hold the room’s throat open against the high-altitude pressure that wants to flatten the wicker like a trodden basket.

The Weaver is hairless, swollen-bellied from drinking the condensate that runs down the greasy hazel struts, and entirely blind. Its eyeballs have shrunk to dry grey marbles, but its ears are enormous, broad, leaf-like flaps of skin that twitch toward every hiss of steam. It works in the dark, naked, its skin covered in a crust of dried lime and dried almond-sap that makes it look like an unbaked effigy left out in the rain.

Sometimes, when the wind outside drops from an anvil-roar to a long, singing shriek, you can feel the Weaver’s fingers working through the wickerwork directly behind your spine. They do not touch your skin—they have no interest in flesh—but they pass within a millimeter of your shoulder blades, following the run of a single split-cane tendon, checking its wetness, testing its pull with a dry *twick-twick* of a yellow thumbnail, before vanishing back into the dripping darkness of the strut-pocket to splice another yard of gut.

None of them look up. To look up would mean imagining an end to the spire, and there is no end; there is only the next pocket where the mud is still runny, and the next lath that needs a knee pressed against its curve until the hemp can bite. If you shouted to them of the ground—of dirt that stays flat for a mile, of stones that do not groan underfoot, of air that does not taste of wet hide and hot lime—they would think you were describing the state of being plastered into the wall: that heavy, cold immobility that comes when the last hazel rod is tied, the mud slides shut over the mouth, and the screaming of the high wind finally stops.
