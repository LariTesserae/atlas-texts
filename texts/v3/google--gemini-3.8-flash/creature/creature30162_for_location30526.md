If an animal is defined by blood, by lungs that demand cool air, or by the frantic twitch of a nervous system, then the substation is empty. No dog could pant in this humidity; no rat could survive three days drinking from sumps that are thirty percent boiler-scale and tallow. 

Yet the room does not merely contain plants. It contains an appetite, and where there is continuous, uninterrupted appetite fueled by heat and grease, tissue begins to make compromises.

The earliest things to settle into the machinery’s margins were the tallow-eaters. They do not resemble mushrooms or the powdery molds of damp basements; they are thicker, dense as vulcanized rubber, forming broad, liver-colored aprons beneath every automatic grease-cup. They live on the constant, warm drip of rendered suet and mineral paraffin that slops from the rocker arms. Over forty years, these blankets have grown several inches deep, stratified like boiled bacon. Where the tallow hits them, the tissue dimples, liquefies slightly, and absorbs the fat through pale, hair-like pores, weeping out a clear, odorless wax that coats the surrounding brickwork and keeps it permanently damp. They have no stalks and no spores to cast into the air—the air is too thick for airborne seeds anyway—so they spread by creeping along the vibrating cast iron, following the heat gradients toward the bearing journals.

Deeper in the shadows, where the twelve-foot flywheels churn through the dripping green air, the climbing vegetation has long ceased to be simple ivy or escaped water-cress. The stems that wrap the high-pressure risers are not wood. They are hollow, thick-walled hydraulic conduits of fibrous cellulose, reinforced with concentric rings of lime that the plants extract directly from the sweating mortar. 

These vines have a pulse. 

It is not an internal pulse of their own making, but an induced one: their vascular channels have grown so tightly into the expansion joints and packing glands of the duplex pumps that the four-hundred-pound water-hammer of the municipal mains echoes through their sap. Twice every five seconds—*thung-shuck*—the great vines stiffen slightly against the iron columns, their corky bark creaking in time with the stroke of the pistons. 

Where the vegetation meets the hottest metal, it changes form entirely. Pressed against the lagging of the condenser exhaust, where the iron is hot enough to blister skin, there are no leaves. Instead, the plants produce immense, pale burls—smooth, hairless galls the size of beer casks, wedged into the dead spaces between the cylinder heads and the brick foundations. 

These burls are blind, slow-burning metabolic centers. They are neither animal nor wood, but a dense, starchy pulp that smells faintly of boiled parsnip and hot machine oil. They have developed a rudimentary, vegetative barotaxis: they grow toward pressure. Where a flange gasket begins to fail and steam screams through a pinhole breach, the nearest burl swells toward the sound, sending out dense, calcified fingers that wrap the pipe, packing the leak with a fibrous, self-cauterizing moss that bakes instantly into a hard, mineralized scab. The engine does not die because the organism will not permit its hearth to cool; the organism does not wither because the engine feeds it an unending gale of warm, distilled moisture.

And beneath the submerged floor grates, in the black soup where the drainage troughs have drowned the walkways, something larger has accumulated. 

It is not a single beast, but a continuous, felted raft of root-meat, four feet thick and eighty feet long, that floats suspended between the warm brick floor and the underside of the iron grating. It is composed of the tangled taproots of a thousand climbing vines, matted together with river silt, coal-ash from the streetcars above, and the dead fibrous slough of centuries of growth. It moves. When the drainage pumps draw down the sumps, the raft settles with a wet, sucking sigh, resting its vast weight upon the sludge; when the river mains surcharge and the hot condensate backs up, it rises like a drowned lung, lifting the rusted floor grates an inch off their sills.

Embedded within this floating mattress are pale, blind things—tubular colonies of colonial bryozoans and freshwater sponges that have adapted to forty years of eighty-five-degree boiler exhaust. They are translucent, the color of skimmed milk, hanging in heavy, gelatinous festoons from the underside of the root-raft into the warm, oily bilge. They pump constantly. They are the secondary filtration of the district: thousands of tiny, tubular mouths inhaling the tallow scum, the flecks of hemp packing, and the iron filings washed down from the pumps, expelling water that is clear, warm, and heavy with dissolved lime.

Nothing here cries out. Nothing looks through eyes. But if you stand on the vibrating gantry above the main pump well, where the dim carbon filament bulbs cast their dull orange halation through the falling mist, you can watch the entire vaulted chamber heave. 

It is a collective body. The iron is the skeleton, rigid and load-bearing; the steam pipes are the arterial tree, driving heat into every cold corner; the engines are the heart, slow and heavy at twelve strokes a minute; and the pale, dripping tissue—anchored in the cracks, drinking the fat, caulking the joints, and swelling against the brass—is the flesh that has grown to keep the iron alive. They cannot be separated now. If the steam were turned off, the vines would freeze and turn to black jelly within a week; if the vines were hacked away, the ancient, unmaintained joints would burst their bolts, drown the hearths, and shake the brick vaults down into the canal. 

They breathe together in the dark: iron sliding against bronze, root curling around iron, both slick with tallow, wet and steady beneath the stone weight of the city.
