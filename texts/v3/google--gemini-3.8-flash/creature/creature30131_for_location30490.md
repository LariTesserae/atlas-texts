In the rectangular cisterns, where the water maintains its glass-smooth equilibrium, there is no green scum. What lives there cannot afford the waste of rot.

Deep in the corners of the stone basins, clinging to the submerged chisel marks, are clusters of tiny, blind isopods, each no longer than an oat flake. They are translucent as river quartz, their jointed carapaces so thin that the dark, iron-rich water shows through their bellies. They do not swim; they lack the paddles for it. Instead, they creep across the dressed basalt with microscopic deliberation, grazing on the film of airborne dust that settles from the clouds. When a drop falls from the lintel above with that hollow *tink*, they freeze, pressing their flattened bodies into the copper-bruised grooves until the surface tension of the pool stops trembling. They have no eyes to lose, only two pairs of pale, thread-like antennae that taste the mineral balance of the mountain: too much sulfur, and they pull into the seams; too much lime, and they mate.

Above the water line, in the narrow, dry space between the double-skinned basalt walls where the river gravel serves as insulation, lives the true bulk of the shelf’s breath. 

It is an ecology that depends entirely on what the mountain carries upward on its drafts. The south gorge acts as a chimney; on hot afternoons in the valleys four thousand feet below, swarms of midges, flying ants, and the downy seed-heads of river poplars are pulled up the rock faces, chilled instantly by the thin air, and dropped dead or stunned onto the terrace flagstones. 

A species of flat-bodied jumping spider—the color of chipped gneiss, with legs banded in charcoal and dull cream—waits for this fallout. They spin no broad webs; the air is too calm to warrant them, and silk is expensive in a place with no grease. Instead, they lay single, taut trip-lines across the carved drainage rills. They move with an unnatural, clockwork hesitation, taking three paces, freezing for twenty minutes, then pivoting without a sound to watch a dead moth drift across the curb. They have learned the geometry of the chisel marks; they use the copper-scored scars on the blocks as trenches to avoid the slate-colored lizards that patrol the perimeter at noon.

And inside the dwellings—past the stepped thresholds where the sand has crusted into permanence—the shadows belong to those who match the masonry.

They are not many. Perhaps four, perhaps six; it is hard to tell because they rarely gather in the same chamber, and they do not keep fires to mark their presence. 

To call them men would be accurate in the way that calling the dwarf willow an orchard would be accurate: the blueprint is there, but the height and the juice have been taken out of them. They are short-coupled, broad-ribbed people whose chests have swelled to the shape of barrels to wring life from the thin, iron-flavored air. Their skin is neither pale nor dark, but a weathered, slate-drab gray, deeply cross-hatched with fine, dry cracks that look like the crustose lichen on the retaining wall. 

They do not wear woven wool or tailored leather. The cold here does not bite with the fury of a blizzard; it simply drains, hour by hour. To meet it, they wear heavy, stiffened wraps made from the felted down of the valley seeds they sweep from the alleys, beaten together with fine clay and greased with the fat of high-altitude marmots taken from the cliffs above the gneiss shoulders. The garments have no sleeves, only slits for the hands, and they are stiff enough to stand upright on the floor when stepped out of, like hollow basalt drums.

Their movement is the most remarkable thing about them: they have forgotten how to hurry. A stride down the two-shoulder alley takes the better part of a minute. They place their bare soles—calloused to the thickness of shoe-leather, the toes splayed wide and gray with stone-meal—flat against the polished river flags, rolling the weight from heel to ball with a rolling, liquid grace that makes no scrape. They step precisely into the slight depressions left by the builders, as if the path were a wooden track laid down for an axle.

They speak, but not in words that travel. The basalt dwellings, with their dry-stacked corbelled vaults, are natural acoustic traps; a loud shout would shatter against the stone and rattle out over the drop-off to die in the clouds. Instead, they communicate through a vocabulary of dull clicks, made by pressing the tongue against the back of the teeth, and a low, chest-resonant hum that travels through the bedrock rather than the air. A mother does not call to an elder; she sits on the sunken floor of the hearth-circle, rests her heels against the stone, and pulses a rhythm through her ribs. Thirty paces away, in the corner of another cell, the elder feels the vibration rise through the gravel backfill and into his tailbone, and he nods once in the dark.

Their life is an inventory of tiny adjustments. 

In the morning, when the clouds drag low and the stone begins to sweat, they emerge. They carry small, rectangular scoops made of thin, hammered copper—the very tools, perhaps, that the builders used to smooth the cistern edges, worn now to the thinness of a willow leaf. With these, they walk the rills. If an isopod has drifted into a dry groove, they nudge it back into the basin with the copper tip. If a patch of the wiry sedge has shed three yellow blades into a drainage notch, they lift them out with their fingernails and tuck them into their waistbands to be chewed later for their salty moisture.

They do not farm; the terrace cannot be plowed. They live on the wind’s harvest. In the leeward corners of the dwellings, where the dead insects collect in drift-lines like gray snow, they stoop and gather. They press the dried bodies into dense, flat cakes using flat stones kept in the hearths, binding them with the sweet, gelatinous film scraped from the north sides of the water basins. It is a slow, bitter food, tasting of dry chitin and granite dust, but it requires no fire to prepare, and it leaves no ash.

They have no names for the days, because the day here does not change its face. The sky stays pale, the shadows stay sharp, and the water stays three-quarters full. When one of them dies, there is no excavation—the bedrock would laugh at their copper chisels, and there is no earth to heap. 

Instead, the others lift the body and carry it to the drop-off at the southern point, where the mountain terrace ends in three thousand feet of clean, gray space. They do not drop it. They wedge the dead one into one of the narrow, vertical expansion joints left in the outer retaining wall, standing upright, facing inward toward the alleys. 

Over sixty or eighty years, the thin, dry air and the morning cloud-sweat do their work. The skin tightens against the bone; the hair silts away on the drafts; the flesh turns brown, then slate, then chalk-white, until the body is indistinguishable from the gneiss shoulders that frame the town. The joint becomes full. The wall remains plumb. 

At midday, when the sun stands directly overhead and the basalt shadows shrink to nothing beneath the walls, the inhabitants retreat into the corbelled rooms. They sit along the perimeter with their chins resting on their knees, their gray wraps drawn tight around them, their breath so slow that a spider might mistake their ankles for dressed stone. 

They do not sleep, exactly; they idle, like the water in the cisterns, waiting for the cold drop to fall from the roof, waiting for the cloud to rise from the valley, keeping the terrace exactly as it was when the last wedge was driven, neither growing nor diminishing, held fast in the dry jaws of the gneiss.
