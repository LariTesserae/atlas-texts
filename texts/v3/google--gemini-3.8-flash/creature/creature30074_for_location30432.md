In the dead center of the chamber, where the three largest hawsers hang closest together, the acoustic pressure forms an interference pattern—a narrow vertical spindle of relative cancellation, barely the width of a barrel, where the crushing C-sharp drops by a handful of decibels. 

Here, the suspended mist does not shiver into rings. It gathers into heavy, pendulous swags of web, woven from a silk that has incorporated the room’s ambient zinc and iron. The spiders that spin them are flat-bodied and the dull yellow of curdled cream, their legs tucked so tightly against their carapaces that they resemble the brass washers dropped by machinists into the sumps centuries ago. They do not spin to catch flying things—nothing flies through the shear of that low vibration without having its wings beaten to paste against the air. Instead, they spin to catch the *fall*.

Whenever the overhead crankshaft passes through a harmonic node, a shower of dead-weight matter—exhausted root-mites, flakes of calcified lime, droplets of uncombusted tallow that have leaked through the gland-packings above—slips out of the downdraft and settles into the web-funnels. The spiders do not move to claim it immediately. They wait for the galvanic clicks. When a spark arcs from the root hair to the conductive silk, the shock boils the moisture inside the fallen debris, bursting the hulls of the mites and rendering the grease into a thin, easily lapped broth. Two or three spiders will shift toward the blackened spot with a dry, clockwork hitch in their joints, dip their needle-like palps into the sizzling residue, and return to their flat, washer-like stillness before the charge has fully dissipated into the peat.

Below them, resting in the four-foot mat of rhizomes, is the chamber’s ballast.

It is difficult, at first, to distinguish it from the floor, because the rhizomes have grown up around its flanks like rising dough. It is a single, immense sow—or something whose ancestors were swine before the gallery was sealed—broad-backed as a brewer’s dray, its skin hairless, slick, and marbled with the grey-violet hue of wet slate. 

She has no ears to speak of. The auditory canals have long since calcified into smooth, ivory cones, completely sealed over by scar tissue and thick plugs of fibrous moss-wax to keep the unbroken drone from liquefying her inner ears. She does not need them; she hears through the flat broadness of her breastbone, which rests flush against the compressed root-pelt. Every cycle of the six-stage engine above travels directly from the iron casing, down the braided hawsers, through the damp peat, and into her ribs. Her own heartbeat has long since lost its mammalian autonomy; it has entrained. Her massive, sluggish heart contracts exactly once for every six strokes of the pump above. If the machine were to slip a bearing or throttle down for repairs, her blood would simply pool in her extremities, stalled by the sudden, terrifying lack of tempo.

Her breath comes out in slow, wet plumes that smell of sour fern and machine oil. She does not forage. She does not possess the legs for it anymore; her trotters have broadened into splayed, cartilage-heavy pads that have rooted her in place, the fibrous moss wicking into the thick horn of her hooves until she is physically moored to the chamber floor. 

She feeds by displacement. 

Because her bulk presses deep into the springy rhizomes, she creates a permanent, shallow depression—a sump within the sump. Into this low bowl drains the overflow of the weeping roots: lukewarm water dense with microbial sludge, grease-scum, and the stunned, drowned silverfish that slide down her sloping flanks. Every twenty minutes or so, her lower jaw—wide as a grain shovel, lined not with teeth but with corrugated ridges of black baleen—drops into the puddle with a soft, sucking sigh. Her tongue, immense and pale as a turnip, rolls forward to draw the slurry back against her gullet. She swallows without lifting her head.

Around her nostrils, where the moist air is warm and rich with carbon dioxide, clusters a species of blind, wingless hover-fly that never leaves the circle of her snout. Their life cycle lasts seven hours. They hatch from the mucous margins of her lips, mate in the downdraft created by her exhaled breath, lay their gelatinous egg-ribbons along the crevices of her snout-leather, and die, their light husks drawn back into her nostrils with the next inhalation to serve as protein for her lungs.

She is not alone in the moss-bed, though she is the only one of her scale. In the deeper trenches beneath her flanks, where the heat generated by her slow, fermenting digestion raises the root-pelt to nearly a hundred degrees, move the wire-worms. They are not true worms, but the blind, degenerate larvae of a beetle that has lost its hard shell entirely, existing now only as eight-inch cords of translucent, pinkish gristle. They burrow continuously through the root-weave, aerating the peat around her skin and eating her sloughed epidermis as it softens in the steam. In return, their tracks create channels that pull the stagnant water away from her belly, preventing the wet rot that would otherwise eat through her hide.

When the galvanic sparks jump above, she does not flinch. A faint, shuddering ripple passes through the fat beneath her shoulder-blades—a mechanical twitch that looks almost like pleasure. Her eyes, sunken beneath heavy, tallow-thick lids that have grown together with skin, never open. There is nothing to see anyway; the dull, fibrous noon through the sap-bladders never shifts, never casts a long shadow, never warns of night.

She is simply an organ of the room. The iron engine pounds above to drain the mines; the weeping moss drinks the engine's heat; the sow drinks the moss; the mites drink the sow; the spiders wait for the lightning to cook the mites. The entire knuckle of roots is an unbroken circuit of wet iron and warm flesh, singing its single C-sharp into the dark under the earth, perfectly balanced, utterly blind, and requiring nothing from the sky.
