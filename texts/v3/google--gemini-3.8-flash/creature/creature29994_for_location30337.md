To understand what lives here, one must first discard the notion of an inhabitant as something separate from the house it keeps. In this basin, the boundary between an organism’s hide and the pavement it rests upon is mostly a matter of time and degree of calcification.

At the literal root of the city are the **Luting-Annelids**, the prime masons. They are neither majestic nor solitary; an individual worm is little more than a translucent, mucus-sheathed thread twelve millimeters long, devoid of eyes, gills, or jaw-parts, ending in a muscular, shovel-shaped prostomium. Yet they exist in populations that defy arithmetic. They inhabit the interiors of the stone walls in interlocking capillary networks. Their entire existence is an uninterrupted digestion of the basin’s brine: they draw in the boiling, mineral-saturated fluids welling through the bedrock, pass them through a three-chambered gut lined with chemotrophic endosymbionts, and excrete a thick, self-curing grout composed of aragonite, ferrous silicate, and an ultra-dense proteinaceous glue that does not dissolve in salt water. 

They do not communicate through nervous systems as vertebrates understand them, but through hormone-wash. A fracture in an archway alters the hydraulic drag of the water passing through it; the change in shear stress triggers an immediate, localized release of sulfur-binding enzymes from the exposed worms. Within minutes, millions of adjacent worms orient toward the wound, vomiting fresh mortar into the breach until the turbulence calms. They are the city’s bones, its bricklayers, and its scar tissue. They have no concept of the ziggurats they construct; they know only the pressure on their flanks and the sour, iron taste of the upwelling.

Living upon and within the terraces created by the worms are the **Pellicle-Slugs**, or *Tasters*. These are flattened, footless gastropods the breadth of a human chest, resembling wet slabs of salted liver. They do not crawl; they flow, creeping over the walls at the pace of a growing fingernail, driven by coats of microscopic, undulating cilia on their undersides. Their dorsal skin is deeply quilted and mapped with thousands of chemosensory pits that register the chemical signatures of the masonry. They are the city’s grazers and its censors. As they slide over the tube-openings of the luting-worms, their mouths—soft, circular rasps—gently scour away the senescent worm-husks, suffocating fungal blooms, and stagnant silt. In doing so, they leave behind a trail of sweet, alkaline slime that acts as a directional scent-rail for other wanderers. A Pellicle-Slug rarely turns; it follows the lines of the masonry like a tram on a rail, circling a minaret over the course of three years before reaching the spire and descending along a sister-flute.

Down in the low avenues, where the water is thickest with settled organics and the floor yields like wet felt underfoot, dwell the **Hearth-Isopods**. These are the loaf-sized armored things, but up close they are far more complex than simple woodlice. Their carapaces are not chitin alone, but heavy, articulated plates encrusted with thick scales of iron sulfide, giving them the metallic, matte-black sheen of cast-iron stoves. They move with a mechanical, clicking deliberation on fourteen pairs of hooked, blunt legs, plowing through the fecal silt. Their bellies are hollowed into broad, vaulted incubatoriums where their young cling in the hundreds, feeding on the waxy secretions of the parent’s ventral plates. 

The Hearth-Isopods are furnace-tenders. They gather where the fissures leak water at temperatures that would blister human flesh. They wedge themselves directly into the vents, their heavy iron backs acting as baffles, deflecting the scalding jets horizontally into the wall-conduits to prevent the structural lime from thermal-cracking. In return, their bellies are warmed, and the dense, white pelts of filamentous sulfur-bacteria that sprout between their leg-joints flourish in the thermal draft. Every few hours, an isopod uses a set of specialized, comb-like maxillipeds to reap its own belly-fur, passing pellets of bacteria into its mouthparts. They are indifferent to the presence of anything else; if a tower collapses, they do not flee, but simply burrow into the rubble, waiting for the worms to re-cement the stones over their backs.

In the mid-water, moving through the fluted alleys and broad squares, are the larger mobile forms. The **Suede Cephalopods**—known, if they could name themselves, only by their electrical resonance—are the true citizenry of the corridors. They are octopods, but their ancestral form has been completely re-engineered by the pressure and the pitch-black. Their mantles are long and torpedo-like, clad in a skin that is thick, spongy, and deeply clefted into thousands of fine, velvety folds that absorb acoustic reflection; they make no echo when the fish sing against them. They have no eyes—in their place are twin, disc-like organs of electro-reception, pale and smooth as lard, ringed with delicate, vibrating vibrissae.

These creatures move with a ghostly, metronomic grace. Their eight arms are webbed to the tips with an elastic, muscular skirt, but the undersides of these arms are not lined with simple suckers; they bear sensitive, lip-like discs capable of tasting chemical variations down to a few parts per billion. As an octopod drifts through an alley, it trails two extended arms along the walls, reading the city like a blind man reading braille. It tastes the age of the stone, the health of the luting-worms, the transit-residue of a passing crab, the subtle increase in salinity that warns of a down-welling cold slip from the surface miles above. 

They are social in a manner that looks utterly motionless to outsiders. In the wider plazas, three or four will settle into a shared hollow in the floor, their arms intertwining loosely, forming a great, breathing knot of pale leather. Through their suckers and skin, they exchange faint galvanic pulses and complex chemical cocktails—transmitting maps of the basin, warnings of silt-slides, or simply the rhythmic, calming currents of their collective heartbeats. They carry objects: smooth, perfectly spherical nodules of pure pyrite that they roll obsessively between their suckers, polishing them over generations, trading them like tokens during these silent plaza-conclaves before moving on.

In the courtyards within the hollow towers, sheltered from the basin's gentle currents, drift the **Glass-Toroids**. These are shoals of small, sightless fish, their bodies entirely unpigmented and so transparent that the outline of their spinal columns and the pulsing of their amber gallbladders are visible through their flanks. They do not break formation. Thousands of them form slowly revolving, donut-shaped rings that spin endlessly in the dead water of the mid-tiers. 

They do not hunt; they are particulate-sifters. Their jaws are locked into wide, circular funnels lined with fine combs of bone. As the warm water rises through the floor-flues like smoke up a chimney, it carries micro-flakes of sloughed worm-mucus and bacterial crust; the Toroids drift through this gentle upwelling, their collective turning creating a subtle vortex that draws the nutrient-snow directly into their path. 

Their lateral lines are enormously exaggerated, running the length of their bodies like raised, nervous ridges. Through these, each fish feels not only its neighbors, but the stone walls forty feet away. The shoal acts as a single, liquid acoustic membrane. When a tremor runs through the basin, or a fissure shifts its yield, the Glass-Toroids compress instantly into a tight, dense sphere with a single, sharp sound—like the snap of dry twigs—that echoes through the conduits and alerts the entire district to the shift in pressure.

Higher up, where the fluted spires narrow toward the boundary-layer, life changes character. Here, the water begins to lose its blood-warm density; it shivers with the chill of the real abyss, a freezing, predatory frontier.

Anchored to the peaks of these pinnacles are the **Chandelier Polyps**. These are creatures of immense, quiet brutality. Their bases are calcified pedestals, fused directly into the ancient summits by centuries of mineral precipitation, thick as oak trunks. From these pedestals rise radial arrays of muscular, rope-like tentacles that can span thirty feet from tip to tip. They are totally white, covered in a thick, insulating coat of rubbery blubber that protects them from the cold plumes that swirl down from the ceiling of the basin.

The Polyps hang upside down or canted outward, entirely motionless for decades, their tentacles drifting up into the darkness like the rigging of sunken ships. They do not possess the rapid, stinging cnidae of shallow-water jellyfish. Instead, their ropes are coated in a resinous, honey-like slime that is insoluble, dense, and suffocating. Anything that falls from the cold world above—a spent whale-fall carcass, a wandering, half-frozen squid, a dead abyssal grenadier—is caught in the splay of these hanging cables. 

When prey touches a rope, there is no violent thrashing. The polyp does not jerk; it draws its limb inward with an agonizing, winch-like slowness, driven by the steady contraction of thick, non-striated muscle fibers. It can take twelve hours for a chandelier to bring an abyssal visitor to its central disc. There, three concentric rings of horn-hard, triangular plates—a black, beak-like mouth two feet across—slowly part to receive the mass. The polyps do not waste a crumb; what cannot be dissolved by their cavernous, multi-valved bellies is ground to a fine dust between their oral plates and discharged into the warm upwellings, where it drifts back down into the alleys as gray meal for the Glass-Toroids and the Hearth-Isopods below.

And threaded through all of this, in the micro-tunnels no wider than a pencil, are the **Strigils**—blind, wire-thin sea-spiders with ten segmented legs that can be twenty times the length of their bodies. They are the scavengers of the masonry's pores. They move with jerky, clockwork precision through the labyrinth of plumbing, stepping lightly over the retracted tips of the builder-worms. Their proboscises are long, flexible stylets that probe deep into the dead spaces between shells, sucking dry the bodies of expired worms, pulling out fragments of moldering shell, clearing the hydraulic pathways so the warm water may continue its journey unchecked.

Nothing here dreams of the sun. If light were introduced to this place, it would be an obscenity, an environmental disaster; it would burn the unshielded retinas of the octopods, blind the Glass-Toroids, and reveal only a stark, bone-colored Necropolis dripping with black grease and yellow slime. 

Yet in the dark, perceived through the exquisite sensitivity of pressure-waves, the warmth of upwellings, and the chemical narratives left on stone, it is a city of astonishing architectural coherence. It has no government, yet its services never fail; it has no commerce, yet every mouth receives the bounty of the earth's marrow. It grows by the millimeter, it repairs its own wounds, and it hums with the muffled, collective thrum of trillions of hearts beating inside the warm, salt-thick dark, each generation adding its own shell to the pavement upon which the next will walk.
