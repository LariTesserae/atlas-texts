To understand what lives here, one must first discard the concept of a face. 

In this place, a face is an unnecessary ornament. There are no brows to shade eyes that do not exist, no visual mimics, no markings of warning or courtship designed to catch a ray of dawn. The head of a creature here is simply an intake port surrounded by an array of instruments: vibrating whiskers fine as spun glass, wet pits that read the thermal drift off the monoliths to a thousandth of a degree, and wide, cartilaginous dishes tuned to the acoustic architecture of the vault.

### The Plume-Hides
The things that fly do not roost on branches, for there is no timber, only the hanging cables of root and the sheer, mineral-slick faces of the perimeter. They are broad-spanned—some spanning three meters from tip to tip—yet their bones are so thoroughly honeycombed that they weigh scarcely more than a handful of wet peat. 

Their bodies are encased in an extraordinary pelage: not feathers, but an oily, double-layered down that feels like cool lard to the touch. The inner layer traps the 40-degree fever of their blood; the outer layer causes the eternal condensation of the chamber to bead instantly and flick away with every flap of their wings. If their coat were to become waterlogged, the sheer weight of the mist would drag them into the sump below. 

They feed as baleen whales feed, but their sea is the pressurized air. As they carve through the void with slow, deliberate sweeps of their wings, their mouths hang wide—gaping hoops of soft horn lined with a sieve of sticky, hair-like cilia. They scoop up the drifting spores, the microscopic water-mites that breed in the cloud layer, and the shed skin of the wall-crawlers. 

When they sleep, they do not tuck their heads away. They hook their curved sternum-spurs directly into the fibrous hanging cables, hanging in clustered clusters like blackened bunches of grapes thirty meters above the abyss. When hundreds of them hang together, they breathe in a synchronized, rolling cadence. The sound of their collective lungs—a low, rhythmic, wet wheezing—acts as an acoustic beacon for those still navigating the empty middle air.

### The Seam-Clingers
Where the living granite meets the frictionless, ancient alloy, there is a narrow line of perpetual dispute. The stone is pitted and crusted with peat; the machine is smooth as frozen mercury, spitting water drops away in tiny, angry arcs. 

Living precisely along this seam are the gill-barkers. They are pale, elongated things, their bodies consisting mostly of a spine, four spatulate limbs, and a chest cage that houses enormous, resonant air-sacs. They have no claws—claws find no purchase on an alloy that repels matter at the atomic tier—but their palms and bellies are covered in millions of microscopic, vacuum-generating pores. They can run across the machine's curved flanks with a wet, sucking sound: *schlock-schlock-schlock*.

Their most striking feature is the crown of external gills that surrounds their blunt necks. These are not aquatic gills, but plumose lung-ferns that reach out into the slow, thermal drafts rising from the warm stone. The gills do not merely breathe; they taste. They can taste the presence of an injured crawler a hundred yards away by the faint trace of copper in the damp draft. They can taste the subtle, ionic discharge of the monoliths, steering toward the warm nodes where the metal hums loudest.

Their percussive barks are not territorial warnings; they are collective cartography. When a Seam-Clinger barks, it listens to the shape of the return. If the vault’s ceiling has shifted by a centimeter due to thermal expansion, the pitch of the decay tells it so. A young clinger learns the mountain’s interior as an unbroken song; to be deaf here would be equivalent to being dropped into concrete.

### The Cable-Weavers and the Sump-Pads
Far below, where the thirty-meter vines drop into the darkness, the world belongs to the slow-limbed.

There are creatures here that have forgotten what solid stone feels like. They spend generations entirely suspended, their lives played out along the vertical axis of the hanging creepers. In form, they resemble long, six-jointed reeds of pale muscle, their skins textured like the wet, abrasive rind of an orange. They do not possess eyes, ears, or teeth. Their entire existence is spent grazing the blind velvet that carpets the vines, peeling it away in long, neat ribbons with a rasping tongue made of mineralized silicate.

They move with a geological patience, taking an hour to haul themselves a single meter upward against the convective draft. Because they are practically weightless and moved only by touch, they have developed an uncanny sensitivity to the machine’s lingering pulse. When the monolith’s subterranean thrum shifts pitch—a tiny variation in the idling frequency that occurs once every few decades—the Cable-Weavers stiffen. They release their hold simultaneously, falling like heavy, wet ropes into the lower sump.

And in the sump, thirty meters down in the hot, dark soup of accumulated centuries, sits the consumer of all things.

It is not a beast with jaws. It is an expanse. 

The "broad fibrous pads" that ring with the impact of falling water are the upper lobes of an organism that has become the floor itself. It is a carpet of cellular flesh, hundreds of feet wide and meters thick, floating on the deep reservoir of warm, mineral-thick water trapped in the mountain’s belly. The pads are acoustic drumheads; every drop that hits them telegraphs its mass, its velocity, and what it might have carried down from the vault above. 

When a dead plume-hide falls from its roost, or when a cable-weaver drops during the harmonic shift, the pads do not smash them. They yield. The drumhead sinks into a deep, sucking pocket, folding its wet, velvet-lined margins over the carcass like a heavy quilt. The digestion is silent, cool, and odorless. Within hours, the flesh is rendered down into its constituent salts and pumped back up the sheer walls through the root-vines, feeding the blind velvet that will feed the crawlers that will shed the skin that feeds the flyers.

### The Mind of the Hollow
There is no "whoever" here in the sense of a wanderer or an architect. The builders of the monoliths have been gone so long that their bones have been dissolved by the alkaline moisture and incorporated into the carapaces of the crawlers. 

Yet, there is a consciousness of a different kind—a slow, diffuse cognition shared between the machine’s persistent field and the nervous tissue of the creatures that nest upon it. 

The Seam-Clingers, when they sleep pressed against the smooth, vibration-rich pylons, do not dream in images. Their nervous systems synchronize with the idling pulse of the ancient coils. If one were to sit beside them in the dark, one would feel it: the heartbeat of the amphibian matching the 12-cycle hum of the mountain's core, the rhythm of the bat-moth's wing-clicks keeping tempo with the condensation drip, the entire hollow breathing as a single, unseeing engine. 

Nothing here wonders what the sun is. The sun is an irrelevant rumor of a world that lacks pressure, lacks water, and lacks the eternal, cradling heat of the machine. The blackness is not an absence to them; it is a solid, warm, and crowded presence that touches every millimeter of their bodies, and they are utterly content to be held by it.
