To understand what lives here, one must begin with the blood.

In the veins of these people, the fluid is dark, thick as unboiled syrup, crowded with red cells that carry what little ghost of air the mountain yields. A stranger’s heart would hammer itself to exhaustion within an hour here, drowning in its own thin plasma, but the people born to the terraces have chests like beer tuns—their ribs bowed far outward to house lungs of immense surface area, their collarbones riding high and shelf-like under skin that rarely sweats. 

They are not tall. Height is an extravagance the cold does not permit. Their centers of gravity sit low, grounded in calves that are wide, knotty, and slightly bowed, tipped with broad feet whose toes remain perpetually splayed from gripping the irregular pitch of dressed diorite. They do not hurry. To break into a run on these stairs is considered either a sign of sudden madness or an omen of catastrophic collapse; movement is a steady, pendulum-like trudge, knees unhinging with a soft, habitual clicking, the torso leaning forward into the slope so that the weight of their carried loads—clumsy bundles of dried dung, cakes of tallow, slabs of unmortared slate—is borne not by the spine, but by broad hempen bands braced directly against the forehead.

Because the light is so unforgiving, their faces have become things of utility. The skin is thick, cross-hatched by the scouring of dry wind until it resembles the bark of dwarf willow, stained a deep, coppery mahogany by the unfiltered sun. Their eyes are slitted, hooded deeply under prominent, heavy brow-ridges that act as natural lintels against the sky’s glare; the whites are never white, but mapped with yellowing webs of broken capillaries and stained around the edges by grease mixed with the soot of dung-smoke, which they smear beneath their lids from infancy to soften the diamond blaze of midday.

They are an unnoisy race. Rage, laughter, and mourning are held behind the teeth; an open mouth loses heat and water, and water is the city’s quiet deity. Their language has evolved without open, ringing vowels. It is a tongue of the back palate—pharyngeal stops, low wet clicks, and clicks made with the tongue against the teeth, sounds that do not disturb the rhythm of the lungs. Two men meeting on a high causeway where the wind cuts like a skinning knife do not exchange greetings; they merely lean their foreheads together for a second, feeling the heat of the bone, or touch the coarse wool of each other’s shoulders with the backs of their knuckles before passing on.

Their domestic lives are turned entirely inward, buried deep into the mountainside. The front of a dwelling may face the blue abyss, but its heart is a sunken chamber chiseled into the living permafrost, insulated on four sides by the rubble-fill of the terraces. Here, the true animal architecture of the city reveals itself. 

The heavy, barrel-chested beasts that crowd the alleys—the *chal*—are not merely beasts of burden; they are stoves that breathe. They are ancient, stoic creatures, somewhere between a goat and a small ox, with thick, double-hinged knees that allow them to fold themselves down upon the cold flagstones like carpets. Their wool is never sheared clean; it is peeled in mats as it sheds, saturated with an amber-colored grease so foul-smelling and water-resistant that rain forms spherical beads upon their backs and rolls away without ever touching the hide. 

At night, when the sun drops behind the western razor-ridges and the air instantly solidifies into an iron chill, the family does not sleep in beds. They crawl into the low stalls behind their *chal*, sliding between the animals' massive, rumbling bellies and the rear terrace-wall. The beasts chew their cud through the dark hours with a slow, grinding wetness, their stomachs fermenting tough tussock-grass and moss-lichen, radiating a heavy, humid heat that smells of swamp and ammonia. A child born in the middle tiers will spend its first five winters tucked directly against the flank of a grandfather beast, its hair tangled with the animal’s grease, waking every morning with a faint ring of grey frost around its nostrils where its small breath hit the ceiling stone.

There is another, wilder citizenry that occupies the interstitial spaces of the masonry. The walls are not solid rock; they are skins of massive flagstones holding back millions of tons of fist-sized scree. Within that porous, loose-rock dark lives the *pith*—the short-eared, slate-grey rodents. They are communal, fat on the dust of ground grain and the dried leavings of the pens, moving through the terrace-drainage channels in silent, soft-footed rivers. They are neither hunted nor exterminated; the people view them as indicators of structural shift. If the *pith* come pouring out of the weep-holes in a grey, squeaking flood at dusk, it means the scree behind the third tier is shifting, or that ice has wedged a retaining cornerstone loose. When the rodents flee, the stone-masons arrive within the hour, carrying their green serpentine hammers and wedges of dried horn.

Above them all, the eagles hold the sky-line. These are not the proud, nervous raptors of the lower crags; they are heavy, pot-bellied things, white-chested and leaden-winged, dulled by generations of domesticity. They have the temperament of fat hounds. They sit on the protruding rafters of the upper granaries, their talons hooked into the weathered birch poles, watching the alleys with yellow, unblinking eyes. When a skittering *pith* breaks cover between the legs of a *chal*, an eagle does not dive with a theatrical shriek; it simply drops like a wet pelt, kills with a single dry snap of its beak behind the rodent’s ears, and hops clumsily back to its perch, shaking the frost from its crest.

The social order of this place is dictated entirely by water and stone. There are no priests, but there are the *Seepers*—men and women whose ears are trained from childhood to press against the raw basalt faces in the lower cellars, listening for the internal migration of the mountain's moisture. They can tell, by the hollow tone of a drip thirty feet within the shale, whether a cistern will fill by spring or whether a fissure has diverted the melt-run into an unreachable fault. The Seepers hold the keys to the brass pegs that plug the drainage flutes; they measure out the grey, mineral-heavy liquid in shallow stone ladles, five mouthfuls per adult per shift of the sun, salt-heavy and tasting of crushed slate.

Work is continuous, but it has the tempo of moss growing. The stone-dressers work without iron, using balls of dense, green river-stone brought up centuries ago from the drowned lowlands. They strike the grey diorite ten thousand times to make a single straight edge, their wrists swollen and thickened with calluses that look like horn. The dust from this tapping settles over everything; it greases the stairways, gets under the fingernails of infants, and turns the spittle of the old men into a pale, lime-colored paste. The masons are easily spotted: they are deaf in their left ears from the ringing fracture of the rock, and their thumbs are flattened, broad as spade-tips.

Death here is dry and clean. The mountain will not permit the digging of graves—the permafrost resists the maul, and wood for pyres has not been seen for a thousand generations. When an elder ceases to breathe behind the flank of their beast, the family strips the body of its felt pins and its bone toggles. The corpse is carried not down, but up, through the narrow, stepped lanes where the beasts pull back to let the bearers pass, out through the highest breach in the uppermost wall where the masonry simply runs out into the raw scree.

There, in the clean, dry wind that tastes of nothing at all, the body is laid out upon a flat table of basalt. It does not rot. The air is too parched for corruption; the flesh simply hardens, darkening from brown to the color of dried plum, shrinking tight against the barrel of the ribs until the ribs look like the wicker frames of their carrying baskets. The pale eagles do some of the work, but they are well-fed and lazy; mostly, it is the frost that works them, splintering the dry tissue into dust, and the wind that carries the dust out over the white sea of clouds below.

Those who remain do not look up at the summits often. The peaks are considered unfinished, rude, and dangerous—the work of an earth that has not yet learned how to lie flat. Their gaze is always on the stones underfoot, watching for the hairline crack in the flag, the moist dark line that signals a leaking cistern, or the broad, flat hoof-prints of their beasts, frozen into the perennial mud of the shade, remaining there unchanged while generations pass above them without a sound.
