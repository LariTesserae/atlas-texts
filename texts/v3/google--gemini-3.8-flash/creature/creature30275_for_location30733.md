Where the floorboards warp upward into their shallow A-frames, they create dark, hollow triangular runs beneath the timbers. In these interstitial crawlways live the things that keep the courtyard’s dry starch in motion. 

Chief among them are the *sieve-beetles*—though they are beetles only by courtesy of their gait. They are the width of a cooper’s palm, their bodies flat as shingles, shaped from overlapping scales of yellowed horn that have been stitched to a leather under-carapace with waxed flax. They possess no mouthparts designed for chewing; instead, their faces are blunt, perforated copper-colored grilles through which they inhale the floury cedar silt. They do not digest it. As they scuttle beneath the boards, their dry, rattling bellies act as bellows, expelling the dust back through lateral vents in a steady, superfine plume that keeps the floor-joints from binding. Where a sieve-beetle has paused, the dust sits in exquisite, ridged sand-dunes no taller than a fingernail, vibrating gently to the rhythm of its internal dry clockwork.

Above them, grazing upon the creeping blotting-paper ivy, move the yard’s ruminants: low, heavy-bodied beasts no taller than a calf, known in the language of the joints as *trestles*. 

A trestle has no skin; its exterior is a dense, rhythmic lattice of steamed ash laths that have been curved while green and lashed together at every intersection with strips of split willow. Through the gaps in this wicker rib-cage, one can see that the creature has no organs of red meat. Its interior is a hollow cavity filled with tightly packed, carded lamb’s wool, through which a single thick cord of twisted hemp runs from forehead to rump like a spinal bowstring. 

The trestle moves with a stiff, high-stepping gait on four unjointed pegs of boxwood shod in hard deer-hoof. It does not eat the ivy for moisture, which does not exist, but for the tension stored in the plant’s running stitches. As the trestle crops a leaf, the sound is like a seamstress snapping a thread between her incisors—a crisp, rhythmic *snip-tock*. The harvested fiber is wound directly around the animal’s central cord by a rotating whorl of bone housed inside its chest. As it grazes, the bowstring tightens, and the beast grows lighter on its feet, its footsteps becoming almost soundless until, fully wound, it can leap sideways across the five-angle corner, passing from the floor to the western wall without breaking stride, its hooves gripping the vertical split-shingles with a soft, dry clatter.

When two trestles meet upon a narrow balustrade, they do not fight. They press their flat forehead-boards together and stand immobile for hours, their internal spindles clicking faintly as they balance their respective tensions, transferring wraps of thread from one to the other through interlocking bone gears behind their teeth until both are equally strung.

The ones who tied the cords—the makers whose calloused thumbs left whorls in the slip-clay—are not gone. They have simply settled into the logic of their work.

They are three or four in number, though because they occupy several planes of the courtyard at once, an observer might count seven or eight. One of them is currently seated upon a low three-legged stool that is pegged directly into the hazel ceiling rafters; he hangs upside-down to the floor below, though his heavy linen skirts hang downward toward *his* feet, draping over his knees like boards of pressed felt.

These are the *Joiners*. To look at their flesh is to understand what happens to a human body when the last drop of moisture has been gently replaced by cedar silt and unspun tow. Their skin is the color of bleached ash-wood, extremely clean, mapped with fine, dry cracks like the crazing on old slip-ware. They do not blink, for their eyes have no tear-ducts; the eye is a spherical button of polished horn set into a ring of soft mole-velvet that sweeps the surface clear of chalk-dust whenever the brow furrows.

When a Joiner moves, the sound is entirely of the workshop: the dry, whispering slide of hemp through a drilled hole, the muted *thunk* of a dry dowel settling into an auger-bite. 

Their fingers are unusually long, the knuckles reinforced by splints of shaved willow bound with pale thread. They carry their tools not in belts, but embedded in the meat of their forearms, where slots have been worked between the sinews: bone awls, flat-headed wooden mallets, and scrapers made of split flint, each handle polished by decades of dry contact until the grain of the horn and the grain of the skin are indistinguishable.

They do not speak with lungs. To produce voice, a Joiner rubs the flat edge of an adze-scraped fingernail against the ribbed edge of his linen cuffs, producing a quick, whistling chatter that sounds like a swallow darting through a dry barn. Across thirty paces of timber, another Joiner—standing vertically on the north partition wall, one foot wedged between two wicker panels—answers with three sharp taps of an awl-haft against a hazel rafter. The vibrations do not travel through the air; they pass through the dry, taut skeleton of the yard itself, felt by the sole of the foot as a tiny, resonant pulse.

Their work is never repair, for nothing here rots, and nothing wears down through rust. Their work is the *governance of the warp*.

A timber left in this warm, starchy air does not stay inert; it listens to the hemp that binds it. Over weeks, a rafter will draw itself toward its twin, compelled by the five-angled logic of the center post, until the space between them closes by a sixteenth of an inch. If left untended, the courtyard would slowly fold its own walls inward, like an envelope being creased by invisible fingers, until the Hazel ceiling and the planked floor met in a single flat seam of pine.

To prevent this, the Joiners spend their centuries whittling wedge-pins. 

One of them sits now by the drift of flour-silt. He holds a short length of white pine between his bare, split-soled feet. His flint knife does not peel curls from the wood; it separates the fibers with a steady, dry hiss that smells intensely of resin and summer sun. He does not hurry. A martin of stitched calfskin hops from a balustrade and lands on his wrist, its blunt bone beak resting against his thumb. The Joiner does not shoo it; he works around it, his flint blade passing within a thread’s-breadth of the bird’s belly-seam. The bird remains motionless, save for its head, which clicks ninety degrees to the left to watch a flight of wool-moths flutter from the pith-rushes.

When the wedge is finished, the Joiner will not drive it home with a hammer’s blow. He will place it gently into the joint between two rafters. Then he will take a long strand of hemp, splice it into the creeping ivy that roots in the wall, and lead the plant’s dry, paper-leaved tip to the wedge. Within three days, the ivy will sew the wedge into the wood, wrapping it in thousands of tiny running stitches until the timber sighs—a sound like a chest of drawers closing in an empty house—and the space between ceiling and floor remains held, held open, held dry, thirty feet and an eternity apart.
