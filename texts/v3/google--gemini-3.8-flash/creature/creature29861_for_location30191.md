To understand them, one has to begin with the heat, which is not an ambient property of the mountain but something they manufacture and defend with their lives. Their individual body temperature hovers near the edge of biological failure—somewhere above a bird’s fever, running so high that to hold one against the cheek would feel like pressing against a boiled egg.

Individually, they are neither strictly swifts nor bats, but something that has solved the high-altitude problem by stealing the virtues of both and paring away the luxuries. They are the size of an outstretched hand, but astonishingly heavy for their volume, dense with pectoral muscle that anchors not to a thin keel, but to a broad, dish-shaped breastbone packed with dark, iron-black meat. Their skin is unusually thick, slate-grey, and lubricated by a clear, tallowy sebum that smells faintly of mutton suet and sour grass. The covering over this skin is ambiguous: close to the flesh it is a woolly, down-like fur, but toward the tips it stiffens into split, vane-like bristles that lay flat in overlapping scales, creating a waterproof, wind-breaking carapace through which cold cannot find a seam. 

Their faces are blunt, almost crushed, dominated by two large, bulbous nostrils rimmed with fleshy, erectile valves that can seal against the ice-needles of the outer gales. Their eyes are small, cloudy beads, milky with protective films; in this darkness they have little use for sight, though they possess a curious, faint sensitivity to the blue end of the light spectrum that tells them when the slit far above has begun to turn from black to the grey of dawn. Beneath the flattened nose, their mouths are wide, curved clefts lined with double rows of serrated, comb-like denticles—not true teeth, but hardened calcified projections of the jawbone used to crack the stony seed-cases of dwarf pine and alpine sedge that they glean from the lower valleys miles to the south.

They do not sleep as solitary creatures do. To be an individual among them is to cool down, and to cool down in this chimney is to die. On the rounded ceiling and down the polished belly of the vault, they hang in a living mosaic—a *shingling* that may be four bodies deep at the center and taper to two at the edges. The innermost tier clutches the rock with four-jointed digits tipped with curved, needle-sharp claws of blue horn; the next tier clutches the backs of the first, burying their blunt snouts into the oily fur of their fellows’ necks, and so on, until the outermost surface is an undulating, breathing crust of backs and folded wings. 

This mass is never still. A slow, peristaltic circulation runs through it constantly. The ones on the outside, chilled by the falling condensation and the faint draft from the throat above, work their way inward, claw over shoulder, squeezing into the humid crevices between their pack-mates. The ones buried deepest, threatened with suffocation or heat-stroke in the 110-degree heart of the clump, loosen their grip and shoulder their way out toward the cooler periphery. The dry, rustling clap that occasionally fills the vault is this convection in action—a hundred bodies shifting position at once, their flight-membranes, which are thick as oiled kidskin and edged with short, stiff quills, snapping against each other like leather apron-strings.

Their young are born—or hatched, for the shells are soft and leathery, discarded and devoured within minutes of emergence—in the dead heart of these clusters during the height of the summer melt. A newborn is an obscene, translucent bag of yellow fat, naked except for a ridge of stiff bristles along the spine, with enormous grasping feet that seem entirely out of proportion to its limp, squirming body. It does not fall because it is pinned between three or four adults who share its incubation promiscuously. The mothers feed them not on raw forage, but on a thick, semi-solid curd regurgitated from deep secondary crops: a rich emulsion of crushed valley-seeds, beetle-grease, and mineral salt scraped from the exposed seams of the mountain’s roots. Under this diet, the young double their weight every four days, swelling into tight, oily sausages of concentrated caloric reserve. By autumn, before their primary feathers have even fully cleared their sheaths, their bodies are so charged with lipids that ancient hunters could have dried them, inserted a rush through the mouth into the gut, and burned them like tallow candles.

Beneath this roof of fever, the cave has generated an under-world that exists solely to process what falls from the ceiling. 

The mulch floor is alive to a depth of several feet. It is not cold dirt; it is warm, slightly spongy, and gives off a steady, alkaline heat from the fermentation of guano, seed-husks, and molt-scurf. Here, the dominant life is a specialized race of dermestid beetles the color of scorched horn, slightly larger than a thumb-joint, with broad, flattened carapaces adapted for burrowing through the damp bone-meal. They move with a mechanical, rustling urgency. When a fledgling loses its grip on the vibrating mass above and plummets into the dark, it does not lie there long. The impact barely registers before the mulch beneath it begins to boil. Within ten minutes, several hundred beetles have anchored themselves to the skin; by morning, the soft, fat-slick corpse has been reduced to a fragile wickerwork of cartilage and quill-stems, licked clean of every trace of suet.

Moving above the mulch, on long, hair-thin legs that lift them clear of the beetle-swarms, are the cave-crickets. They are entirely white, their skin so thin that the dark pulp of their digestive tracts shows clearly through their abdomens. Their antennae, twice the length of their bodies, whip through the humid dark in perpetual, nervous arcs, tasting the chemical drift of the air. They do not scavenge meat; they pasture on the fringes of the liver-colored cyanobacterial mats that slick the dripping wall-troughs, grazing on the greasy slime that forms where the ammonia vapors condense against the cooler slate. They are skittish, hypersensitive to barometric shifts; hours before an exterior blizzard begins to howl across the ridge above, the crickets abandon the lower floors and retreat into narrow, dry fissures, their antennae pressed flat against the stone to wait out the vibration of the peaks.

In the driest corners—where the vertical draft of the chimney creates dead-air pockets, such as the cleft holding the ancient horn-and-pebble artifact—the arachnids keep watch. They are pale, small-bodied weavers with spindly, six-inch spans, their legs marked by faint, translucent amber bands. They do not make webs designed to arrest flight; any such structure would be instantly shredded by the flyers. Instead, they drop single, dry guidelines across the corners of the stone—taut tripwires anchored with drops of brown resin. They wait on these lines for the wingless parasites that drop from the birds: bloated, flattened hippoboscid flies that crawl through the slate pelts like crabs and occasionally tumble off when their hosts shake themselves. When a parasite strikes a tripwire, the spider descends not with a rush, but with an eerie, measured stepping, its long tarsi feeling the vibration, before wrapping the fallen pest in dry, dusty silk and carrying it back into the seam behind the ibex horn.

The flock’s relationship to the world outside is one of violent, daily exile and desperate return. 

At the first blue glimmer that penetrates the chimney’s throat, a change runs through the vault. The chittering takes on a harder, more rhythmic tempo, rising in pitch until it sounds like water boiling under immense pressure. Then the drop begins. They do not fly up into the cleft; the air in the chimney is too hot and thin to provide lift for their heavy, muscle-bound bodies. Instead, they peel themselves from the ceiling in small knots, plummet down through the humid vault, and, catching the powerful upward draft of the thermal chimney just above the floor, shoot vertically toward the light like stones from a sling.

They breach the narrow razor-slit into the sub-zero gale with a wet, whip-like crack of wings, their nostrils snapping shut, their pelt-bristles flattening instantly against their skins. Outside, in an atmosphere that would freeze human spit before it hit the ground, they are completely transformed. The clumsy, shifting shingles of the cave become dark, broad-winged darts, slicing through the thin blue ether with an ease that seems impossible for things so heavy. They drop through the freezing void in long, steep parabolas, diving thousands of feet down the mountain’s shoulder toward the green timberline and the cloud-meadows where the seeds are ripe and the low-altitude air is thick enough to breathe.

All day the cave is left to the beetles, the crickets, and the quiet condensation dripping into the mulch. The temperature drops five, perhaps eight degrees, but the deep heat stored in the stone and the fermenting floor holds the perimeter against the frost. 

Then, as the alpine sun strikes the western ridge and the mountain shadow turns purple, they come back. They arrive not as an army, but as a succession of exhausted, solitary bolts falling out of the freezing sky, folding their wings at the very lip of the slit and tumbling into the darkness to be caught by the warm updraft. Their hides are rimed with hoarfrost; their bills are dusted with yellow mountain-dust and the crushed husks of seeds hauled up from the timberline. They hit the greasy, polished stone of the dome with a heavy, wet *slap*, their claws finding the grooved rock instantly. 

For a few minutes, each returning flyer shivers violently—a deep, muscular seizure that burns the last of the valley-sugars to generate an immediate envelope of heat. Then they begin the crawl. They shoulder their way into the nearest cluster, pushing their frozen snouts into the warm, oily armpits of their neighbors, murmuring in that deep, bone-vibrating register that has not ceased in this chapel of rock for five thousand years, adding their handful of warmth back to the mountain's only hearth.
