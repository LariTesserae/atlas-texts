They call themselves the *Oilers*, though in the municipal registers fifty levels above—where the names are still entered in calfskin ledgers by clerks who have never seen a walking-beam—they are listed under the heading: *Department of Pumping and Auxiliary Traction, Sub-Vault Service (Perpetual).*

No one down here has heard that title in eighty years. Down here, you are either an Oiler, a Fire-tender, or one of the *Swaddled*.

The Oilers are small people. Three generations of seventy-four-degree steam, atmospheric pressure three atmospheres above datum, and the endless necessity of slipping between moving three-ton iron counterweights have bred out height. A man five feet tall is considered dangerously long-limbed; he will lose an elbow or a shoulder-blade to the governor-linkage before he turns thirty. Their collarbones are thick and set high, their ribs barrel-shaped to haul the wet, greasy air into lungs that are perpetually slightly inflamed—a condition they call *the brass-rot*, though it rarely kills them. It merely produces a rhythmic, barking cough that fits neatly into the off-beat of the No. 3 pump’s exhaust stroke.

They do not wear clothes of woven wool or cotton; damp heat and paraffin rot normal cloth within six weeks. Instead, they dress in garments cut from the inner bast of the pale-heart willow itself. Stripped from the living beams during the spring surge (when the ground-level thaws force an extra thousand gallons an hour through the sump), the bark is beaten with lead mallets against the cylinder casings until the fibers separate into a tough, dun-colored felt that smells faintly of wintergreen. It is stiff when dry, but down here it is never dry; it stays limp, cool, and slick as an eel’s flank, protecting their bellies from the red-hot mica inspection ports and the dripping cylinder-grease that would scald bare skin.

Their feet are their most important tools. They do not wear boots. Leather turns to jelly in the sump; wood clogs slip on the vascular cambium of the deck. An Oiler’s foot is nearly square, with widely splayed, prehensile toes that can hook into the grooves worn into the living floor-timber. The skin on their soles is yellow, calloused two inches thick, and entirely insensible to hot peat-cinders or the sharp, shed carapaces of the boiler-ticks. A veteran Oiler can run full tilt across sixty yards of wet cork-bark in total darkness, his toes reading the grain of the roots beneath him like print, knowing precisely when he is ten paces from the edge of a flywheel pit by the greasy vibration tingling in his arch.

Because the noise inside the vault is an iron wall that never falls, the Oilers have no spoken grammar. They have voices—high, whistling grunts used exclusively to frighten the marmoset swarms away from open gear-trains—but among themselves they speak *stroke-talk*. 

It is an intensely physical sign language built from the movements of the walking-beams. A conversation looks like a sequence of stylized convulsions: a snap of the wrist for the trip-valve; a flat, downward push of the palm for low-pressure steam; an undulating shimmy of the ribs that means *the packing on Dynamo Seven is burning dry*. For long-distance communication across the seventy-pace voids between engines, they use their birch cudgels, striking the cold-water return mains in staccato bursts. The sound bypasses the air entirely, traveling through the iron marrow of the plant to be felt through the palms of every worker touching an engine frame across three acres of vault.

They sleep high. The floor belongs to the huntsmen and the whip-tailed voles, who will chew through the ear-lobes or fingers of a motionless sleeper before the shift-bell rings. Suspended forty feet up in the groin of the willow arches are the *hammocks*—woven cradles of wild osier hung by iron turnbuckles directly over the main cylinder heads. It is the warmest, most suffocating part of the vault, but it is safe. The air up there is eighty-five degrees, heavy with the sweet, narcotic stench of boiling willow-cambium and lard-vapor. A man sleeps naked in his basket, his skin glistening like an oiled plum, lulled by the three-foot rise and fall of the beam engine rocking beneath him, breathing in rhythm with the forty-stroke pulse that has governed his family since the city above forgot why the pumps were cast.

There are women among them, though an outsider would find them difficult to distinguish. Their hair is shaved to the scalp to prevent it being caught in the high-speed governor belts, and their breasts are strapped flat with willow-bark bindings to leave their arms free for swinging between catwalks. They give birth in the dry pockets behind Boiler Four—the *Oven-Beds*—where the heat keeps the newborn infants from catching the lung-chill that follows the sudden five-second downpours. 

The infants are strange, pale grubs. They spend their first two years strapped to their mothers’ backs in willow-fiber scabbards, their tiny fists instinctively clutching the cudgel-loops, their ears already growing the thick, fibrous flaps of skin—an overgrowth of the external meatus—that down here serves as natural ear-plugs against the dynamo-shriek.

Diet is a matter of municipal calculation that has curdled into biology. Once a week, on Tuesday mornings, the *Weight* comes down.

The Weight is an iron box, six feet on a side, that descends through an unlined brick shaft forty yards north of the central switchboard. It hangs from a three-inch braided wire cable that vanishes into the wet blackness of the roof. The Board of Works above sends down coarse grey salt, bars of yellow lye soap, kegs of pickled ox-hearts, and kegs of lard so old it has turned the color of axle-grease. In return, the Oilers must fill the box with the only currency the Upper City recognizes: *condensate wax*.

This is the heavy, amber-colored paraffin tallow that the Oilers scrape from the willow-calluses where the rocker arms enter the wood. It is prized above ground for street-lamps; it burns without soot, smelling intensely of swamp-willows and old iron. If the wax is light, the next week’s salt is halved.

To supplement the salt-meat, they farm the machinery. 

The *boiler-ticks* are their cattle. An adult tick, gorged on pipe-lagging and mineral scale, can be pried from a steam line with a flat crowbar. The Oilers drop them directly onto the hot cylinder-tops; the tick cooks within its own iron shell, bubbling in its fat until the ventral plates pop like chestnuts. The meat inside is grey, sweet, and tastes strongly of sulfur and tallow, but it keeps a man’s teeth from loosening in his gums. 

The blind swifts, too, are harvested. The boys—wiry children of seven or eight whose bones have not yet set—climb into the dynamo housings while the copper rotors are spinning, reaching into the air-gaps with long split-cane tongs to pluck the birds from their nests of mud and saliva. The swift-flesh is stringy and rank with midges, but their eggs, gathered by the bushel from behind the commutator brackets, are whipped with condensed willow-sap to make a thick, yellow paste that the fire-tenders smear on their salt-beef.

And then there are the *Old Ones*—the tenders who have grown too stiff to dodge the beams. 

Down here, a man does not retire; he becomes *lignified*. When an Oiler’s knees swell with the steam-dropsy until he can no longer climb the osier ladders, he descends to the floor-bays, the wide empty spaces of cork-bark where the air is still and the dynamos sound like distant surf. He smears his limbs with the thick, black leakage of the willow-sleeves to keep the voles from tasting him, and he becomes a *Bark-Tender*. 

He carries a curved knife and a wooden mallet. His single duty is to prevent the willow from completely burying the iron. 

He sits for eighteen hours at a stretch before a pillow-block or a valve-chest, paring away the creeping cambium, cutting back the pale, fleshy suckers that reach out every night toward the oil-cups. It is a slow, rhythmic war. The tree wants the iron; the iron needs the tree’s resin to spin without seizing. The old man sits in the red ember-light of the mica windows, half-blind, stone-deaf, his back leaning against the vibration of seventy tons of reciprocating metal, slicing off ribbons of wet, white wood that fall into his lap like noodles. 

Eventually, usually during the heavy autumn rains when the vault floor swells three inches and the sap runs thick and black as molasses, an old tender stops cutting. He does not fall over. He simply remains seated against the bearing housing, his feet planted in the cambium, his birch cudgel across his knees. 

Within forty-eight hours, the white filaments find him. The pale-heart willow does not distinguish between cast iron and human gristle; both are porous, both hold mineral salts, both are stationary. The roots run through the felt of his bast-apron, thread themselves between his splayed toes, and enter the soft meat behind his knees. By the time the next Tuesday Weight drops from the ceiling, he is no longer an Oiler; he is an irregular swelling in the floor-timber, a grey mound of living bark that pulses faintly forty times a minute, precisely in time with the stroke of the low-pressure pump, helping to grease the bearing he spent sixty years keeping clear.
