They do not take off the ivory until they are inside the amber gut-skin of the huts, and even then, they do not take it off all at once.

The eyes of the people on the shelf are ruined things—not blind, but scarred around the rims with pink salt-proud flesh, the sclera stained the yellow of curdled lard by the perpetual rebound of glare. When the ivory strips come down, their lids stay narrowed to wet slits. To open them fully in that space would be like drinking straight lye. They see the world as a trembling, over-silvered blueprint: the darker masses of whale-ribs, the grey-white blur of moving bodies, and the sharp, hot streaks where brine shoots through the chink of a plank.

Because their throats are perpetually coated in the aerosolized mineral wash, their voices have almost entirely degenerated. A mature adult does not possess a register for song or shout; they have only a wet, rasping drawl, like a wet boot drawn out of deep muck, used exclusively for the three or four gutter-clicks that indicate structural failure—a severed stay-rope, a cracking slate, a drowning child who cannot reach a cleat. All else is carried by the mitts. The mitts are stiffened with tallow and whale-glue, stitched with split sinew, and tipped with small, flat studs of river-pebble. When they gesture, they do not merely shape signs in the air; they strike them against their own ribs, their leather-clad thighs, or the wet posts beside them. The conversation of three women mending a siphon-trap sounds like dry beans shaken in an inflated bladder: *clack-tick, pad, clack*.

Under the sealskin tunics, their bodies are built for a specific, desperate economy. They have no waistline to speak of; their bellies and chests are thick, barrel-like, and heavily padded with hard, yellowing visceral fat fed by a diet of raw, oil-rich organ meats. Their legs are bowed from generations of bracing against vibrating, slippery wood, the toes permanently curled, thick-nailed, and prehensile enough to grip the notched poles while both hands are busy with the harpoon or the gouge. They never wash the grease from their skin; to wash would be suicide. Instead, they scrape it off once a cycle with curved ribs of mountain-hares, shedding a thick, grey curd of accumulated grit and dead skin, then immediately re-anoint themselves with the warm, sour grease rendered from the sifter-beasts. They smell of cold fish-meal, ammonia, and ancient stone.

They sleep in clumps. The huts have no floor space for spreading out; they are merely round, hanging baskets lashed to the sheer face, lined with four or five layers of dried hides that have been softened by chewing until they are limp as flannel. A family sleeps wedged together in a tangle of thick, grease-stiff hair and heavy limbs, their bodies generating a collective furnace-heat that turns the interior into a dripping, tallow-scented oven. The outermost sleeper always keeps their ivory slit-guard hung around their neck by a leather thong, because waking here is instantaneous: a single shift in the tension of the suspension gut can mean the basket is dropping into the sluice.

They eat with their teeth directly against the carcass. Cooking is an abstraction they know only through legends of the low-country; heat here is purely metabolic. When a lung-eel is hauled flapping from the froth, it is pinned against a hemlock balk with an elbow, split from throat to tail with a ground-slate blade, and its dark, blood-heavy livers are eaten while the muscle fibers are still rippling in the salt spray. The meat is dense, chewy, and tastes intensely of iron and sea-cabbage. Children, whose teeth are small and sharp as bone needles, are given the warm fatty tail-knots to chew on until their jaws ache, their mouths permanently stained a bruised purple by the blood.

Sharing their quarters are the fist-birds—the things the climbers call *skirrs*. 

They are not pets; they are tolerated vermin and temporary stoves. The skirrs have no sense of territory, only an unquenchable, manic hunger that matches the shelf’s kinetic roar. Their body temperature is astonishingly high—touching one through its dense coat of greasy pig-bristles is like touching a warm loaf fresh from the baker’s trough. They will nest anywhere: in the hood of a child’s tunic, between the folded ribs of an unworked hide, or inside the carcass of an eel hung from the rafters. The people tuck them into their sleeves during long watches on the lower cradles, using the frantic, vibrating heat of the bird’s chest to keep their fingers from deadening into gray frost. The skirrs do not seem to mind the confinement; they merely peck viciously at the leather, their hard, triangular beaks drumming a tiny, ceaseless tattoo, until they are hurled back out into the mist to dive for more grease-skin fry.

Then there are the carcasses that drop.

The siphon-holes overhead do not merely spout water; they are the outlets for some vast, drowned drainage network that honeycombs the upper ice-caps. At irregular intervals, the grey torrent chokes, turns thick with curdled slush, and belches out the things that live in the dark lakes above. 

These are the white things: great, blind, paddle-tailed pigs of the ice-melt, six or eight cubits long, with no eyes, no pigment, and skin as thick and soft as boiled lard. When one shoots from the tube, it drops through the spray like a slaughtered calf, tumbling end-over-end toward the lower cauldrons. The hunt is not an ambush; it is an aerial collision. Men and women leap from the swaying platforms on long, looped gut-tethers, their harpoons weighted with lead ballast, driving the iron-spiked points into the creature's blubber in mid-fall. 

The tether snaps tight with a dull, leaden *thwack* that shakes the frame of three tiers of huts. The hunter and the beast swing together through the blinding halo of the waterfall, drenched in mercury-colored spray, before being hauled up by four or five haulers working a wet capstan made of fossil wood. The beast does not fight with teeth—it has only toothless, horn-lipped gristle for sieving blind cave-crustaceans—but it thrashes with the terrifying, blunt weight of five hundred stones of cold fat. If it strikes a walkway, the walkway goes. If it pins a hunter against the fluted scarp, the hunter’s ribs are turned to flour inside their greasy tunic.

Death on the shelf produces no ceremonies. A dead human is treated with the same dry, practical efficiency as a spent rib-timber. 

The air is too wet and salted for rot; it mummifies or it mineralizes. If a man dies of the chest-rattle—the drowning-cough that takes them when the salt-lime builds up in the lungs—his companions do not cast him into the chasm. The chasm belongs to the torrent, and the torrent is waste. They strip the tallow from his thighs to grease the gut-lines; they salvage his ivory; they use the broad, flat plate of his pelvic bone as a shovel-blade for the lime-scrape. His clothes are soaked in grease, beaten with river-pebbles until soft, and passed to a girl whose wrists have outgrown her cuffs.

What remains of him is laid into the runnels behind the huts, where the mineral water sheets thinnest. Within three moons, the extreme chemical load of the water has done its work: the bones are encased in a pale, sparkling crust of calcium spar and calcite, welded to the limestone cliff until they are simply another rib in the amphitheater's architecture, another spur of stone to lash a cradle to.

They live without yesterday and without tomorrow. Their world is too bright for memory and too fast for hope. A hunter standing on the outer rail at noon—shielded behind bone, her lungs burning with brine, her fingers trembling with the ceaseless, high-speed vibration of the timbers beneath her feet—sees only the circle: the sun split into six blinding suns by the spray, the white water boiling into yellow scum, the black shapes of her kin fluttering their mittened hands against the slate, and the endless, furious rain of fat and meat falling from the sky into their waiting, grease-smeared jaws.
