To understand the life here, one must begin with the sole of the foot.

The six-legged grazers—the low-slung, broad-barrelled things that browse the ribbon-weed—do not walk upon the stone so much as they seal themselves to it. Their pads are broad as washbasins, rimmed with thick skirts of calloused muscle that secrete a pale, saponified lard. When a foot comes down on a cold shelf of pig iron, this grease displaces the beadlets of oily dew, creating an acoustic bond so total that the beast does not hear the mountain’s engine through its ears, but through the marrow of its three pairs of legs. 

They are named for no recorded lineage, but their bodies have the dull, permanent honesty of livestock that have never seen a barn. Their hides are hairless, folded like oiled harness-leather, the color of wet slate with patches of mottled liver-pink where the galvanic currents of the rock have raised heat-blisters in the subcutaneous fat. They have no necks to speak of; the great wedge of the head runs straight into the humped shoulder. Where eyes would be smashed by shifting gravel or blinded by the absolute dark, there is only the dish: a wide, shallow basin of naked cartilage, dead white and mapped with concentric rings of sensory pits that twitch like the gills of a stranded fish.

With this dish, they smell the electrical breath of the lodes. They know when a lead-vein half a mile down is being pinched by the counter-tilt of a basalt rocker, discharging a faint, sour spark of static that tastes, across the thin air, like tin foil pressed to a filling.

They move in trios or small, mute clusters, their movement timed to the rockers. They do not feed constantly. Instead, they stand for hours in a catalepsy so deep that their blood slows to a cold, sluggish syrup, saving their heat while the great granite beams rise through their silent arcs. But the moment the bedrock gives that soft, underwater *thrum*—the telltale shudder of a rocker settling its millions of tons into its shale socket—the herd wakes. The slackening of the stone opens microscopic fault lines across the shelf. With spade-shaped incisors capped in thick, self-sharpening enamel that smells of iron filings, they plunge their faces into the freshly widened cracks, tearing loose the bitter, tuberous roots of the ribbon-weed before the rock can settle shut again and shear off their muzzles.

Their calves are born small, leathery, and wet, dropped directly into the icy slush of the condensation-troughs. A newborn cannot stand for several hours; its limbs are soft, like unvulcanized rubber. The mother straddles it, lowering her massive, grease-slicked belly over the calf until her glandular warmth softens the tallow-crust on the stone beneath them. She feeds it not milk, but an amber, petroleum-thick fluid regurgitated from her first stomach—a distillation of mineral grease, chewed moss-fibres, and iron-salts that coats the young one’s gut with a heavy, protective rind.

Above them, in the wool-hung gulfs, life has taken another shape entirely: not of ballast, but of surface area.

The gliding mantles that traverse the voids are almost entirely rib and skin. Their bodies are no thicker than a man’s wrist, but from their flanks stretch vast, ribbed awnings of black, corrugated dermis that can measure forty feet from tip to tip. This membrane is not bat-leather or feather; it is dry, thin as parchment, and textured with fine, microscopic grooves that channel the condensating mist toward the creature’s mouthparts through capillary action alone. They never drink from the ground; they drink the sky’s grease as it settles on their wings.

To maintain flight in an atmosphere so thin it barely resists the hand, they rely on the hot sighs of the rift-valves. They drift in long, looping spirals through the indigo dark, suspended over miles of empty air, waiting for the mountain to exhale. When a deep valve breathes out its greasy, heated plume, the mantles fold their wing-spars slightly, tucking into the rising thermal with a faint, hollow whistle of air across their ribs. 

Their heads are narrow, long, and toothless, terminating in a hard, prehensile beak of black horn used to pry into the joints of the rock. They do not attack the six-legged grazers; indeed, they have no weapons for violence. Their relationship with the herds is one of quiet, methodical scavenging. 

When a mantle drops from the void onto a high shelf, it does not land gracefully. It stalls its enormous black awnings against the thin air and falls the last few feet with a soft, sodden *whump*, its wings collapsing inward like an umbrella dropped in the mud. Dragging itself forward on the elbow-joints of its wing-spars—where blunt, hook-like thumbs are tipped with cold-tolerant cartilage—it creeps toward the resting herd-beasts. 

The grazers do not stir. They allow the mantle to clamber over their flanks, its parchment wings rustling like dry reeds against their oily hides. The mantle is searching for the ticks.

These ticks are not the quick, spindly parasites of the lowlands. They are heavy, calcified hemipterans the size of winter apples, their shells the dull, greasy blue of gunmetal. They do not burrow into the skin; they drill into the seams where the grazers’ subcutaneous grease-glands vent to the surface, cementing their horn-rimmed mouthparts directly into the duct. Once anchored, they swell on the animal's thick, cold fat until their carapaces are strained to translucence, revealing the dark, tallowy slurry churning within.

The mantle hooks its curved beak beneath the rim of a tick’s shell, braces its thumbs against the grazer’s ribs, and levers the parasite free with a wet, sucking snap. It swallows the tick whole. A single grazing beast may carry thirty or forty of these fat-bulbs in the warm folds behind its middle legs, and a mantle will spend an entire shift of the mountain’s rockers picking a host clean, its long, blind neck darting rhythmically into the folds of flesh while the great grazer breathes its slow, grease-fouled breath into the rime.

And then there are the things that belong neither to the shelf nor to the air, but to the engine’s throat.

Down in the fissures, where the granite rocker-stems meet their iron sockets, the condensation does not remain cold. It runs down the basalt faces, gathers the tallow-weeps, and is superheated by the friction of the stone-strokes into a perpetual, boiling froth that pools in the lower sumps.

Here, in the absolute black of the vents, live the siphon-worms. They grow in dense, tubular thickets that sheath the iron fulcrums like coarse black hair. Each worm is a sleeve of mineralized horn, three to six feet long, anchored to the metal by a root-foot of pure, chemically precipitated lead. Inside the tube is an animal that is little more than an oscillating pump. It has no brain, no eyes, and no limbs; it is a muscular cylinder that expands and contracts in inverted rhythm with the granite rockers overhead.

When the rocker lifts, the vacuum pulls the warm, petroleum-laden sump-water up through the thicket. The siphon-worms open their feathery, cinnabar-red branchiae, filtering the droplets of grease and the iron-reducing microbes that thrive in the scald. When the rocker drops and the enormous pressure blows the exhaust vapor back toward the rim, the worms snap their tubes shut with a dry, rhythmic clacking like millions of tiny dice shaken in a cup. 

The sound of the siphon-beds—that vast, dry, subterranean rattling that precedes every warm plume from the vents—is the only true voice the mountain has. The herds know it; the mantles know it. When the rattling rises from the clefts, the gliders turn their heads into the void, the grazers brace their three-paired legs against the stone, and the ticks tighten their grip on the tallow-ducts, all of them leaning together into the hot, greasy breath that keeps the ice from taking the world.

Nothing here dies of old age in the way lowland creatures do. Their metabolisms are too slow, their tissues too thoroughly pickled in lead-salts and petroleum-paraffins for simple decay. Instead, they run out of clearance. 

Over decades, a grazer’s joints become encrusted with the iron that precipitates from its food. Its broad, sensitive footpads stiffen into horn; its gristle-dish thickens and ossifies until it can no longer read the static hum of the lead-veins. One day, when the rocker swings through its thirty-foot arc, the beast misjudges the stroke. It does not lean into the pulse; it is a fraction of a second slow. The granite counterweight, descending with the indifferent weight of five thousand tons of basalt, catches the beast in its seat.

There is no scream. There is only the wet, heavy sound of fat and lead-veined bone giving way, followed by the deep, resonant *thrum* as the rock bottoms out in the shale.

Within an hour, the moss has begun to reach for the grease that oozes from beneath the stone. The ribbon-weed puts down fresh taproots through the marrow. The mantles gather at the edge of the crushed area, their black sails rustling, waiting for the rocker to lift again in forty hours so they may pick the cold tallow from the impressions left in the pig iron. Nothing is wasted, because the engine does not stop, and the things that live upon it have no other place to fall.
