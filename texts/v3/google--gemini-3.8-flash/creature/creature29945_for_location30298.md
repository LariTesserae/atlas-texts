To find the makers of the slate enclosures, one must listen not to the air, which carries nothing, but to the floor.

The basalt beneath this world is an unbroken acoustic web. Miles of jointed hexagonal pillars act like a system of organ pipes pressed horn-to-horn, vibrating with the collective weight of everything that crawls across them. And among those vibrations—amid the dragging scrape of the shield-bugs and the high, needle-thin ticking of the stilt-legged predators—there is a strike that is heavy, rhythmic, and deliberate: the blow of an ironstone against a lever.

The beings who hold those stones are not tall. Height is a liability where a fall onto a sheared fault-line will shear a body in two. They are broad, low-slung, and three-sided in their basic symmetry, moving with their bellies suspended barely three fingers above the razor shards. 

They have no soft skin. Their exterior is a thick, overlapping armor of mineralized plate—neither bone nor shell, but a dense, layered composite of horn and compacted slate-dust that has the matte, oily sheen of wet graphite, though it is parched to the core. Their limbs are four, arranged in two opposing pairs that work like the arms of an anchor: short, massively muscled with dry, corded sinew, ending not in fingers or claws, but in broad, cleft pads of horn that are hard enough to crack shale, yet pliable enough to deform over a knife-edge without splitting. Where the pads strike the stone, they leave white, chalky bruises.

Above this chassis rests a wedge-shaped head that lacks any opening for breath. Because there is no water to lose and no combustion of fuel requiring oxygen, they do not inhale. They do not possess lungs; their interiors are cool, dense, and cellularly close to the dry pith of petrified wood. 

Where eyes would be, their skulls widen into broad, polished plates of hollow bone, ribbed like fans. These are tympanic shields. When a hunter reaches an intersection of basalt columns, it lowers this plate until the bone touches the rock, locking its skeleton directly into the mineral strata. Through this contact, the void ceases to be dark. To the hunter, the basalt forest rings like a cathedral: it feels the slow, shearing drift of a herd of shield-bugs four miles distant as a low, grumbling hum; it registers the sudden, spring-legged strike of a harvestman as a sharp, glassine chime; it senses the dead weight of the stacked-slate stations as dull, dampened nodes in the ringing earth.

Their tool-use is an extension of this acoustic sense. They do not build enclosures to shelter from weather, for there is no weather. They build them to manipulate the vibrations of the floor.

The low walls of stacked slate are acoustic blinds. By arranging shingles of differing thicknesses and varying angles of fracture, they create pockets of silence where the deafening hum of the deeper crust is filtered out, leaving only the delicate, high-frequency scritch of tendons and claws nearby. Sitting inside one of these dry-stone rings, a hunter is practically invisible to the sensory bristles of the harvestmen, who hunt by feeling the displacement of stillness.

Inside the blinds, they wait. Their waiting is not the patience of a cat or an ambush bug; it is an inert state that mimics the permanence of the surrounding rock. A hunter will wedging its pads into the cracks of a slate shelf and cease all motion for thirty, fifty, or eighty cycles of the shield-bugs’ slow creep. Its internal chemistry drops into a crystalline stasis; its joints set like mortar. Only the tympanic plate remains alive, singing faintly with the news of the floor.

When the herd finally draws near—the shield-bugs moving with the agonizing slowness of mineral stalagmites, scraping the sulfur blooms from the fault walls—the hunters move with shocking, mechanical leverage. 

They do not strike with fangs or venom. They use the ironstone wedges.

Working in pairs, they drive a chisel-shaped nodule of hematite under the rim of a shield-bug’s carapace with a single, two-handed blow from a stone maul. The sound is an explosive *clack* that shivers through the rock for leagues. Before the shield-bug can press itself flat against the basalt, the second hunter inserts a flat pry-bar of dense volcanic glass, putting its entire, squat mass onto the lever. The great bug is popped from the stone like an oyster from its shell, exposing its pale, fibrous underbelly to the dry air.

There is no blood to spill. The creature’s fluids are thick, resinous pastes that immediately skin over and harden in the parched heat. The hunters scrape this gray, mineralized fat into flat slate dishes. It is not eaten with teeth—they have no jaws—but pressed into an opening beneath the chest-shield, where a set of internal grinding stones, turned by powerful muscular contractions, slowly mill the salt-cake, the dried muscle, and the sulfur-crust into a fine, energetic flour.

Nothing is discarded. The great shield of the prey is levered away to become the lintel of a new blind, or knapped into wide, flat plates to patch an older wall. The long, dry dorsal cords—taut as bowstrings—are stripped with glass flakes, soaked in the resinous paste to keep them from shattering, and braided into the thick, rigid hawsers that span the chasms.

These ropeways are their roads. Where the basalt drops away into sheer vertical rifts forty cubits deep, these braided tendon-lines hang taut across the blackness, anchored to massive stone bollards they have hammered into the fault-lines. To cross, they do not swing or climb; they possess specialized notches along the inner curve of their horn-pads that clip onto the cord, allowing them to haul themselves across the void upside-down, their carapaces hanging over the silent abyss, moving with the dry, rhythmic clatter of shuttles through a loom.

Intelligence, in them, has no literature, no songs, and no curiosity about the height of the black ceiling they will never touch. Their memory is engraved directly upon the floor.

Where an individual has knapped black glass for ten generations, the slate beneath its resting place is worn smooth—an extraordinary thing in a world where nothing is ever round. In these places, one can see the true nature of their thought. Around the tool-making stations, they use the hard hematite nodules to incise geometric patterns into the floor: vast, intersecting grids of lines, pecked cubit by cubit into the rock. 

These are not maps of geography, for geography does not change here. They are maps of pitch. A line cut three inches deep across a basalt prism changes the tone that prism produces when struck; a series of notches along a step-fault deadens the ringing of an approaching predator or focuses the sound of a distant salt-bloom. They are tuning the world. They are carving the endless, razor-sharp floor until the entire oceanic hollow hums with a language only their breast-shields can interpret.

When one of them reaches the end of its structural integrity—when its horn-pads have split beyond repair against the flint-edges, or its internal grinding stones have worn smooth as glass—it does not lie down to be mourned. It walks to the nearest dry-stone enclosure, wedges itself between the stacked shingles to reinforce the wall, and pulls its four limbs inward against its plastron. 

There, in the unchanging warmth, it simply ceases to mill its stones. 

Within a few years, the air has pulled the last fraction of volatile oils from its joints. The tendons that held its limbs together dry into rock-hard horn, then crack. The next hunter to use the blind will reach out with its ironstone maul, casually strike away the brittle plates that have fallen into the interior, and stack the heavier fragments of its ancestor's shell into the wall, wedging them between the razor slates to keep out the ringing of the deep.
