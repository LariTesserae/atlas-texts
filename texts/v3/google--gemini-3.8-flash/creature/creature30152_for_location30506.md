The light does not allow for hiding, so nothing here has developed the machinery for concealment; instead, they have perfected transparency and geometry. 

The amphipods mentioned in the rock’s margins are not alone, though their solitary, measured crawling gives that illusion. They are only the most obvious gear in an ecology that measures itself not in seasons, but in the twenty-minute hydraulic inhalation and exhalation of the stone.

Look closer at the glass-clear grazer. It is roughly the length of an almond, but infinitely narrower. Its carapacial plates are not chitin as surface creatures know it, but a composite matrix: a hydrated chitin scaffold deeply infiltrated with amorphous, biogenic opal, cross-linked with aluminum ions scavenged from the spodumene. Because the light in the atrium is perfectly flat and shadowless, the amphipod is nearly invisible until it shifts against a cleavage plane. When it does, its internal anatomy reveals itself as an optical trick: its digestive tract is a thread of dense, chartreuse-gray paste—concentrated archaeal sulfur—coiled tightly beneath an unpigmented heart that beats with a rapid, mechanical fluttering, eighty times a minute. 

Its needle-legs do not terminate in soft pads. They end in single, atomically sharp dactyls composed of pure, sclerotized silica, tipped with traces of tungsten and iron drawn from the deep pore-water. These tips do not slip on the polished volcanic glass. Instead, they find the sub-microscopic sub-lattice dislocations in the obsidian, wedging into the crystal structure with microscopic purchase. As the animal walks, the contact of silicate against silicate produces a very faint, dry clicking, like the hands of a dozen pocket watches spinning out of control, entirely drowned out by the hiss of steam.

It has no eyes; where optic lobes would be in a surface crustacean, this creature possesses two paired, bulbous organules of high-density calcified lithium salts suspended in a viscous oil. These are not photoreceptors, but piezostimulated statocysts. They do not see the laser-bright white radiance; they *hear* it. They register the terahertz oscillation of the surrounding walls as a localized geography of hums and notes. The creature steers toward the hottest, loudest fracture zones, where the microbial felt is thickest and the stone rings most fiercely under the weight of the mantle.

Below the amphipods, clinging to the submerged margins of the rills where the boiling brine runs shallowest, is the film they crop. It is not a passive slime. 

The biofilm is composed of a consortium of rod-shaped *Euryarchaeota* interwoven with filamentous chemolithoautotrophs that extrude hollow tubes of pure elemental sulfur. Under the coherent white light, the mat behaves like an optical fiber bundle. The filaments grow parallel to the direction of the light waves, channeling the photon flood downward into the interstitial depths of the colony. The archaea do not possess chlorophyll—they do not photosynthesize in any solar sense—yet they exploit the light through a crude, direct thermal photophoresis: the intense light keeps the tops of their sulfur tubes at ninety-two degrees Celsius, while their anchored bases against the cold spodumene sit at eighty-four. Across this eight-degree drop, over a distance no thicker than a single sheet of paper, the colony generates a persistent electrochemical gradient. They are powered by the drop in temperature between the light and the dark rock underneath.

Where the rills spill out and widen across the dark pavement, the water thins to a meniscus only a few cells deep. In these shallows lives something that moves even less than the grazers: the *calices*.

To a casual glance, they resemble the discarded droplet rings left by evaporating lithium. They are circular, sessile discs, roughly the diameter of a coin, cemented flat against the pavement. They are colonial hydrozoan-analogues, flattened until they possess no vertical dimension greater than a fraction of a millimeter. Their bodies are discs of living glass—flexible, proteinaceous membranes stretched over a radial skeleton of flexible lithium-carbonate needles. 

The *calices* do not graze. They are passive filter-crawlers, anchored to the glass floor by chemical etching. Around their perimeters, invisible under the surgical glare, are hundreds of gossamer cilia that whip the steaming film of brine over their dorsal surfaces. They feed on the free-floating archaea and the shed exuviae of the amphipods, drawing them into a central, microscopic pore that dilates and constricts with the chamber’s pressure shifts. 

When the twenty-minute drought arrives—when the capillary weeping halts and the moisture flashes instantly into dry air—the *calices* do not die. They undergo an instantaneous, glass-like vitrification. They allow the lithium salts in the dying water film to crust over their backs, forming an airtight, crystalline shroud. For three minutes, while the chamber turns stark and dry as an oven, they sit as inert mineral rings, indistinguishable from the salt around them. 

Then comes the hydraulic shudder. 

Deep in the northern boundary, the rock groans—a low, seismic thrum that rattles through the floor, instantly deadening the high-frequency terahertz hum. The amphipods stop scraping instantly. They drop flat, driving their needle-legs into the micro-fissures of the obsidian, locking their joints with a physical snap and flattening their streamlined, wedge-shaped carapaces against the stone to present zero profile to the flow.

A heartbeat later, the brine explodes through the micropores. It does not trickle; it erupts in a frothing, scalding surge, flashing instantly into boiling froth as the pressure drops. The rills fill to overbrimming; waves of ninety-eight-degree capillary water wash across the central pavement. 

Under the impact of this fresh, mineral-dense pulse, the lithium crusts on the *calices* dissolve in seconds. The discs snap back to life, their cilia beating furiously in the turbulence, catching the rush of fresh microbial sludge, organic sulfur, and deep-mantle ions dredged up from aquifers miles deeper.

And in this brief window of liquid violence, something else appears from the fracture knife-shards. 

From the narrow, millimeter-wide fissures at the bases of the upright rhyolite spires, thin, ribbon-like forms emerge against the current. They are unsegmented, pale as milk-quartz, and no wider than a heavy thread, but they can be nearly two feet long. They are nemertean-like vermiforms—predators that move not by crawling, but by peristaltic swimming through the rushing sheets of brine, undulating like strips of clear plastic caught in a gutter. 

These worms have cuticles made entirely of a self-lubricating, cross-linked fluoropolymer slime that prevents the razor-sharp rhyolite edges from slicing them in two. As they glide across the floor, they hunt by chemical scent, tracking the plume of sulfur-chitin waste trailing from the amphipods. 

When a worm reaches a locked-down amphipod, it does not strike with jaws. It wraps its ribbon-body around the crustacean’s joints, extruding a super-basic, caustic digestive fluid that dissolves the biogenic opal binding the amphipod's carapace. It is a slow, methodical dissolution carried out in the blinding, shadowless white light, bathed in the hiss of steam, while the boiling water rushes over them both.

Within four minutes, the surge eases. The brine stops erupting. The water thins back to sheets, then to ribbons, then to a vanishing sheen. The ribbons of worms withdraw backward into the knife-cracks, their bellies clouded with digested silica and paste, leaving only empty, paper-thin fragments of dissolved crustacean armor to be swept away or cemented into the salt rings.

The humidity climbs toward ninety percent. The vault overhead begins its dense, rhythmic condensation. The drops gather, swell, and drop through the coherent light—*tink, tink, tink*—striking the dark stone floor with surgical precision. 

The amphipods that survived begin to scrape again, their glass limbs ticking against the obsidian, grazing their way down the newly-wetted rills toward the next pulse of the mantle.
