The water makes everything heavy, so the things that move here have evolved a peculiar density.

At the lowest tier of the ecology, where the basalt meets the synthetic underpinnings of the city terraces, life begins in the dark. The conduit-heaps look, at first, like discarded lengths of wet braided rope left beside the warm outfalls. They are *skimmers*—not true lungfish, not quite amphibians, but long, pale, ribless things whose skin is an unpigmented, translucent leather through which their slow, yellow blood can be seen pumping against the warmth of the steel. They have no eyes; in the saturated dimness of the lower channels, vision is an expensive redundancy. Instead, their broad, shovel-shaped snouts are covered in tiny electro-receptive pits that register the hum of the gravitational conduits. When a vent exhales a plume of warm, mineralized moisture, they do not swim so much as roll over one another, a frictionless knot of thirty or forty bodies vying for the center of the heat-gradient, their bellies pressed tight to the vibration of the conduits until the metal cools, at which point they unspool into the sheet-wash with a wet, heavy slap.

They feed on what the water carries off the stone: the sloughed flakes of the black lichen, and the oily curds of *silt-yeast* that grow wherever the synthetic composites of the city sweat their plasticizers into the puddles.

Higher up, where the sheet-water runs only ankle-deep over the vast, uncracked plains, the herds move.

Seen from a distance through the rolling stratus, the grazers—the *pelt-kine*, as the sparse terrace-dwellers call them—look less like mammals and more like dark, slow-moving islands of saturated moss. Their bodies are barrel-shaped and low to the ground to shed the high-altitude winds that peel off the edge of the shelf. Their fur does not grow in strands; it is a dense, felted mat saturated with an amber tallow so thick that rainwater never touches their skin. It runs off their humped shoulders in distinct, braided gutters, dripping from the points of their long guard-hairs in a steady percussion that accompanies their walking.

They have three-toed hooves, split deeply and lined on the under-cushion with a coarse, abrasive cartilage that bites into the polished surface of the obsidian like wet pumice. When they run—which is rare, usually triggered only when a thermal shift cracks a slab of stone with the sound of a cannon shot—their hooves make a rhythmic, hollow suction, like boots pulling out of clay.

The pelt-kine do not chew rock; their teeth are broad, flat plates of self-sharpening enamel that work against one another in a lateral grinding motion, mowing down the pale, tough biofilms that coat the city’s exhaust grates. A herd will linger over a single geothermal outlet for three weeks, their broad, moist muzzles pressed against the ironwork, inhaling the sulfur-sweet vapor while their young huddle beneath their bellies, out of the rain. The calves are born nearly hairless, coated in a thick, gray fetal wax that takes a month to dissolve under the continuous weeping of the sky.

Hunting them is almost impossible for an earthbound predator because there is no cover on the glass flats; thus, the hunters come from the margins of the air.

The drift-vultures—though they are raptors only by convergence—are shaped like heavy, blunt planks. Their wingspans exceed five meters, but they have almost no muscle for flapping; the air at four thousand meters is thin, but the massive heat-plumes rising from the city-roots create violent, reliable vertical rivers of warm air. A drift-vulture will launch itself from the lip of a three-hundred-meter residential drop and simply hang, tilted ten degrees into the mist, drifting for days without a single downstroke. Their feathers are not soft; they are lacquered, rigid vanes that click together like slats of thin wood when they bank.

They do not hunt the great kine. Instead, their eyes—large, orange globes set deep within heavy bone visors to shield them from the blinding glare when the clouds rip open—are fixed on the structural expansion joints of the terrace-cities. There, in the dry, fibrous glass wool that seals the seams between composite decks, live the *felt-mice*. The mice are tiny, aggressive scavengers that steal the shed tallow of the kine to line their nests, living in colonies of thousands within the warm skeletons of the buildings. A drift-vulture hunts by diving down the narrow, canyon-like alleys between the empty residential monoliths, dropping its heavy, iron-tipped talons into the seams with the precision of a chisel, ripping out a yard of insulation and the thirty shivering, pink bodies tangled within it before catching the next updraft back to the high needles.

And between these things, moving through the fog with an unhurried, almost mineral cadence, are the people.

There are not many of them. They do not occupy the cities so much as accompany them.

To an outsider, they would appear disproportionately tall, their frames elongated by generations spent under the subtle, upward tug of the gravitational anchor-spires that punctuate the plateau. Their skin has the gray-white quality of peeled willow bark, untouched by direct sun except during those twenty-minute intervals when the cloud-ceiling shatters; when that happens, they do not squint or shield their faces, but stand perfectly still, turning their palms and throats toward the searing white iron of the sky, drinking the radiation through skin that has developed an extraordinarily high melanin-response, darkening to a bruised purple in the span of an hour and fading just as quickly when the damp grey returns.

They wear garments made of hammered tallow-wool and woven glass-fiber taken from the maintenance depots, slicked with grease so the water runs off them as it does from the basalt. Their faces are dominated by wide, membrane-shielded nostrils that warm and humidify the freezing air before it hits the lungs. They rarely speak aloud. In an environment where sound is either deadened by the fog or carried unnaturally far along the flat sheet-wash, speech is crude and indiscreet. They use a system of soft, glottal clicks—almost sub-vocal—and a complex sign-language that involves the flexing of the forearm tendons, which can be read by touch in the total darkness of the internal transit tunnels.

These inhabitants live mostly within the synthetic mountain-roots, where the air smells of ozone, warm wet stone, and the slightly sweet, enzymatic smell of the water-clearing filters. They are not builders; the cities were cast in whole, crystalline blocks before their ancestors arrived, intended for populations that either never arrived or have long since been translated elsewhere. Instead, the present dwellers are a race of caretakers whose duties have slipped into the realm of biological function.

They do not maintain the machines so much as clear them of life. A family’s daily route might involve walking ten kilometers of cantilevered terrace to scrape the black liverwort from an intake cowl with copper knives, or clearing the tangled, semi-conscious carcasses of skimmers from an exhaust vent so the subterranean turbine doesn't stall. They catch the pelt-kine only when an old bull falls into a drainage trench, using bone-handled winches to hoist the enormous, tallow-soaked carcass from the water, wasting nothing: the tallow becomes their oil, the hide their roofing, the flat enamel teeth their scraping blades.

They have no art that lasts longer than a puddle. Their primary cultural expression is the carving of *run-channels*—delicate, mathematically precise grooves cut into the surface of the basalt with hardened diamonds. When the sheet-wash flows over these grooves, the water is diverted into microscopic diversions that create distinct, chordal tones, turning acres of empty, rain-swept stone into vast, hydraulic instruments that sing only when the clouds are lowest and the water is deep.

A traveler along the high terraces might hear it from miles away: not the sound of a voice, nor the sound of iron, but a low, crystalline chord rising out of the wet floor, sustained for hours without variation, until the wind shifts, the moisture thins, and the plateau returns to the silence of stone waiting to become old.
