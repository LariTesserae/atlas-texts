The hands that laid the herringbone floor were never meant to open flat. 

They belong to the quarry-turners—things no larger than the dried husk of a bee, though with none of a bee’s soft fuzz or flight. Seen pressed against the slick black of the slate, one appears at first like an escaped drop of molten solder that has cooled into five or six overlapping scale-plates. They are entirely flat, built to slide into the shear-planes of slate without displacing a single droplet of the heavy, viscous water. 

They have no faces to speak of. Where an eye or a jaw might be, there is only a beveled front edge, hard as a chipped ruby and smelling acutely of iron filing. Beneath this edge emerge their working limbs: three pairs of segmented needles made of a pale, bone-like enamel that does not soften in water. These are not legs for scurrying; there is no scurrying in this thicket, where the water resists like warm suet. They walk by wedge and lever, driving their needle-tips into the microscopic grain of the rock and drawing their dense, coin-thin bodies forward with a slow, hydraulic shiver.

To watch a quarry-turner lay a tile is to understand that, to them, masonry is not an art of shelter, but of *truing*. 

They do not live inside the archway they have built; they are not sheltered by it. Rather, the seam between the two slabs of slate is an open wound in the quarry’s geometry—a place where the stone has parted without permission—and the turners are frantically, quietly rectifying it. They harvest bits of slate-dust, silt, and their own chalky, bitter saliva to press and fire those tooth-sized tiles, curing them under the sheer hydrostatic pressure until they ring like porcelain. They lay them down because the slate beneath is uneven. They cannot abide the raggedness of the natural fracture. 

When the white, glass-hard roots of the liverwort force a tile upward, the turners do not panic. Two or three of them will gather around the split. They do not have voices, but they communicate through that dry, microscopic clicking—their enamel knees tapping rhythmic stress-patterns into the stone floor, listening to the return echo with the sensitive plates on their undersides. Then, with an awful, unblinking patience, they will begin to saw through the root with their front edges, working for four weeks until the vegetable fiber snaps with a sound like a broken violin string, upon which they immediately scrape the area clean and bed another tile in its place.

Yet they are not alone in the seam. 

Further in, where the sideways gravity tugs the horned thicket into a horizontal lawn, live the measure-keepers. These are the larger forms of the pale, watch-spring worms, though here they have ceased to uncoil. They have wedged themselves into the intersections where three or four rigid hornwort stems cross, wrapping their pale, lard-like bodies in tight, greasy knots around the joints. They are virtually inert. A measure-keeper takes thirty winters to complete a single turn around a stem. Their skin is semi-translucent; if one of the curdled luminescence-spots drifts near, you can see the dense, black iron-grit moving down their straight digestive tracts with the glacial pace of a creeping glacier. 

They serve as living plumb-bobs for the cleft. The sideways pull of the south-facing slab acts upon their heavy, mineral-filled guts, causing them to sag at a very specific angle. By brushing against these taut, suspended worms, the turners know precisely how far the space has leaned—whether the great weight of the slate mountain above them has shifted by a fraction of a micron.

And there is something deeper still, residing in the recursive pocket where the cleft should have terminated against solid rock, but didn't.

If you were to push your fingers past the glass thorns, past the three-inch archway with its precarious keystone, your hand would enter a zone where the water is no longer merely dense, but entirely motionless, held in a kind of hydraulic gel. In this depth floats the Litigant.

It is only vaguely organic—perhaps six inches in length, shaped like a long, tapered needle of dull lead, completely limbless, hovering vertically against the horizontal lean of the plants. It does not touch the walls; it does not touch the moss. It stays suspended in the center of the impossible throat by the balance of pressures alone. Along its gray flanks run dozens of tiny, recessed pores, each one exuding a slow, amber grease that smells overwhelmingly of wintergreen and bruised mint. 

The Litigant is the reason the arch was built. The archway is a gauge. 

Every season, the capillary creep of the water carries the Litigant perhaps a hair’s breadth closer to the herringbone floor. It has no eyes, no fins, no mouth, but its leaden surface is scored from end to end with the same crisp, chisel-cut grooves found on the keystone of the portal. It is growing, but it does not grow by cellular division; it grows by mineral accretion, drawing iron and lead out of the dead reliquaries and discarded quarry chains far above, plating itself layer by microscopic layer, becoming heavier, longer, and colder.

The turners know it is coming. They are paving the floor to receive it. 

When the Litigant finally arrives at the three-inch arch—whether in ten years or two hundred—its tapered tip will enter the opening precisely. If the keystone holds, if the tiles beneath have been leveled without a single error of a thousandth of a millimeter, the leaden thing will seat into the masonry like a plug into a barrel. The fracture will be pinned. The sideways pull will equalize. The impossible pocket of space will seal its lips, locking the turners, the mint-smelling grease, the glass-brittle liverwort, and the white worms into a single, permanent, airtight fossil under the drowning dark of the quarry.

Until then, they work without rest. In the total blackness, a tile is pressed down. A root is severed. A needle-leg taps the slate: once, twice, three times—a dry, tiny click that dies the moment it leaves the stone, heard by nothing except the cold, stiff green that is already crowding in to choke it.
