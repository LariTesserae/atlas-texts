The first thing to understand about the life of the plateau is that it does not fight the current; it mechanically engages with it. Nothing here possesses flimsy fins or buoyant bladders; nothing floats that cannot clamp, wedge, or endure being slammed into solid rock at the speed of a falling timber.

In the seams between the megaliths—those five-foot-wide scouring trenches—the porcelain-white crabs do not merely shelter; they are the living grout of the world. They are called *wedges* by no one, for there is no one to name them, but their bodies have evolved into the exact geometry of keystones. Their carapaces are flat, trapezoidal shields of dense, cold-precipitated calcite, as thick as paving tiles and completely smooth, offering no purchase to the tearing wash above. They live stacked upon one another in self-locking tiers, ten or twenty deep, their jointed legs tucked flush into lateral grooves in their shells. 

When a lateral shear-current strikes a seam, the pressure drives the crabs deeper into the V-shaped convergence of the stone blocks. The more the water presses, the tighter the colony binds together, transforming into a pavement of living stone that prevents the grit from wearing the trenches any deeper. They feed without emerging: from the margins of their carapaces, they unfurl stiff, basket-like palps made of barbed chitin that project into the cross-wash just above the lip of the trench. These strain out the drifting organic mush—the pulverized meat of whatever was torn apart upstream. They do not have hearts that beat with any speed; their circulation is driven by the rhythmic, shuddering flex of their shells under the varying weight of the current. To be still, to be wedged so tightly that shell grinds against shell until the edges are polished like ivory, is their entire existence.

Deeper in the trenches, where the light cannot reach even as a pewter mist, grow the things that anchor them. They resemble ropes of braided tallow, thick as a man’s thigh and rooted into microscopic drill-holes in the granite. These are not plants, but colonial tube-worms whose sheaths are formed from secreted silica and ground granite dust. They do not stand erect; they stream horizontally in the flumes, sixty feet long, vibrating like taut cables. When the current reaches peak velocity, these living hawsers hum—a dry, droning choir that adds its voice to the sub-audible shudder of the plateau. 

Living along the lengths of these cables are pale, legless parasites like bloated leeches, which hold on with circular mouths armed with rings of horn. They do not drink blood, for the worms have little to give; instead, they drink the friction itself, absorbing the heat generated by the cable’s constant, violent whipping against the stone margins of the trench.

Above the masonry floor, in the open, howling water, life must remain in motion or be pulverized. 

The milky ribbons—the blind swimmers—are not fish in any conventional sense. They lack scales, red blood, and distinct skulls; their bodies are solid, flexible trusses of cartilaginous mesh wrapped in hide like damp rawhide. They have no eyes, nor do they possess the delicate lateral lines of surface fish, which would be torn to ribbons by the turbulence. Instead, their entire skin is an acoustic mirror. They navigate by sensing the rebound of the hydraulic roar off the granite blocks. To them, a townhouse-sized megalith is not an obstacle seen, but an immense, dead silence in the water—a "shadow" of low-pressure cavitation that they can tuck into with an instantaneous, shivering flex of their flank muscles.

These ribbons never sleep. They swim in tight, braided packs that mirror the flow of the shear-currents, weaving through the standing hydraulic jumps like thrown needles. Their mouths are vertical slits lined with serrated bone-plates that can hinge entirely backward to scrape mineral flour and the tough encrustations of shell-grit from the crowns of the megaliths. 

They mate on the tumble. In the dead-zones behind the great bastions, where the current curls into a spinning eddy, two ribbons will whip around one another, knotting their long bodies together with frantic speed before the eddy collapses and casts them back into the main stream. Their eggs are not jelly; they are dense, leaden spheres the size of musket balls that sink immediately, clattering down the steps of the cyclopean stairs like tossed marbles, falling into the seams to be caught in the waiting baskets of the wedge-crabs. The crabs eat most, but enough lodge deep in the gravel beneath the shells to hatch into larval needles that burrow into the dark joints of the platform.

Then there are the heavy ones—the *rollers*.

The twenty-foot scavengers that tumble across the plazas are the true masters of this mechanical sea. Their armor is not like the shell of a turtle, but like the armor-plating of an archaic warship: interlocking slabs of porous, mineralized bone several inches thick, stained slate-blue by the water. They have no pectoral or caudal fins; they have only four short, immensely powerful spades of bone that project from their sides, used not to swim, but to brake, steer, and lever themselves out of crevices.

A roller’s life is an eternal, deliberate wreck. It allows the cross-currents to roll it across the smoothed plazas like a loose ashlar. It tumbles over parapets, crashing down thirty-foot megalithic drops with a sickening, muffled *crunch* that would shatter any creature of flesh, only to strike the lower level, dig its bone-spades into the stone, and pivot. Its gizzard is filled with fifty-pound granite cobbles that it swallows from the avenues; as the creature is rolled by the water, the cobbles within it grind together, pulverizing whatever meat, bone, and shell it has vacuumed from the stone floor with its tubular, rubbery jaw.

They are blind, cold-blooded, and practically unkillable. A roller may take two centuries to cross a single five-mile plaza, being pushed forward an inch at a time during the violent seasonal surges, lodging against a megalith for thirty years, and then being dislodged by a shifting eddy to continue its blind, rolling migration toward the abyss.

At the edge of the world, where the plateau ends and the water boils over the lip from the lightless depths below, the life changes entirely.

Here, on the very curve of the drop-off, the hydraulic forces are so vast that no swimmer can hold its ground. Here live the *discs*. 

They are vast, radially symmetrical animals, thirty to fifty feet across, resembling leathery shields of pale gray flesh anchored directly to the stone lip by massive, vacuum-forming muscular mantles. They overlap one another like shingles on a roof, curving smoothly over the rounded lip of the esplanade. The violent, upward-surging water from the abyss rushes directly over their backs, creating an intense hydrodynamic lift that would rip them from the stone were it not for their shape: their upper surfaces are sculpted into hydrofoil contours that use the water's own velocity to wedge them down harder against the granite.

The discs live on the upwelling. The water rising from miles below is thick with the strange, sulfurous snow of the lower reaches—dead matter from some subterranean depth that has never seen the sky. The discs absorb this through thousands of undulating vents that cover their backs like the slits of a grate, filtering the cold geyser as it leaps over the shelf.

They are ancient. The granite beneath their mantles is not eroded; where a disc sits, the stone retains the crisp, sharp tooling of whatever hand leveled it millions of years ago, protected from the water by the animal's impenetrable seal. Sometimes, deep in the night of the water, a disc will die. Its suction will fail with a sharp, hydraulic *thwip* that echoes across the drowned terrace like a whip-crack. The current immediately catches its edge, peels it back, and rips it from the lip, tumbling the vast sheet of white flesh into the crosswinds of the plateau, where the ribbons converge on it in their thousands, tearing it to ribbons before it can strike the stones of the first plaza.

And behind the discs, nestled in the sheltered dead-space formed where the upward surge curves away from the vertical wall, are things that watch.

They do not have eyes, but they have stillness—a quality completely absent from the rest of the plateau. They are long, segmented creatures of dull iron-colored cartilage, anchored by their tails to the deep seams of the vertical wall, dangling out into the vertical rush like plumb lines. They possess many arms, delicate and multi-jointed, tipped with small, flat spatulas of horn. 

They do not hunt the ribbons, nor do they feed on the crabs. They hold small fragments of stone.

With slow, rhythmic motions that run completely counter to the howling madness of the water around them, these hanging things tap the stones against the masonry wall. *Clack. Clack. Clack.* They are not feeding; they are clearing the grit from the joint lines. They work tirelessly, feeling the fit of block against block with their horned fingertips, working their narrow spatulas into the seams to dislodge pebbles that might wedge the megaliths apart. 

Whether they are animals acting on an instinct born of some long-forgotten symbiosis, or whether they are the final, degraded remnants of the hands that hauled the stones into place—now stripped of memory, language, and fire, reduced to an amphibious reflex that knows only that the wall must not fall—cannot be known. 

They hang in the cold pewter gloom, suspended over miles of empty water, tapping the stone, listening to the vibration in their marrow, and keeping the seam clean.
