The yard does not support a population; it supports an occupancy.

Whatever lives here must conform to the pressure regime. At forty-five degrees, with the air thick with mineral vapor and the stone continuously sweating into the gutters, animal heat is an expensive error. An active blooded creature—anything that pants, shivers, or burns calories to maintain thirty-seven degrees of internal fuel—would starve within three cycles of the accumulator, exhausted simply by the effort of keeping its tallow soft.

The things that inhabit the cut have abandoned that speed. 

The most visible of them are the *gasket-men*, though whether they were once men who entered the cut during its working life or whether they are simply an offshoot of the oak-cambium that learned to detach its roots and walk on four calcified pads is an academic distinction the yard has long since smoothed over. 

There are never more than four. They are tall, narrow, and deliberately flat, built like cedar timbers that have been river-seasoned for forty years. Their skin has the texture of wet harness leather that has dried in the shade—gray-brown, cold to the touch, and completely hairless, cross-hatched with fine, shallow fissures along the joints where the skin must bend over the knee and elbow. They wear no garments, but are coated in a thin, waterproof layer of tallow and oak-tannin which they scrape with their broad, spade-like fingernails from the cradles of the rocker beams. This grease does not spoil; it smells faintly of walnut husks and cold axle grease.

They do not sleep in beds or nests. When the barometric pressure drops and the ceiling ribs begin their long, cold weeping, a gasket-man will simply choose a vacant section of rail, turn his back to the limestone retaining wall, and sit. He brings his knees up toward his breastplate, tucks his chin into the hard, gristly notch of his collarbone, and goes entirely gray. In this posture, his respiration slows until the interval between breaths is measured not by his own chest, but by the rocker beam’s stroke: nine minutes to take the damp air in through broad, slit-like nostrils that do not flare; nine minutes to let the odorless white vapor trickle out across his lips. 

If you were to lay a hand against his rib cage while he sits, you would not feel a heartbeat. You would feel only a low, liquid peristalsis, like the sound of cold syrup being forced through a linen filter. They feed on the condensation that forms on the brass sleeves of the siphons. It is not pure water; it is a dense, mineral tea carrying dissolved wood-sugars, copper carbonates, and microscopic iron salts that leach continuously from the interface where the roots have bitten into the machinery. A gasket-man will stand before a bronze fitting for half a shift, his long, pale tongue pressed flat against the sweating metal, drinking the run-off until his throat clicks shut with a small, hydraulic seal.

They have tasks, though they perform them with the maddening lack of urgency that belongs to geological formations. 

One will spend an entire morning walking the length of the number-two track. His feet are bare, wide-soled, and flat as flagstones, the skin beneath them half an inch thick and encrusted with sharp chips of purple slag that have worked their way into the horn of the heel without drawing fluid. Every twenty paces, he pauses. He bends at the waist—his spine moving with the dry, fibrous flex of a seasoned ash stave—and examines a rail joint. If the cold dew has carried a piece of grit into the expansion gap between two lengths of steel, he inserts an index finger, hooks the pebble out, and drops it into the center of the four-foot. He does not hurry. If a rocker beam begins its slow, five-minute descent while he is beneath it, he does not scramble; he leans aside just enough for the counterweight to pass his shoulder with two inches to spare, the downdraft of displaced air ruffling the dry edges of his ears like dead leaves.

Below the level of the gasket-men, in the wet dark beneath the calcified root-plates, live the *spindles*. 

These are smaller—perhaps the size of an otter, though without any of an otter’s sleekness or frantic energy. They are segmented, soft-shelled creatures whose bodies are formed of dense, fibrous rings of cartilage that look precisely like turnings from a wood-lathe. They lack eyes entirely, navigating by the thermal gradients that roll off the ironwork. A spindle will emerge from the gravel when the gutters fill with their pencil-thick streams of runoff, dragging its heavy, cylindrical body with six short, stubby limbs that end in broad horn scrapers. 

The spindles are the cleaners of the bearings. They climb the stationary frames with a suction-footed, hydraulic grip, hauling their bulk up twenty feet of vertical iron to reach the bifurcations of the dwarf oaks. There, where the wood embraces the cast-iron trunnions, a steady, microscopic paste of pulverized iron-dust and dead sapwood collects. To the spindle, this is meat. It inserts its soft, tubular snout into the clearance between the metal journal and the living timber, vacuuming the paste clean, preventing the bearing from galling, and leaving behind a slick, amber trail of mucous that cures in the forty-five-degree air into a lubricating film as slick as graphite. 

When their bellies are full of iron-meal, they do not defecate; they creep down into the stagnant sumps beneath the gantries, press themselves into the cracks between the foundation ashlars, and slowly mineralize. Over decades, their bodies turn entirely to iron-stone, cementing the foundations tighter against the hydraulic heave of the valley floor.

There is no language in the cut. Sound is an interruption of the work, and the work has no need of names. The gasket-men do not look at one another when they cross paths on the open slag between the tracks. One will simply step off the calcified root-plate to let the other pass, his eyes—flat, amber discs without white or pupil, like drops of dried shellac—fixed on the gray light falling through the ceiling. 

Yet they are aware of the machinery’s health with an intimacy that resembles hearing. If a brass poppet valve sticks three hundred yards away in the dark of the tunnel mouth, a gasket-man will stop mid-stride. He will tilt his head, his leathery throat vibrating slightly as he samples the shift in the barometric draft. Then he will turn, his broad feet striking the clinker with dull, solid thuds, and walk directly to the failing valve. He does not use tools. He will place his long, grease-darkened thumbs against the valve stem, lean his entire seventy-pound torso into the stroke, and wait—ten minutes, twenty minutes—until the hydrostatic pressure builds beneath his hands and the valve clears itself with that sharp, brass *chink*.

Once it vents, he steps back. He watches the ring of odorless white vapor rise toward the braided heartwood ribs above, dissipate into the steady gray light, and vanish. 

Then he wipes his palms down the sides of his leathery thighs, looks down the long, empty track where the water has already ceased to run in the gutters, and resumes his waiting.
