To survive in the pit is to inhabit a machine made of dust, and so the people who live here have the dry, matte texture of things that have never been washed in deep water. 

They are small-bodied, broad-shouldered people whose chests seem over-developed for their stature—a shape formed by lifetimes of pulling themselves up sheer wicker ladders and breathing an atmosphere that is permanently thick with bird-fluff, parched clay, and the sour flour of lime. Their skin does not tan to a hide-dark brown like that of the nomads on the desert rim above; instead, it takes on the faint, reddish-purple undertone of under-baked tile, perpetually powdered with the pink silt that settles out of the air. Even their hair, bound tightly in coils around bone needles to keep it clear of the climbing ropes, carries a chalky frost of bird-dander and efflorescence that no brushing ever quite clears.

Because the pit is an echo chamber that magnifies every snap of a wicker spar into an iron crack, they are largely a silent folk. Speech among them is not a sustained stream of breath, which would only be shredded and lost against the card-shuffle roar of the swallows; it has broken down into a vocabulary of percussive clicks made with the tongue against the teeth, short, whistling exhalations, and knuckle-taps delivered directly against the terracotta walls. 

The hollow ceramic tubes mortared into the brickwork are their acoustic conduits. A woman on the fifth tier does not shout down to the sumps; she bends her mouth to the rim of an empty amphora embedded flush with the landing and taps out a rhythm with a river-pebble. Three tiers down and forty cubits along the curved face, the vibration coughs out of an identical hole into the ear of a boy cleaning a catch-pan. Through these thousands of clay mouths, the whole pit murmurs to itself in a parallel dialect of knocks, scrapes, and hollow wooden notes that only the human ear, trained from infancy, can disentangle from the dripping of the lime-traps.

They do not live on the open ledges, which are the domain of the swallows and the rope-rigs, but behind the face. Where early settlers dug out collapsed aggregate or followed the natural settling-vaults of the brick bulk, they have scooped out low, barrel-roofed chambers that run back into the cool earth behind the retaining walls. 

Inside these cells, the air is still and heavy with the smell of pressed almond tallow and sour hog-wash. The inhabitants sleep not on mats, but in shallow, boat-shaped depressions ground directly into the lime-concrete floors, lined with sheepskins that have gone stiff with dried sweat and clay. In the hottest months, when the upper levels of the pit collect the desert’s overhead heat like a burning-glass, the families migrate downward, level by level, sliding into the damp, mold-smelling crypts of the lower third where the walls sweat cold mineral grease and the sound of the pigs rooting in the sump-muck is a continuous, wet squelch outside their curtains of split cane.

Their vision is strange, shaped by verticality and the constant presence of edges. They have deep-set, wet-rimmed eyes that squint against the lime-drift, with heavy, hairless lids. They rarely look up toward the sky—to do so in the open light invites a blinding flake of falling guano or a dusting of pulverized brick—and instead keep their gaze cast downward at an angle of forty-five degrees, reading the footway six cubits ahead. 

Their toes are extraordinarily long and mobile, the skin beneath the balls of their feet as thick and ridged as camel-sole, grooved to grip the lip of the finger-drawn bricks. Children learn to walk not on flats, but on the bevels; a child of four can run along a catwalk no wider than an ox’s shinbone with a wicker basket of crushed gourd-seeds balanced on the nape of its neck, its heels hanging over thirty cubits of air without hesitation.

Work is divided by the weight of moisture. 

The youngest and lightest—the thin-wristed girls and boys whose bones have not yet set thick—work the upper thirds. They are the vine-tenders and egg-gatherers. They spend their days suspended in wicker harness-cradles that swing out into the sunlit core of the pit, checking the hemp lines that carry the pale gourds, pinching off yellowed leaves, and sliding their arms shoulder-deep into the horizontal earthenware pipes to draw out the blind, naked squabs of the swallows before they can fledge. They carry wooden laths tipped with copper hooks to skewer the mastiff bats where they sleep in the deeper ventilation sleeves, dropping the limp, brown-furred bodies down into the catching-cloths held by the old men below.

The middle tiers are the domain of the clay-kneaders and the salt-scrapers. The water that condenses on the brick is not pure; it is heavy with soda, potash, and the bitter nitre drawn out of the bird-droppings. Men with wooden scrapers pare this fur-like white crust from the damp walls each morning, packing it into coarse linen bags to be traded up at the desert rim for salt-fish, lead weights, and tallow. Their hands are ruined by the alkali—creased with deep, black fissures that they pack with sheep grease and resin to keep the skin from splitting to the bone.

Down on the sump floor live the swine-masters, who rarely ascend. They are heavier than the climbers, their joints swollen by the cold damp that never leaves the bottom flags. They speak even less than the others, moving among the bristly guinea hogs with short, blunt-ended hazel switches, steering the runts away from the slick drainage troughs and dragging dead bats from the sump screens before the grates can choke. 

These floor-dwellers are the custodians of the bone-balance troughs. They watch the ox-bone counterpoises with a slow, mechanical patience, judging by the thickness of the slime in the runnels exactly when a trough will tip. When the wood groans and the clay tongue falls, spilling its gray wash across the tiles, the swine-master stands motionless in the spray, his greasy leather kirtle streaming with runoff, his face turned away until the thunder of the swallows has rolled itself out against the upper rims.

They eat very little meat that is not small and winged. Their diet is a mash of gourd-flesh dried on the tiles and ground with parched barley, enriched with the grease of boiled bat carcasses and the fat, white crickets drawn from behind the weep-holes, which they toast over charcoal braziers until the shells crack like parched grain. The hogs are slaughtered only twice a year, during the dry turn when the dew-drip fails and the sumps must be scoured with sand; their meat is salted down in the deepest, cold earthenware cylinders that penetrate five cubits into the desert subsoil.

To an outsider, the pit would seem like a place of punishment or an ancient trap that has slowly filled with human vermin, but to those within it, the open desert above is a dead, formless waste—an unlivable void devoid of edges, where the sound of the world spills away into the sky instead of being caught, counted, and turned into something you can hear. 

They know that the walls are settling. Every few months, in the dead of the night when even the mastiff bats have flown out through the rush-hurdles to hunt across the dune-crests, a sudden, gunshot *clack* echoes through the basin—a brick cracking under three hundred years of cumulative load, or a foundation course shifting another grain into the lime-mud below. 

When it happens, no one wakes with a shout. In the black chambers behind the walls, a hand simply reaches out in the dark, finds the nearest terracotta pipe, and taps twice: *Here.* 

From three paces to the left, through two cubits of solid brick, comes the muffled, answering knuckle-knock: *Here, still.*
