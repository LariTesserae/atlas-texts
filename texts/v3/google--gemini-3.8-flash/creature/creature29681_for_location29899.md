Because time does not pass by the sky, it passes through the bodies of the people, though it does so with a strange, delayed reluctance.

They are tall, broad-palmed, and walk with the flat-footed, deliberate cadence of people who have never had to run from a storm or hurry before sundown. Their legs are noticeably long between knee and ankle, an adaptation to the high, repetitive rise of the cyclopean stairs. Their skin is the color of wet river-clay on their right sides and pale parchment on their left—a literal tanning of the flesh that has held for generations, for they orient their lives, their thoroughfares, and their domestic benches strictly along the axis of the fixed light. To sit facing the wind with the pale gold on the right cheek is their natural posture of rest; to enter a shadow is an act of deliberate withdrawal.

They wear garments made of three materials only: grease-cured ibex hide, which does not stiffen in the cold; skirts and tunics of felted mountain-wool, heavy as lead and dyed only by the natural shades of the fleece; and broad aprons of split reed, worn smooth by the friction of carrying stone. They pin these robes at the shoulder with long, polished splinters of ibex shin-bone, sharpened at the tip and bored with an eyelet through which a rawhide thong is knotted. They do not wear hats. Their hair is black, coarse as horsehair, and chopped off evenly at the jaw with flakes of fresh-broken flint, leaving their ears exposed to the steady, invariant draft.

They do not have words for *morning*, *evening*, or *night*. Instead, their language divides existence into *The Face* (the stone and skin touched by the sun) and *The Leeward* (the permanent cold gray). 

Because the light never fails, they have no communal hour of sleep. A family does not lie down together in the dark. Instead, individuals sleep when their marrow grows heavy, retreating into the deep recesses of the great dry-stacked halls, where the air is dry, dead-still, and perfectly black. They sleep on low pallets of dried moss that have been compressed into springy, fragrant boards several inches thick. Their rest is short—rarely more than three or four hours of motionless, breath-shallow slumber—after which they drink a bowl of cold meltwater from the gutters and walk immediately back out into the sharp, two-hours-past-noon light to lift, chip, or braid.

Their work is not an industry; it is a metabolic function, like breathing. They build because the fault lines continue to settle, infinitesimal fractions of an inch at a time, requiring the terraces to be wedged and counterweighted to keep the aqueducts level. 

They use hammer-stones of black basalt brought up from the depths of the lower fissures. Two men will stand beside a five-ton block of dolomite for what might be several days, striking it with alternate, rhythmic thuds. They do not sweat profusely; the biting cold evaporates their moisture instantly, leaving a fine frost of salt on their temples. When they speak, it is never in full sentences. The absence of echo and the immense distance that sound travels has made them fiercely economical with their breath. A speaker will emit a low, guttural syllable—almost a clearing of the throat—and point with a blunt, scarred finger. The other understands. Their voices have the timbre of stones rolled together in a leather sack.

Their diet is spare and cold. They do not light fires for cooking; smoke in this still, clear air would linger for days and choke the nesting swallows whose droppings fertilize the dwarf rowans. 

They eat the thick, rubbery caps of a pale gray shelf-fungus that grows on the north foundations of the granaries, scraped off with bone spatulas and eaten raw. They milk the ibex, not by penning them, but by approaching them as they stand upon the parapets. The ibex, huge beasts with horns like ribbed scythe-blades that curve back nearly to their rumps, are not domesticated in the lowland sense; they are civic co-inhabitants. A mason will lean against the flank of a standing doe, stroke her dry beard, and draw a half-gourd of dense, yellow milk that smells of bitter rhododendron. The milk is mixed with dried rowan-meal—pounded from the crimson berries that cluster on the cliff-clinging bushes—and pressed into small, hard cakes that keep indefinitely in the cold draft.

Beneath the human tier, other forms of life have found their own unhurried geometry.

Along the precise line where the shadow meets the light on every stone wall, there lives a species of blind, flat-shelled snail no larger than a lentil. They do not wander. Each snail spends its entire multi-year life within a two-inch band, grazing the microscopic blue-green film that forms where the warmth of the sun-face bleeds into the perpetual moisture of the shade. Their shells are made of translucent calcite, so clear that their slow, dark pulses can be seen through the lime. When a mason must move a block, he will carefully prize the snails from the seam with a reed splinter and place them upon the corresponding shadow-line of the new wall; it is considered an ill omen if the boundary of the living line is broken.

The swallows that nest in the chalk seams are different from the birds of the low valleys. Their plumage is not iridescent, but a matte, soot-black that absorbs the pale gold light without reflecting it. Their wing edges are fringed with down so fine that when three hundred of them drop simultaneously from the cliff face into the ravine to hunt, they make less sound than a breath held in the throat. They drop like plumb-bobs, their wings tucked tight against their bodies, snapping open only at the very floor of the rift to skim the water gutters. 

The midges they hunt do not breed in stagnant pools—for there is no heat to hatch them—but emerge from the wet moss cushions. They are minute, pale, and wingless for the first half of their lives, creeping like silver dust among the moss roots before growing four long, filament-like wings that do not beat, but simply catch the uniform cold draft, allowing them to hang suspended in the air like dust motes caught in a sunbeam. They neither rise nor fall; they are an architecture of living smoke, anchored to the contours of the rock by the drafts alone.

Because nothing decays, death in this city is not an ending that requires disposal, but an induction into the stone.

When a person ceases to breathe, they are not buried—there is no earth to receive them, and no fire to consume them. Instead, the body is washed in the clay-lined cisterns until the skin is entirely free of salt and grease. It is then carried to the dry, leeward galleries beneath the great watch-towers, where the draft blows coldest and most steadily. 

There, propped against a wall of dressed slate, the body dries. Over decades, the skin tightens until it is indistinguishable from the oiled rawhide of their aprons; the muscles cure into dense, gray sinew; the hair remains fixed. These dry elders are not hidden away. They are seated along the lower steps of the amphitheaters and placed as lintel-weights in the long galleries. A mason working on a difficult joint will often wedge a hammer-stone beneath the foot of an ancestor to keep the timber plumb while he drives an oak pin through the joint. There is no horror in this, nor any worship; the ancestors simply remain part of the city’s ballast, helping to hold the dry rock against the slow creep of the fault lines.

And in the sunken basin, around the deep, black pool where the silverfish wait, the life is oldest of all.

Those who have grown too stiff to lift the basalt hammers—whose joints have begun to take on the mineral stillness of the dolomite—eventually descend the tiered stone benches. They go down alone. They do not return to the high terraces. They sit upon the lowest step, with their heels resting in the black water, their backs against the sun-warmed limestone of the basin wall.

They sit there for seasons that have no names, watching the silverfish. 

The fish are long, narrow, and ribless, like ribbons of hammered tin. They do not turn their heads, for they have no necks and no eyes; they maintain their position against the imperceptible convection of the pool with small, rhythmic ripples of their dorsal fringes. Once every several weeks, one of the old ones on the steps will reach into a reed pouch, withdraw a pinch of dried rowan-meal, and let it fall upon the mirror-surface. 

The water does not ripple. The meal does not scatter. A single fish will rise from the depth, vertical as an icicle, draw the dust into its toothless mouth without disturbing the reflection of the sky, and sink backward into the blackness until it is once again only a silver needle suspended in the cold glass of the world.
