To live here, an animal must make its peace with the pulse.

The ground does not rest, and so the creatures that remain are those that have surrendered the need for absolute stillness. A rat cannot bear it; the vibration shakes the fine fluid of its inner ear until it sickens and crawls away toward the dryer marsh. But things without ears, or things whose senses are tuned to the broad, hydraulic shiver of the earth, settle in and grow fat.

Down in the sludge troughs, where the warm washings from the cylinder heads meet the bayou silt, there are eels. They are not the pale, blind things of mountain caves, but thick-bodied swamp eels that have worked their way up through the low drainage culverts and stayed because the water never chills. They lie in the dead angles behind the iron wing-dams, their bellies pressed into the gray lime-slurry. They are coated in an unnaturally heavy slime—an excess of their own secretion, worked up to protect their skin against the faint sting of alkaline lye and mineral grease. When the flywheels sweep down into the bilge, throwing up their rhythmic sheet of spray, the eels do not startle. They understand the arc of the iron; they have learned the displacement of the water to the fraction of an inch, nudging forward into the brief vacuum left behind the counterweight to snap at the drowned wood-borers and beetle larvae turned up by the churn.

Higher up, where the heat is dry and sulfurous around the main steam header, the population belongs to the hair-felt and the lagging. 

In the decaying magnesium wraps, generations of a heavy, dark-bodied cricket have hollowed out miles of chalky galleries. They are nearly white when they molt, stained only by the soot that filters through the canopy, and they are entirely deaf. They do not chirp; in an engine bay where two horizontal beams strike sixty tons of displacement every minute, a mating song is meaningless. Instead, they court by striking their abdomens against the tin jacketing of the pipes. A male will drum a rapid, dry tattoo against a blistered brass plate, and a female three yards off, clinging to the underside of an ironwood root, feels the copper line ring beneath her tarsal claws and crawls toward the heat.

They share the lagging with the roaches—not the nimble, scurrying things of dry cupboards, but the great, broad-shouldered bark roaches that have moved in from the river oaks. Here, fed on tallow drippings, starch paste from the fermenting house, and the damp mold that skins the spruce sluices, they grow to the length of a man’s thumb. Their shells are not slick and glossy, but matte, pitted with lime-dust, and often grown over with the same faint green diatomaceous film that covers the brickwork. They move with an unnatural, drunken slowness, having no predators from the air to fear.

The frogs the engineer sees are only the outliers. The true colony lives in the tangle where the river figs have swallowed the crane gantries.

They are small, hylid tree frogs, but their color has shifted away from the bright wet greens of the outer bayou. Here, in the permanent, lime-streaked dusk, their backs are the gray of boiled pig iron, blotched with irregular liver-colored patches that match the rust scale on the flanges. They do not gather in the open water to spawn. The sump water is too greasy; it would suffocate their eggs in an oil slick within an hour. Instead, they lay their clutches in the hanging cups of the wild taro and the deep, water-filled hollows where three roots meet against an iron girder. The tadpoles hatch in suspended cisterns of warm condensation, six feet above the catwalks, feeding on the airborne soot and the pollen that falls through the gaps in the fig branches. When the pressure relief valve trips—that sudden, deafening tear of high-pressure steam that strips the duckweed from the pond below—the frogs do not scatter. They merely flatten their bodies into the wet channels of the bark, their throats pulsing in time with the long, dying sigh of the boiler draft.

And then there is the man, or the succession of men who have become a single figure through repetition.

He is not young. Young men cannot endure the air; it enters the chest like wet wool and sits there until the lungs rot out with marsh-ague. He is dry-jointed, his skin cured by ninety-degree vapor and sulfur smoke until it has the color and resistance of neat’s-foot leather. His hearing was taken twenty years ago by the blow-down valves, so he lives in a silence defined entirely by bone-conduction: he feels the engines through the ash-wood soles of his clogs. 

He knows when a crosshead pin is running hot not because he sees smoke, but because a particular high, dry frequency ceases to travel up through the floorboards into his shins. He can tell the depth of the bilge by the slight alteration in the bounce of the catwalk under his stride.

He lives largely in an alcove behind the accumulator, where the bricks are baked clean and dry by the proximity of the return flues. It is the only place in the two hundred paces where paper does not turn to pulp in forty-eight hours. His presence is recorded in small, greasy artifacts: an iron spoon resting on a lead ingot; three horn buttons laid out on a valve chest to dry; a small, flat tin of salt pork whose grease he shares with the packing nuts. 

He does not fight the river fig. When a root thick as an arm crosses the lower inspection hatch, he does not axe it away; he takes an old rasp and files the wood flat on the under-edge so the door can swing its four inches of necessary clearance. He has lived here long enough to know that if you cut the wood, it bleeds white sap that gums the linkage, but if you coax it with grease and tallow, it grows around the machinery like a socket, holding the aged castings true against the settling of the mud.

He does not speak, even to himself. The place does not permit it. When he eats his noon meal—cold salt mullet and cold rice pinched from a greaseproof rag—he sits on the lowest rung of the gantry ladder with his heels hooked over the iron bar, watching the duckweed knit itself back together over the hole the blow-off valve made ten minutes before. 

He watches the small green frogs climb down the unheated brass lines to drink the sweat off the fittings. He does not touch them; they do not drop from the metal as he passes. They have lived together under this ceiling of leaves and rusted trusses so long that to the frogs, his clogs are only another beam engine, slightly lighter in the stroke, moving with a slower, more irregular displacement across the iron floor.
