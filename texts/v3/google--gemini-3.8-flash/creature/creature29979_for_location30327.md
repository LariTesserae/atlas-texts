To understand what lives here, one must begin not in the open glare, but inside the blanket.

Beneath the lime and ochre crust of the mosses, down in the half-meter of brown, anaerobic tea where the living cushion transitions into compressed fiber, the roar of the water changes character. It ceases to be an acoustic event and becomes a mechanical massage. The entire two-meter slab of peat oscillates against the basalt at roughly forty-two cycles per second—the fundamental frequency of the three-phase dynamos churning miles away.

In this pressurized, shivering broth live the ribbons.

They are not true leeches, though they share their flattened, muscular cross-section and lack of limbs. They are blind, pale platyhelminths the length of a human hand, so translucent that the darker, tannic fluid they ingest forms a winding, root-like shadow through their centers. They do not burrow through the peat; the peat is too dense. Instead, they glide along the microscopic liquid films separating the dead stems of the dwarf sedges, lubricated by a thick, cold mucus that smells faintly of vinegar. They possess no eyes, no ears, and no lungs. Their skin breathes the dissolved oxygen forced into the water by the roaring air-entrainment gates upriver. To these creatures, the industrial tremor is not a disturbance; it is the current that circulates their air. When a pump station occasionally trips and falls silent, the ribbon-worms knot themselves into dense, suffocating balls at the basalt floor and wait, paralytic, until the vibration resumes.

Higher up, where the peat meets the shearing alpine sun, life must protect itself from being burned to chalk.

The stoneflies that crawl across the rivet-heads of the hydro-casings belong to an ancient, wingless lineage whose chitin has turned an opaque, light-absorbing jet. They are heavy, deliberate creatures, built like tortoises. Their tarsal claws are doubled and curved into sharp carbiner-like hooks, evolved to find purchase in the pitting of rust and the micro-crevices of unpolished steel. They do not seek the moss; the moss is too cold. They graze exclusively on the thermal halo of the intake housings. 

These casings, warm from the friction of thousands of tons of pressurized water grinding through bronze impellers, support a strange, rubbery felt: an encrusting black lichen that feeds on a diet of airborne machine grease, iron scale, and the relentless UV radiation. The stoneflies shear this felt with mandibles hardened by biomineralized zinc. Their nymphs do not live in the calm seeps, but inside the catastrophic violence of the bypass chutes themselves. Flattened like copper pennies, equipped with hydraulic suckers on their bellies, they cling to the basalt directly beneath the white jets, grazing the violently whipping ribbons of emerald algae that can survive nowhere else. If one loses its grip, the velocity of the water tears it to shreds against the rock in a fraction of a second.

Then there are the birds—the solitary, grease-legged things that stand at the edge of the discharge basins.

They are not gulls or herons, though they carry the long, stilt-like tarsi of shorebirds. Their plumage is not soft; it has the hard, oily texture of grebe feathers, lacquered against the freezing spray by massive uropygial glands that secrete a waxy, petroleum-scented tallow. Because they forage near the intakes, their legs and the lower margins of their bellies are perpetually stained with the dark, emulsified lubricants that weep from the pump-gland packings: a dark, iridescent collar of mineral grease that shields their skin from the tea-colored rot of the bog.

They are profoundly, biologically deaf. Their auditory canals are sealed by fleshy, valvular plugs, protecting their brains from being liquefied by the 130-decibel thunder of the spillways. They hunt entirely by bone-conduction and optical acuity. Their eyes are exceptional: shielded by a permanently semi-closed nictitating membrane that acts like a polarized lens of deep amber, filtering out the blinding optical glare of the mirrors of water and the razor-bright basalt. 

They stand motionless for hours on the steel catwalks, their toes locked through the diamond mesh. They are waiting for the dynamos to clear their screens. When an intake gate backwashes, it ejects a sudden, churning mass of drowned matter from the upper snowfields—shredded moss, frost-killed beetles, and the pale ribbon-worms dislodged by hydrostatic shock. The bird drops like a plumb bob into the foam, strikes with a bill that is blunt, serrated, and cold as iron, and returns to the vibrating steel rail before the downdraft of the spray can pin it to the rock.

Under the catwalks, in the dry, triangular hollows beneath the corrugated iron shacks, something warm-blooded has found a seam.

It is a vole, or something that was once a vole before the plateau was hoisted into this thin, screaming air. It has lost its ears; they have withered away into fur-covered nubs to escape the roar. Its pelt is soot-grey, dense, and completely water-repellent. These animals do not venture out onto the open moss, where the searing sun would cook their pale, unpigmented skin. Instead, they live along the network of rusted lattice pylons and steam lines.

They have hollowed out intricate labyrinths within the decaying, yellowed fiberglass lagging that wraps the insulated pipes. Here, three inches away from boiling water and suspended ten feet above the saturated peat, they raise litters of four or five blind, hairless young in ambient temperatures of nearly thirty-five degrees Celsius. At night, when the searing sun drops behind the fault-blocks and the plateau plunges within minutes to fifteen below freezing, the voles emerge to scavenge the service gantries. They move with their bellies pressed flat against the warm metal of the conduit housings, their vibrissae trembling as they read the vibrations of the girders, harvesting the dead stoneflies that have dropped from the colder bolt-heads above.

Nothing here sings. There are no territorial calls, no courtship flights, no rustling of dry leaves. Any sound smaller than an explosive decompression is swallowed instantly by the uniform, concussive medium of the water-engines. 

Instead, courtship and territoriality are carried through the iron itself. The birds stamp their heavy, tallow-greased feet on the resonant sheet metal of the weir plates, sending rhythmic shudder-patterns through the steel that can be felt by other birds a mile downstream. The voles press their jaws directly against the pylon angle-irons to feel the tooth-clicks of their rivals traveling through the rivets.

Even the plants participate in this mechanical ecology. The creeping cushions that are slowly scaling the housings of the pump stations are not the same species that form the peat flats. They are darker, their leaves thickened into succulent, purple-black scales that sweat an acidic resin. This resin attacks the unpainted plate steel, etching microscopically fine pits into the iron into which the plant anchors its tough, wire-like rhizoids. They are eating the machines. Millimeter by millimeter, fed by the continuous condensation dripping from the cold pipes and the low heat radiating from the bearings, the mat is welding itself to the industry that created its microclimate.

In the long afternoon, when the sun turns the mist plumes into towering, prismatic walls of white light that throw sharp, moving rainbows against the dark basalt cliffs, the landscape feels not like an wilderness interrupted by machinery, but a single, massive organism made of meat, moss, and cast steel. The pumps do not belong to the people who abandoned the control shacks; they belong to the grease-legged waders whose perches they provide, to the penny-shaped nymphs glued inside their bypass throats, and to the silent, blind things sliding through the cold, vibrating tea two meters down, breathing the machine-churned froth of the mountain.
