The first things you notice are not creatures at all, but their drafts.

Because the world is forever setting and never cool, whatever lives here shares the chemistry of the slurry. The simplest things do not have blood; they have *slip*—that thin, creamy suspension of red river-silt and chalk that ceramicists use to join wet handles to wet cups. 

In the long, sagging troughs where the water gathers milky and lukewarm, you find the **Slip-Maw**. It does not look like an animal until it leaves its depression. In the water, it is merely an oil-slick of thicker, gray-green sediment, two or three yards across, resting over the joints between the pavers. But when a seam begins to dry, it gathers itself inward with a sound like wet linen being pulled from a tub. It has no skeleton; its firmness comes entirely from the surface tension of its mud. It moves by slumping—leaning its mass forward into a herringbone furrow, letting the scalding water carry its leading edge, and dragging its tail like a wet rag.

The Slip-Maw feeds on the salt blooms. As the potash and soda crunches along the drying margin of a plaza, the maw flows over the crystals, dissolving them back into its own interior brine. If you were to dip a finger into its center—though the heat would blister you—you would feel thousands of tiny, calcined grit-teeth, no larger than grains of grog, grinding together in the dark of its gut, breaking down the alkaline crusts to keep its liquid from curdling into solid brick. When it dies, it simply fails to find water before the floor’s under-bake catches it; it stiffens, shrinks by a seventh of its volume, fissures with the delicate, hexagonal crazing of drying mud, and becomes just another raised tile on the endless plain.

***

Farther out, where the steam thins enough to reveal the towering, shuddering scaffolds, there are things with weight and intention, though their anatomy is clumsy, as if invented by an apprentice who had only seen a horse or a man once, through thick smoke.

They are called the **Tampers**.

They are tall—twelve, fifteen feet—and their texture is that of "leather-hard" clay: firm enough to support their own bulk, but soft enough that if they bump against a wooden stake, a deep, blunt gouge remains in their flanks, showing the straw chaff mixed into their meat to give it tensile strength. They have no faces, only a blunt, rounded swelling where a crown might be, marked by two deep depressions made by an enormous thumb, left there when their clay was still soft.

The Tampers do not walk so much as they *seat* themselves. Their lower halves are heavy, rectangular pillars, broad at the base like the footings of an adobe wall. They lift one block-like limb with a slow, agonizing suction—*shluck*—and bring it down with a concussive thud that drives the boiling groundwater up through the joints for twenty paces around them. 

Their entire existence is an anxious, rhythmic labor. They follow the phantom architecture. When a forty-foot wall of green cobble erupts from the floor, a herd of three or four Tampers will congregate at its base within the hour. They do not build it, nor do they tear it down; they press against it. They lean their broad, flat chests into the bulging masonry, their skin adhering slightly to the wet mortar, holding the wavering courses true with their immense, dumb bulk while the lime cures. 

Sometimes they are too slow. A wall shears, and twenty tons of hot stone and bubbling mortar collapses over them. They do not cry out—they have no lungs, only hollow chambers inside their ribs where hot steam collects and vents through fissures in their shoulder blades—but they do not die either. They are simply incorporated. Months later, walking past a heap of tumbled rubble that has begun to fire in the radiant heat, you might see the pale, straw-flecked calf of a Tamper protruding from beneath a slab of terracotta, its toes still twitching slightly with the rhythm of the floor’s slow, deep thrum.

***

In the deeper air, where the ceiling of ochre steam sags lowest, live the **Tensioners**.

If the Tampers are made of the floor, the Tensioners are made of the scaffolding. They resemble vast, loosely articulated trusses of unplaned timber, fifty or sixty feet long, held together not by pins or mortise-joints, but by thick wrappings of black, greasy rawhide that shrink and stretch with the humidity. They cling to the underside of the leaning towers, straddling the gulfs between masonry and sky.

To look at one is to look at a cluster of construction poles that has decided to crawl. They have no organs, save for a central knot of wet, knotted hemp—a heart the size of a hay-bale that groans and tightens under tension. The Tensioners live by counter-weight. They anchor their rear spars into the sockets left by fallen scaffold-poles, reach out across the empty air with a jointed foreleg of split cedar, and catch the ropes that snap when a tower begins to heel over. 

When a rope breaks, a Tensioner catches the trailing end in its wooden fork, leaning back into the void, its timbers bowing until the resin smokes and boils out of the grain. They will hang there for days, perfectly motionless, their rawhide joints creaking like the hulls of ships, keeping a useless, empty arch from collapsing into the mist. They seem to experience a kind of timber-ecstasy in this strain; if the arch finally falls, the sudden release of tension often snaps their backbones, and they tumble into the scalded shallows, their dry limbs floating like drift-timber while the little Slip-Maws crawl over them to lick the sap.

***

And then, rarest of all, are those things that have been fully fired: the **Clinkers**.

They are small compared to the monumental ruins—scarcely larger than a human child, though shaped like nothing that ever breathed. They are hollow vessels of vitrified red stoneware, their surfaces dark, slick, and glassy with salt-glaze. They are the only things here that make a sharp sound. When they move across the herringbone floor, their many needle-pointed feet—tips of fired white porcelain—click with the rapid, dry clatter of dice rolled on a marble table.

The Clinkers do not belong to the mud or the water. They are creatures of the deep bake. They come up from the joints where the heat is greatest, where the mortar has turned to white glass and the air above the brick is an invisible, shimmering shimmer that will cook meat in seconds.

Inside their hollow, ringing bodies, a handful of red-hot charcoal sloshes about like loose teeth. They are scavenger-kilns. They scuttle through the ruins with a jerky, clockwork gait, searching for the raw, unfired things: the stray coils of damp clay dropped by the earth, the severed limbs of Tampers, the soft, fatty pockets of vegetable straw that haven't yet charred. They seize these with small, iron-hard pinchers of stoneware, cramming the cold mud into their open bellies, where the heat inside cooks the material, vitrifies it, and deposits it out their vents as tiny, perfect, useless terracotta beads.

They are the only things that seem to possess an eye. It is not an eye of jelly, but a "peep-hole"—the same small, circular plug an artisan leaves in the wall of a kiln to judge the color of the heat. Through this round hole in their terracotta chests, one can see the white-hot draft within, flickering with an angry, ancient intelligence that understands nothing of the world except that it must be baked, that the softness is a disease, that the ultimate state of all matter is the clinking, ringing permanence of the pot.

***

Sometimes, if you sit very still on a dry course of pavers while the steam braids itself into ropes around your knees, you will see something that does not fit these categories.

It will be a thumb. Just a thumb, thirty yards across, pale as lard and smeared with river-silt, rising smoothly through an opening in the herringbone floor like a breaching whale. It does not belong to a giant hidden beneath—the floor is solid brick for three hundred leagues down. It is simply an event: an isolated act of pressing. 

It descends into a fresh mound of wet, gray mud that was not there five minutes before. It rotates once, leaving the delicate, swirling grain of its skin stamped forty feet wide into the surface, displacing three tons of boiling slip with a wet, heavy sigh. 

A dozen Slip-Maws will immediately surge toward the rim of the new print to drink the chalky water that pools in its whorls. A Tamper will turn its faceless head toward the sound, take three bone-shaking steps toward the fresh pit, and begin, with infinite, stupid patience, to tread the edge smooth with its square feet. 

The thumb withdraws into the steam, disappearing without a splash, leaving behind only the smell of turned soil and the sharp, clean burn of lime, waiting for the fire to come up from below and fix the mistake forever.
