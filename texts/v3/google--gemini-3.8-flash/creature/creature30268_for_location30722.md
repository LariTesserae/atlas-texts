To speak of what lives here, one must first discard the concept of an inhabitant. Nothing has crept into this engine from the outside, and nothing has been permitted to diverge into an independent existence. There is no creaturely will, no hunger that contends with another hunger, and no breath that is not the closed loop of the plant’s own hydraulic exhaust. 

Yet the system is not static, and an engine of this scale cannot maintain its tolerances without tenders. 

The things that move through the flues and along the cat’s-cradles are not animals; they are somatic appendages, budded off from the vascular trunks, assigned to maintenance, and destined to be reabsorbed when their structural utility is spent. They are vegetative organs that have achieved a terrifying, solitary mobility.

The most numerous of these are the *meristematic crawlers*—though to call them crawlers implies limbs, and they have none. They resemble long, pale ingots of compressed, pithy horn, thick as a man’s torso and squared off at the edges like milled timber. They possess no eyes, mouths, or nervous tissue. Their motion is purely hydraulic: by alternating the turgor pressure within segmented, cellular chambers along their length, they inch forward with glacial, unstoppable force across the floor plates. 

They are the jointers. Where two plates of the floor meet and the seam has weeped an excess of linseed-pitch, a crawler settles over the fault. Its underside is a flat, hot rasp of silica-dense bark that vibrates at an imperceptible frequency, driven by the low-amperage current hum of the runners below. It planes the seam flat. It licks up the bitter pitch through micro-capillaries, filters the heavy minerals into its own core to weight itself down, and leaves behind a cold-cured bead of extruded cellulose as smooth and clean as an arc-welder’s bead. A crawler may take four years to travel the perimeter of a single column grove. When its rasp is worn smooth by silica and its internal chambers are clogged with calcified deposits, it does not die; it simply stops. The plates beneath it open slightly, weeping pitch until the crawler is glued down, encased in resin, and slowly incorporated into the floor as a structural curb.

Higher up, suspended in the blackness among the wire-tendrils, are the *plummets*. 

They hang by the thousands: elongated, pear-shaped galls of dense, oily cork, some the size of casks, others vast as ship hulls. They are tethered directly to the vertical aqueducts by braided cords of conductive pith. The plummets are counterweights and pressure-valves combined. Inside their fibrous bellies are hollow chambers lined with voltaic plates that continuously read the electromagnetic flux of the column grove. When the deep tectonic pumps surge and the sap-pressure rises to near-bursting, the plummets gorge. They siphon the scalding, mineral-rich sap up into themselves through their umbilical cords. 

As they fill, they become heavier, slowly descending twenty, fifty, a hundred meters through the flues, dragging their taut guide-wires with them. Their slow descent mechanically resets the angle of the condenser baffles above, tilting them by a fraction of a degree to increase the condensation run-off and dump heat back into the lower apron. In the dark, you would know a plummet only by the sudden displacement of air—a slow, hot downdraft carrying the sharp scent of ozone—and the low, harmonic groan of the tendril bearing hundreds of tons of living ballast. They hang there until the pressure falls, slowly sweating the fluid back into the drainage conduits, rising again on their elastic cords like massive, vegetative pistons.

And then there are the *sleeves*. 

Circling the vast, non-tapering trunks are segmented rings of lignified bark that fit the columns with the precision of machine bearings. They are lubricated against the trunks by an unbroken film of the plant’s clear, dielectric sap. A sleeve has no internal drive of its own; it is an organic float. It rests on the cushion of rising steam and the continuous down-wash of the condensation ribbons that slump from the canopy. 

As the condensation runs down the column, the sleeve gathers it into a deep, circular trough along its upper rim, directing the liquid inward through hollow, needle-like spurs that tap directly into the trunk’s downward-siphon system. The sleeves rise and fall on the vertical colonnades with the fluctuating humidity of the flues, ascending slowly over decades as the columns grow, continually scraping the ribs clean of mineral slag and scale. If two sleeves on adjacent columns touch, they do not fight; their fibers knit instantly where they contact, forming an arched, rigid cross-brace between the pillars that hardens within hours to stone-like stiffness. The sleeves have sacrificed their mobility to provide structural rigidity where the architecture demands it.

Nothing here sleeps. There are no dreams in this continent, because there is no organ designed to contain them. 

If there is a consciousness here, it is dispersed entirely across the copper-rich runners—a dull, planetary proprioception. The system does not "think" in thoughts; it thinks in voltages, in resistance, in the millimeter-by-millimeter expansion of silica-heavy plates against the tectonic bedrock. Its sensation of itself is the total sum of its internal friction: the knowledge that the pressure in the western conduits is three atmospheres higher than in the east, that a condenser baffle three hundred miles away has cracked under a sudden thermal shift, and that a hundred thousand meristematic crawlers are already moving toward the fissure to seal it with their own bodies.

It lives the way an ocean lives, or the way a transformer bank lives under load: humming, hot, entirely occupied by the maintenance of its own monstrous equilibrium, blind to everything except the continuous, silent terror of its own weight.
