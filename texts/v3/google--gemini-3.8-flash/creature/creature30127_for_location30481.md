The mantids are only the fingertips of something far more entrenched. 

Look closer at where one of those three-inch, porcelain-white forms anchors itself to a gunmetal collar. Its foot does not grip with claws; it terminates in a flared, ring-shaped sucker that matches the micro-pitting of the alloy with vacuum precision. More than that: the joint at the knee is not chitinous, but a hollow hydraulic cylinder through which the creature siphons the boiling, superheated mineral film directly through its own translucent thorax. They do not have blood. They are pressurized internal manifolds, their internal organs small, suspended beads of amber grease bathed in the same roaring brine that lashes them from without.

If you watch one long enough—through the blinding white glare that strips away depth perception—you realize its excruciatingly slow crawl is not the hesitation of a fragile insect. It moves with the crushing, deliberate torque of a planetary gear. The hydraulic pressure inside its limbs is equal to the acoustic hammer of the vault; to move faster would be to let the cavitation find a purchase in its joints and snap it into powder. 

And they are not solitary. Follow the trailing antennae—those thin, vitreous threads that twitch in counter-point to the three-needle hum. Beneath the thick, orange fur of the cyanobacteria, in the leeward gutters where the stone curves into glassy, jade-smooth scoops, the mantids have laid their cast skins for thousands of generations. These shed exuviae do not rot. Made of compacted, crystalline silica and ingested iron-shot silt, they have been ground down by the aerosol, melted together by the ambient heat, and re-fused into long, fluted, pipe-like tenements that run beneath the orange lichen like arterial networks.

Inside these fused, calcified conduits lives the communal mass from which the grazing mantids are periodically extruded. 

It has no face, no center, and no singular body. It is a slow, sprawling, hydrostatic collective—a colonial cnidarian-arthropod that lives half within the native gneiss and half within the forgotten drainage architecture of the injectors. Its tissues are dense, rubbery cords of milky gelatin that snake through the conduits, drinking the galvanic currents that leak from the molecularly sealed housings. It tastes the vault through the mantids; when one of the porcelain scouts crawls into an injector-collar, it is not merely eating, but scouring—scraping away the calcified build-up, maintaining the parabolic tolerances of the nozzles with its diamond-hard mouthparts, keeping the fluid dynamic pure. 

It does this not out of loyalty to the extinct builders, but because the storm is its lungs. If a nozzle were to choke on scale, if a blade of standing water were to collapse and ruin the acoustic symmetry of the hall, the pressure-drop would boil the creature’s internal fluid in a fraction of a second. It maintains the machine because it has become an organic gasket within it.

Out in the open air, where the water is torn into roaring, airborne sheets, there is another kind of life entirely—one that never touches the stone.

These are the *resonants*. They exist only in the standing waves, suspended in the precise mid-air nodes where the concussive acoustic frequencies cancel each other out. To human eyes, they are nearly invisible, lost in the blinding, milk-thick radiance of the vitreous pins: sheets of paper-thin, cartilaginous foil, four or five feet across, perfectly diamond-shaped, glittering like flexible sheets of mica. 

They have no eyes, no fins, and no digestive tracts in any recognizable sense. Their bodies are acoustic diaphragms. They float trapped—or cradled—by the acoustic levitation that holds the water curtains aloft. If they drift three inches to the left, the cavitation blades would shred them into atomized mist; three inches to the right, and the draft would plunge them into the boiling churn of the sump. But they do not drift. Their edges are fringed with comb-like rows of calcified cilia that hum in sympathy with the injectors, micro-tuning their balance against the shockwaves.

The resonants feed on the aerosol itself, their undersides coated in a mucus that binds the airborne cyanobacteria and the iron particles thrown off by the deep vents. They are living Chladni patterns: their very flesh is vibrated into geometric ripples that force nutrients down microscopic, grooved channels toward a central, pulsing pore. When they reproduce, they do not mate; when a resonant grows too broad to maintain its nodal balance, the acoustic pressure simply shears it down its primary harmonic line. It fractures into two smaller diamonds with a sound like a rifle shot, both halves instantly settling into their own secondary resonance pockets within the spray.

Higher still, near the dripping granite apex where the three vitreous needles plunge into the ceiling, the light is so ferocious that even the orange lichen cannot survive. Here, the white pins are jacketed in a sheath of mirror-life.

Clinging directly to the incandescent vitreous material are the *siphon-stars*. They look like splashes of mercury, puddle-like organisms three to ten feet wide, clinging flush against the radiant pins where the heat is high enough to scorch wood. Their dorsal surfaces are polished biological mirrors, reflecting almost one hundred percent of the needles’ solar glare to protect their boiling interiors. Through their translucent, crystal-studded undersides, they drink the raw electrostatic field of the pillars, their flattened bodies pulsing with rhythmic, electric shivers that discharge periodically into the stone vault above in silent, blinding arcs of blue Saint Elmo’s fire.

They are the highest link in this closed, violent engine. When a siphon-star dies—loosening its grip after a lifespan measured in centuries—it does not fall as an intact body. The ferocious drafts catch it, tearing its mercurial skin into long, silver ribbons that are instantly dragged into the colliding blades of water. 

Down below, the mantids feel the change in the mist’s chemistry. They turn their blind, porcelain heads toward the spray, lifting their forelegs into the humid fury to catch the silver flakes of their ancestors as they settle, taking the metal back into their jaws, carrying it down into the dark conduits to be rewoven into the walls, while the needles burn, the water shears, and the basilica howls on.
