To survive on the shoulders of the drowned cordillera, flesh must surrender any pretense of fragility. Here, life does not spread itself out; it clenches.

Closest to the stone, in the paper-thin boundary layer where the current’s friction against the dark gneiss drops the water’s speed from a dead run to a vicious crawl, the stone-huggers live in two dimensions. They are creatures without height. 

Chief among them are the *shear-plates*—mollusks the width of iron skillet lids, their shells not domed like abalone, but beveled flat at the edges to a razor taper. The current does not peel them up because it cannot find an edge; the water simply presses them down with the weight of twenty atmospheres. Their undersides are entirely foot: pale, dense muscle that secretes an insoluble, proteinaceous cement that sets under tension. When a shear-plate feeds, it does not lift its skirt. It opens micro-channels beneath its armor, drawing the racing water across combs of ossified radulae that scrape the thin biofilm of microscopic diatoms from the rock. If one ever loses its seal, the water catches its rim and it does not tumble—it is vaporized against the next spur of quartz, shattered into a dusting of calcium before it has fallen twenty feet.

Above them, where the water tears free from the rock, the kelp is not solitary. Anchored into the petrified holdfasts are the *cinch-leeches*. They are not true annelids, but segmented, gristle-bodied scavengers the color of wet licorice. They have evolved exclusively to live upon the bronze ribbons of the giant kelp, wrapping their muscular bodies around the stipes like braided rope. They possess no eyes; their heads are blunt rings of hooked chitin that bite directly into the leathery skin of the kelp. They ride the violent, whipping flags of the forest, letting the manic thrash of the fronds shake drifting krill into their ventral mouths. To touch one would be like touching a vulcanized tire; they hold themselves against the drag with an isometric strength so unrelenting that, if pried free, their internal tissues tear before their grip breaks.

Where the human works sit bolted to the saddles, a different sort of opportunist has taken hold.

The iron caissons and the drowned turbine housings are the only places where the hydraulic gale is broken. Behind these colossal, rusted barriers lie dead-water vortices—funnels of rotating, bewildered water where the tearing speed suddenly drops into a dizzying spin. 

In these sheltered pockets, suspended within the bellies of seized steel cowlings, live the *rotor-octopuses*. 

They are vast, thick-skinned cephalopods, entirely lacking the delicate, translucent frills of open-ocean squid. Their skin is mottled like lead and iron slag, tough as cured steer-hide, and textured with wart-like dermal nodules that disrupt the drag. They do not hunt by swimming; they nest within the hollow rotor hubs, wedging their mantles into the gaps between sheared iron blades. They reach out into the margins of the roaring flume with two hyper-developed, muscular tentacles—limbs as thick as the hawser cables themselves, surfaced not with soft suckers, but with circular, tooth-lined suction cups that can bite into scoured granite or rusted steel. 

They wait by the mouths of the chutes like trappers beside a canyon run. When the schooling torpedo-fish—the *steelfins*—are driven down the flumes by the current, blinded by cavitation bubbles and moving too fast to steer, the rotor-octopus lashes out into the current wall, snags an ingot of thrashing silver, and drags it back into the dead-water eye of the turbine to be cracked by a beak the size of an anvil.

Along the great four-inch cables that span the gorges, life has adapted to the music of human rust. 

The cables hum incessantly, plucked by the speed of the ocean into a sustained, bone-rattling C-sharp that vibrates through the rock for leagues. This vibration keeps the cables miraculously free of soft silts, but it attracts the *wire-spiders*. These are pycnogonid-derived sea spiders, but magnified to grotesque scale by the cold, oxygen-saturated water. Their legs—eight spindly struts of flexible, semi-translucent cartilage—span nearly six feet. At the tip of each limb is a double claw shaped precisely to grip the twist of industrial steel wire rope. 

They do not swim; they can only crawl. They spend their multi-decade lives walking tightropes across the three-mile drop, suspended between the summits. They orient themselves head-on into the storm, their spindly profiles offering almost no cross-section to the water. The vibration of the wire acts as their primary nervous system: through the steel strands, they feel the impact of a dying fish three hundred yards away, or the shifting ballast of an anchor bolt giving way in the cliffs. Their mouths are long, needle-thin proboscises that they plunge through the rusted seals of the old cable-crawlers or into the flesh of the barnacles that try to claim the dead-ends.

And then there are the masters of the flumes: the *iron-dolphins*, though they are not cetaceans at all, but some ancient, convergent offshoot of hyper-muscled, warm-blooded sarcopterygians. 

They are twelve to eighteen feet of dense, unyielding muscle, shaped like artillery shells, with blunt, heavily ossified skulls that are essentially solid bone. Their skin is smooth, gray-black, and scaleless, weeping an oily, drag-reducing mucus that allows them to slip through the current with almost zero surface wake. They have no visible neck; their bodies taper directly from a massive, barrel-chested torso into a rigid, crescent caudal fin that vibrates at frequencies so high it produces its own faint hum, harmonizing with the cables.

These creatures do not fight the weir; they ride it like a weapon. They patrol the upper limits of the drowned range, where the swells overhead flare the light into jade curtains. When a school of steelfin banks against a hydraulic wall, the apex hunters drop out of the sunlight, riding the express-train downwellings. They fold their pectoral fins into tight slots along their ribs, becoming living projectiles. 

They plunge through the cavitation chutes at speeds that would tear a man’s limbs from his torso, using the sheer velocity of the water to slingshot themselves into the school. They do not snap with their jaws as they pass; they strike with their bone-plate foreheads, ramming through the schools at fifty knots, killing by sheer hydrostatic concussive force. Behind them, they leave a wake of stunned, pulped silver, which they then circle back to collect within the lee of the peaks, banking hard with fins that groan under the load like the wings of diving aircraft.

There are signs, too, that something else lingers inside the deep caissons—something that was not born of the sea.

In the largest of the diving bells, trapped beneath the lip of an inverted steel air-pocket that has held its cold, stagnant bubble under immense pressure for over a century, there are scrape marks on the rusted ladders. Things that have learned to breathe that compressed, dead air—or things that have learned to wait in the damp dark of the hollow caissons where the sea cannot reach with its claws. 

Sometimes, when the current peaks during the lunar springs and the vibration of the cables rises to a scream that boils the water on the surface of the steel, pale, thumbed limbs—jointed like crab legs but tipped with leathery, fleshy pads—can be seen reaching out from the inspection hatches of the cable-crawlers. They test the slipstream. They feel the tension of the bolts. They drag loose scrap, dropped rivets, and broken mussel shells into the flooded airlocks, wedging them into the seams, tightening their iron houses against the day the mountain finally shears the works of man into the abyss.
