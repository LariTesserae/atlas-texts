They did not climb down from the summit, and they did not drop from the sky. 

To the high alpine world—the three-toed ptarmigan, the crustose lichens clutching the granite ribs, the wind-stunted sedges half a mile below—this crack does not exist. If one of these creatures were nudged over the lip into the sub-zero draft that scours the pass, its hemolymph would crystallize within six heartbeats; it would hit the scree below like a bead of fired glass. 

They came from below, through the mountain’s opened bowels. They are the generational drift of the deep haulage levels, the timbered winzes, the mule-barns and tallow-stores of thirty years of unceasing extraction, drawn upward through three thousand feet of vertical airway by the chimney effect of the new blasting. When the dynamite parted this particular tooth of stone six months ago, it did not create a wilderness; it uncorked a flue. The inhabitants arrived riding the column of hot, tallow-tainted exhaust, clinging to the rough rungs of rising pipe, drifting on the convection currents like ash, until the seam trapped them in its dead-end heat.

At first glance the carpet seems uniform, a single crawling fleece of dull copper and soot. But look into the grain of it, where the light of noon dies against the mica faces, and the stratification of this small, crowded world shows itself.

Nearest the lip, where the stone is relatively cool—perhaps only the temperature of a horse’s flank after a trot—live the camel-crickets. They are monstrously deformed by their ancestral life in the lower drifts: pale, humpbacked, completely destitute of wings, their antennae four times the length of their bodies. Here, their whips of antennae are kept in perpetual, trembling motion, not waving through the air, but sweeping the quartz grain of the rock walls, reading the micro-topography. Their legs are muscular and banded with dark chitin, but they never leap. To leap in this crack is to smash one's head against an opposite wall of fresh-sheared granite sharp as broken bottle glass. Instead, they walk with a dry, mechanical gait, their tarsi tipped with double hooks that find purchase in the microscopic tears left by the drill steels. 

They feed almost exclusively on the margins. When a night-moth, blinded by the electric arc-lights down at the tram-terminal, is caught in the mountain’s draft and hurled into the updraft, it cooks in mid-air. Its dried remains flutter onto the ledge like burned paper. The crickets are there to receive it. They do not tear it with the frantic hunger of outdoor scavengers; they surround it like workmen dismantling an engine. Three or four will plant their rear legs against the stone and work their heavy, brown-toothed mandibles into the thorax, methodically pulverizing the dried flight muscles into powder, passing the meal inward to their crops without a sound.

Beneath the crickets, where the cleft narrows to three inches and the rock radiates a heat that would scorch pine needles, the carpet belongs entirely to the dermestids. 

They are shorter than a thumbnail, flat as hammered pennies, their elytra so heavily sclerotized and polished by grease that they reflect the faint amber glare of the lower vent-glow like drops of melted pitch. These are the true masters of the heat. Their bodies contain almost no free water. What little moisture they retain is locked within a wax layer so dense that their shells feel cool to the touch, like smooth river slate, even while the granite beneath them is hot enough to raise a blister on human skin. 

Their life is lived entirely in the crawl. Because the space is narrow and the population counts in the tens of thousands, they do not walk on stone; they walk on each other. The lowest layer grips the rock dust; the second layer grips the backs of the first; the third moves over the second. There is no panic in this stack, no frantic churning. It is a slow, hydraulic circulation. A beetle will burrow downward into the mass, wedge its spatulate head between two neighbors, lever its way toward the rock face to graze on the microscopic crust of engine-oil soot and blown grease, and then, hours later, be displaced upward by the blind thrust of another feeder below.

The vibration governs them more than the sun. 

At seventy-two strokes per minute, the lower mills are not a sound but a physical pulse that displaces the rock by fractions of a micron. The insects have grown into this meter. Watch the antennae of the crickets: they do not twitch at random; they nod in strict, metronomic unison with the fall of the five-ton stamps three levels below. It is their internal clock. When the stamps pause—when an ore car derails on the lower incline or a bearing runs hot and the thrum suddenly dies—the entire population freezes. The silence is instantaneous and absolute. Millions of tiny, jointed legs lock against the quartz crystals; the crickets pull their antennae flush against their flanks; the beetles drop their bellies to the stone. In this dead stillness, the seam becomes a museum of bronze and horn. They will remain so for ten minutes, an hour, three hours, waiting for the mountain’s mechanical heart to restart. When the first heavy *whump* of the stamps echoes back up the flue, shivering the stone dust on the ledges, a collective shudder passes down the crack—a brief, dry whisper like salt poured onto parchment—and the slow crawl resumes.

Deeper still, in the absolute dark where the fissure tapers into a blind, knife-edge wedge no thicker than an ax-blade, something else has found a way.

Here, the heat reaches its maximum, boiling off the vent pipes through microscopic fissures in the floor. In this dead corner, where not even the crickets venture because there is no room to turn their leg-joints, the shed skins have accumulated into a dry, felted mattress. Living within this felt are pale, translucent pseudoscorpions, no larger than mustard seeds. They are blind, ivory-colored, and ghostlike, their pedipalps held forward like miniature ice-tongs. They do not drink; they do not eat the soot. They hunt the minute, microscopic mites that graze upon the shed hairs of the dermestid larvae. 

These tiny predators move with an uncanny, liquid smoothness, entirely unaffected by the heat, their flat, waterless bodies sliding between the papery husks of dead generations like thoughts through an empty mind. When they mate, they dance upon a floor of crushed cricket legs, their pincers joined, rocking back and forth to the rhythmic, seventy-two-beat pulse of the pistons below, perfectly insulated from the winter sky that hangs, clear and deadly cold, just six feet above their heads.

Nothing here dies of old age in the way outdoor creatures die. There is no decay, no wet rot, no bloat. When a beetle reaches the end of its grease-fueled life, its joints simply seize. Its legs remain locked in the crevice of another beetle's back until the friction of the moving swarm wears them away. The dead are not buried; they are eroded. The living scuff over them, their sharp tibial spurs filing down the dead shell, grain by microscopic grain, until the carapace collapses into flakes, then into brown dust, and finally settles into the bottom of the crack to join the pulverized rock from the blast that made them.

They are not an infestation. They are a new kind of sediment—a living, segmented skin that the mountain has grown in response to the fire driven into its roots. They will stay as long as the coal arrives by train at the lower boilers, as long as the water stays hot in the expansion pipes, and as long as the heavy iron shoes continue to fall against the broken ore in the dark below. If the mills stop forever, the crack will cool in a single afternoon. The wind will drop down from the pass, find the cleft, and turn the five feet of bronze armor into five feet of brittle, silent gravel before the sun goes down.
