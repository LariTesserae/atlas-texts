To survive here, a body must first solve the problem of never being dry.

The smallest things have solved it by becoming minerals themselves. In the micro-crevices where ceramic plates butt together beneath the weight of three-hundred-tier hab-stacks, there are sheets of flat, scuttling arthropods—not true insects, but jointed, slate-colored things the size of copper coins, whose carapaces are reinforced with the very lime and silica needles they graze upon. They do not have lungs; the air is too thick, too prone to drowning a dry trachea. Instead, they breathe through lateral gill-slits that depend on the streaming condensation. They graze the petroleum-black chemotrophic mats like sheep on pasture, their mouthparts leaving tiny, clean tracks through the grease that are erased by new bacterial growth within hours. When an arc-welder crawls overhead or a valve blows hot zinc-spray down the flue, they do not run; they pull their limbs flush into scalloped margins on their bellies and seal themselves to the stone with a ring of glandular wax, waiting out the heat like barnacles on an exhaust manifold.

Higher up, where the conduit bundles form suspended forests three miles above the drainage grates, the long, pale things make their runs. 

Up close, they are less reptilian than mammalian, though it is a mammalhood stripped of every softness. Their hides are thick, whitish-grey, and cool to the touch, like the rind of salt-cured pork, continually sloughing a clear, viscous lipid that causes the boiling runoff to slide off their flanks without chilling their cores. They have no visible neck; the skull is a heavy, blunt wedge built to punch through snarled fiber-runs and dense tapestries of dangling ground-wires. Their eyes are vestigial—milky, pea-sized spheres sunk deep beneath protective cushions of gristle, entirely blind to shape—but their lower jaws are split into twin mandibles that rest directly against the ceramic ribs of the towers as they move. They feel the city. They read the harmonic shifts of the basin the way an open-sea fish reads pressure waves: the shudder of an overburdened pump four kilometers north tells them of an eddy of warm wastewater; the sharp, rhythmic ring of a transit sled decelerating signals a roosting ground where heat has pooled.

They run in loose packs of eight or ten, their multi-jointed ribs expanding and contracting with a dry, bellows-like hiss that is swallowed instantly by the ambient roar. Their paws are wide, splayed, and possess three opposable thumbs tipped with black, self-sharpening horn that locks into the fluting of the architectural tiles. They eat everything that cannot wedge itself into a seam: the blind skimmers they snatch out of the air with lightning-fast lateral snaps of their necks, the dead machinery-motes that drift in the cross-drafts, and the soft, water-bloated bodies of their own sick, which are nudged off the catwalks into the vertical dark without ceremony.

In the flooded alleys and arterial gutters of the bottom-most floor, the six-foot scavengers—the things that thrash like salmon through the calf-deep runoff—are almost purely mouth and muscle. The water they swim in is not cold; it is the temperature of fresh blood, thick with suspended carbon and motor oils. These creatures, pale as parsnips and nearly cylindrical, are built like pneumatic pistons. They have no scales; their flesh is wrapped in a tough, rubbery sleeve of scarred skin that is impervious to the caustic bite of battery-wash and high-salinity flushes. Their mouths are vertical slits ringed with concentric rows of amber-colored, chitinous grinding plates that churn continuously, like the teeth of a tunnel-boring rig. They do not hunt by sight or even by smell, for the chemical stench of the water would overwhelm any delicate receptor; they follow the thermal plumes. Where an exhaust port vomits boiling broth into a cold-water return, hundreds of them congregate in a boiling, white-bellied knot, rolling over one another, rasping the encrusted algae and thermal lichen from the lips of the pipe until the copper shines pink and raw.

And then there are the people.

To call them human is technically accurate, but functionally misleading; they are as shaped by the trough as the silica crust on the grates.

They live in the "in-betweens"—not inside the pressurized hab-stacks, which are reserved for the hum of high-density processing racks and the banks of automated switching gear, but in the interstitial dead-zones: the triangular spaces beneath cantilevered rails, the hollow crawlways behind heat-exchangers, the hanging platforms slung with non-corroding cables beneath the bellies of the great expressways.

Physically, they are pale, compact, and distinctly barrel-chested, their ribcages widened to accommodate lungs that must work twice as hard to draw oxygen from the dense, wet, low-pressure soup of the basin floor. Their skin is rarely seen bare; it is rubbed continuously with a dense, tallow-like grease rendered from the sump-threshers, which keeps the skin from ulcerating under the twenty-four-hour barrage of condensation. Where the skin is exposed—the broad foreheads, the wide, flat fingers—it is thick and dead-white, scored with the pale yellow calluses of people who have never touched dry wood or cool dirt.

Their eyes have adapted to the relentless, shadowless glare of the luminous underbellies and the cherry-red quartz lagging. They have developed thick, amber-tinted nictitating membranes—a second set of clear eyelids that they keep drawn across their eyes almost constantly, filtering out the blinding flares of structural arc-welders and the sting of airborne zinc. Viewed in the yellow haze, their eyes look like polished river agates, devoid of pupils.

They do not speak with the voice. In a world where the background noise sits at the decibel level of an ongoing explosion, speech is a waste of breath. The dwellers communicate in two ways: by skin and by iron.

For close conversation, they lean together until their foreheads touch, or they press their palms to the soft hollows behind each other’s collarbones, letting the deep, guttural vibrations of their vocal cords conduct directly through the skeleton. It is a language of low-frequency grunts, clicks, and humming resonances that bypass the ears entirely, felt as a heavy, fluttering tickle in the breastbone. 

For distances across a catwalk or between scaffolding tiers, they use the architecture itself. They carry small, dense rods of case-hardened alloy, tapped against the structural frames in rapid, staccato sequences. The ceramic-composite towers and steel trusses act as enormous, instantaneous telegraph wires. A tap-series struck against a pylon at Tier Twelve travels down through the foundational pilings and rings out clear and sharp through the soles of someone standing two miles away in the calf-deep drainage of the sump. Through this continuous, iron-borne percussion, they trade news of line-breaks, harvest-runs, and structural shifts. They know when a block is being decommissioned five levels above because they can feel the wrenching groan of its bolts through their heels hours before the cranes arrive.

Their economy is one of scavenging the metabolism of the great machine. They are neither engineers nor masters; they are parasites of the highest order, occupying the same ecological niche as remoras on a shark. 

They set purse-nets woven from discarded fiber-optic cores across the mouth of the high-pressure storm-vents, catching bushels of the leathery skimmers as they tumble out on the thermal exhaust. They scrape the white blooms of lime from the expansion joints to mix with the bitter, iron-tasting petroleum-mats, pounding the slurry into dense, grey cakes that they dry on the hulls of high-temperature steam lines. These cakes taste of sulfur and wet fat, but they are dense with calories, sustaining a lean, muscular, nearly fatless people whose lives are spent in perpetual climbing.

They have no concept of night. Time is measured not by clocks, but by the "Cycles"—the periodic hydraulic purges when the high conduits open their valves and let the five-mile columns of cooling water drop into the depths. When the purge begins, the sound changes from a roar to an earth-shattering hum that makes the teeth rattle in their sockets and turns the runoff in the gutters into knee-high, frothing rapids. At the first sign of the purge-thrum, the dwellers move with an economy of motion that looks like choreography: tethers are clipped into redundant load-eyes; children—strapped to their mothers’ backs in waterproof, oiled-leather cocoons—are tucked into the leeward pockets of the hab-facades; the filter-nets are hauled in, heavy and dripping with protein.

They do not look up, because there is no sky to look toward—only Tier 400, or Tier 1,000, sweating its greasy dew down onto Tier 399. They do not look down, because the bottom is simply where the water runs fastest before it is drawn back into the subterranean intake turbines to be boiled again.

They believe, if they can be said to have a religion, that the city is a living animal that must not be angered into stillness. To them, a cold pipe is a terrifying thing—an omen of death, a dead limb of the god. If a conduit stops weeping, if its hum drops below the threshold of feeling, they avoid it, packing their slung beds and greasy fiber-tethers and moving sideways along the rail-guides toward where the steam is thickest and the roar is loud enough to shatter bone.

They live fast, their joints swollen with early rheumatism from the perpetual salt-damp, their ears deaf to anything softer than a steel-press, their skin smelling permanently of zinc and the warm broth of the gutters. Yet they move through the vast, vertical labyrinth with an eerie, flowing grace, swinging over drops of two thousand meters on lengths of recycled braided wire, their amber-lidded eyes fixed on the next handhold, completely at home at the bottom of the loud, weeping world.
