The first thing that becomes clear about whatever stays here is that it has surrendered its calendar. 

There are no breeding seasons in the basin because there are no winters, no droughts, and no twilights. The biological clocks of the inhabitants have unspooled into a flat, unbroken continuum of feeding, shedding, and dropping young. Growth is rapid, soft, and slightly bloated, sustained by the unrelenting ninety-five-degree broth and the steady rain of organic grease carried down from the rolling stock above—tallow from axle boxes, spilled grain swells, the washings of livestock wagons, and the thick, nutrient-rich scum of city runoff.

At the floor level, where the inch-deep water sheets across the basalt slabs, the crabs rule the horizontal plane. They are not a marine species, nor quite the river crabs of the region’s waterways; they are something descended from industrial stowaways, likely introduced via ballast wash or timber rafts. Their carapaces are flat, square, and the color of wet anthracite, so smooth they seem stamped out of tar-sheet. Because they have no natural predators in the shallow flats, they have lost their skittishness. They do not scuttle sideways into crevices when shadows pass overhead; they crawl forward in straight, blunt vectors, their heavy pincher-tips clicking rhythmically against the uncured concrete as they scrape at the formwork seams. Where the aggregate is still leaching lime, the water produces a spongy, white-gray mineral deposit; the crabs graze this line continuously, their mouthparts foaming with a gray froth of calcium carbonate and machine oil. 

Beneath them, in the sumps—the rectangular drop-pits where the water collects before the bilge pumps throw it outward—the mudfish inhabit an entirely different tier of density. They are brown bullheads and channel scavengers, but they have altered in the dark. Their skin is scaleless, thick, and covered in an opaque, protective slime that shields them from the caustic alkalinity of the fresh concrete. Their eyes are cloudy, vestigial nubs, blinded by the sulfurous chemical bath, but their sensory barbels have grown long, pale, and branching, like wet rootlets. In the intake pools, they do not swim so much as hover, stacked four or five deep against the current, their blunt snouts wedged into the steel grating of the pump suctions. They feed on whatever the sheet-wash delivers over the lip: crab molts, drowned beetles, pellets of industrial grain, and the soft, white corpses of the roaches that lose their footing on the walls. When a bilge pump kicks into its stroke, the water drops six inches in three seconds; the fish thrash in a dense, muscular braid, a boil of slate-gray backs and whipping tails, entirely unalarmed by the suction, having learned the exact perimeter of the safety grates.

Above the water, clinging to the vertical slabs where the moisture forms sheets of rolling grease, the wharf roaches operate in sudden, nervous twitches. Unlike the crabs, they are ghostly pale—bleached of pigment by generations spent under sulfur-yellow electric filaments and degraded exhaust light. Their bodies are three inches long, flexible as parchment, and so flat they cast almost no shadow. They do not venture into the swift currents of the floor; they live in the boundary layer, the quarter-inch zone of wet air that coats the concrete walls between the waterline and the high-pressure steam conduits. They feed on the lime-green unicellular scum that gathers along the formwork joints. When a drop of water falls from an overhead rivet and strikes among them, forty or fifty will scatter at once in an explosive, silent starburst, their thin legs skating effortlessly across the slick basalt before freezing again in perfect, rigid stillness, waiting for the vibration to pass back into the concrete’s steady hum.

Higher still, where the air grows stiflingly hot from the uninsulated condensate loops, the bats hold the ironwork. They are pipistrelles, but they do not resemble the dry, velvet-winged creatures of church belfries and river eaves. Here, the moisture never allows them to dry. Their fur is permanently plastered to their tiny ribs in dark, glistening points, giving them the appearance of drowned shrews hung up by their heels. They do not hunt by echolocation in the ordinary sense; the basin is so loud—an impenetrable solid block of centrifuge roar and pump churn—that acoustic signals are shattered the moment they leave their throats. Instead, they hunt the thermal currents. They hang directly above the low-pressure vents where columns of hot, oily steam rise toward the track beds. These steam plumes are thick with drain flies and minute, blind gnats that breed in the algal gutters below. The bats do not swoop; they drop from the flanges, tumble through the steam columns with their mouths agape, scooping up hundreds of insects in a single descent, and then use the mechanical updraft of the boiler exhaust to fling themselves back onto the lower lip of the I-beams. Their breeding is relentless. The underside of the beams is coated with a deep, moist asphalt of their guano, which never dries into dust but instead ferments into a warm, sour mulch that slides down the web of the steel in long, slow tears, dropping into the sumps to feed the fish.

And in the corners where the floor meets the pump plinths—where the concrete was poured thickest to deaden the vibration of the machinery—there is another presence that is harder to categorize. 

Rats inhabit the basin, but they have been remodeled by the water. They are Norway rats, but their coats have failed them; the relentless heat and the chemical slick have stripped away their coarse guard hairs, leaving them with skin that is bald, wrinkled, and gray-pink, like the hide of a suckling pig or an olm. Their tails are swollen, flattened slightly at the base, and used as rudders when they cross the open bays. They do not live in holes—there is no earth to dig, only basalt and uncured aggregate—so they nest directly upon the yellow-painted shut-off valve wheels and the broad, flat iron housings of the electric motors, where the metal is too hot for the water to settle. They sleep curled in damp knots of six or seven, their hairless flanks heaving in time with the low-frequency pulse of the distant centrifuges. They are completely deaf; the volume of the space has long since destroyed their tympanic membranes, and they navigate purely by the heavy, rhythmic thrum of the floor against their footpads. When they hunt, they slide into the inch-deep water with a flat, greasy ripple, stalking the crabs from behind, pinning the carapaces with their rubbery paws, and biting clean through the shell behind the eye stalks with teeth stained sulfur-yellow from the runoff.

Occasionally, there are signs of human intervention, but never of human residence. A yellow wax pencil mark on a junction box. A broken length of high-pressure hose left coiled near a gutter, its brass fitting already turning bright turquoise under the acid condensation. The human beings who come here do not stay; they are tenders who descend through the iron trapdoors in the track beds, sealed into heavy rubber suits and canvas respirators, their ears plugged with wax against the crushing weight of the noise. They move through the bays like divers on a seabed, their lanterns throwing thick, butter-colored cones through the mist, checking the packing glands on the pumps, clearing a dead mudfish from an intake valve with a steel rake, and retreating up the vertical ladders within twenty minutes, their suits dripping with warm slime.

To the things that live below, these visits are no more significant than the falling of an iron rivet or a shift in the track ballast above. The water continues to sheet across the gray basalt; the pipistrelles drop through the steam plumes; the hairless rats groom their wet skin upon the motor blocks; and the mudfish, blind and heavy-shouldered, keep their snouts pointed forever upstream into the hot, mechanical mouth of the city.
