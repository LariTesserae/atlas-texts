Where rock and machinery have no distinct lineage, life does not bother with the distinction either. 

The tallow smell begins with the things that grease the faults. 

Between the faces of the grinding iron strata, in the narrow, lightless clearances where miles of stone shift without screeching, dwell the *gasket-swarms*. They are soft, vast, flattened organisms, pale as salt pork and thick as mattressing, that have wedged themselves into every shear-plane. They do not merely tolerate the incomprehensible tonnage of the moving plates; they crave it. Their bodies are built of dense, cross-laminated cartilage and pressurized lipid chambers, like industrial cushions of living lard. As a basalt cam rotates or a reciprocating terrace sinks three yards into the gorge, these creatures are crushed to within a hairsbreadth of their fluid limits, and in response, their skin weeps a warm, pale, tallowy sebum directly into the seam. It is this secretion that drowns the friction, turning what should be a cataclysm of tearing metal into a muffled, wet exhalation. 

They feed on the pulverized flour of the rock itself—iron shavings, sheared pyrite, and the mineral froth that leaches out of the cold-rolled zinc. When a plate withdraws, opening a temporary vacuum, the gasket-swarms swell back into the void with a soft, tearing sound, their blind margins rippling with rows of horn-lipped siphons that vacuum up the fresh filings before the next stroke comes down.

Because the rock does not stay still long enough to form soil, nothing here can afford a permanent root. The loop-ferns and liverworts that carpet the lower ledges are not truly plants; they are vascular cables that haul themselves along. Their rootlets are tiny, ratcheting hooks of biological cast-zinc that drag their heavy, compost-warm mats across the cliffsides, fleeing the cold downdrafts and following the thermal seams. 

Grazing among them are the *caliper-striders*. These are creatures the size of wolfhounds, though built on the plan of folded compasses: three long, jointed limbs made of a dull, grey composite that resembles vulcanized horn reinforced with longitudinal ribs of native wire. They possess no eyes, for the copper gloom holds little information worth seeing. Instead, their heads are blunt, double-ended wedges that act as acoustic and galvanic sensors. They drive their needle-like feet directly into the crevices of the basalt, anchoring themselves against the shuddering tremors of the terraces. 

A caliper-strider does not eat by chewing; it inserts a long, flexible stylus of flexible horn into the egg-bearing fissures of the rock, tapping into the clusters of brass-shelled embryos laid by the mica-crabs. It vibrates this stylus at high frequency, liquefying the egg contents through the shell and drinking them out like yolk through a pinhole. When they sleep, they lock their limbs into rigid tripods, their joints chemically seizing with a chalky calcium precipitation that makes them nearly indistinguishable from natural iron struts—until a sudden shift in the ground dissolves the lock, and they skitter off on their point-contacts, silent as spiders on a drumskin.

Higher up, where the air thickens with greasy fog and the heat from the subterranean exhausts begins to shimmer against the black needles, the ecology ceases to crawl and begins to drift.

The leather mantas that navigate the spires are not solitary; each is an archipelago. Their dorsal surfaces—wide, dimpled expanses of brown, methane-stretched hide—are pitted with steaming salt-wells where whole communities of blind, gossamer-winged scavengers breed. These are the *siphon-moths*, insects whose carapaces are brittle films of amber and whose proboscises are hypodermic tubes of spring-tempered bone. They do not feed on nectar, but tap directly into the manta’s primary gas-veins, drinking the warm, enzyme-thick lipids that keep the great bladders supple. 

In return, the moths act as a nervous perimeter. When a manta drifts too near a razor-crested iron needle—where a sudden gust might slice its belly open—the moths detect the electrostatic differential between the stone and the creature's hide. Tens of thousands of them will simultaneously beat their wings against the manta’s skin, generating a synchronized, localized sting that causes the great animal to spasm its ventral flukes, steering lazily away from the blade in the nick of time.

Deeper still, down in the blind manifolds that plunge vertically toward the rift’s unplumbed heart, life takes on a strictly galvanic logic. 

There are things down in the vertical shafts that never see even the twilight. They resemble long, segmented ribbons—thirty, fifty, a hundred yards in length—suspended like plumb-lines in the stagnant, tallow-scented air. These are the *pile-worms*. Their bodies are alternating discs of greasy, acid-soaked muscle and wafer-thin plates of biological copper. They do not move. One end is anchored by a barbed zinc talon into a high iron vein; the other trails miles below into the hot, mineral-rich brine pools that boil silently in the dark. 

The worms generate their metabolic heat by acting as living salt-bridges between the rift’s differing metallic strata. Their blood, dense with vitriol and dissolved iron, does not pump by muscular contraction, but by continuous, low-voltage electro-osmosis. If you were to grasp one of the pile-worms where it hangs in the mist, your hand would not feel a heartbeat; you would feel the dry, unvarying thrum of a transformer, humming at sixty cycles against the palm, its tissue cold on the negative side and uncomfortably hot on the positive.

And yet, despite the blind, frantic machinery of the place, there are moments that suggest an emerging, terrible intention.

Occasionally, along the stepped reciprocating terraces, figures are seen that do not scuttle or drift. They are upright, five or six feet high, but they are not men; their forms are too fluid, too temporary, like statues cast from slag that have not yet fully cooled. Their skin is a dark, slaty skin that constantly forms and sloughs off a crust of fine, grey scale. They have no faces, only a horizontal, lip-like fissure across the dome of the head that vents short, rhythmic puffs of the same white, odorless steam that rises from the mountain’s exhausts.

These are the *Tenders*, though what they tend is entirely unclear. They possess long, multi-jointed fingers made of bare, exposed bone that clinks like ironware when they move. They spend days—sometimes weeks—standing beside a single basalt cog or a sliding zinc face, with one hand laid flat against the moving surface. 

They do not seem to feed. They do not mate in any way that involves two bodies; instead, when one becomes too encrusted with zinc-oxide and mineral scale to move, it simply allows itself to be caught between two converging rock-pistons. It is folded into the seam without a cry, crushed into the tallow-layer, its minerals contributing to the next iteration of the stone, while from the warm compost of the nearby liverworts, a new Tender slowly pulls itself free of the green, wire-like fronds, still wet, still steaming, its fingers already flexing in search of a cold iron edge to monitor.

They seem to be listening. If you watch them from a terrace above, through the copper haze, you see them lean their blunt, steamy heads against the cliffs, feeling the internal revolutions of the rift—not as if they are waiting for a machine to break down, but as if they are waiting for it to finally, after an eternity of blind, grinding trial and error, hit upon the one combination of parts that will allow the whole mountain to wake up.
