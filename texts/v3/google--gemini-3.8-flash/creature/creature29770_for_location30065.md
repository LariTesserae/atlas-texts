The col is not an ecosystem in any standard sense; it is a strainer. 

Rising hot from the valleys five thousand meters below, the violent updrafts of midday act as a chimney, sweeping up millions of tons of biological dust: wind-blown spores, dead aphids, thistle-down, frost-killed midges, and the microscopic detritus of forests and riverbeds. When these currents hit the sheer face of the hornblende and stall out against the thin, freezing violet of the upper sky, the fallout drifts down into the maze of stone lamellae like gray snow. 

This aeolian drift is what feeds the col.

Between the stone teeth, in the moist, hair-thin seams where the bronze gutters weep, lives an animal that has surrendered width entirely. It is a rodent, or the compressed descendant of one—a creature no thicker than a folded sheepskin ledger, perhaps an inch and a half across its ribcage, but nearly two feet long from its blunt snout to its hairless, calloused tail. Its body is flattened laterally, like a sole or a dried ling-fish, its ribs hinged to collapse entirely inward so that it can wedge itself six feet deep into the slate clefts where the shadows remain twenty below zero.

Its fur is not soft down; it is a stiff, greasy coat of barbed guard-hairs, the color of dirty hornblende, heavily oiled with an unfreezable sebum that repels liquid water and clings to stone. It does not run. It levers itself through the ribbons of slate on spatulate claws that are broad, shovel-shaped, and worn smooth by the constant scraping of quartz. 

It lives by licking. In the morning, as the sweating begins, it follows the line of sun-strike, moving just inches ahead of the freeze-line. It presses its rubbery, bifurcated upper lip against the stone and scours the wet mica clean of the wind-dropped silt, consuming the thawed insects, the pollen crust, and the mineral salts sweating out of the iron plates. Its metabolism is absurdly slow, punctuated by long stretches of torpor where its body temperature drops to within a fraction of a degree of the rock around it, its heart knocking once every forty seconds against its compressed breastbone.

It is from this animal that the hair of the drip-guides was harvested.

The one who harvested it, and who hammered the copper, lives in the stall at the base of the horn.

She is human, though the environment has worked on her until the boundary between her flesh and the mineral world is largely a matter of temperature. She does not sleep lying down; there is no floor space for it, and the contact with the stone would leach her core heat within an hour. Instead, she wedges herself upright in the corner where the dry-stacked hornblende meets the living rock, wrapped in five layers of yak-felt and the smoke-cured skins of the flat-ribbed rodents, sewn together with split mountain-sheep sinew. 

Her frame is deformed by the space. Her clavicles are narrow, her shoulders naturally rolled inward into an almost permanent hunch, but her thoracic cage is massive, jutting forward like the prow of a skiff—a barrel-lunged bellows built to pull sustenance from an atmosphere that has almost nothing to give. 

Her skin, exposed to the ruthless scald of the troposphere, is not brown or tanned; it is the deep, liver-colored purple of dried beef, blackened around the cheekbones by smears of charred tallow to cut the glare. Her eyelids are swollen, thick-lidded, and slit-like, rimmed with the red crust of chronic actinic inflammation. When she opens them, her eyes are the clouded, watery hazel of mountain people whose corneas have been pitted by decades of blowing ice-grit.

She moves only when the sun dictates. Her life is calibrated to the movement of the needle-shadows across the quartz basins. 

At the first yellow burn of light across the eastern horn, she stirs with a slow, dry stiffness, like an iron hinge being forced. Her joints click audibly in the freezing stillness. Before the frost has even begun to sweat, she is out among the stone leaves, moving sideways—never frontally—her hips canted at an angle, her bare, horn-callused feet finding the polished ledges of quartz she has stepped on ten thousand times. 

Her work is maintenance. The copper channels warp under the violent thermal shifts; every morning, the expansion and contraction levers them out of their beds. With the stone mallet, she taps them back into alignment, her touch surprisingly delicate, striking the soft, greened metal with dull, wooden thumps that carry no echo into the vast drop below. She clears the shale lids of ice-skins. She clears the rodent droppings from the drip-guides.

When she drinks, she does not cup the water or use a vessel. She waits until a quartz basin fills to the depth of three fingers, removes the shale slab, and lowers a thin reed of beaten copper to the bottom. She drinks slowly, sucking the water up in measured, rhythmic draws. It is bitter with iron, silted with mica powder that grinds between her remaining molars, and so cold it induces an immediate, blinding ache behind her brow-ridge, but she holds it in her mouth until her tongue warms it before letting it down her gullet.

Her calories do not come from the col. In a dry niche behind the three copper thermal pins, wrapped in bladders of grease, are cakes of parched barley meal, brick-hard bricks of tea, and rancid tallow brought up months ago—or years ago—from the valleys that sit beneath the cloud-floor. She shaves them with a small blade made of ground scrap-iron, no larger than a thumbnail, chewing each crumb for minutes until her saliva breaks it down into an oily mash.

She does not speak, not because of a vow, but because the air is so dry it turns the vocal cords to stiff rawhide. When she breathes heavily, during the midday scramble to empty the basins before the shadow-line overtakes them, her lungs make a wet, reedy wheeze that sounds identical to the wind moving through the slate lamellae.

In the late afternoon, when the western horn finally eclipses the sun and the col drops forty degrees in the space of twenty heartbeats, she retreats to the stall. The three copper needles, having absorbed the unmitigated glare of the day, are hot to the touch at their bases, radiating a tiny, localized halo of warmth into the hornblende floor—scarcely enough to warm an open palm, but enough to keep her blood from solidifying while she sets her knees against her chest.

A single alpine chough, blown high off its migratory route across the range, sometimes alights on the tip of the highest needle before dusk. Its feathers are glossy, oiled black, its beak yellow as butter. It does not fear her; they are both intruders held in suspension by the pressure of the lower air. She reaches out a hand—her fingers thick, blunt, and ending in nails split down the grain like cedar shingles—and offers it a pinch of barley dust mixed with tallow. 

The bird takes it from the leather of her palm with a sharp, dry *tock* of its beak, hesitates, and then launches itself out over the edge, dropping like a lead weight into the violet abyss toward the clouds, where there is breath, and soil, and an end to the light. 

She remains, her back pressed hard against the living rock, watching the copper gutters crack and split with the frost, waiting for the dark to finish closing the book.
