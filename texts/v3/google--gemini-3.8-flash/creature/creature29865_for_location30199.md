Underneath the rust-colored moss, where the peat transitions from living fiber to anoxic black butter, the earth is inhabited by things that do not swim or crawl so much as they displace. 

These are the blind miners of the bog: segmented, spade-snouted worms the thickness of a man’s calf, their ringed cuticles impregnated with nodules of pyrite that scrape against the granite sub-floor with the sound of a distant trowel. They do not turn over soil in search of rotting leaf-litter—there is little true rot here, only cold tanning and mineral saturation. Instead, they chew through the dense seams of bog iron, ingesting the metallic mud and passing it behind them as dense, extruded ingots of slag that form porous, subterranean causeways. When a section of the peat-shelf sags under the weight of the rain, it is often these compacted slag-trails beneath that keep the whole sodden terrace from sloughing into the gorges.

Above them, within the spongy, waterlogged mattresses of the sphagnum itself, live the things that drink the oil. 

Around the natural seeps, where bitumen oozes cold and viscous into the grease-slicked puddles, the water striders are broad as dinner plates, their legs jointed like heavy calipers and tipped with dense, water-repellent hair that refuses the surface tension of water only to skate effortlessly across the heavier petroleum slicks. They do not skate quickly. They slide with a languid, hydraulic grace, their pedipalps tipped with hollow, needle-like styluses that pierce the bitumen scum to draw up the long-chain hydrocarbons. In their abdomens, specialized anaerobic chambers harbor sulfur-reducing bacteria that glow with an unsteady, faint greenish phosphor—not to attract mates in the fog, for they are entirely without eyes, but as a byproduct of a metabolism that cracks cold crude into animal heat. When a step disturbs the moss near a seep, these striders do not scatter; they sink slowly into the black fluid until only the dull, green-yellow smolder of their bellies remains visible through the oil.

In the rainwater gathered within the tough, leather-skinned rosettes that crown the dwarf podocarps, another small world maintains its cold neutrality. These aerial cisterns are miniature lakes of iron-water, skimmed with morning ice. In each sits a solitary, crab-like creature with a carapace of calcified slate, flat enough to wedge between the stiff leaves. They are patient beyond measure. They have no gills and no lungs; they take their oxygen directly through porous plates on their ventral sides, feeding on the airborne spore-fall of the galvanic mosses. When a droplet falls into the rosette from the needles above, the vibration ripples through the basin, and the crab shifts its legs a fraction of a millimeter to re-orient against the current, maintaining a life that unfolds over centuries in a single cup of water.

The ungulates that move in deliberate files across the shoulders of the cordillera are known to the ground only as a rhythm.

They are called *moros* by no one, for there is no one here to name them, but they carry the bearing of ancient, unhurried cargo. Their legs are short, thick as bridge-timbers, and terminate in broad, split hooves whose undersides are textured like the tread of an industrial tire—rough, vulcanized horn that grips the slippery, granite sills without slipping. Their fleece is not wool, but a dense, layered thatch of hollow, wiry bristles that exude a pale, paraffin-scented lanolin in such volume that water does not simply bead on them; it shoots off their flanks in sheets, leaving their inner skin dry and unnaturally warm.

Their heads are low, carried on thick, rigid cervical columns that do not turn easily to the left or right. They do not graze with a sweeping motion; they scrape. Their incisors are reinforced with a hard enamel rich in fluorapatite and native copper, giving their teeth a tarnished, greenish-bronze patina. They apply these heavy chisels to the copper-veined rocks, shaving off the dense pelts of galvanic moss in deliberate, measured strokes. Because the moss conducts the mountain’s low-frequency electrical currents, the moros’ digestive tract has evolved to accommodate it: their multiple stomachs act less like fermentation vats and more like crude wet-cell batteries. The copper and sulfur salts they consume are broken down by powerful, acidic secretions, generating internal galvanic potentials that stimulate their sluggish muscular systems. A moro resting on a cold ledge does not shiver; it hums. If one were to place an open palm against the beast’s brisket, one would feel not merely the heavy, rhythmic stroke of its four-chambered heart—beating perhaps ten times a minute—but a steady, low-voltage thrum, a warm vibration like that of an idle transformer box.

They have no vocal chords. Their nostrils, lined with thick, filter-like whiskers that trap the airborne coal-dust and mineral aerosols of the fissures, close tightly between breaths. To communicate across the deadened distances, they use their mass. A bull moro will halt upon a granite shelf that spans a subterranean vent, lower his skull, and strike the rock with his fore-hoof. The blow is dull, lacking treble, but the shockwave travels through the bedrock and the peat-mattress for miles, arriving at the bellies of the other files as a distinct, codelike tremor: *pressure venting; move upward; ice on the western sill.*

Where there are grazers of metal, there must be those that harvest the grazers, though predation on the cordillera has none of the sprinting violence of the open plains. 

The apex stalker of the dwarf thickets is a creature whose ancestry has long blurred between amphibian and therapsid. It is long-bodied, low to the turf, perhaps five paces from its blunt snout to the stub of its heavy, balancing tail. Its skin is not furred, nor is it scaled like the canopy lizards; it resembles the wet, leaden hide of an elephant seal, but studded along the spine with rows of cold-formed magnetite tubercles. These tubercles are sensitive to magnetic anomalies; as the creature moves through the mist, it steers itself by the seams of native iron beneath the peat and the distinct electromagnetic field thrown off by the living batteries of the moro herds.

This hunter—the *cauter*—does not stalk from downwind, for wind is meaningless in this dense, acoustic foam. It stalks along the lines of the rock's resonance. It moves only when a fissure vents or when the iron-grass clicks under a gust, masking its slow, heavy advance in the terrain’s own mechanization. Its jaws are broad, lined with flat, crushing plates rather than shearing fangs; it does not tear throat-flesh, for the moros’ necks are too thick and oiled to pierce. Instead, the cauter uses its weight to pin an ungulate against a stone ledge, pressing down with three tons of cold, slab-like muscle until the prey’s ribcage yields under the hydraulic leverage. It feeds slowly, over days, its stomach acids capable of dissolving mineralized bone and horn, leaving behind only the pure, copper-stained teeth of its kill lying in the moss like discarded mintages.

High above, in the horizontal labyrinth of the dwarf podocarps, the armored lizards move through their generational circuits. 

Their slate-gray scutes are not bone, but layered chitin cemented with silica, each scale overlapping the next like the shingles on a mill roof. They do not hunt the cauter, and the cauter cannot climb into their densely woven crowns. They live upon the lichens that encrust the black bark—crusts that look like dried lead paint and taste of sulfur—and upon the large, slow-moving pupae of the oil-striders that haul themselves up into the branches to undergo a transformation that often lasts through three winters. 

The lizards’ eyes are covered by fixed, transparent spectacles of clear horn, washed continually by the drizzling mist. They have two hearts: one small, high-pressure organ that services the brain, and a larger, lower-pressure peristaltic vessel that creeps the blood through their cold, muscular tails. When two males meet upon a bough that cannot support them both, they do not fight with claws or teeth. They simply press their heavy, iron-sheathed snouts together and lean. It is an engineering contest, a measurement of friction, torque, and mass. They may stand pressed against one another for twelve hours, completely motionless in the rain, while the piston-vents thump in the valley below, until the weaker one’s claws lose their grip on the wet timber and it drops into the peat three meters beneath, uninjured, dense as an anvil, to begin the three-month climb back to the canopy.

And in the deepest, widest saddles of the range, where the steam-pistons beat their three-second measures day and night, there exist the colonies of the *sediment-weavers*.

They are colonial organisms, neither coral nor termite, that build upwards from the edges of the thermal fissures. Utilizing the hot, silica-rich brine thrown off by the piston-exhaust, they secrete vast, tiered funnels of gray-white sinter that catch the condensed water and mineral lubricants. Within these funnels, millions of pale, worm-like zooids work the slurry, aligning the needles of iron-grass into tight, structural conduits that channel the geothermal warmth through the peat mats. 

Around these chimney-mounds, the cold does not bite. The air is warm, smelling of warm tallow and grease, and here the moros gather in the dark hours, pressing their wide flanks against the humming sinter walls. They sleep standing, their bronze teeth resting against the rock, their heavy eyelids closed against the gold light of the breaks in the cloud. The vents thump beneath them—*rise an inch, fall an inch, hiss*—and the beasts breathe with the rock, their oiled hides shedding the mist as it forms, neither wild nor tame, the living fittings of a high, cold engine that has no need of hands to turn.
