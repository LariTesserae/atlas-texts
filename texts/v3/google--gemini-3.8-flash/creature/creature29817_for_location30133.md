To live here, an organism must first surrender the habit of hurry. In eighty fathoms of still, sunlit water that has never felt a squall, speed is not an advantage; it is an architectural error, an unnecessary expenditure of salt and sulfur that would only fray tissues engineered for total equilibrium.

The dominant life of this flooded highland is therefore built on the principle of the sail, the sheet, and the membrane.

Down among the rhyolite terraces, where the ribbon-moss forms its three-meter mattress, the true residents do not burrow so much as they settle into the weave. They are broad, leaf-like animals—bilaterians so flattened they possess almost no third dimension, measuring several yards across but no thicker than a wet pane of parchment. Their flesh is a cloudy, semi-opalescent white, veined with the pale lilac of the shade-filaments they pasture upon. They have no eyes; in a world where light is constant, uniform, and without predators, sight has withered into an unnecessary metabolic tax. Instead, their entire dorsal surface is a single, continuous photoreceptive skin. They do not look at the light; they lie under it like drying laundry, sensing the angle of the sun’s slant against the black stone, adjusting their margins by fractions of an inch an hour to avoid the sharp cast of the cliff shadows.

When they feed, they do not bite. They extrude a cold, clear gastric mucus from their underbellies directly into the moss, waiting hours for the plant-matter to break down into an absorbable broth, which is then drawn up through millions of microscopic pores along their underside. If two of them overlap on a terrace, they do not fight or withdraw; they simply fuse their margins for days or seasons at a time, sharing vascular pressure across the seam, parting only when the thermal plume beneath them shifts and one of them is nudged aside by the buoyancy of its own warmed blood.

Higher in the water column, suspended between the tops of the wrist-thick palms and the ceiling of the lake, live the siphonophores and their kin—though to call them that is to mistake their scale. These are not the modest, drifting strings of a coastal bay. Here, where there is no turbulence to snap a colonial stem, they have grown into monumental, solitary architectures. 

Some take the form of hollow, octagonal bells the color of sea-salt, five spans wide, holding themselves perfectly static at forty fathoms by means of tiny, rhythmic exhalations of mineral water from their rims. Within these bells, where the downwelling sun is concentrated as if by a burning-glass, live communities of tiny, jointless beings—creatures that are little more than drops of amber oil surrounded by four long, feather-tipped cilia. They do not leave the bell. They tend it. They groom the inner surface of the glass-clear flesh, scraping away the dust of silica that occasionally drifts up from the deep scarps, feeding on the bell’s shed cells while providing it with the gentle, continuous stirring of water its interior requires. 

Between the bells drift the filaments: single, hair-fine tendrils that drop forty or fifty feet from an invisible, gas-filled float at the surface. These tendrils do not sting to kill; there is nothing here that needs killing. Instead, they are coated in an adhesive, sweet-tasting resin that catches the tiny, slow-rising bubbles of sulfur and methane born in the fissures below. The creature drinks the gas directly, dissolving the bubbles into its sap-like circulatory fluid, utilizing the geothermal simmer of the bedrocks precisely as its neighbors utilize the midday sun. 

At the surface itself, beneath the taut, unwrinkled skin of the water, exists another population entirely—the inverted ones. 

They cling to the underside of the surface tension as if it were a floor of ice. Most are disc-shaped, shaped like shallow plates of horn or amber-colored chitin, their tops completely dry, exposed to the thin, freezing mountain air, while their undersides trail long, comb-like gills down into the eighty-degree bath below. They live in the thermal contrast. Their shells are cold to the touch on top, dusted with frost in the high-altitude twilight, while their viscera are gently cooked from beneath. They navigate by the stars and the unclouded sun, steering with three paddle-shaped lobes that row through the surface film with the faint, rhythmic click of dry fingernails. They are the only creatures in the basin that know there is an *outside*—a world where the water ends and the emptiness begins—though they treat it merely as a ceiling that must not be broken, lest the buoyancy of the wet world below spill into the air and drop them to the stone.

And in the deep fissures, where the black hornfels fractures with that sudden, bone-clean *clink*, are the listeners.

They are not vermiform, nor are they polyps. They are colonies of calcified, slow-growing crusts that coat the lips of the vents like thick, pale drippings of tallow. They have no brains, but they possess an immense, interconnected nervous lattice that runs through the stone itself, threaded through the microscopic cracks of the shattered rhyolite. They feel the mountain lift. Every twitch of the fault-line, every degree of heat added to the subterranean water, registers as an electric ripple across miles of rock. 

When a rock face settles—when that hard, ringing strike travels through the liquid—these encrustations respond not with movement, but with light. For ten seconds after a thermal fracture, a cold, lime-green luminescence spreads along the edges of the scarp, outlining the fresh wound in the planet with an intense, unblinking fire. The flat ribbons on the terraces feel the change in light and stretch their margins; the great bells in the middle depths hum in resonance with the shockwave; and then the glow fades, the water warms again, and the silence settles back over the garden, thick and uninterrupted, as the plateau resumes its long, blind climb into the sky.
