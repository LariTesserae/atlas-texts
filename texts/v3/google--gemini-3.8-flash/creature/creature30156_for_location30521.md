To understand what lives in this place, one must first discard the notion of hearing as an ear-bound sense. In a basin where sound carries with the density of struck iron, every living thing is an acoustic instrument. They do not merely live in the roar; they are organized by it. Their tissues are mapped to its harmonics—the deep C-natural of the shearing cross-beams, the metallic chatter of the disc-schools, the low, seismic groan of the drowned timber-masonry answering the tilt of the world.

Clinging directly to the iron-hard grain of the lintels are the *cleat-slugs*, though they are slugs only in the slowness of their foot. They are broad, flat, shield-shaped mollusks the size of burial slabs, their shells made not of calcium carbonate but of a compacted, horn-like keratin that absorbs the hydraulic battering without cracking. They live their centuries-long lives pressed flush against the wood, sliding along the ancient tool-marks—or instinct-marks—left by the trellis-weavers. Their bellies do not crawl; they rasp. Using radulae tipped with mineral teeth, they shear away the overgrowth of pale bryophytes, not to clear the wood, but to feed upon the bitter chemical sap that still sweats from the petrified veins of the heartwood. They are the groomers. Where a cleat-slug has grazed, the black, petrified timber shines like polished basalt. Between their shells and the beam, in the dead-water boundary layer where the current cannot reach, live colonies of tiny, pale worms whose translucent hairs vibrate in sympathy with the slug's heartbeat, cleaning its breathing-slits in exchange for the sloughed skin of its foot.

Farther out from the timber, hanging into the roaring stream, are the *harp-lines*. They look like rigging left behind by whatever mind built the trellises, but they are flesh. Each harp-line is a colonial siphonophore rooted into the joins where two colossal beams lock together. Anchored by a knot of fibrous, cartilaginous tendons as thick as an oak trunk, they extend single, unbranching cables of muscle and stinging net hundreds of feet out into the black water, held taut by the brutal draft of the gyre. They do not drift; they hum. The current plays them like cello strings, tuning them to frequencies that would shatter human ribs. Their long, pale cords capture the drifting dead—shredded moss-tips, the calcified husks of the coin-fish, fragments of shed skin from the deeper chambers. Along the length of each cable, blind, white scavengers resembling pale crayfish scuttle back and forth along the living cord, never swimming, entirely dependent on the tightrope of the siphonophore for purchase against the crushing drift.

In the open gulfs between the uprights—those vast, three-mile chasms of unlit, freezing brine—the pelagic life maintains an uncanny, mechanical discipline. The small disc-fish do not break formation. They school not in clouds, but in planar lattices, sheets of silver-white coin that hang vertically in the current like chainmail curtains. Their plates are heavy, thick with barium and lime drawn from the moss-bed, and when the gyre shifts by even a fraction of a degree, the entire school flips in unison. The sound is not a splash, but a single, dry, subterranean *clack*, like a mountain of slate collapsing a mile away. They feed on the cyan exudate shed by the moss, their mouths tiny, toothless apertures on the undersides of their discs that vacuum the glowing slime as it tears loose from the pillars. Because they have no eyes, their laterally compressed heads are dominated by twin acoustic pits lined with exposed nerve fibers, feeling the approach of a wall or a predator through the change in the wood’s low-frequency drone.

And in the hollows—the long, tubular galleries bored into the interiors of the timbers when the wood was still soft enough to yield to biological will—dwell the true heirs of the trellis.

The twenty-foot salamanders mentioned in the oldest records are only the sentinels, the young. They stay near the apertures, where the water is fast and the disc-fish are swept past their blubbery snouts like leaves down a gutter. But deep inside the wood-stone, where the water is entirely dead, trapped in chambers that have not exchanged their volume with the gyre in ten thousand years, live the *Cradle-Lords*.

They are the mature form of the sentinels, but they have ceased to resemble amphibians. In the silent dark of the heartwood, their limbs have atrophied into splayed, fleshy rudders, and their skin has thickened into an ivory-white, rubbery armor that fits perfectly into the contours of the timber halls. Some have grown so large that they can no longer leave the chambers they were born in; they have become part of the masonry, their ribs expanding to brace the ancient joinery from the inside, their blood-vessels running into the microscopic pore-canals of the petrified wood to drink directly from its deep mineral store.

They do not sleep, for there is no day, but they enter states of torpor that last for generations. Their broad, flattened skulls, four feet across and soft as tallow, rest against the sill of their chamber's entrance. They have no tongues; their throats are cavernous vacuums lined with pale, comb-like baleen. When a Cradle-Lord feeds, it does not strike. It simply relaxes its diaphragmatic walls, and the sheer differential pressure between the dead-water chamber and the roaring gyre outside drives hundreds of gallons of water—and everything swimming in it—violently down its gullet. The disc-fish are crushed against the internal baleen with the sound of breaking pottery; the salamander swallows the meat and regurgitates the lime plates, which spill out of its breathing-slits and settle into the joins of the floor, slowly cementing the timber-masonry with biological stone.

The Cradle-Lords possess an intelligence that is slow, hydraulic, and utterly alien to creatures of the sun. They communicate through the wood itself. By drumming their heavy, cartilage-plated lower jaws against the roots of the beams, they send percussive thuds traveling miles through the woven megalith. A thump struck on the northern perimeter of the basin will reach the southern pylons forty miles away in a matter of seconds, carried through the dense iron-wood and the cold water. They exchange neither names nor thoughts, but structural reports: *the current is fat with lime; the fifth lintel bears an increasing torque; the peat-mat above is thick and undisturbed.*

Above them all, at the ceiling of the drowned world, where the water meets the underside of the continental peat-mat, lives an inverted ecosystem. Here, the current is sluggish, baffled by millions of hanging, hairy rootlets that descend from the floating turf. In this stagnant upside-down marsh, blind, eel-like things with translucent lungs—long since filled with water and repurposed as hydrostatic bladders—graze upon the rot of the peat. They are soft, colorless, and fragile, their skin so thin that the faint cyan moss-light from five hundred feet below shines through their bodies, revealing the slow peristalsis of their guts and the pale white cords of their spines. They are the only creatures here that die of decay rather than predation; when they perish, they do not sink. Their oil-rich flesh floats upward, lodging in the tangles of the peat, where it is slowly absorbed by the root-webs of the sky-swamp.

Nothing here aspires to leave. There is no concept of a "surface," for the peat is miles thick, compressed by wind and ice above into a tundra of frozen mud that bears no memory of light. There is no concept of a "bottom," for beneath the lower lattice of the timber lies only the abyssal floor of the high mountain trench, miles down, cold, silent, and littered with the iron-hard shavings shed by the cleat-slugs over ten million years. 

The life here does not see itself as surviving in a ruin. It is the ruin's flesh. The wood and the bone have become the same substance; the water is their shared breath; and the great, roaring sound is the only clock that matters, measuring an eternity that has no need of an end.
