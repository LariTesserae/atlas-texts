If you listen beneath the tectonic drone—if you press your cheek against the flat, unpolished porcelain of the terrace until your cheekbone takes on the vibration of the world’s unfinished core—you can hear that the thickets are not still.

They do not rustle, because rustling requires a softness that does not exist here. Instead, they *click*. 

Deep in the graphite ferns, where the stalks grow so dense they form rigid, self-supporting cages, live the things that have never known blood. They have no common lineage with anything that ever crawled out of an ocean, because here there was never an ocean to leave behind. They are creatures of tension and cleavage planes, assembled from the same materials as the scree: paper-thin leaves of dark green mica articulated by hinges of dry, braided carbon, sliding over one another with the crisp, dry friction of shuffling cards.

They have no mouths, for there is nothing to digest, and no lungs, for the thin air contains no purpose for breath. They live entirely on the shear. Where two plates of stone grind together under the pressure of the mountain’s slow extrusion, an immense, silent electrostatic charge pools along the fracture lines. The mica-crawlers seek these seams out. They anchor themselves across the razor gaps with claws of fibrous quartz, bridging the split. When the charge discharges through their bodies, their mica plates flex, fan out, and snap back flat with a sharp, ceramic *tink* that carries through the rock long before the sound could cross the dead air.

They do not die as organic things die. When their joints wear smooth from the vibration—when the friction of the world’s birth-scream finally grinds their carbon ligaments into gray talc—they simply cease to articulate. They remain anchored to the rock face, becoming another brittle layer of mineral lace, over which their offspring, hatched from dry, hexagonal crystal seeds that split in the cold, slowly lever themselves.

Farther out, where the cliff slants forty degrees into the bruised sky and the gravity pulls sideways toward the void, there are larger, slower things. 

They look, at first, like discarded sheets of the gray volcanic glass, ten or twelve feet across, lodged haphazardly across the gulfs. But they are not stone; they are suspended by long, taut filaments of mineral hair that stretch between the spires like the rigging of a ship. These are the *baskers*, though they do not bask in warmth. They hang over the ink-black shadows, perfectly horizontal relative to the sky’s broken seam, their edges so sharp that a passing dust-mote would be cleaved in two. They harvest the sky-light. On their upper surfaces, an intricate, dendritic pattern of native silver blooms and contracts over the course of centuries, conducting the faint radiance downward into their dense, crystalline cores. They do not turn or flap. If one falls—snapped by a sudden shift in the chasm’s geometry—it does not tumble like a leaf; it cuts through the air like a dropped guillotine, vanishing into the lower gray obscurity without a sound, leaving only a hairline scar where it sheared through a spire of graphite on its way down.

And then there is the builder of the shelter.

He is there, inside the stacked-slate box. He does not sleep, because sleep is a restorative process of the wet flesh, and his flesh has long since abandoned the pretense of liquid. 

He sits on his haunches, his knees drawn up toward his collarbones to fit the low, unmortared roof. His skin has the look and feel of parchment that has been scorched in a kiln until all the oil has burned away—taut, pale, and frosted with the white meal of powdered talc. It has conformed so closely to the underlying bone that every suture of his skull, every ridge of his ribs, stands out like a knife-edge under the ash-violet light. His eyes are open. They do not blink; they have dried into cloudy, spherical lenses of horn, gray-yellow and dry as river pebbles, fixed perpetually on the slit of the shelter’s entrance.

He is not dead. Death is a cessation of biology, but here, where reality is still cooling like setting glue, biology was simply suspended halfway through its collapse. The tectonic roar vibrates the marrow of his teeth, and that vibration is his pulse. It keeps the stiffened hinges of his heart from completely setting into stone. 

Beside his feet lie the two river-smoothed cobblestones. They are an obscenity in this landscape: perfectly round, soft-shouldered, carrying in their curved grain the memory of millions of years of swirling, liquid water. He touches them occasionally. When he does, his hand moves with the agonizing, mechanical hesitation of a glacier. His joints do not glide; they grate, releasing tiny, microscopic pinches of dry calcium dust that drift down to settle among the floor slates. He picked those stones up in a place that no longer has a name, when his lips could still part to make a sound and his tongue was not a dry strip of leather glued to the roof of his mouth. He holds them because their lack of edges is the only proof he has that an edge is not the only way a world can be made.

Every forty or fifty years, when the quartz spire beyond his shelter lengthens by another fraction of an inch, he crawls from the box.

His clothing is made of the braided fern-hair, a heavy, rattling cloak of leaden gray that does not keep him warm—warmth is a myth—but shields his parched skin from the razor-gales of falling talc. He crawls on all fours across the porch of basalt, moving with a terrifying, insectile steadiness, out to where the descent-cord hangs from its pale wooden wedge.

He takes the graphite rope in his hands. His palms are hard as horn, grooved by centuries of the same fiber. Hand over hand, a single heave taking an entire day, he pulls the cord up from the dim roar of the lower shelf.

He is fishing the unfinished floor.

Down there, three thousand feet beneath the ledge, the rock is still ductile. Down there, matter is being forced through the iron die of the world, and occasionally, it produces things that are not stone, not crystal, but the first blind, calcified attempts of matter trying to become will.

When the horn hook finally reaches the lip of the shelf, it is rarely empty. 

Sometimes it catches on a knot of thick, fibrous wire that writhes slowly, without muscles, responding only to the release of pressure as it leaves the depths. Sometimes it hooks a nodule of white, porcelain-like marrow that pulses with a slow, heavy rhythm, once every three days, like an isolated organ that forgot what body it was meant to belong to. 

He unhooks whatever the abyss has yielded. He carries it back into the low-roofed shelter. He sets it against the back wall, where hundreds of these dry, calcified relics already sit in silent rows, arranged by weight and hardness. 

He does not study them. He does not eat them. He merely packs them into the dry-stacked slate, using them to plug the gaps between the shards, building his walls thicker and thicker against the sound. He is waiting for the wall to be dense enough to keep the hum out of his teeth. Until then, he sits, the river-stone resting between his chalk-white shins, his dry ears tuned to the single, eternal chord of the mountain rising beneath him, thumb by thumb, toward an ash-colored sky that never turns to night.
