The water film on the fluted walls is not merely wet; it is alive. 

Under the glare, the sheer surfaces are glazed with a slick, greenish-amber veneer of endolithic cyanobacteria and microscopic diatoms that have bored into the very calcite matrix of the stone, softening it into something like cold suet. Grazing this film are creatures no larger than a grain of coarse salt: pale, translucent planarians and flat-shelled limpets whose carapaces are formed entirely of precipitated lime. They move along the concave gutters of the flutes with an imperceptible, hydraulic crawl, leaving behind trails of scrubbed white rock where they have eaten the algae down to the raw bone of the wall. When a falling drop shears down a flute, they do not wash away; they flatten their edges, creating a seal so vacuum-tight that the water simply breaks over them in a momentary silver bead and continues its fall.

The midges that spin the geometric grease-chords do not fly in the common sense. Their wings are reduced to vestigial, feather-like halteres that serve only to stabilize their balance against the convection currents drifting up from the floor. They hatch from the dampest crevices high under the rim, emerging colorless and blind. Their entire adult existence is an architecture of tension: using a gland beneath their lower jaws, they pull out a single, tallow-scented thread, weighted at one end with a droplet of concentrated mineral oil, and anchor it between two stone ridges. These lines do not merely wait for prey; they catch the pollen and scorched leaf-fragments blown over the lip from the scrub-country above. When a chord is loaded with organic dust, the midge crawls down its own line, methodically rolling the detritus and grease into a pellet with its forelegs, consuming it, and then drawing the thread clean again behind it.

The skinks are the shepherds of these lines. They are so thin-skinned that when they lie against the stone, the rhythm of their hearts is visible as a rhythmic shadow fluttering against the rock behind their ribs. Their feet have broadened into disc-like pads that exploit the capillary action of the wet stone, allowing them to remain glued upside-down beneath overhangs for days without shifting a muscle. They do not drink from the floor; when the drop falls and makes its sharp *tink*, the vibration travels through the stone, and the nearest skink will dart toward the impact site, licking the pulverized spray before the hot air can drink it. Their skin has no pigment, but their scales are cut like tiny prisms, so that as they shift under the triple-bounce of noon light, they throw faint, flickering rainbows across the flutes, like oil on water.

Below them, the floor of silted gypsum is not as dead as its flat expanse suggests. Beneath the dry crust, at a depth of three or four fingers where the powder turns cool and sulfurous, lives a blind, legless burrower—a amphisbaenian, thick as a reed and ivory-skinned. It does not dig tunnels; the gypsum flour is too dry and loose to hold a bore. Instead, it swims through the silt with a slow, undulant stroke, displacing the powder as if it were heavy brine. It feeds on the desiccated hulls of midges and swallow feathers that have drifted down from the heights and been buried by the slow drift of dust. Once every few weeks, during the breathless dead-center of the night when the amphitheater radiates its stored heat into the open sky, one of these worms will nose out through the crust, leave a meandering furrow like the trail of a dropped rope, and lie motionless on the flat flags to chill its blood before the sun returns to seal it down again.

And in the deepest angle of the perimeter, where the shadow of the lip hangs longest before noon, the human presence is not an absence, but an endurance.

The one who tends the tools is resting inside the wall itself. 

Behind the fluting, dissolution has opened a horizontal seam no wider than a bread-oven, running back ten or twelve cubits into the cooler, unbleached core of the limestone plateau. There, out of the blinding light that turns eyes milky within a season, a woman lies upon a woven mat of split salt-grass. 

Her skin is stained the uniform, chalky cream of the stone from a lifetime of living within its dust, rubbed on intentionally to keep the three-directional sun from cracking her flesh like dried mud. Her eyelashes and brows are caked with gypsum flour, making her gaze appear wide, ancient, and pale. She moves almost without displacing air. In this basin, where a dropped stone sounds like a gunshot and a dry heel scuffs like sandpaper, she has learned a gait that rolls the weight from the ball of the outer foot to the metatarsal, disturbing neither the dust nor the silence.

Her life is measured entirely by the *tink* of the water-drops. 

Her ancestors climbed down into the basin when the plateau above dried into bitter scrub, following the cool breath that sighs out of the sinkhole at dawn. They stayed because the basin does not share its water with the wind. 

She does not wait for rain; it almost never rains here. Instead, her work is the stewardship of condensation. When the high noon begins to tilt and the shadow of the rim creeps like a blue blade across the chalk floor, she emerges from the crawlway with the patinated scrapers and one of the hair-banded gourds. 

She goes to the saucer depressions. She does not scoop the water roughly—that would stir the chalk silt from the bottom and render it unpalatable. Instead, she carries a strip of bleached bird-skin with the down still on it. She lays the down gently against the meniscus of the pool until it is heavy and saturated, then lifts it over the mouth of the gourd and wrings it out between thumb and forefinger with a slow, circular pressure. It takes an hour to draw three fingers of sweet, cold water that tastes faintly of old stone and shell.

Afterward, using the chert fans, she tends the flutes. With light, shaving strokes, she clears away the excess limy crust that threatens to bridge the grooves and choke the downward flow of the dew. The stone under her blade yields like hard cheese, curling away in delicate ribbons that fall into the folds of her lap to be carried back to the niche. She knows the sound of every flute in the basin; she knows which ones deliver their drop in sixty breaths and which require five hundred. 

When her gourd is half-full, she climbs the pecked notches—her bare toes curling into the rounded depressions with the mindless certainty of root-tips—to check the midge-chords strung in the upper tiers. With a short, blunt stylus of polished cobble, she harvests the excess tallow-grease the insects produce, rolling it onto a ball between her palms. It is her only fuel, her only ointment against the dry mineral bite of the gypsum, and the grease she uses to bind the split hair of her mother and grandmothers around the lips of the brittle gourds.

At the highest notch, she pauses. She does not look up through the great, blue, pulsing circle of the sky; the light above is an enemy, full of hawks and thirsty winds that would peel the water from her skin before she could draw twenty breaths. Instead, she looks down into the white bowl of the floor.

Down there, the mound of ancient rubble looks like a sleeping animal whose breath has slowed to the pace of geological time. The swallows dart through the warm middle-air beneath her, their bifurcated tails opening and closing like bone shears, hunting the midges that have drifted from the wall. They make no sound now. Nothing down here makes a sound that lasts longer than a heartbeat. 

She descends backward, notch by notch, her calloused feet finding the white indentations without her looking down. Below, in the basin’s center, a single skink runs three paces across the glare and freezes, its tiny, transparent body holding perfectly still between the sky and the stone, waiting for the needle of the next drop to fall.
