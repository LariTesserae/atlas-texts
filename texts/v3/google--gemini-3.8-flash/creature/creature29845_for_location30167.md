The lentils are not the only things grazing the felt. Where the rotting horsehair has sloughed off to expose the hot iron beneath, the bare skin of the pipe is often embraced by something that looks, at first, like an extra layer of lagging that has slipped.

It has the color of wet tallow, or the fat that rings a cold ham. It is flat, no thicker than an unrolled boot-tongue, but it can be six feet long, wedged firmly into the crescent-shaped crevice between parallel steam conduits. It has no legs to speak of, only a lateral fringe of stiff, horn-colored bristles that click dryly against the zinc sleeves as it works its way along a seam. It does not crawl so much as it hydraulicizes itself forward, pushing a slow, fluid swelling from its tail to its blunt, eyeless spade of a head.

These are the *skimmers*, though no one is there to name them. They live entirely on the thermal gradient. Their dorsal hide is thick, dry, and cornified, like the calloused heel of an old laborer, capable of resting indefinitely against steel running at seventy degrees without blistering. Their ventral side, however, is a wet, pale gutter of mucus that drinks directly from the pipe’s perspiration. They slide over the mats of sulfur mold, scraping them down to the magnetite with a rasping, circular mouthpart that leaves clean, bright tracks in the rust. Where a skimmer has fed, the iron looks as though it has been scoured with coarse emery paper.

Because food is thin and the heat is steady, their life operates on an expanded clock. A skimmer may spend three months traveling the length of a single twenty-foot accumulator bypass. When it meets a flange, it does not turn back; it humps its broad mass over the rim of bolts with an agonizing, hour-long deliberation, its bristles finding purchase in the lime-crust, pausing for two days at the crown where the steam leak is hottest to let the vapor cook through its flesh. 

Higher up, in the choking tangle where the dropped wrench would have bounced, the architecture belongs to things with limbs.

They are long-bodied, spindly, and pale as lard, with the double-jointed, inward-turning wrists of chimney sweeps. It is difficult to say whether they were once quadrupedal mammals that took to the rafters before the shafts were sealed, or some extreme, degenerate offshoot of the pipe-dwelling geckos that infest the deeper ballast tanks. Their skin is entirely devoid of hair, translucent enough in the warm shadow that the dark, sluggish pulse of their mesenteric vessels can be tracked along their bellies. They do not have toes; their four extremities terminate in hard, black spatulas—caliper-like pincers derived from fused claws, tipped with a rough, resinous pad that adheres to greasy cast iron even when the moisture is pouring in sheets.

They do not leave the pipe bundles. To drop to the iron trench plates below is death; they possess almost no muscle mass for jumping or running, only the inexhaustible, locking tendons of things that hang. They spend their generations suspended belly-up beneath the four-foot supply mains, their long, pale ribs arching around the curvature of the lagging like extra hoops of banding iron. 

They hunt by listening to the metal. 

Without eyes, their heads have widened into flattened, triangular dishes of bone and sinus, held constantly against the pipe steel. Through the metal, they hear the entirety of the hall’s quiet anatomy: the distant surge in the boiler core miles down, the slow scraping of a skimmer three tiers below, the sudden, brittle fracture of a lime crust on a bypass valve. When a drop of rust-water lets go from an overhead rivet, they know its trajectory before it strikes. If it falls near them, one will extend an arm—slowly, without the twitch or startle of a surface beast—and cup its spatulate palm beneath the drip to catch the alkaline warm bead against its skin, drawing the wetness directly into its pores.

They eat the skimmers when they can pry them loose from the cold seams, working their thin, chisel-like incisors between the beast’s horn-bristles and the iron until the vacuum breaks with a soft, wet *plop*. But mostly they lick the stalagmites. 

The gray pillars of lead-carbonate and petrified fat that link the pipes to the deck are rich with the mineral salt of long-dissolved gaskets. An adult hanger will drape itself across a stalagmite for weeks at a time, its long, gray tongue working into the crevices where the lime is softest, taking in the trace lead, the sulfur, the ancient vegetable oils that were boiled into the flax packing when the engines were first assembled. Their droppings, when they fall, are small, white, and heavy as musket balls, rolling across the trench plates until they settle into the slots of the floor drains, slowly cementing the lower tiers even tighter.

And deeper down, where the water collects in the warped depressions beneath the fallen cranes, there is a third presence—one that does not climb, and does not feed on the lagging.

In the black puddles that are smooth as lead, the water does not evaporate because the air above it is already saturated with oil mist. Beneath that skin, in the two inches of tepid brine that sits over the three-inch iron plates, there are the *dwellers of the sump*.

They are large, flat, and circular, some of them four paces across, lying so flush with the cross-hatched plates that they seem to be part of the municipal casting. They have no skeleton, only a vast, disc-shaped mantle of tough, leathery gristle that has absorbed so much machine grease over fifty years that it has the consistency of vulcanized rubber. Their color is the precise olive-black of the puddle floors. 

They do not hunt; they are filters for the slow descent of the room. Everything that rots above eventually drops: the dead felt, the dried flakes of mold, the spent ticks, the heavy white pellets of the hangers, the occasional infant hanger that loses its grip when a pipe groans in its sleeve. When these fall, they strike the surface of the pool with that single, loud *tock*, then break the oily skin and drift to the bottom.

The disc does not strike. It simply puckers. A slight depression forms in the rubbery flesh beneath the fallen speck, drawing it into a central, vertical pore where it is digested by an internal wash of weak acid that smells faintly of vinegar and battery lead. 

Around the margins of these great black discs, half-submerged in the rust-slick, sit the solitary remnants that might once have possessed another shape. Sometimes, by the heat of the eighty-degree bulkhead, you can see what appears to be the back of an old coat, gray and stiff with lime, wedged between two structural stanchions. 

It does not move for weeks. It has two legs, but the knees are fused by calcified deposits into a permanent crouch, anchoring it to the deck plates like an iron casting. The torso is thin, narrow as a birch slat, the ribs clearly mapped under skin that has turned the slate-gray of cold boiler iron. It has hands, but the fingers have worn their nails down to flat, blunt pebbles from decades of scraping grease out of the lubrication boxes on the dead crane carriages.

If you stood before it, it would not turn. It has no interest in anything that does not give off heat. Its eyelids have grown shut, sealed by the same lime that rims the bypass valves, but its throat works—a dry, rhythmic click that occurs every forty seconds, matching the distant, low copper resonance of the shifting pipe sleeves. It lives off the condensate that runs down the bulkhead, catching it in a groove it has worn into the rust with its lower lip. 

It is not dead; it is simply finished with changing. It has found the precise thermal shelf where the body’s expenditure matches the heat radiated by the deep core. Its heart beats perhaps four times a minute, thick and heavy with blood that carries more iron than brine. When the great valve ball shifts in its seat with that heavy, hollow *clonk*, the crouched thing’s fingers twitch once against the stanchion, their pads tasting the vibration through twelve inches of forged structural steel, confirming that the floor is still there, that the core is still hot, and that the long, dark afternoon of the metal is still going on.
