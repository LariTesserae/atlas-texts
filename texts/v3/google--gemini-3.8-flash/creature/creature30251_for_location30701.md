To live here, an animal must have no hollow places inside it.

The nineteen-second stroke is an executioner of empty spaces. A lung with an air-pocket in it acts as an amplifier for the concussion; the shockwave travels four inches through the mineral broth, hits the soft belly, and tears the wet tissue against its own breath. The things that endure on the basalt flags have solved this either by becoming entirely solid, or by filling their interiors with the same heavy, uncompressible grease that floats on the paving.

The lowest of them are the slates. 

They are gastropods, though they look less like snails than like the basalt flags themselves, broken free and given a slow, hydraulic purpose. A full-grown slate is four feet across and perhaps three inches thick, its shell flat, hexagonal, and encrusted with the same green copper-scale that armors the steam pipes. They possess no eyes—there is nothing to see through the yellow soup that a light-sensitive mantle cannot tell them—and no gills. Instead, their blood carries oxygen drawn directly through their wet, glandular skirts, bound to a copper-based hemocyanin that turns their meat the color of curdled moss.

They live by scraping. Their radulae are ribbons of black iron chiton as wide as an adze, capable of shearing away the greasy felt that skins the stones and the soft, mineral lime that deposits around the cable collars. A slate moves four inches in an hour, sliding on a cushion of its own cold, tallowy mucus that keeps the scalding drizzle from boiling its foot. When the nineteen-second *thung* arrives, a slate does not flinch; it simply drops its perimeter flush to the basalt, creating a hydraulic seal against the stone. The vibration passes through its shell, through its dense, liver-colored muscle, and down into the foundation without finding a single bubble of air to rupture.

Where the slates graze, they leave tracks of clean, gray basalt that remain pale for half an hour before the iron-scum knits over them again.

Higher up, where the roots of the aroids knot around the ceramic insulators, the grazers are longer, quicker, and considerably more fragile. These are the tallow-eels, though they are not true eels, but some blind offshoot of lungless salamander that has traded limbs for the ability to thread through narrow conduit breaches. They are four to six feet in length, as thick as a wrist, and their flesh is translucent, so that the copper core of their digestive tracts—crammed with shredded scald-reed pith and the fibrous husks of moss—shows through their flanks like a dark vein.

The tallow-eels do not use the water to travel. The water is where they hunt the slates’ egg-ribbons, but they live suspended in the aroid thickets, hanging by prehensile tails from the transformer cages, their skins perpetually basting in the warm sulfur rain. They have no lungs; they breathe through their skin, which must never dry. If an eel falls from the gantry into the boiling splash beneath a relief valve, it does not drown; it poaches in seconds, turning milk-white and stiff, its skin sloughing off in ribbons to join the yellow grease on the floor.

Because they have no ears, the eels hunt by the rhythm of the wall. They know the nineteenth second as an electric prickle in the skin of their bellies—the build-up of the static charge that precedes the valve-trip. During the six seconds of the steam scream, when the Basin is drowned in white heat and thunder, the eels uncoil and drop. That six-second window is the only time they are fast. They hit the flags, gorge on whatever rot the concussions have knocked loose from the gantries, and whip their way back up the ironwork before the silence returns and the water settles flat.

And then there are those who maintain the drop-traps.

They cannot be called an expedition, nor are they a lost tribe; they are simply the third or fourth generation of the municipal water-corps that stayed behind when the lower switching stations were walled off from the dry quarters above. There are perhaps fourteen of them living in the transformer towers—specifically within the hollow, ribbed housings of the oil-cooled rectifiers, thirty feet above the water line, where the iron is warm to the touch but dry enough to sleep on.

They have changed, though not as quickly as the plants. They are completely deaf, their tympanic membranes having ruptured in childhood or been intentionally pierced with brass pins to spare them the agony of the relief valves. Their voices are gone; they communicate with one another by striking the iron braces of the towers with short lead billies, a code of sharp, mechanical taps that carry through the frame of the building better than speech ever could.

A Sump-tender is rarely taller than five feet, with a heavy, barrel-like ribcage that has widened to take in the sulfur-thick air. Their skin has the gray-orange tint of scalded pork that has healed under tallow, hairless and thick, with great, pale calluses on the soles of their feet that allow them to wade through four inches of near-boiling water without boots. Boots are dangerous; water gets into a boot, traps against the ankle, and boils the meat to the bone before the laces can be cut. They go bare-legged, their loins wrapped in thick aprons of oil-boiled canvas that drag in the water behind them like beaver tails.

Their day has no morning. It has only the "draw"—the four-hour period when the engines below slow their stroke to twenty-six seconds, and the steam purge drops to a wet, whistling sigh.

During the draw, they come down the ladders. They move with an odd, syncopated hitch, counting the seconds between concussions in their hips. A tender will wade out toward the central manifold, carrying a broad iron scraper and a willow basket lined with sheepskin. They do not harvest the ferns—the ferns are bitter and loaded with copper salts—but they shave the pale, weeping liverwort directly from the flanks of the seventy-foot organ pipes. The liverwort is their salt and their bread; mashed with tallow skimmed from the scum-wells and dried against the warm casings of the insulators, it forms a gray, rubbery cake that smells like turnip greens and machine oil.

They have an intense, superstitious regard for the amber inspection bells. The tenders will not step between a lamp and the wall; they believe the amber light is what keeps the floor from dropping another foot into the silt. When an inspection lamp flickers or goes dark, two of them will wade out with heavy bronze pry-bars, their heads hooded against the falling drizzle with greased leather sacks. They will kneel in the scalding water, completely submerged for ninety seconds at a time, their lungs empty, their hands working by feel in the boiling mud to clear the sediment from the brass lugs and thread a new tallow-wick through the casing before the next stroke comes down to crush their fingers against the basalt.

They do not look up toward the ceiling. The slits three hundred feet above—where the grey daylight of the city shows through the greasy glass—do not interest them. To a tender, the city overhead is merely a cold reservoir that leaks water into their world, an engine that requires endless venting, a sky that drops tallow instead of snow. 

If you were to sit in the high gantries and watch them through the steam, you would see no faces, only the rounded, glistening humps of their backs as they stoop over the flags, indistinguishable from the slates, while behind them the liver-colored aroids shake, the valves scream their white noise, and the deep, brassy heart of the district beats its nineteen seconds into the stone.
