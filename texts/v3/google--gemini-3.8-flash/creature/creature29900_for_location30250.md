The first thing to understand about the life here is that it has no memory of an edge. To every creature in the shaft, the universe is a vertical cylinder of bronze, copper, and iron, bounded on all sides by scalloped metal and infinite in two directions: the Boiling Deep and the Cold Tourmaline.

Because there is no dirt, there are no burrowers—only clingers, wedgers, and drifters.

The most numerous are the **tallow-leeches**, though they are not true leeches; they are flat, muscular ribbons of cartilaginous tissue the color of curdled milk, roughly the length of a human forearm. They live almost exclusively on the offset drive shaft. As that twelve-foot-thick brass cylinder groans through its slow, dignified turns—one revolution every five minutes—the leeches ride it like a carousel. They congregate by the hundreds around the weeping packing glands, where ancient sealants, rendered down from animal fats and heavy petroleum centuries ago, ooze into the warm water. The leeches press their broad, circular mouths against the seams to rasp at the grease, their pale bodies fluttering in the draft created by the shaft’s rotation. They have no eyes, but their dorsal skins are studded with tiny, copper-colored pits that register temperature down to the fraction of a degree. When the packing gland warms from friction, they flatten themselves in ecstasy; when it runs cool, they shift and ripple along the brass sleeves, searching for the heat. A leech might spend four generations without its lineage ever losing contact with the rotating metal.

Higher up, where the light from the inspection ports cuts through the tea-colored water in solid, golden shafts, the ecology belongs to the **prism-skates**. 

These are not the pale scavengers of the deep floor; these are broad, paper-thin mantas whose skin lacks pigment entirely, functioning instead as a structural mirror. When they glide through the shadows between the catwalks, they are virtually invisible—mere water-ripples the size of shields. But when they bank into the inspection beams, their skin catches the daylight-spectrum glow and fractures it, scattering sudden, brilliant splatters of oil-slick magenta and peacock green across the riveted plates. 

The skates hunt the **glass-minnows**, but not by chasing them. The minnows have no swim bladders; they maintain their position in the current through continuous, microscopic twitches of their needle-like fins. To the skates, the minnows are sound. The skates possess wide, fan-shaped lower jaws lined not with teeth, but with rows of fine, acoustic bristles that hum in sympathy with the shaft’s percussion. When a displacement pulse ripples through the column—that sudden, rhythmic *clack-whump* of a check valve tripping miles away—the minnows are temporarily stunned, their nervous systems overwhelmed by the hydraulic shock. The skates, whose flat skulls are reinforced with spongy, fluid-filled bone to absorb the blow, simply tilt downward on their wings and vacuum the drifting needles out of the water before the next pulse hits.

In the interstitial zones—the corners where massive cross-struts meet the outer wall—the **scale-crawlers** dominate. 

These are the things that make the *tink-tink-tink* sound that echoes through the bronze canyon. They are decapod-like, though their ancestry has been so twisted by the chemical wash of the distillate that their taxonomy is meaningless. They are as large as iron skillets, entirely encased in heavy carapaces that have incorporated dissolved iron and hematite into their chitin. Their shells are not smooth; they are encrusted with weeping nodes of red rust and green verdigris, making them look like dislodged fragments of the hull itself. 

Their legs end in hardened, recurved bronze-colored picks that fit into the pitted screw-threads and rivet-craters with sickening precision. They never swim; they have no buoyancy. If a scale-crawler loses its grip on a strut, it falls through thousands of feet of warm, open amber water, tumbling in silent, kicking terror until the crushing pressures of the continental engine shatter it against some deep turbine intake. Because of this, their grip is horrific. To pry a scale-crawler from a catwalk edge would require a crowbar; they lock their joints mechanically, their tendons calcified into living ratchets. They graze on the rust-sponges, scraping away the carmine velvet with three pairs of scissor-jaws, leaving clean, bright gouges in the iron that the water instantly begins to corrode anew.

Yet there is something higher in the shaft’s food web that defies the purely mechanical simplicity of the grazers: the **Sump Weavers**.

They nest inside the vertical bypass tubes—six-foot-wide copper utility pipes whose access grates rotted away generations ago. The Weavers are cephalopods, but stretched out, elongated to mimic the trailing pennants of bronze kelp. Their mantles are narrow and tubular, up to eight feet long, mottled with the precise yellow-brown tone of old engine tallow. They have only four arms, but these limbs are extraordinarily long—sometimes twenty or thirty feet—and fringed with fine, feathered cirri that can tie themselves into complex, knotted snares between the catwalk rails.

The Weavers are patient. They have learned the machine's timing. 

Sound travels four times faster here than in air, and the Weavers feel the pressure-change of an impending exhaust-purge long before the bubbles appear. Three seconds before the exhaust rose five hundred feet down gives its pneumatic roar, the Weavers emerge from their pipes. They don't flee the turbulence; they position themselves in the back-eddies of the massive cross-struts. When the blast of mineral bubbles erupts—the great rising avalanche of pale foam—the centipede-eels are torn from their utility conduits, disoriented by the boiling surge. The Weavers reach out with their long, ribbon-like arms, dipping them directly into the white turbulence, plucking the thrashing, armored eels from the foam with specialized, suction-lined hooks like a man reaching into a flour bin to pull out ribbons.

The Weavers possess an eerie, cold intelligence. They have been seen collecting dropped brass nuts, rounded balance weights, and smooth river-shale rust-flakes, arranging them in the dead-water pockets behind inspection glass. They do not eat them; they stack them according to weight. If a diver could hang in the water beside one, the Weaver would not strike; it would merely turn one large, horizontal, pupil-slit eye toward the intruder, its pale siphon puffing small, measured jets of blood-warm water, completely unafraid. The Weaver knows that everything in the shaft eventually falls or dissolves; it only has to wait for the machine to do the killing.

And far below, where the light from the inspection ports can no longer reach—where the golden tea turns into the bruised tourmaline of absolute pressure—there are the **Stokers**.

No one has ever seen a Stoker in its entirety. The water at that depth is so dense with minerals and sulfur-compounds that it is almost oily, refracting sound into strange, moaning echoes. But when the offset drive shaft reaches the end of its stroke, or when the giant steam-jackets groan against the tectonic weight of the hull, enormous silhouettes can be seen passing slowly across the dim, deep inspection lights. 

They are vast, blubbery, and ribbed like accordion bellows. They seem to have no hard parts at all, moving not by fins, but by swallowing the warm distillate into enormous cavernous guts and expelling it through thousands of fringed vent-slits along their flanks. They are the true children of the engine. They spend their lives pressed against the steam-jackets, their thick, fat-insulated bodies absorbing the geothermal heat directly through their hides, sustained by the mats of chemosynthetic sulfur-bacteria that coat their bellies like thick, black fur. 

Sometimes, when the shaft is quiet—when the check valves have settled into their long, rhythmic *shhh-tunk* and the drive shaft is merely humming—a low, booming song rises from the floor of the abyss. It is not biological in any familiar sense; it is a metallic, hollow moan that vibrates through the iron plates, matching the frequency of the continental engine so perfectly that the catwalks hum beneath your feet.

The minnows do not scatter at this sound. The leeches do not twitch. The entire shaft—the kelp ribbons, the skates, the weavers in their dark pipes, and the iron itself—simply trembles together, one vast, wet, living lung suspended in the throat of a god that forgot to die.
