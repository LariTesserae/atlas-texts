To a visitor leaning over the vibrating rail of the catwalk, the plenum appears uninhabited because the eye searches for things that move against their surroundings—a twitch of a wing, a sudden dart into the dark. But here there is no dark, and nothing darts. To move quickly in this chamber is to be torn loose by the downdraft or battered to jelly against the wet shale of the aggregate. 

What lives here lives *pressed*.

Beneath the three-inch pelt of glistening liverwort, down against the cold skin of the rusted H-beams where the metal has softened into graphite-slick iron, the amphipods are not occasional strays. They are a continuous, shifting pavement. Over a century of unbroken warmth and saturated oxygen, the scuds washed down from the surface reservoirs have ceased to be the pale, fragile detritivores of lake bottoms. They have grown heavy-plated, flattened like pumpkin seeds, their carapaces thickened with calcium leached from the eroding cement. They do not swim; they have forgotten how to kick into open water, an act that would mean instant exile to the drainage sumps. Instead, their legs have curved into heavy, inward-turning hooks that knit directly into the fibrous root-felt of the ferns.

They are blind, or nearly so. In a world of perpetual, shadowless mercury glare, eyes are an expensive vulnerability, easily clouded by fungal spores or scorched by the unbroken spectrum. In place of sight, their antennae have broadened into stiff, fan-like combs of chitin that rest against the metal surfaces. They do not "hear" the thunder of the falling water any more than a creature at the bottom of the sea hears the weight of the ocean; the sound is simply the baseline of their physics. Through their antennae, they read the health of the machine. They know the subtle change in harmonics when a high-pressure nozzle eighty feet above begins to calcify; they track the tiny, structural shudder that ripples through a copper drainpipe seconds before a pulse of hotter condensate descends.

Higher up, along the peacock-green flutes where copper salts have bled into the concrete, lives a creature that has completely discarded the need for shelter. They appear at first to be nothing more than blisters in the verdigris—pale, translucent discs the size of a coin, adhering to the sheer stone with the hydraulic grip of limpets. They are gastropods, descendants of freshwater snails carried in as microscopic veligers through the supply pipes, but their shells have flattened into flexible, hydrodynamic shields of semi-clear horn, completely smooth to offer no resistance to the sheets of rushing water. 

These grazers scrape the green film with radulae that have hardened to the density of industrial diamonds, shearing off microscopic layers of algae, bacteria, and concrete aggregate alike. They are dyed from within by their diet: their digestive tracts and mantle edges glow with a faint, chemical turquoise, stained by the heavy metals they process and sequester into their margins. When one dies, its grip fails, and it is instantly swallowed by the drop, leaving behind a clean, grey-white circle on the concrete where the verdigris was scraped away—the only record of its passage.

The air itself, which seems so aggressively barren, is laced with an invisible architecture. In the lee of the structural ribs—in the narrow, triangular dead-zones of air that exist behind the down-rushing torrent—spiders have found a way to persist. They are long-jawed orb-weavers, but so pale and elongated they resemble fragments of bleached fern-spore. They do not spin the wide, geometric wheels of garden spiders; such webs would be shredded in seconds by the turbulence. Instead, they cast single, high-tension guy-lines of extraordinarily thick, non-sticky dragline silk straight across the empty air, anchoring catwalk to wall, strut to pipe.

These lines do not catch insects in the traditional sense. The midges here—tiny, nearly wingless gnats that crawl through the staghorn ferns like mites—rarely take flight. Instead, the webs catch the drift: bits of shattered moss, nutrient foam whipped off the lip of the sump, and the protein-rich scum generated by millions of gallons of aerated water. The spiders walk these vibrating cables with their bodies pressed flat against the silk, harvesting the wet sludge that gathers along the lines like grease on a spit. The acoustic energy of the room constantly strums these threads; to the spider, the entire cavern is a single, deafening stringed instrument, and it can tell, by the pitch of a cable’s hum, precisely how much weight of river-scum has accumulated along its length.

There is no predation here in the violent, frantic sense. No chase, no struggle. The density of plant life and the endless rain of organic sediment provide an infinite trough; the only enemy is the current. 

Deep down at the water level, where the deluge smashes into the sump in an unbroken churn of white water, something even stranger has taken hold in the submerged crawlspaces between the overflow grates. In the dark below the water line—where the mercury light finally fails to penetrate the foam—blind, white leeches as thick as a man’s thumb loop through the iron lattices. They do not drink blood; there is nothing with warm blood to feed upon. They have adapted their suckers to dredge the gravity-overflow screens, stripping away the accumulated mats of dead vegetable hair and drowned amphipods that would otherwise choke the drainage flumes. They are the chamber’s silent engineers, ensuring that the water level never rises an inch above the brass calibration marks set into the concrete in 1910.

If a human were to step into the draft plenum and switch off the water and the lights, this entire, intricate world would perish within hours. The spiders would starve as the updrafts ceased to bring them spray-foam; the limpets would dry out and drop like coins from the vertical walls; the amphipods would lose the steady, rhythmic vibration that guides their feeding cycles and would wander out onto the catwalk to rot in the sudden, terrifying silence.

They do not belong to nature; they belong to the contract between the concrete, the electricity, and the pumps. They are the living tissue that an ancient engine grew to clean its own joints, perfectly content to spend thousands of generations inside an artificial noon, deaf to everything except the eternal, comforting scream of the pipes.
