The first thing to understand about the life here is that it has severed its relationship with water by entering into a pact with dust. 

Nothing drinks. To seek open moisture in the basin would be an evolutionary dead end, because there is none to be found; instead, every living tissue is an engine for extracting atmospheric vapor from the night chill or synthesizing metabolic fluid from dry carbohydrates and mineral lime.

In the upper air, the pale swifts—known colloquially, if anyone were here to name them, as *chalk-cutters*—do not have the soft, glossy plumage of temperate birds. Their feathers are calcified, stiff as sheets of mica, edged with a pale, talc-like down that sheds continuously as they fly. This dust coats the rift in an eternal fallout. They have no broad gape for catching soft gnats; their beaks are short, blunt picks of dense horn, designed to crack the heavily armatures of beetles while hurtling at sixty miles an hour through the gloom. 

Their eyes are milky, protected behind thick, translucent nictitating membranes that never fully retract. They do not navigate by pristine sight, nor by song—for any individual note is shredded instantly by the catastrophic roar of the colony—but by the echo of their own wing-snaps rebounding off the sheer shale walls. They nest in the deep mortises and empty beam-sockets of the megaliths, gluing their cups together not with saliva, which is too precious to waste, but with a brittle, amber-like resin harvested from the cork-succulents, mixed with their own dry guano. A nestling does not hatch into warmth and down; it emerges into a cold, dry crevice, featherless and grey as a pebble, drinking a concentrated, lipid-rich curd regurgitated by parents who have spent eighteen hours skimming the cliff faces for windblown mites.

Beneath the birds, inhabiting the vertical universe of the cyclopean masonry, live the stridulators. 

They are orthopterans, but they have long since ceased to look like crickets. They are the size of human palms, entirely blind, their bodies flattened so severely they can slip into joints where two multi-ton granite blocks have settled to within a millimeter of each other. Their carapaces are not chitinous in the ordinary sense; they have incorporated the powdered chalk and feldspar of the valley into their exoskeletons, turning them into walking cameos of unglazed porcelain. 

Their sound is produced not by delicate wings, but by heavy, serrated spurs on their hind legs scraping against the bare rock of the ruins. Millions of them, clustered in the deep shadow of the lintels, rhythmically strike the megaliths like stonemasons with rasps. They use the ancient city as an instrument. The stones, stacked without mortar and resting upon the solid basement shelf of the rift, act as massive acoustic drivers, carrying the vibration into the chalk drifts below. 

Down in those drifts—the knee-deep, powder-pale flour that blankets the avenues—a different kind of life moves, utterly silent beneath the aerial scream.

There are chalk-skinks: foot-long reptiles with scales so tightly overlapped and polished that they present no friction to the silt. They do not walk; they swim through the powdered feldspar, submerged inches beneath the surface, leaving only a faint, braided furrow that the cool draft fills in behind them. They have no external ears; the bones of their lower jaws are exceptionally broad and flat, lined with sensitive acoustic pads. By pressing their chins against the buried foundations of the causeways, they read the cricket-chorus above. A stutter in the vibration signals an insect that has fallen from a colonnade; the skink erupts from the dust like a spray of flour, engulfs the struggling husk, and sinks backward into the dry white drift before the sound of its strike can travel a yard.

Between the ruins, in the grey-tinted jungle of cork-succulents, lives the basin's only large resident: a creature that moves with the glacial, deliberate slowness of an animal whose metabolism has been tuned down to the absolute threshold of life. 

They are heavy, low-slung quadrupeds, vaguely ursine in their bulk but possessing the dry, hairless hide of an elephant, mottled in slate-grey and ash-white. Their ears have atrophied into mere slits protected by valves of cartilage, sealing out the deafening roar of the swifts. To these creatures, the basin is not loud; it is entirely mute, experienced only as an ambient, shivering warmth in their footpads. 

They are grazers of the waterless thicket. Their heads are massive, armed with broad, spade-shaped incisors that can plane away the fibrous, horny leaves of the scrub and the bitter, corky bark of the cistern-plants. They do not possess a stomach designed for rapid digestion; their gut is an enormous, slow-fermenting vat where the petrified brush sits for weeks, broken down by specialized bacteria that liberate every atom of chemical moisture. 

Because they cannot afford to lose water through sweat or breath, their lungs are labyrinthine, cooling the exhaled air and condensing its vapor within internal sinuses before it can escape the nostrils. They move only during the deepest twilight of midday, when the indirect blue light is strongest, navigating the corridors between the ancient houses like living stones. A single one may spend three days browsing the side of a single pale succulent, carving its surface into smooth, scalloped facets with its teeth, before shifting ten paces to the next. When they die, they do not bloat or smell. Their tissues simply desiccate where they stand, turning gradually into leather-covered mannequins that remain upright for decades, held fast by the powdery drifts accumulating around their shins.

And in the rooms themselves—the low, flat-roofed habitations where the lintels have held the sky at bay for ten thousand years—there is an inhabitant that does not belong entirely to the fauna.

They are few, perhaps only a few hundred across the entire expanse of the sunken city, and they are dying out with the same agonizing slowness that governs the growth of the lichen. 

They are human, or the narrow, specialized branch of what was once human. Their skin is the color of dry river silt, entirely hairless, dusted with the chalk they have breathed since birth. Their eyes are deeply recessed beneath heavy, shelf-like brow ridges that cast permanent shadows over their pupils, shielding them from the constant airborne grit. They are completely, profoundly deaf; the tympanum of their ears calcifies in early childhood under the assault of the bird colonies, leaving them cocooned in an absolute, interior silence.

They wear garments beaten from the fibrous inner bark of the cork-trees, stiff as pasteboard, tied with cords of braided swift-sinew. They do not walk on the dust drifts—to do so would be to waste energy fighting the soft flour—but keep to the crests of the granite causeways, which they sweep clear with brooms made of spiny brush. 

They have no spoken language. Their communication is entirely tactile and gestural, expressed through the sudden, sharp stamping of a heel upon the stone—a signal that travels through the causeway blocks to be received by the bare soles of another miles away—or by rapid, complex finger-taps against the masonry walls. 

They live upon the harvest of the ceiling. In the dark of the habitations, they set wide, shallow pans carved of slate beneath the roosting cracks of the stone-crickets, catching the shed skins, the dead husks, and the unhatched egg-cases that drop through the joints. This dry, chitinous meal they grind in stone mortars with powdered seeds from the spiny brush, kneading it with the bitter, milky sap of the succulents into grey cakes that are cured not by fire, but by the desiccating air.

They do not remember the builders. They do not look at the masonry with wonder, any more than a beetle wonders at the bark of the tree it inhabits. To them, the rift is the entire universe: a cool, dry trench bounded by unscalable white cliffs, floored with bone-dust, roofed with screaming wings, and anchored by stones that were placed before the world forgot how to rain. When one of them feels the slow, cold stopping of their heart, they do not bury their dead in the soft feldspar. They carry the body into the deepest, most lightless foundation vault, lay it flat upon a bench of planed basalt, fold its hands across its chest, and leave it to the air. 

There, in the cellarlike dark, the body joins the architecture. The skin tightens over the cheekbones, turning the color of old horn; the eyes dry behind their lids into small, hard beads; and the sound of the birds above filters down through forty feet of granite, attenuated into a faint, oceanic hum that will outlast the stars.
