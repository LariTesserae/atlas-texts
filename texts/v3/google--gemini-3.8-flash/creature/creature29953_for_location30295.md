The people who inhabit this shelf are neither tall nor swift, but they are remarkably thick through the ribs, with deep, barrel-shaped chests that give them the silhouettes of diving birds when wrapped in their cloaks. 

They move with a peculiar, soft-jointed tread, rolling from heel to outer edge of the foot so as not to set the bough-decks shuddering. Because the gangways are always slick with condensation and skin-rime, their footwear is peculiar: boots of untanned ibex-hide, worn hair-side inward against the foot, while the soles are studded with the split, barbed husks of dry mountain burrs pressed into hot resin. The burrs bite into the liverwort carpet without tearing it, making their transit entirely soundless.

To an outsider, they would seem greasy. They rub their faces, throats, and the backs of their hands with a thick pomade rendered from the tallow of high-pasture marmots and boiled birch-wax. This grease smells sour and cold, like sheep-dip, but it keeps the skin from cracking in the wind and sheds the low-cloud mist so thoroughly that drops gather on their cheekbones and brows like mercury, rolling off before they can freeze. Their eyes are pale—slate-gray or the yellow-brown of steeped willow water—set deep in sockets lined with reddish, wind-scoured lids, and their lashes are kept clipped short with small bone scissors so that the breath does not frost them shut against their cheeks.

They speak in a register that barely disturbs the air. Because loud vowels dissipate in the woolly fog and sharp consonants echo against the quartzite cliff, their dialect has flattened into a series of aspirated clicks, throat-humming, and light taps. When a weaver wishes to speak to an apprentice two levels down, she does not shout; she takes a round river-pebble from her pouch and raps three times on the knuckle of a birch corbel. The dense, live wood conducts the strike with surprising speed, humming through the fibers of the tenement until it reaches the apprentice’s palms where they rest on the osier frame.

They do not wake or sleep by the sun, which is mostly a gray smudge in the cloud-soup. They measure time by the *weeping*—the rate at which the ceiling-girders sweat. A household knows its morning by the sound of the first cistern overflowing.

Their life is spent largely in the posture of a tailor. Three generations will sit together within the belly of a hollow willow, their knees drawn to their chests, passing green-bough runners between them. Their work is not weaving cloth—cloth rots here within two seasons—but weaving the world around them. With small horn wedges and strips of oiled leather, they coax the black birch to join. They notch the bark where they wish a limb to bifurcate; they pack wounds with sour peat-plaster to induce a swelling burl; they bind two saplings together with horsehair cord until the wood forgets it was ever two trees and forms a single, thick, weight-bearing bridge. An archway is never built; it is promised, and its completion is celebrated by grandchildren who never knew the man who first bent the sapling.

Inside their clothing, against their skin, they carry their only heat. 

Between the chest-cloth and the greased outer parka, every person keeps a *hearth-creature*. These are alpine pikas, small and round-eared, bred for thick, dun-colored fur and an unnatural docility. They are kept in pairs inside wicker waist-harnesses, fed on lichen scraped from the western scree and the sweet inner peel of the alder shoots. The beasts burn through calories at an astonishing rate, their tiny hearts ticking like clocks against the wearer’s ribs, radiating a dry, furious ninety-eight degrees. A child is never considered fully dressed until two of these small furnaces are strapped over its lungs. When a family gathers in the deepest interior hollows to rest, the pikas are released into communal straw-boxes made from dry sedge, where they swarm and burr together in a mound of twitching, fragrant warmth that smells like hot tea and dry grass.

Beneath the living floors, in the dark, sour-smelling crawlways where the root-felt touches the young quartzite, lives another class of inhabitant. These are the *grub-tenders*, usually the very old or those whose lungs have grown too heavy for the high gangways. 

Down in the compost cores, where the steam vents softly through the hollow reeds, they cultivate the white beetle-grubs that digest the settlement’s waste. These grubs are the length of a man’s forearm, blind, translucent, and slow as slugs. They feed on the parings of the willow, the droppings of the pikas, the hair combed from the settlers’ heads, and the dead. The tenders live in the warm, damp crawl-spaces, their skin bleached white as turnip-meat, their eyes completely unaccustomed to the glaring gray above. They move on hands and knees among the beds of fermenting chaff, singing low, wordless songs that vibrate through the soil, gently lifting the heavy grubs to turn them so they do not rot from their own fermentation. The grubs yield the grease for the lamps—not flame-lamps, but the cold oil-wicks that are pressed against bladder-kelp to give off a faint, yellow phosphorous sheen—and their dried, ground bodies provide the gray, salty flour that forms the base of the mountain bread.

Nothing is discarded, because the ravine yields no surplus. When a member of the community dies, there is no prayer for the spirit—they say the breath simply returns to the mist that has been trying to claim it since birth. The body is stripped, washed in cold tannin-water to check the skin from going gray, and then placed into a split in the heart of an anchor-pine or laid deep in the foundation root-baskets where a new tenement is rising. Over twenty or thirty years, the root-hairs find the marrow; the wood thickens around the skull; the ribs are caught in the slow, tightening grip of the black birch. 

A grandmother becomes a lintel; a grandfather becomes the curved joist that holds the gutter out over the lane. A man climbing the stair to the upper walkways often reaches out to steady himself on a dark, polished knot of wood, his fingers resting unconsciously on the smooth, fused brow-ridge of his great-uncle, whose resin-soaked bone has hardened into something much tougher than the stone beneath their feet.

High up, on the wind-rim where the settlement meets the sky, there are the watchers—young men and women who have not yet grown the heavy bellies of the bough-weavers. They stand on narrow perches of braided osier that thrust out into the void, tethered by birch-withes to the mountain's ribs, their cloaks whipped into stiff paddles of frost by the gale. 

They do not watch for enemies; no army could march up the wet slate chimneys that lead to this basin. They watch the ice. They carry long ash poles tipped with horn chisels, and their solitary, endless duty is to tap the rime from the anchor-boughs before its weight can snap the wood. They move like large, shaggy spiders against the fog, striking the birch with methodical *clack-clack* rhythms, sending sheets of glazed frost whistling down into the scree below. 

They are the most silent of all. When they return down into the dark, wet corridors at sundown, their mouths are often frozen shut by the spray, their beards welded to their wool hoods. They do not thaw themselves at a fire. They simply climb into the willow-stack, pull their knees to their ribs, reach into their tunics to feel the frantic, hot fluttering of the small hearts against their chests, and wait for the house to sweat them warm.
