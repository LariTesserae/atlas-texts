To look at the builders is first to see only their luminescence—faint, watery cyan beads trembling in the dark like grease droplets on cold broth—and then, as the eyes adjust to the scale of millimeters, to realize that their bodies are entirely liquid-filled and clear.

They have no common name, for no line has ever been dropped to dredge them, but their anatomy belongs to the deep amphipods, warped by the staggering weight of six vertical kilometers of ocean into something resembling glass clockwork. A single individual is barely two millimeters long, arched like a segment of orange pulp, with a carapace of chitin so thin it cannot be felt as a solid; it yields like the skin of a scalded tomato. Beneath this envelope, their internal organs are suspended in clear lymph: a coiled, saffron-colored gut, a heart that beats with the dry, fluttery motion of a watch balance, and the great twin lime-sacs that occupy nearly two-thirds of the dorsal cavity. 

These sacs are the center of their existence. They are living kilns, concentrating calcium and carbonate ions drawn from the bitter brine through feather-like branchial plumes tucked under the rear abdominal skirts. When a builder works, it does not move with the hurried, frantic scrabble of a terrestrial ant. Its movements are hydraulic, dampened, almost agonizingly deliberate. It anchors its rear four pairs of legs—short, hooked talons of transparent amber—into the hardened chalk below, braces its abdomen, and hunches. The dorsal plates part with the slow slide of an iris diaphragm. From the pale cleft emerges a single, glistening bead of calcified curd.

The front legs, tipped with fine, spatulate brushes of chitinous hair, immediately take up the droplet. They do not pour it; they trowel it. Working in teams of four or five, standing carapace-to-carapace so tightly that their side-plates click like playing cards being shuffled, they stroke the milky paste into place along the lip of an unfinished parapet. The syrupy, salt-choked water reacts with the secretion instantly, leaching out its binding oils and leaving behind an insoluble lattice of aragonite needle-crystals. Within twelve seconds, what was a glandular slime has become stone, cold and matte-white, capable of bearing the weight of a thousand crawling sisters.

They do not possess eyes. Where eyes might have evolved on their blunt, hooded heads, there are only twin pairs of antennules—segmented antennae covered in sensory pits so sensitive they do not merely smell the water; they map its shear-planes, its viscosity, and the microscopic variations in its salinity. When two builders meet in an alleyway, there is no hesitation, no circling. Their antennae flick forward, a feather-soft clash of sensory lashes that lasts less than a tenth of a second, exchanging complex chemical digests: *this archway lacks calcium; the brine in the lower gutter is stagnant; five tiers down, the wall is flexing.*

Their social organization is not tiered by caste or birth, but by calcification. 

A newly hatched builder is soft, completely clear, and almost weightless, capable of swimming with drunken, frantic jerks of its pleopods. These juveniles are the riggers and the glazers. They climb to the very tips of the fluted spires, trailing lines of sticky biological silk that catch drifting mineral flakes, or they graze upon the cyan-blooming moss in the drainage troughs, their bellies glowing brighter as they ingest the chemotrophic cells.

As a builder ages, however, its lime-glands outgrow its legs. The shell thickens, clouding from the clarity of water to the milky opacity of skimmed lard. Its movements slow. It can no longer climb the vertical ribbons; it descends, drawn by gravity and the density of its own mineralizing flesh, into the foundation layers. Here, among the subterranean buttresses where the basalt cleft pinches shut, these elders become the heavy masons. They pump out massive, crude footings, anchoring the spires into the volcanic glass with thick, gouty puddles of cement. 

Eventually, a mason can no longer lift its abdomen. Its legs fuse with the mortar it has laid; its joints calcify. Rather than struggle, it pulls its antennae inward, presses its forehead flush against the masonry, and allows the younger workers to trowel the next tier of lime directly over its back. In the deepest tenements, the walls are not merely stone; they are an ossuary of millions of translucent carapaces, their hollow bodies serving as the structural aggregate that keeps the viaducts from buckling under the trench’s crushing water-column.

Through the gaps in this calcified hive slip the ribbon-eels. They are neither predators nor pets, but something closer to living transit currents. 

No thicker than the hair from a horse’s mane and perhaps four inches long, they are utterly blind, pink-white, and smooth as peeled willow bark. They do not have fins; they have only an undulating fringe of skin that ripples with infinite patience. The architecture of the city has been shaped around them over generations: every high-rise block is pierced by circular conduits, bored smooth and lined with lime, through which the eels glide. As they slide through these tunnels, their slick bodies pull the syrupy, motionless brine behind them, creating micro-currents that draw fresh, mineral-rich trench water into the interior wards and flush stale, ammonia-heavy brine out into the basalt fissures. 

The builders treat an eel with the casual disregard a city dweller reserves for a municipal tram. When an eel slips down an avenue, its flanks brushing the walls, the builders simply flatten their legs against the stone and let the cool, slippery flank of the fish slide over their backs. Sometimes, tiny clusters of juvenile builders will latch onto an eel’s tail-tip with their claws, riding the fish across the great open chasms between basalt faces, dropping off when their transport brushes a distant tower.

High above the street level, drifting between the highest spires like balloons adrift in a cathedral, are the hydromedusae. They are the size of coriander seeds, their bells composed of a stiff, crystalline mesoglea that does not collapse even under the abyssal pressure. Within the rim of each bell sits a ring of sulfurous-yellow light-organs, which pulse out of phase with the builders’ cold cyan, casting a weird, chromatic twilight across the upper tiers. 

These medusae are passive filters. They drift downward until their hair-like tentacles, studded with micro-stings, drape across the balconies of the high-rise hives. They do not sting the builders—the builders’ chitin is too dense—but instead lift away the microscopic detritus of the city: sloughed skin, dead fungal mats, and the sticky threads of waste mucus extruded by the masons. When a medusa becomes heavy with debris, it sinks into a gutter, where dozens of young builders immediately swarm over its bell, methodically scraping it clean with their mouthparts, eating the collected filth and releasing the light-bell to bob back up into the dark rafters of the cleft.

And in the eaves—the jagged, untouched corners where the new basalt was broken raw and the lime-trowels have not yet reached—wait the scavengers that keep the metropolis from choking on itself.

They are eight-legged, flat as melon seeds, with hides like gray wet felt. They have no eyes and no mouthparts suited for biting; instead, their heads terminate in a pair of heavy, inward-curving pedipalps that end in shearing shears of black chitin. These mites do not hunt by pursuit; they wedge themselves into the cold crevices of the rock and wait for the vibrations of a misstep. 

When a builder—blinded by a sudden mineral burst or weakened by an exhausted lime-gland—slips from a causeway and tumbles into the dark water below, it does not fall far. The viscosity of the brine makes a fall resemble the settling of a feather. But before it can reach the floor, a mite’s leg reaches out. The pedipalp snaps shut with a muffled, brittle *crunch*—the only sound that ever comes close to breaking the silence of the cleft. 

The mite does not tear its prey. It pierces the soft membrane between the thoracic plates and pumps in an enzyme so corrosive it liquefies the builder's tissues in seconds. The predator drinks the nutrient broth, but it does not discard the mineral hull. Its gut is lined with a specialized chemical chamber that precipitates the consumed calcium, and within an hour of feeding, the mite creeps out of its crevice, crawls to the nearest crumbling colonnade, and vomits out a dense, gray plaster of reclaimed lime, which the nearby builders instantly fall upon, smoothing it into the roadbeds with their brushes. Predation here is not an end; it is simply the city’s reclamation service, an unfeeling, digestive loop that ensures not a single atom of calcium is ever lost to the abyss.

At the geographic heart of the cleft, suspended by eighty-four separate suspension bridges made of woven lime-silk, hangs the Great Vesicle. 

It is a sphere the size of a plum, ribbed like a melon and pitted with thousands of hexagonal chambers. Inside is the city’s only warmth. The water within the Vesicle is trapped, heated by the slow, concentrated chemical combustion of millions of decomposing egg-capsules to a temperature perhaps two degrees higher than the surrounding brine. 

Here dwell the brooders. They are builders that have ceased all locomotion; their legs have withered into soft, hooked stumps, and their lime-sacs have atrophied, replaced by immense, distended brood-pouches that cause their carapaces to split down the center. They hang suspended in rows within the hexagonal cells, their antennae intertwined into a continuous, pulsating mat of sensory velvet. 

Between their clinging legs, they roll the eggs—translucent, perfect spheres no larger than grains of table salt, each containing a tiny, cyan-glowing speck that beats in time with the deep, hydraulic thrum of the trench wall outside. The brooders do not leave their cells. They are fed by long lines of workers who carry drops of chemotrophic moss-paste in their mouthparts, passing it from tongue to tongue in an unbroken, silent chain that stretches from the deepest drainage gutters all the way into the Vesicle’s warm core.

There is no memory in this place. A worker who builds a flying arch across a chasm does so without the concept of the chasm, without the concept of the arch, and without the concept of the tower it connects. It knows only the chemical gradient that cries *build here*, the mechanical stress on its front tarsi that commands *thicken this line*, and the quiet, persistent flicker of the cyan lantern in its own belly.

Yet, as the basalt fissure creeps open by another fraction of a micron—a soundless, colossal groan that displaces fifty cubic centimeters of brine—a thousand antennae lift simultaneously in the dark. The city does not tremble; its material is too fresh, its joints too flexible. The builders do not flee. They merely shift their trowels. A thousand fresh beads of lime are squeezed from a thousand parted plates, and the metropolis begins, with cold, mindless precision, to widen the foundations to fit the growing earth.
