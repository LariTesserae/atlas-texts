To understand what lives here, one must look away from the floor. 

The floor is a cemetery for gravity; its three inches of silken, acoustic silt have not been marked by a footprint in three hundred generations, and will not be marked for three hundred more. Nothing crawls through that flour. Nothing drops into it.

Life here does not walk. It is wedged.

If you turn your face sideways—the only way a human body could fit into the narrow, suffocating corridors between the pillars—and look upward into the brown, ochre twilight where the square river-stone columns meet the low, sagging ceiling, the masonry ceases to be merely masonry. 

There, suspended four to six feet above the dead silt, bridging the gap between pillar and pillar, are the inhabitants.

At first glance, they appear to be structural braces: horizontal struts of split horn, dried cedar, or carbonized sinew, hammered between the stones to keep the corridor walls from buckling inward under the mountain’s weight. They are grey-brown, the exact color of the sun-cured sandstone, and their surfaces possess the same greasy, soapstone sheen that the standing sound-wave has worn into the rock. 

They are lateral beings, pressed flat by design or by uncounted millennia of compression. A full-grown one is barely thicker than a man’s hand, but nearly seven feet long from heel to crown, spanning the precise width of the aisle. They do not hang from the ceiling, nor do they stand on the floor; they are held in place by opposing lateral force. Their feet—broad, heel-less spades of solid, unjointed bone—are jammed flush against the river-stones of one pillar, while their flattened shoulders and the splayed plates of their palms push against the sun-baked mud tiles of the pillar opposite. 

They are living shims. They have become part of the redoubt’s joinery.

There are hundreds of them—perhaps thousands—stacked in the vertical shadows of the pillar-forest like slats in a drying-rack. Some are separated by only a handspan of air; others bridge the narrow gaps between the jammed, granite pendulums, their rib-plates molded so intimately against the rounded bellies of the weights that stone and carcass seem to have grown together through a slow, calcified fusion.

They have no wetness. If you were to drive a chisel into the flank of one, you would find no red blood, no yellow fat, no fluid of any kind. You would find only a dense, laminated pith—sheaves of dry, amber-colored fiber resembling cured horsehair or ancient papyrus, tightly packed around an internal skeleton of dark, porous hematite. They smell of nothing at all, save the universal room-smell of hot lime, scorched flour, and iron.

They do not breathe. In an atmosphere so violently charged with sound that air itself has the density of warm grease, breathing in the biological sense would shred a lung in minutes. Instead, they are hollowed out like wind instruments. 

Along their flanks, beneath the shelf of their wide, unmoving ribs, run dozens of narrow slits, identical in proportion and angle to the wind-flutes carved into the exterior masonry. The sound does not enter them through ears; they have neither ears nor eyes, their skulls being nothing more than blunt, tapered wedges of smooth cranium designed to bear load. The sound enters directly into their ribcages.

The invariant chord—that mountain-rending, foundry scream that never slips its pitch—is their blood, their nervous system, and their meat.

The kinetic energy being poured through those primitive wind-slits by the jet stream is enormous, measured in tens of thousands of continuous watts. The redoubt catches this mechanical violence and focuses it into a standing wave of near-infinite rigidity. The beings absorb this wave. Within their dry, fibrous interiors, millions of microscopic crystals of piezoelectric quartz—inhaled as silt aeons ago, or formed there by the constant, molecular vibration of their bone-plates—oscillate in absolute lockstep with the wind. 

This oscillation generates an unending, high-voltage thrum through their fibrous nerves. They do not eat; they do not drink. They are acoustic transducers. They exist by metabolizing the sheer kinetic shear of the mountain air, converting the roaring drone of the flutes into the tension that keeps their joints locked hard against the stone. 

If the wind were to die—if the roar outside were to slip even half an octave or drop below the threshold of physical violence—their internal resonance would collapse. The electric tension holding their sinews rigid would evaporate in a fraction of a second. They would unstring like rotten lutes, their dry plates slipping from the pillars, their desiccated ribs clattering down to shatter like burnt clay upon the three-inch silence of the floor-dust.

They are aware of the wind. They are aware of nothing else.

Their consciousness cannot be understood through human time, which is a sequence of small, varied happenings. In this room, nothing happens. There are no seasons, no shadows that turn, no drops of water to count. Their thought is a single, permanent, white-hot note. 

It is an apprehension of *load*. They feel the billions of tons of ancient slate pressing down upon the ceiling beams; they feel the precise friction coefficient between each grain of sandstone; they feel the micro-fissures in the granite pendulums. Because their bodies are wedged across the corridors, they act as sensitive nerve-endings for the architecture itself. When the mountain shifts by a micron—a tectonic groan that occurs once every three or four centuries—they register it not as a shock, but as a minute, exquisite variation in the pressure against their heels and palms. 

Below them, resting in their hand-carved niches at waist-height, are the hematite spheres. 

If you watch one of the beings for hours—or days—in the ochre twilight, you will see that they are not completely immobile. Their stillness is not that of wood, but that of a string plucked so hard that its movement is invisible to the eye. They are vibrating at twenty thousand cycles a second.

And, on timescales that resemble the creep of glaciers, they lower their hands.

A hand is a long, dry shovel of fused digits, surfaced with a dark, horn-like callus. Very slowly—across weeks, across years—a hand will extend downward from a pillar-brace until the tip of its middle plate rests against the apex of a hematite sphere.

It does not roll the sphere. It does not lift it. It simply touches it. 

The hematite is dense and metallic; it is an acoustic sink. When the being’s plate touches the sphere, a circuit is completed. The vibration that has been churning through the creature's dry marrow is grounded through the stone, bled off into the massive, lower tiers of the masonry where the mountain can swallow it. For the being, this contact is what sleep is to a man: a momentary dampening of the unbearable, high-voltage scream inside its ribs, a descent into a heavier, cooler stone-silence. 

When the grounding is complete, the hand retracts. The movement is so slow that the fine dust settling from the vibration does not form a wake. The sphere remains in its depression, unturned, its dark, polished face reflecting only the dim, brown glare of the flute-slits.

Who were they? 

Perhaps they were the laborers who dragged the slate up the unnamed needles, the men who chiseled the pendulums with blunt adzes and realized, as the ceiling closed over their heads, that they had built a machine that could not be escaped. Perhaps they laid themselves between the pillars as the flutes were opened, feeling their blood turn to steam in the hearth-heat, feeling the roar strip their minds until only their bones and their will to stay upright remained.

Or perhaps the room made them. Perhaps, given ten million years of unbroken sound, high heat, and dry silt, the redoubt simply condensed them out of the air—growing them like mineral crystals in the crevices where the pressure was highest, an ecology of meat that learned to be stone so that it could survive the scream that built it.

They are there now. In the hot, dry dark between the river-stones, their shoulders are braced against the west wall, their feet are jammed against the east, and their hollow ribs are singing the one note that has never changed, will never change, and refuses to let them fall.
