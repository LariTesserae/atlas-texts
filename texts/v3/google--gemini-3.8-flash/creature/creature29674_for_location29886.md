To survive on the shelf, an animal must be, before all else, an acoustic isolator. 

Because the peat-floor rings with the concussive force of a trip-hammer every few seconds, any creature with a rigid, terrestrial skeleton would quickly suffer internal hemorrhage; their long bones would shear, and the delicate membranes of their middle ears would liquefy. The life that moves across the open stratum has solved this by abandoning the unbroken column.

Chief among the surface-dwellers are the **Gallow-Gaits**. They look, from a mile across the gray mist, like abandoned hoisting derricks blown along by the gale. Standing three men high, they are quadrupeds whose legs are not bone, but segmented masts of fibrous, laminated cartilage bound with thick sleeves of gristle. Within each joint sits a bursa the size of an ale-cask, filled with a viscous, yellow synovial fluid that curdles under sudden impact and loosens under slow shear—the same non-Newtonian principle that keeps the great taproots from shattering against the granite dead-strata below. 

The Gallow-Gaits do not walk with an even cadence; they step only on the off-beat. They have evolved an acute seismic sensitivity in the broad, dish-shaped calluses of their feet—pads made of a tough, spongy wood-flesh that matches the density of the turf. They stand motionless, trembling, their tiny, eyeless heads tucked deep into chest-harrows of coarse, grease-caked wool, listening to the ground. When the deep *whump* of a piston-tree punches through the floor, they use the momentary upward heave of the stratum to kick forward, swinging their great, loose limbs through the freezing slush, locking their joints only when the shockwave has passed. If one of them misjudges the rhythm—if a combustion chamber misfires or a root-crank binds—the misplaced vibration will buckle its hocks with a sound like a snapped pine spar, leaving it stranded on the shuddering floor to be pounded to a jelly by its own weight.

They feed on the aftermath. Their chests are slung with heavy, scoop-like jaw-plates of dark keratin, which they use to scrape the top half-inch of the world: a cold, fatty lard comprised of dead spore-mass, pulverized liverwort, and the machine-oil drippings shed by the high flywheels. 

Closer to the engine-trees, where the heat makes the slush boil into an eye-stinging mist of vinegar and grease, life turns predatory and frantic.

Here live the **Lard-Weevils**, though they are beetles only by distant courtesy. They are as wide as broadswords and as flat as shingles, their carapaces formed from a dense, slate-colored enamel that is impervious to both boiling acid and the scouring frost. They do not have legs so much as rows of broad, copper-colored rasps along their underbellies. They live by wedging themselves directly into the friction-points—into the terrible, squealing gaps between the spinning lignified flywheels and the fibrous pedestals that hold them. 

The heat there is great enough to char pine, and the pressure would flatten a hog, yet the Weevils thrive in the shear-zone. Their bodies secrete an oily, cold tallow that prevents them from being incinerated by the friction; in return, they feed upon the cambium-slurry that leaks from the crushed veins of the tree. They are parasites of torque. When an engine-tree finally fails—when its great wooden piston-arms twist and explode into a storm of five-ton splinters—thousands of these Weevils are flung screaming through the fog like scorched tiles, only to land in the slush, right themselves with a wet, heavy flap, and crawl toward the nearest rising tap-spindle, guided by the sound of new wood groaning under steam.

In the lateral folds—those deceptive valleys where the land seems to tuck into itself and the geometry of the horizon goes soft—the predators wait. 

They are called the **Still-Dogs**, though they bear no teeth and have no bark. They are long-bodied, low-slung carnivores made mostly of dense muscle and wet, fibrous skin that mimics the rubbery black turf so perfectly that a man could step on one’s flank and assume he had found a dry hummock. They possess no eyes; in a world lit only by gas-flashes and cold blue arcs, sight is an expensive liability. Instead, their snouts are split horizontally into five thin, tongue-like leaves of skin, each one lined with copper-rich nerves that taste the galvanic drift of the air.

The Still-Dogs do not chase. They wait in the places where the ground-currents arc between the deep root-systems. When a flock of the rust-stained, leathery **Vane-Wings** is knocked out of the sky by a sudden belch of peat-gas or a down-draft of freezing steam, they tumble into these folds, their wet, parchment wings heavy with engine-grease. The Still-Dogs slide across them like wet tarpaulins. They do not tear; they have no teeth to dull against grit. Instead, they evert their stomachs—tough, thick-walled sacks smelling of lye and old marsh-gas—and envelope the fallen flyers whole, digesting their skin, fat, and hollow, methane-filled bones before the slush has time to freeze over them.

And then there are the **Flume-Eels**, churning the black trenches where the water runs scalding hot off the ironwood roots. 

They are the oldest things here, thicker than a ship’s mast and twenty paces long, with skin the color of an old kettle and no head to speak of—just a blunt, muscular wedge fringed with hundreds of pale, quivering barbels. They do not breathe air; they suck the water through gill-slits lined with metallic nodules that precipitate bog-iron out of the slurry, building thick, scaly collars around their necks that serve as ballast against the violent current. 

These eels are drawn to the static. Where the roots arc out of the mud and ground themselves with blinding, violet snaps, the eels rise in the foam, twining around one another in cold, heavy knots. They press their bellies against the live wood, soaking in the high-voltage drift until their pale skin glows with the dull, sickly blue of a foxfire swamp. They do not hunt with jaws; they use the charge stored in their iron-lined hides to stun the clouds of aerial larvae that drop into the boiling flumes. When an eel dies, it does not float. Its iron-heavy carcass sinks into the floor, where the fibrous peat slowly swallows it, drawing the metals down into the lower pipes, recycling the beast back into the fuel that feeds the trees above.

There is, too, a presence on the plateau that does not fit easily into the catalog of beasts. 

The things that move between the derricks in the farthest reaches—where the fog is so thick that the horizon vanishes entirely—walk upon two legs, but they are not men. The wind-shear and the concussive earth have shaped them into something more like living pit-props. They are called the **Barrow-Grooms**. 

They stand seven feet tall, wedge-shaped and narrow-shouldered, their flesh like dry, salt-cured peat, bound about the chest and loins with strips of tough, fibrous bark peeled from the non-moving bulkheads of the trees. Their faces are flat, blank plates of gray horn, devoid of nose or mouth, pierced only by two deep, circular pits that whistle when the gale cuts across them. Underneath their skin, one can see the slow, hydraulic heave of their own circulatory fluid—a thick, brown sap that does not freeze even when the rain turns to needles.

The Grooms do not build shelters, for any wall raised here would be rattled down by nightfall. They do not hunt the beasts, nor do the beasts touch them. Instead, they seem to serve the engines. 

With long, three-jointed fingers tipped with broad, spade-like nails of iron-hard wood, they spend the perpetual twilight tending the seams of the plateau. Where a piston-tree’s grease-duct begins to clog with boiled liverwort, a Groom will stand for hours in the scalding spray, clearing the channel with slow, deliberate scrapes of its thumbs. Where a lateral fold has opened too wide and threatens to tear the pressurized root-veins apart, two or three Grooms will gather, hauling great blocks of dead bog-iron on their shoulders to wedge into the meat of the crack, steadying the land against its own violence.

They make no tools. They do not speak. When the wind drops for a brief, terrifying second between the strokes of the five-mile pumps, you can hear them: a low, resonant drone that issues from their chest-flues, perfectly tuned to the sixty-cycle hum of the static arcing through the mud beneath their feet. 

They are not masters of the foundry. They are its valves. When one of them grows old and its cartilage turns to brittle charcoal, it simply lays itself down in the intake trough of an active macro-stem. The great, barge-thick taproot rises, pauses at the top of its stroke, and descends; and the Groom is drawn down into the dark, wet cylinder of the world, taken back into the boiling sap, while the flywheels scream overhead in the gray, unending rain.
