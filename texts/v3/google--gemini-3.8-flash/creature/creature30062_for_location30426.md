The creatures here are not built for pursuit, nor for flight; they are built for grip. To lose hold is not an injury, but an annihilation. The white void below does not catch or cradle; it simply receives what is cast down, diminishing it to a vanishing point. Thus, the anatomy of everything that lives upon the chords is characterized by an obsessive, mechanical fixation to the bark.

### The Underside Creepers (*The Tallow-Husks*)

The rubbery things that crawl beneath the ribs are larger than they appear from above—some reaching the length of a dory, though flattened until they are no thicker than a man’s forearm. They have no eyes; in a light that offers no shadows, an eye would be nothing more than an open ulcer. In place of a face, their blunt, shovel-shaped heads terminate in a ring of pale, muscular lobes that work continuously, tasting the cold air for the bitter smell of pressurized sap.

Their skin is not chitinous, but feels like scalded leather: smooth, greasy to the touch, and entirely hairless. It is smeared with a clear, alkaline slime that does not freeze at two degrees below, serving both to insulate their slow-moving organs and to chemically soften the petrified cambium they feed upon. 

They do not possess legs. Instead, their bellies are lined with double ranks of wide, horny plates—like the treads of an earthmover—overlapping each other and edged with inward-curving hooks of dense, amber-colored keratin. As they inch along the vertical flank of a bough, these plates flex in counter-rhythms, biting into the fibrous wood with a wet, chewing sound. 

When a hydraulic surge strikes the chord, they do not attempt to run. They cannot. Instead, a valve within their dorsal sinus opens, flooding their internal tissues with heavy fluid and collapsing their bodies completely against the bark, reducing their profile to a slight, hydro-dynamically neutral blister. If the shock fractures the bough beneath them, they are torn in two along their segment seams without ever letting go; the hooks remain embedded in the wood even after the soft interior has been sheared away.

Because they have no natural enemies capable of hunting them across the sheer curves, their life spans are governed entirely by the lifespan of the wood they graze. They grow slowly, layer upon layer of yellow, flavorless blubber accumulating over their vital nodes, until they become so heavy that their own anchoring plates tear out from the rotting fiber, and they slip, silent and unstruggling, into the white emptiness.

### The Ephemera of the Ruptures

Where a fresh rupture vents its scalding, saline sap into the freezing glare, life does not wait for generations. It appears within minutes, drawn from microscopic cysts long embedded in the petrified grain.

These are tiny, brittle-legged things no larger than lice, perfectly transparent, resembling flakes of mica that have learned to run. They emerge from the split pores of the wood the moment the steam billows out. Their bodies are osmotic sponges; they gorge themselves on the hot, salt-heavy moisture until their abdomens swell like glass beads, turning milk-white. 

Their entire reproductive cycle is an emergency lasting between twenty and forty minutes—the precise window before the exposed sap is either consumed or sealed under the advancing sheath of frost. They do not mate on the surface; they copulate in mid-scramble, tumbling over one another in the verdigris moss, laying chains of spherical eggs directly into the boiling seams of cellulose before the cold locks their joints. When the frost-line finally overtakes them, they do not hide. They freeze solid where they stand, their tiny claws still fastened to the splintered wood, their bodies becoming part of the crystalline rime that the windless air slowly flakes away.

### The Filament-Spiders

Deeper in the dead angles—where two ribs cross and crush against one another in their slow, blinding collisions—live creatures that are scarcely more than animated tension. 

They are the size of an outstretched hand, bone-colored, with eight long, jointed stilts that taper to needle-sharp points. They do not spin webs to catch food, for there are no flying insects in the void. Instead, they spin stays. Their silk is thicker than gut-string, glistening with a pearlescent, non-freezing oil. 

A spider will drive one end of its line into the solid bark of an ancient, stationary rib, then walk backward out onto a newly sprouted spear-shoot as it bolts across the chasm. The animal rides the surging wood like a sailor on a yardarm, paying out the line behind it under immense tension. When the shoot reaches the limit of its leap and shudders to a halt, the spider anchors the other end, testing the hum of the thread with its forelegs.

They feed upon the dead tissue that sloughs off the wood during these arrests—scabs of bark, dried foam from the sap-boil, and the crushed bodies of the mica-lice. They live entirely within the vibration of their lines. By resting their palps against the taut silk, they can read the structural health of an entire sector of the gulf. If a cord begins to whine at a pitch that indicates structural failure, the filament-spider severs its own anchors with specialized scissor-jaws and steps deliberately off into the void, riding a solitary dragline down until it catches the flank of a lower, slower chord.

### The Cord-Folk

The humans who inhabit the three gall-huts have ceased to resemble the stock from which they came. They are long-bodied, short-thighed, and wide across the chest, their lungs expanded to pull life from the thin, iron-scented air. 

Their eyes are their most striking deformity. To endure the flat, shadowless white, their eyelids have thickened into scarred, calloused folds, leaving only a horizontal slit no wider than a knife-blade. The whites of their eyes are not white, but a dull, vascular slate-gray, veined with yellow grease that shields the retinas from the fierce mineral burn of the void. They wear no goggles; their own flesh has grown into the necessary mask.

They go greased from head to foot in the rendered fat of the tallow-husks, which smells of wet straw and copper. This grease is their only defense against the dry frost; without it, their skin would split open to the fascia within an hour of labor. Their fingers are permanently curved, the palms thick with yellow horn that deadens the pain of holding rough laths and hemp-lines at temperatures where iron sticks to the flesh.

#### Their Economy of Motion

In the settlements, movement is considered a material expenditure. A man does not stand up to speak; he speaks where he is anchored, his mouth near the wood. Their clothing is made of the beaten inner-bark of the chords—a stiff, felt-like fabric that does not hold water, lined with the paper-thin skins of the tallow-husks peeled off in whole sheets.

They do not sleep in beds. To lie horizontal without an anchor is a horror to them, an invitation to the void. They sleep upright or curled in tight wicker baskets slung from structural pegs driven eight inches into the non-living heartwood. Even in their dreams, their fingers twitch against the lashing-cords, checking the knots.

#### Language and Tone

They speak almost entirely in an unvoiced register—a system of clicks, dry palate-chucks, and low whistles that carry cleanly across the naked grain without vibrating the air. Loud speech is considered an obscenity, not out of superstition, but because sound here is diagnostic: if a man is shouting, he is drowning out the groaning of the rib.

Their vocabulary contains seventeen words for different types of internal fracture, distinguished by the pitch of the wood’s complaint:
*   *Khel* is the sound of deep-core crystallization, like a distant pistol shot, which means the span will hold for weeks yet.
*   *Sst-prak* is the wet, tearing hiss of hydraulic delamination—the warning that a surge is coming and the bark will turn to grease and spear-wood within thirty heartbeats.
*   *Vohl* is the absence of vibration: the dead silence that occurs when a bough has cracked entirely through at its root and is merely resting against its neighbors, waiting for a breath of frost to slip its seat.

#### The Return to the Grain

They have no soil, and they do not burn their dead. Fire is a precious, terrifying tool, used only to heat iron drift-pins or to boil tallow in stone pots; it is never fed with wood, which they regard with a mixture of engineering reverence and physical dread.

When an inhabitant dies—usually by the sudden failure of a peg, or the "white lung" that comes from breathing wood-dust in the cold—the body is stripped of its grease, its bark-wrappings, and its sinew-tethers. The survivors carry the corpse to the nearest active fracture, where the wood is still weeping and the green growth is raw.

With bone chisels, they widen a seam in the wet cambium, insert the body feet-first, and wedge it tight with laths of fresh wood. Within three hours, the hydraulic fury of the chord does the work of an entombment. The rapid, seizing growth pours its steaming sap through the joints, the needles of frost seal the mouth and eyes, and the aggressive fiber knits itself over the chest, crushing the bones with tons of cold, blind pressure. 

Within a day, the person is simply a knot in the white span, an irregularity in the grain that the crawler-beasts scrape over without stopping, their horn-plates clicking against the submerged ribs of the dead as they feed on the bitter sap that rises through them.
