At first, nothing moves that is not shaken by the drafts.

The city appears as an ossuary of grey stone and black glass, its streets choked with the wreckage of shattered selenium fronds and drifts of soot that shift whenever the floor hums. If you stand in the colonnades and wait for a lung to expand or an eye to blink, you will wait through centuries of thermal spikes. 

Yet there are tracks in the emery dust.

They are not footprints. They are parallel grooves, three inches apart, ruled with the clean precision of a scribe’s compass, cut through the fallen vitreous ash from the threshold of one lintelled cell to another. Beside them run series of tiny, regular indentations—punctures where something hard and needle-pointed has struck the stone to gain purchase against the roaring convection.

The grazers come out when the updrafts reach their shrillest pitch, when the watch-chimneys are screaming on their lowest bass frequencies. 

They are small, flat, and broad, built like trilobites hammered out of dull specular hematite and sheet mica. They have no soft tissue; if you were to split one open with an iron wedge, there would be no cavity inside, only an intricately laminated interior of alternating carbon foil and wafered quartz, like the leaves of a high-voltage condenser. They do not walk by muscle, for they have no fluids to swell or contract; they move by heat-differential. 

Their undersides are cold relative to the searing basalt beneath them, clad in dark selenium plates that drink the infrared glow of the fissures. As the thermal gradient passes through their thin, shield-shaped carapaces toward the screaming air above, their articulated skirts flex. It is a strictly physical crawling—a bimetallic creep that drags them forward tooth by tooth along the abrasive flags. 

They feed not by ingestion, but by contact and shearing. They creep into the thickets of graphite cane, using the saw-toothed margins of their carapaces to fell the brittle stalks, harvesting the electrostatic charge that clings to the fibers. When the blue triboluminescent sparks leap from the mica leaves, these creatures do not flinch; they crowd into the discharge, their layered bodies absorbing the static, storing it across the micro-fractures of their quartz plates until their rims glow with a faint, violet corona. 

They leave behind them only powder: the graphite scraped down to talc-like lubricant, the selenium stripped of its metallic skin. When they reach their limit—when the continual grinding of the stone scours their plates too thin—they do not rot. They simply cease to cycle heat. Their joints lock as they cool toward the median temperature of the floor, and they become another paving stone, incorporated into the drift within a few thermal tides.

***

In the deeper rooms, behind the walls of dry-stacked peridotite where the drafts are baffled down to a dry, dry whistle, the makers are still present.

To recognize them, one must discard the notion that a body is an envelope containing a liquid life. In this pocket, life is an arrangement of stresses.

They are tall, narrow forms—seven to nine feet in height—that lean against the corbelled walls or stand upright in the shallow recesses carved into the jambs. In the red infrared gloom, they look like bundled rods of dark, sintered iron or pillars of basalt left unfinished by a mason. There are no faces, for there is no atmosphere here to carry speech and no light that requires an open lens; where a skull might sit, there is only a tapering pylon of densely layered slate, perforated by dozens of horizontal slits like the gills of a furnace grate.

They are not made of flesh, nor are they machines built elsewhere and brought here. They are native to the high-heat chemistry of the blister: composite organisms of graphitic carbon, native iron, and sintered corundum, held together by the enormous mechanical pressures of their own internal crystallization. 

When they move, they do so with an agonizing, deliberate slowness that belongs to the deformation of stone rather than the agility of beasts. A single step takes many minutes of our time. The sound of their progress is the sound of dry masonry settling: a slow, rhythmic *clack-grind* that sets the slate pavers vibrating beneath them. 

Their locomotion relies on the superlubricity of the graphite sheets that form their joints. Between plates of polished peridotite, micro-layers of pure, dry carbon allow massive, ton-weight limbs to slide with negligible friction, driven by internal strands of aligned carbon nanotubes that contract when heated from below and chilled from above. They are living stirling-engines, walking by the virtue of the pocket’s violence.

They are few now. Perhaps four score remain within the entire elliptical terrace of the city. They do not multiply; there is no generation here, only assembly.

In the central courtyards, where the long troughs of dry gravel lie open to the vault, two of them will sometimes stand together for decades. They do not touch. They communicate through the bedrock, shifting their weight to strike the basalt with calibrated pulses of their iron-shod footings. These seismic clicks travel through the dense peridotite foundations, entering the chimney-towers, which amplify them into deep, resonant thrums that shake the entire terrace.

A conversation between them may take a month of thermal spikes to complete. It is largely concerned with maintenance: the stabilization of lintels that have begun to creep under the mantle-surge, the clearing of glass-slag from the bellows-flutes that ventilate the lower vaults, and the recording of the vault’s slow, fatal closure.

***

For the pocket is dying, though it dies at the speed of granite.

The blister is shrinking. Four hundred fathoms above, the mass of the salt-flats is pressing down; the caprock, slightly cooler than the floor, is sagging. Every century, the basalt ceiling lowers by the width of a fingernail, tightening the throat of the bellows-chimneys, changing the resonant frequency of the city.

The inhabitants know this because the tablets tell them.

In the deep, dry vaults, where the shelves of baked clay are stacked from floor to ceiling, one of the tall figures can often be found at work. It does not carry a light; it has no use for the visible red glow. Its long, three-jointed limbs terminate in hardened burins of black diamond, set into iron sockets.

With these, it scores the clay.

The marks are not letters, but seismograms: long, parallel, micro-grooved lines that record the frequency of the floor’s pulses, the duration of each annealing cycle, and the precise electrical yield of the graphite brakes after every collapse. It works without haste, drawing the burin across the unfired tile with a high, sustained shriek that cuts through the roar of the drafts like a file through tin. Once inscribed, the tile is not baked in a kiln; the room itself, sitting at four hundred degrees of bakehouse heat, vitrifies the clay until it is as durable as jade.

The shelves contain the history of the blister across two million years of thermal pulses. 

There are tablets that describe the city before the terraces were stacked, when the pocket was wider and the vegetation was coarse and wild, sprouting in massive trunk-like columns of pure silicon that choked the entire vault from floor to ceiling. There are tablets that record the arrival of the first carbon-seams through the floor, when the chemistry of the pocket shifted from sulfur-silicate to this strange, dry, graphitic architecture that permits motion.

And there are tablets—thousands of them, stacked in the lowest bins—that are simply blank, smooth slabs of shale, waiting for the centuries to come.

***

Occasionally, one of the inhabitants reaches its end. 

The end here is not decomposition, but *glazing*.

Over many thousands of thermal cycles, the fine grains of abrasive ash carried by the convection drafts work their way into the graphite slip-planes of the joints. Emery dust cuts into the peridotite; the superlubricity fails. The limb begins to drag. The nanotube strands, fatigued by millions of thermal expansions and contractions, lose their elasticity and anneal into stiff, glassy rods of inert coke.

When this happens, the dweller does not fall. It simply stops where it was last balanced, usually in a doorway or an arched opening where the draft is strongest. 

Its companions do not bury it. They do not have the sentiment of wet creatures whose dead soften and corrupt. Instead, they use their diamond-tipped fingers to score into its flank the date of its seizure, its final thermal ledger, and its weight. Then they leave it.

Over the centuries, the blowing ash rounds its edges, polishing its peridotite plates until they shine like the lintels around it. The graphite thickets grow up around its legs, their razor-thin selenium leaves rattling against its knees in the howling upward wind. Violet sparks leap from the mica fronds to its slate crest during the storms, running harmlessly down its iron bones into the floor, illuminating its motionless profile against the red-glowing fissures of the street.

To an outsider, if an outsider could exist in this anhydrous furnace, it would be impossible to tell which of the stone pillars in the courtyards were the walls of the city, and which were its citizens, waiting for a draft that has already passed.
