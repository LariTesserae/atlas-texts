When the wind drops, the city exhales.

The first thing to appear from the deep cuts is not a face, but an elbow—sharp, gray-white, and knobbed like the root of a salt-cedar. It hooks around the frame of a windowless door, followed by a forearm no thicker than an adze-handle, strung with tendons that stand out under skin the color of river silt dried in a saucer.

These are the quarry-dwellers, though they have no name for themselves that relies on the mouth; spoken words have no currency against the iron scream of the midday draft. They are small—rarely taller than the shoulder of a lowlander—and their joints are loose, built for sliding sideways through the narrow, unmortared seams of the terraces. They do not sweat. To bleed moisture through the pores in this rift would be a death sentence; instead, their skin is a dry, impermeable sheath, fine-grained as chamois, shedding only a microscopic scurf of dead keratin that smells faintly of toasted flour. 

Their heads are narrow, wedges of bone designed to tuck into the shelter of their own clavicles. Over their eyes they wear shields cut from single laminations of selenite—sheets of crystalline gypsum so thin they tremble in the still air, bound to their temples with twists of boiled gut. Behind these milky plates, their pupils are permanently contracted to the size of needle-pricks, surrounded by irises of pale, washed amber. Their eyelids are double: the outer fringed not with lashes, but with a stiff, interlocking felt of dry hairs that sifts the talc from the air; the inner a translucent, horn-colored membrane they can draw across the eye while chiseling, allowing them to carve stone by feel and faint shadow without being blinded by flying gypsum flakes.

They do not look into the void of the rift. To look outward is to invite the white glare to scour the mind. They look only at the rock, two inches from their noses.

They have not come out to walk the terraces; they have come out to scrape. 

In the three-hour lull between the gales, the heat does not diminish—it curdles. The eighty-degree air thickens, trapped between the limestone faces, tasting of copper and sulfur. From every blind doorway, dozens of these figures emerge on their haunches, moving with an eerie, rustling gait like dry gourds sliding over gravel. They carry tools made of beaten arsenical copper, soft enough to require constant cold-hammering against flat river-stones, yet dense enough to peel away the friable gypsum and chalk without sending sparks into the combustible thickets of beard-grass.

They scrape because the city is constantly being choked by its own life. 

The anhydrous vegetation—the bladder-ferns and the saffron-briar—does not die and rot; it dies and petrifies, mineralized by the gypsum-laden air until it forms a calcified crust that threatens to seal the terrace mouths and freeze the cantilevered stairs. The dwellers work with a rhythmic, frantic precision, shaving the yellow moss-crusts from the cedar lintels and paring back the bloated, papery bulbs of the ferns before the wind wakes. The scrapings are swept into wide, shallow aprons of hide tied around their waists. Nothing is dropped into the rift yet. The talc floor below must remain clear of coarse debris, lest the violent updraft carry heavy stones back up to shatter against the facades.

Between the terraces, they communicate through the stone itself. A carver will press his forehead against the living limestone, his cheek rested against the tallow-smooth wall, and tap three times with the butt of his copper haft. Two hundred feet away, down four tiers of dry masonry, an old woman seated in an interior chamber will feel the thrum through the base of her skull, interpreting the grain-shifts of the rock as clearly as a shout: *The seam is soft here. The seam is opening.*

In the deep recesses—behind the facades that bake in the white noon—the rock changes from porous limestone to cold, massive alabaster. It is here that their true economy hides. 

They do not dig wells; there is no water table within half a league of the sun-baked caliche flats above. Instead, they *mine* water from the crystal lattice of the stone. Gypsum is twenty percent water by weight, bound chemically into its chalky heart. In the black galleries behind the city, where the temperature sinks into a merciful, tomb-like chill, great earthenware retorts are mortared into the rock with lime-paste. Long ago, the dwellers learned to pack these jars with broken chunks of selenite and build slow, airless fires of dead sulfur-briar beneath them. 

The heat breaks the crystal. The rock sighs out its ancient hydration as a thin, bitter steam, which travels through coils of hollow cedar pipe to condense in stone troughs cut into the floor. The water is dense, heavy with minerals, tasting of plaster and lime; it puckers the tongue and leaves a white ring around the lips, but it keeps their blood from turning to salt. What remains in the retorts is pure, dead plaster of Paris—a snow-white flour which they drag to the mouth of the galleries in large skin sacks.

They are not alone in the rock, though the other lives are as desiccated as their own.

Tucked into the vertical mortar-cracks between the stone tiers live colonies of *scale-mice*—creatures that are not rodents at all, but a form of warm-blooded, hexapodal reptile covered in stiff, overlapping plates of chitin. They have no teeth; they possess hard, horny beaks with which they shear the desiccated roots of the beard-grass where it penetrates the joints. The dwellers do not hunt them for meat—there is no grease on a scale-mouse to justify the effort—but for their bladder-pouches. The creatures accumulate small nodules of dry, crystalline urine that burn with a clean, smokeless blue flame, providing the gentle, steady heat needed to sweat the gypsum in the deep stills without filling the lower galleries with carbon.

Above them, where the suspended cedar plazas hang over the void, are the *net-weavers*. They are humans, but of an older, more elongated caste, their fingers extended by three extra joints of gristle, bound with parched sinew. They spend the wind-hours strapped to the underside of the salt-cedar logs by their heels, hanging upside down over the two-hundred-span drop while the screaming air tears at their linen shirts. They carry long rakes of copper wire, combing the howling downdraft for the aerial flotsam of the upper desert: drifting seeds of the benchland scrub, parched locusts swept off the salt-pans, and the tufts of dry fur lost by coyotes miles away on the plains. To the net-weaver, the gale is an ocean, and the rift is a baleen sieve. 

Now, the ringing silence begins to curdle at its edges.

From the throat of the rift, two hundred spans down, comes a low, dragging gasp—the sound of an empty furnace catching a draft. The three-hour equilibrium is failing. The sun is tilting past the vertical cut of the fissure; the shadow of the northern lip has begun to slide down the chalk face like a black shutter.

The carvers do not rush, but their movements grow angular. 

They gather at the edges of the cedar cantilevers, hundreds of them, their selenite spectacles reflecting the fading midday glare like silver coins. They unhook their hide aprons. Inside each apron is a small dune of white stone-dust—the siftings of two hours of chiseling, mixed with the dead plaster from the water-retorts and the saffron powder of the briars.

The groan in the deep chimney rises to an iron hum. The beard-grass begins to twitch, its brittle, glass-like fibers clacking against the masonry like dry teeth. 

The first surge of air hits them not from below, but from above—a falling wall of hot, dry wind that strikes the limestone amphitheater with a sound like a thunderclap. 

As one, the dwellers shake out their aprons into the void.

A cloud of blinding, talc-fine powder erupts from the terraces, three hundred yards across, instantly caught by the descending column of the gale. The air does not blow the dust away; it swallows it, drawing it down into a twisting, milk-white spindle that screams as it drills toward the bottom of the fissure. For ten seconds, the entire rift is opaque—a vertical hurricane of pulverized stone, glowing with the retained heat of the noon sun, scented violently with cinnamon, copper, and parched earth.

By the time the dust reaches the talc floor to be ground to nothing, the terraces are empty. 

The blind doors are latched with cedar pins. The selenite lenses have been tucked into bags of horn. Deep within the honeycombed core, where the scream of the wind is muted to a steady, skull-vibrating thrum, the dwellers sit in the darkness, side by side, their knees pulled to their chests, waiting for their water to condense drop by drop from the heart of the stone, while overhead the city plays its single, deafening note into the earth.
