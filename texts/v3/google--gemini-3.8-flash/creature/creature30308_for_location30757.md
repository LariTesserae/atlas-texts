To survive the shearing drag of sixty-knot brine, an organism cannot possess edges. It cannot have loose fins, fluttering gills, or eyes that could be scooped from their sockets by the grit-laden wash. Life in the drowned chamber does not swim; swimming is a luxury of stiller basins. Here, to lose purchase on the iron is to become a projectile, tumbling through the tea-colored dark until the body is pulverized against a diversion baffle or drawn down into the teeth of an intake runner miles below.

Everything that endures here is anchored, wedged, or flattened.

Along the rivet-lines, where the wake of each iron dome creates a microscopic dead-water furrow no wider than a finger, the *crust-tapers* make their home. They are not true barnacles, though their outer rind mimics the heavy calcite of deep-sea balanomorphs. Their shells are blackish-red, layered like slag, cemented to the metal by an insoluble, sulfur-rich resin that sets harder than the cast iron beneath it. They do not have feather-like cirri; the turbulence would snap them instantly. Instead, they extend thick, segmented scoops of horn-like zinc-chitin, curved like tiny turbine blades. They don't reach out into the flow; they let the current drive the water *through* them. The internal channels of their bodies are labyrinthine centrifuges, designed so the violent rush of water is forced into corkscrews, depositing suspended flakes of machine-tallow, lead paint chips, and bacterial slime into internal gut-sacks while the stripped brine vents out of lateral exhaust slits with an audible whistle. When thousands of them vent at once in the lee of a pylon, they hum—a thin, reedy counterpoint to the thrum of the floor-plates.

Between these colonies, moving along the rust-slick tracks of the structural seams, are the *piston-lice*. These are the creatures that resemble flattened isopods, but their anatomy has more in common with hydraulic clamps. Their dorsal carapaces are low, hydrofoil domes of dense, oil-blackened enamel, grooved like river-pebbles so the downward thrust of the rushing brine pins them tighter to the steel the faster the water moves. They have no eyes; their heads are blunt wedges of sensory horn that can read the micro-voltages leaking from the cathodic protection grids and the galvanic decay of ancient rivets. 

Their locomotion is a terrible, rhythmic shudder. Beneath their armor, sixteen pairs of short, pick-headed limbs tipped with iron-plated claws ratchet through the pit-marks of the metal. They do not walk; they crawl like heavy machinery, levering themselves forward one limb-pair at a time against the crushing drag. If one loses its grip, its underbelly—pale, scalded pink and soft as boiled pigskin—is exposed for a fraction of a second before the current rips it away, instantly turning it into a ragged smear against the next stanchion. They feed by scraping. Their mouthparts are counter-rotating rasping plates that grind the thick rind of lime, heavy-metal salts, and compressed anaerobic slime directly from the iron, leaving bright, silvery trails of bare metal that the caustic brine oxidizes back to dull leaden gray within hours.

Higher up, where the suspended pedestrian gantries hang like sagging spiderwebs in the boiling murk, a different morphology takes hold: the *droppers*.

They are long, colonial organisms that look like lengths of perished, rubber-sheathed conduit or fraying elevator cables. Fastened to the underside of the steel walkways by fibrous, root-like anchors that bore directly into the cast-iron grain, they trail down fifty, eighty, a hundred feet into the churning void. They have no organs for seeing or swimming; they are pure muscle and fibrous skin, tough as vulcanized tire-carcasses, blackened by centuries of copper-rich wash. The current pulls them taut as bowstrings. They do not fight it; they vibrate with it. 

These cables are hollow, filled with a viscous, amber lipid that stays liquid at near-boiling temperatures. Inside the central lumen of each dropper live millions of microscopic, chemotrophic siphonophores that feed on the electrical current generated by the dropper’s constant, violent flexing—a living piezoelectric generator, harvesting the kinetic catastrophe of the chamber to sustain a dark, internal soup of blind cells. 

Sometimes, when the chamber’s subterranean valves shift and the entire water-column bucks, the droppers are snapped like rotten twine. When this happens, they do not die immediately; the severed lengths whip through the amber murk like blind, thrashing eels, their muscular outer sleeves spasming violently as the sudden lack of tension causes their internal lipid cores to foam and burst in the scalding alkaline brine.

Below them all, where the water is thickest with pulverized slate and the heat rises off the floor-plates in shimmering, distortion-heavy plumes, are the *lee-lards*.

They exist only where structural geometry creates vast, stagnant vacuums: behind the massive, stepped diversion ziggurats and within the dead spaces of the flooded elevator towers. Here, the current does not scour; instead, it leaves behind what it cannot carry. Ancient gear-grease, decaying animal fats that have drained down from the world above, sulfur flocs, and heavy-metal soaps settle into massive, gelatinous drifts that never cool. 

The lee-lards are the masters of these dead-holes. They are not armored, for there is no current here to crush them, but neither are they soft in any conventional sense. They are massive, amorphous lobes of living wax, some the size of boilers, liver-colored and slow, held together by an internal scaffold of spongy, hollow bone that floats near the oily ceiling of the pocket. They have no distinct limbs; they move by shifting their own dense, tallowy mass, rolling across the sediment like balls of cold suet. Their flesh is cool to the touch despite the blood-warm brine around them—their metabolism is so infinitely slow, sustained entirely by the anaerobic fermentation of grease and zinc soaps, that they generate almost no metabolic heat. 

From their undersides, thin, translucent tendrils—like strings of boiled glass—dangle into the silt, testing for the dead that fall from the trusses. When a piston-louse slips, or a dropper snaps and settles into the slack-water, the lee-lard rolls over the carcass with a wet, heavy finality. It does not chew. It exudes a warm, concentrated bath of sulfuric and carbonic acids from its skin, dissolving the mineral shell and the tough hide over months, absorbing the metals into its own spongy skeleton until its bones are so heavy with lead and zinc that the creature can no longer float, at which point it sinks into the bedrock silt and becomes the substrate for the next generation.

And deeper still, within the throat-tunnels of the great turbines that grind in the dark beneath the floor, there is a movement that belongs to neither flesh nor machine.

Nothing with a nervous system could live within twenty paces of those runners; the cavitation alone would rip muscle from bone, and the sonic pulses would rupture any chamber containing liquid. Yet, during the rare moments when a turbine runner chokes on debris—when the thrum falters and the screaming cavitation gives way to a grinding, shuddering silence—something shifts inside the housing.

The maintenance divers of an older world might have called them *runners*, or simply *teeth*. They are vast, ring-shaped colonies of litho-chitin, grown directly onto the trailing edges of the bronze impeller blades. They are shaped like the metal they ride: hydrodynamically immaculate, scarred with pitting where bubbles have burst against them, their surfaces harder than corundum. They do not feed on organic matter; they feed on the cavitation itself. They are structured to funnel the microscopic, imploding steam-voids of hyper-velocity water into specialized internal chambers lined with heavy crystal plates, converting the mechanical violence of the collapsing bubbles directly into metabolic energy.

They are as old as the rotation of the shafts. They have no heads, no thoughts, no awareness of the chamber or the drowned city above it. They know only the rpm of the runners, the cyclic bite of the water, and the eternal, scalded dark. When a runner finally shatters under the centuries of stress, these organisms die with it, their shattered fragments spat out into the chamber as a brief shower of hard, grey grit that the current sweeps away toward the stanchions, where the barnacles wait with their scoops open, ready to begin the work again.
