In the carved niche where the water sits tepid and still, the flatworms do not swim so much as dissolve and reconstitute their shapes across the stone. They are the width of pumpkin seeds, lacking eyes, their margins fringed with microscopic cilia that beat a ceaseless, microscopic current through the mineral film. They have lived in this specific bowl of condensation for so many generations that their gut-branches are stained the exact amber-ochre of the iron seep beneath them. They feed on the invisible: the rain of microscopic skin, spore-husks, and pollen drifting down from the skylight, caught in the ceiling’s drip and ferried down through sixty feet of limestone chimney.

Lower, where the moss pads the bowed stairs, the jade lizards keep vigil. They are utterly tailless—not pruned by violence, but hatched without the need for balance on vertical drops, their hindquarters rounded off like river stones. Their toes are flattened into wide, adhesive pads fringed with microscopic spatulae that find purchase on wet algae and slick, sweating calcite. When they rest, they flatten themselves so completely against the rock that their bodies lose their shadows, becoming mere nodules of jade-green skin pebbled like wet shagreen. Their eyelids are translucent membranes; when they blink, they wipe away the fine mist of the pit without ever losing sight of the dark. They do not drink from pools. Instead, they cock their heads toward the weeping walls, waiting for a drop to break surface tension against their snouts, taking the water in through capillary action along the fine grooves between their scales.

Above them, hanging like discarded rags in the humid draft, are the moth-creatures. Their wings do not have the dry, powdery dust of attic moths; they are furred in a dense, water-repellent down, thick as mole-fur, pale butter-yellow, designed to shrug off the heavy condensation that would ground any other flyer. They spend hours suspended from the aerial roots, their abdomens pulsating with a steady, furnace-like rhythm. When one takes flight, it does not flutter. It displaces air with a slow, heavy sweep, gliding from one hanging orchid basket to the next with a soft, velvet displacement that leaves the candle-flame shapes of the orchid flowers shivering in its wake. They eat nothing in their adult lives. They exist only to climb through the root-tangle, meet, and deposit their gelatinous egg-clusters into the moist throats of the violet aroids, where the heat incubates them in days.

Deep within the gravel cracks, where the thermal vents hiss beneath the surface silt, the isopods dwell in numbers that defy counting. They are not gray like cellar woodlice, but bleached to the color of lard, their carapaces thin and flexible, polished smooth by their constant churning through the boiling mud. They are the furnace-tenders. A fallen leaf from the surface trees—stiff, dry oak or brittle beech that tumbled through the cleft—does not last seventy-two hours down here. The isopods swarm it from beneath, their serrated mouthparts reducing cellulose to black paste while the white mycelium knits over their backs as they work. Sometimes a dozen will carry a dead lizard down into a steam vent, their overlapping plates clicking in the dark like dry beans shaken in a gourd, vanishing into fissures where the water is hot enough to scald human skin.

Among the taproots hangs the true silence of the vault: the whip-eel, or *ribbon-cord*. It is a legless thing, as thick as two fingers and six feet long, its hide completely devoid of scales. Instead, it is sheathed in a heavy, clear mucus that smells faintly of river weeds and wet brass. It breathes through vascularized folds along its flanks, taking oxygen directly from the saturated air. It moves without ever touching the ground, weaving through the suspended root-cables with an agonizing, hypnotic slowness, its head blunted and wedge-shaped, devoid of external ears or visible eyes. It hunts by thermoreception, sensing the lizard’s warm heart through two pit-organs recessed into its upper jaw. When it strikes, there is no thrashing; it loops twice around its prey, pinches the throat until the small jade heart stops, and swallows the creature whole before the warmth leaves the flesh. A ribbon-cord will remain looped in a single fig root for three weeks while a meal melts inside its belly, its skin sweating clear droplets that drip *tock, tock* into the basins below.

And then there are the makers of the trails through the moss—the things that use the stairs.

They dwell back where the dry-stacked limestone wall meets the hollow cavity beneath the subterranean fig’s primary crown. They are small, low-slung mammals, perhaps descended from civets or palm-rats swept down through the rift during an ancient deluge, but reshaped entirely by the cellar. Their fur is sparse, stiff as wire, and stained a greasy, slate-blue by the mud they burrow through. Their ears have shriveled to tight, wrinkled buttons flush against their skulls, but their snouts are long, mobile, and tipped with a star of pink, flesh-bulb tentacles that never cease their frantic, quivering inspection of the air.

They walk the carved stone steps with deliberate, flat-footed care, their long, pale claws clicking with a dull *clink-clink* against the softened limestone. They do not run; nothing in the pit runs. Speed wastes energy in an air so heavy with steam that panting would flood the lungs. Instead, they move with a mechanical, rolling trot, sniffing the base of the wall, using the masonry precisely as it was laid—as a terrace system to access the orchid hammocks and the rimstone pools. 

They feed on the white crabs that pick at the rotting wood, crunching the soft, calcium-poor shells with broad, flat molars. When they sleep, they gather in the collapsed foundation behind the fig, five or six of them piled together in a single, heaving knot of slate-colored flesh, their collective body heat merging seamlessly with the steam venting from the floor. Their breathing is slow, heavy, and wet—the lungs of creatures that have never drawn a breath that was not half-water, living out their lives in the long, hot twilight beneath the earth, completely unaware that there is a sky above the roots that has any other color than the gray-white of stone.
