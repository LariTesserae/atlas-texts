To understand what lives upon the plateau of Oun, one must first discard the notion of pasture and harvest, of green growth and seasonal rot. Nothing here has ever greened. The blood of every creature runs thick with dissolved minerals, and life is an affair of calcium, grease, sulfur, and meat. There are no fibers for weaving; there is no bread. A man who dies in Oun does not decompose into earth, for there is no earth to receive him—he is boiled down in the tallow-vats for his lard, which is needed to dress the leather belts of the winches, and his skeleton is crushed into a gritty, hydraulic lime that hardens underwater to patch the seams of the great cisterns. The dead are literally mortar.

The people—if they can still be strictly called such after uncounted dynasties beneath that flat, milky skylight—are called the *Dredgers* or the *Slag-born*, though among themselves they use only the names of their respective sluice-gangs or dry-dock wards. 

They are remarkably thick in the torso, short-legged, with barrel-shaped rib cages designed to draw life from an atmosphere as warm and heavy as unskimmed broth. Their skin has the hue of a boiled turnip, mottled with slate-gray freckles where the airborne nickel-dust has settled into the pores over generations. They do not sweat as lowlanders sweat; their skin produces an oily sebum, a slick, protective tallow that repels the constant condensation and prevents their flesh from sloughing away under the warm drip of the masonry. Their fingers are blunt and possess no fingernails to speak of—merely thickened pads of horn that can pry a lime-crust off a bronze pin without tools. 

They do not wear cloth. Cloth cannot exist where there is no flax, no hemp, no sheep. Their clothing is made entirely from the cured, hairless hides of the spade-mouthed oxen and the translucent, rubbery belly-skins of the great blind river-worms that dwell in the silt-chambers below the stamping mills. These hides are tanned using stale urine, copperas, and the sour liquor drained from the bellies of dead gulls; they never truly dry, remaining limp, cool, and greasy to the touch, smelling faintly of vinegar and salt. A master lighterman wears a suit of four overlapping pelts, stitched with sinew taken from the tail-tendons of carapaced calves, the seams sealed with boiled pitch so that he may work waist-deep in the canal-locks for sixteen hours without water touching his skin.

They eat from the walls and from the basins. Because there is no grain, the base of all human life in Oun is *mush*—a gray, gelatinous paste scraped from the underside of the flumes, composed of sulfur-metabolizing colonial bacteria, crushed river-crab meat, and the fatty meal pressed from the carcasses of the mast-gulls. Every street has its communal boiler: an iron cauldron twelve feet wide, permanently heated by a low flue-tap from some subterranean furnace, where this paste is boiled with coarse salt and the marrow-grease of the herds into a thick porridge. It tastes of brine and wet iron. It leaves a heavy, alkaline coating on the tongue, which the men scrape off with bronze spatulas before sleeping.

They sleep in tiers, like the birds. Their dwellings are hollowed out of the dead-weight masonry of the aqueduct arches—not rooms, but rectangular slots called *bunks*, stacked four and five high, chiseled straight into the basalt. A family lives within a three-yard cube of dark, warm stone. Because the outside air is always ninety degrees and saturated with moisture, they need no bedding; they lie naked upon slabs of slate polished smooth by their ancestors' hips, their heads resting on blocks of lead or carved tallow-wax. 

Their language has grown thick and wet, shaped by the acoustics of a city where sound does not travel through open air, but rebounds endlessly between parallel planes of wet stone and iron plates. They speak without high sibilants; consonants are swallowed, and their vowels are low, cavernous hums that carry through the rumble of the bronze gears without being scattered by the sound of falling water. Two lightermen standing twenty paces apart on opposite banks of a lock do not shout; they emit a series of hollow, rhythmic glottal clicks that sound to a stranger like the cracking of river-ice, yet carry complete navigational instructions.

They have no gods, for a god requires a sky to look down from, and the sky of Oun is merely an opaque lid of white grease that never clears. Instead, they venerate *The Stroke*—the perpetual, four-beat measure of the Great Basin pumps. Their calendar is not marked by moons, which cannot be seen, but by the *Lift*: the eighteen-year cycle during which the main siphon-valves of the southern reservoirs must be hauled up with balance-weights to be cleared of calcified shell-growth. A man does not say he is forty years old; he says he has lived through two Clearings and half a Lift.

Beneath the feet of the men, in the dark beneath the iron slag, move the *crawlers*.

The most common of these are the buckler-crabs, but they are not the crabs of a sea-coast. They have no use for sand. Their shells are not chitinous in the ordinary sense, but have absorbed so much ferrous oxide from the run-off of the foundries that they are rust-red and heavy as cast-iron skillet lids. They are detritivores, living upon the shed skin of the ungulates, the excrement of the mast-gulls, and the dead fat that separates from the factory run-off. When a lighterman falls from the staging into a deep lock, the crabs do not tear him; they simply climb upon him by the hundred, their peg-legs finding purchase in his greasy hide-smock, their rasping mouthparts working with the blind, mindless diligence of water wearing away stone, until within three days his aprons float up empty, their interior surfaces cleaned as white as lard-pails.

Deeper down, in the sluices that have not seen light since the fourth century of the city, dwell the *pipe-eels*. These are not fish, but blind, legless amphibians that can reach forty cubits in length, their bodies as thick as an oil-cask. They have no eyes, only broad, flat snouts covered in sensory pits that detect the faint galvanic currents generated by the friction of iron scows moving through the water above. They feed upon the blind swallows that fall from the flues, and upon each other. When an eel dies, it bloats with gas and wedges itself into the neck of a siphon; then the *divers* must go down.

The divers are the lowest caste of Oun, recruited from among those whose skin has developed the *scale*—a thickening of the epidermis caused by long immersion in the heavy water of the lower vats. A diver carries no air-hose; he breathes from a bladder made of a gull’s crop filled with dry tallow-scented air, and he carries only a short, broad-bladed spade of soft iron. He greases himself from ears to heels in ox-butter and slips feet-first into a pipe three feet wide, pushing through forty yards of warm, blind, stagnant grease to chop away the obstruction in the dark, working entirely by the sense of touch against the choking suction of the draft. Many do not come back; their bodies remain in the elbow of the pipe until the pressure behind them mounts sufficiently to blow their bones through the bronze grates into the stamping vats, where the stampers break them into meal without a change in rhythm.

The carapaced ungulates—which the men call *sleds* because of their broad, flattened sternums—are the true citizenry of the roofs. They are neither wild nor domestic, but simply *there*, like the masonry itself. 

They have two eyelids: one of thick, gray horn to protect against the sulfur-grit, and an inner, transparent film through which they view their narrow, wet world in shades of pewter and milk. Their spade-shaped jaws are jaw-plates rather than teeth; they do not chew, but plane the brickwork. Over centuries, their grazing has cut smooth, horizontal furrows through the high terraces, six inches deep, along which the rainwater flows as neatly as through a gutter. A sled lives for eighty years. It does not run; its three-jointed legs end in broad, circular pads of horn that act as suction cups on the wet slate. When two bulls fight for a roosting-shelf, they do not butt heads; they press their flanks together and push, with a slow, grinding expenditure of tonnage that may last from noon until the red bell sounds at dusk, their breath coming in whistling, bronchitic gasps, their hooves squealing as they slip an inch at a time across the slag.

The birds—the white mast-gulls—are the scavengers of the upper strata. They are huge, clumsy things that cannot launch themselves from flat ground; they must waddle to the edge of an aqueduct parapet and cast themselves off into the warm updraft, their six wings opening with a sound like the unfurling of rotten sails. 

They are entirely deaf. Generations of living in the mouth of the steam-whistles and beside the stamping mills have destroyed their auditory nerves, so that they communicate solely through the rhythmic vibration of their throat-pouches, which they distend into great, crimson balloons that throb with blood. A flock of five thousand gulls perched along the ridge of a dry-dock will pulse their pouches in a slow, undulating wave that travels from north to south like a ripple of raw meat, signaling the approach of an iron barge or a shift in the wind-plumes. They have no fear of the gray-skinned men. They will perch on the shoulders of a teamster as he greases a hawser, their blunt, hook-nosed beaks snapping at the grease-can, their cold, white breast-feathers brushing against his neck with the weight of wet linen.

And in the dead heart of the city, in the miles of dry-dock that were drained four centuries ago and never refilled, live the *Candelers*.

These are the hermits of the masonry, men and women who have left the sluice-gangs to live among the calcified bones of the old ships. They do not work the iron. They spend their lives gathering the fat that floats upon the surface of the grand reservoirs—the thin, yellow grease that rises from the drowned beasts and the refinery leaks. They boil this grease in small earthenware cups, purifying it until it burns with a small, smokeless blue flame that does not soot the stone. 

They sit by the thousands in the dry basins, each beside his small blue wick, never speaking, their eyes fixed upon the slow, wet crawl of the buckler-crabs across the basalt steps. They believe that the plateau is not stone, but the skull of an ancient animal that died before the light turned white, and that the water sweating from the masonry is its marrow, which must all be drawn out through the bronze conduits before the world can become dry and hard again. 

When a Candeler feels the grease in his own veins begin to chill—when his knees stiffen and his horn-pads turn yellow—he climbs down into the lowest flues, where the water is boiling hot and the blind swallows nest. He lies down upon the grating above the steam-vent, pulling his belly-skin cloak over his face. 

Within an hour, the steam has done its work. The swallows, who cannot see him, land upon his ribs to catch the warmth of his dying. By nightfall, the mud-crabs have found his fingers, and by the next turn of the great bronze wheel, his bones are clean enough to be shoveled into the lime-kiln, where they will wait to be mixed with river-gravel and set into the floor of some new lock, whose gates are already being forged from the scrap of the old world.
