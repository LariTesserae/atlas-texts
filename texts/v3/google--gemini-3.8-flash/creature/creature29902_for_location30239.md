To understand what lives here, one must begin with the crusts, for nothing in this vault exists apart from the metal it consumes and is consumed by.

The rust-pelts are not inert sludge; they are communal skins. Beneath the orange slime, directly against the pitting wrought-iron, live microscopic colonies that cement themselves with ferric hydroxides, forming a living, brittle felt nearly two inches thick in the still waters. This felt is the pasture. Grazing it are the flat isopods—creatures the width of a mason’s trowel, whose segmentations have the dull, wet gloss of boiled horn. Their undersides are not soft, but packed with rows of translucent gill-plates that flutter like the pages of a waterlogged pocketbook, aerating the brine as they drag themselves across the rivet-heads. They have no eyes—not even the pitted vestigial pits seen in cave-dwelling woodlice—only sensory bristles along their skirt-like margins that twitch in sympathetic vibration whenever the walking-beam groans above. 

When an isopod dies, it does not float. Its shell, heavily mineralized with salvaged iron and calcium leached from the rotting mortar, is so dense that it sinks into the tea-colored silt with the dull weight of a dropped hinge. There, within hours, the leeches find it. 

These leeches are not the black, muscular ribbons of open ponds. They are long as knitting needles, colorless as washed tripe, and almost entirely blind. Having little blood to drink from the cold-blooded things around them, they have adapted into fluid-feeders and scavengers of liquefaction. They insert their needle-fine proboscises into the articular chinks of dead isopods, or anchor themselves by their rear suckers to the flanges of water-gates, letting their bodies trail out into the sluggish drift like pale horsehairs. They taste the current for vinegar, for the acid plume that marks where an amphibian has dropped its jelly-strings of eggs or where a mat of sulfur-fungus has sloughed away from a beam.

Then there are the lungless ones—the pale, eel-bodied salamanders that the isopods avoid. They are roughly fourteen inches from their blunt, spade-shaped snouts to the tips of their compressed, paddle-like tails. Their skin is so thin and pigmentless that through the flanks one can see the faint, rhythmic shudder of their dark viscera and the yellow line of their vertebral columns. Their external gills are their most striking feature: three pairs of branching, ramified trees of brilliant scarlet that sprout from the base of the skull, glowing like fresh arterial blood against the grey stone. These gills never rest; they feather the water in slow, measured beats, sensitive not just to dissolved oxygen, but to electrical fields. 

They know precisely when an iron plate overhead is rusting through; they congregate beneath spots where electrochemical current trickles from a dissolving brass bolt into the water, resting their spatulate chins on the submerged railway tracks to absorb the faint, tingling galvanic hum. They are remarkably patient. A salamander will lie across the spoke of a drowned flywheel for three days without shifting an inch, its gills barely drifting, waiting for a blind crayfish to pick its way through the rust-silt within striking distance.

The crayfish themselves are heavy, blunt-nosed beasts, their pincers enlarged to absurd, bulbous proportions—not for fighting, but for levering apart the mortar-joints and breaking the mineral crusts that seal hollow valve-bodies. Their shells are not uniformly pale; they are stained with indelible, concentric rings of rust-red and verdigris where copper-leach has pooled on their backs. They are the quarrymen of the chamber. As they crawl, their heavy tail-fans drag deep furrows through the centuries-old silt, turning over the anaerobic black mud beneath the orange pelt and releasing sudden, burbling bladders of methane that rise to burst against the ceiling with small, hollow slaps.

Yet the vault’s population is not confined to the brine. 

Above the water line, in the warm-damp draft that gathers beneath the zinc ceiling baffles, lives an order of spider that has forgotten silk. Or rather, it has turned silk into something else: thick, mucous-laden ropes that do not snag prey, but act as conduits for condensation. These spiders, long-legged as harvestmen but with globular, milk-white abdomens, construct dense cordages between the fungal ropes and the high iron walkways. They do not wait for flies—there are no flies. Instead, they subsist entirely on the fungal beads and the tiny, spring-tailed collembola that swarm like coarse pepper over the rubbery hyphae. When the walking-beam groans and drops its bucket, the sudden displacement of air sends a shiver through thousands of these suspensory lines, and the spiders scuttle upward in a simultaneous, silent rush, their long legs interlocking until the girder-angles look as though they are packed with shivering wads of wet cotton.

Deeper still, down where the central turbine well drops away into an unplumbed siphon shaft beneath the floor tracks, the life changes character. 

The light from the high clerestory cannot reach this sump; even the green bioluminescence of the wandering isopods dies out at the lip of the pit. Down here, wedged into the intake nozzles where sixty years of river detritus once packed solid and rotted into a greasy, lignite-like peat, lives something larger, or perhaps several things that have lost the need to separate. 

Occasionally, when the walking-beam hits the lowest point of its stroke and the water heaves backward toward the pump-throat, the pool in the shaft draws down by a foot, sucking the tea-colored brine into a slow, circular whirlpool. In that moment, one can see the grey, rubbery rolls of a massive, communal siphon-worm or an overgrown, neotenic amphibian mass—a pale, convoluted knot of tissue, thick as an oil drum, jammed tight into the bronze intake grate. It does not move to flee. It breathes with the pump. Long, fibrous fringes—neither hair nor fin, but perhaps modified gill-arches—trail upward from its hide, swaying in the back-draft of the sump like submerged willow branches. It has grown around the ironwork, its flesh swallowing the rivet-lines and the sheared studs until it is impossible to tell where the machine's corroded housing ends and the creature’s gristly mantle begins.

It feeds on the drift: the dead carapaces that tumble down the silt-bars, the sheets of sloughed bacterial pelt, the constant, rain-like drizzle of fecal pelts and sulfur-pearls dropping from the ceiling arrays. It is the chamber’s sump-plug and its ultimate digestion. 

All of these lives are tethered to the iron clock. When the overhead bucket fills—dribbling its brim over the rusted lip until the weight overcomes the counterpoise—the entire vault prepares. The isopods dig their brassy claws deeper into the bacterial crusts; the salamanders slide off their flywheels into the floor silt; the pale leeches contract into tight, hard buttons of gristle against the stone. 

Then comes the *clonk*—a sound that travels not through the air, but through the water and the bone, vibrating the very marrow of the place. The wave rolls outward, an inch-high wall of displaced tea-water that breaks against the weeping masonry. The bacterial pelts ripple. The fungal ropes swing. The green photophores on the under-carapaces flash once in a scattered, erratic pattern of cold fireflies, and then settle back into darkness as the water levels out, the bucket rights itself with a sucking hiss, and the quiet, continuous grazing begins again.
