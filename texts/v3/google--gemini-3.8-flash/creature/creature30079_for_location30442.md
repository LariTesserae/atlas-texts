The beetles and the salamanders are only the skin-fauna of the gorge, the things that touch the metal where it clears the wet rock. Deeper inside the clearance spaces—where an iron lobe sweeps past a wall of mirror-black basalt with a gap no wider than a playing card—the life becomes flatter, thinner, and less recognizable as animal.

There are the *shims*. They are long, ribbony organisms, scarcely thicker than a leaf of rolled tin, that have given up both eyes and limbs to dwell entirely within the shear zones. A shim is four or five feet in length, dull leaden grey, with an underside covered in thousands of microscopic, suction-tipped cilia that drink the cold film of crude weeping from the seams. They do not crawl so much as they allow the hydraulic suction of the descending linkages to draw them through the stroke. When a six-ton counterweight plunges into its wet sump, a shim rides the displacement wave like a ribbon caught in the wash of an oar, snapping around the eccentric without ever being crushed. Their flesh is not soft; it has the density of vulcanized gutta-percha, self-healing and rubbery, impregnated with so much fine zinc-dust that their dead skins, shed twice a year, do not decay but are flattened into paper-thin gaskets that the rock-joints catch and turn into permanent, functioning packings.

Higher up, where the mist hangs thickest and the copper-colored static from the sky begins to prickle against the damp basalt, there are things that fly, though their flight has nothing to do with the flapping of wings.

They are known—if they have a name at all—as the *governor-birds*, though they are closer to heavy, leathery kites than anything avian. They have no feathers; their skins are tanned hide the color of wet slate, stretched drum-tight over skeletons of hollow, tubular bone that ring like cast-iron pipe when struck. They roost in the small, dry niches where the exhaust valves sneeze out their three-stroke bursts of superheated air. 

A governor-bird does not search for food. It does not eat in any metabolic sense familiar to the lowlands; its gullet is lined with plates of raw, fibrous horn that act as rectifiers, absorbing the violent ambient electrical gradient between the ion-charged clouds above and the grounded iron of the gorge bed. When one takes off, it simply lets go of the stone with its four broad, nail-less pads and drops directly into the rhythmic draft of an exhaust manifold. The pulse of steam catches it like a piston head, tossing it forty feet straight up into the mist. It circles there in the tight, rectangular airspace between the crags, never tilting its stiff, parabolic wings, merely riding the predictable acoustic nodes created by the thrum of the floor. When it lands, it does so with a flat, leaden *thwack* against the vertical basalt, adhering instantly by capillary action against the slick, oiled stone.

In the deep sumps—the blind, black basins at the foot of the rift where the distilled petroleum gathers into pools six fathoms deep—the motion of the rock-arms slows. The strokes here are long, hydraulic, and ponderous, thirty seconds to the down-thrust, displacing tons of clear, odorless oil that has never seen sunlight.

Here lives the *baleen-lung*. 

It is the largest creature in the rift, nearly the size of a canal barge, but it possesses no head, no fins, and no tail. It is a single, massive bellows of ribbed cartilage, anchored between two basalt buttresses that move toward and away from each other on a forty-second cycle. The animal has grown around these crags until its tissue is indistinguishable from the mineral strata; its calcified ligaments have penetrated the microscopic fissures of the stone, drawing mechanical energy directly from the displacement of the mountain.

As the rock-walls squeeze together, the baleen-lung is compressed, its massive internal chambers emptying with a low, sub-audible moan that can be felt in the marrow of the legs five miles down the drainage. As the walls withdraw, the bellows expands, drawing through its thousands of comb-like strainers the cold, dead oil of the sump. It does not digest flesh; it filters out the tiny, crystalline scales of graphite left behind by the zinc beetles, the drift of shed fern-spores that never rot, and the dense, paraffin wax that precipitates from the rock’s own perspiration. It is an organism that exists solely to clear the oil of its sediment, ensuring the bearings do not foul. It has been doing this for so long that the rock face behind it has smoothed into an immaculate, spherical pocket that fits the curve of its dorsal sac to the millimeter.

And there is something else—something that moves against the tempo.

It is rarely seen entire, because it lives only in the dry interstices between the wet strokes: the narrow pockets of space that exist for less than a second between an iron rod’s extension and its recoil. 

It is roughly human in scale, but its proportions are stretched and spindly, like a creature drawn out on an anvil. Its skin is not flesh and not iron, but a dry, fibrous pith, like the inside of an ancient cedar log that has been soaked in tallow. It has no face—only a vertical, grease-caked seam down the front of its narrow head that opens slightly to taste the static charge in the air before a cycle turns.

This thing—the *tender*, though it tends nothing—climbs the sheer basalt faces by tucking its long, segmented fingers into the microscopic clearances of the journals. It moves not with the speed of an animal, but with an uncanny, pre-calculated anticipation. It places a hand where an eccentric cam will be in three-tenths of a second; it ducks its narrow, hairless head through the sweep of a twenty-foot flywheel just as the balance-weight clears the timber-moss. It does not run from the machine, nor does it worship it. It inhabits the gaps. 

Sometimes, if one could watch from the motionless clouds above, the tender can be seen perched on the highest rock-joint—where the natural iron of the rift's main beam twists out of the mountain to meet the sky—sitting with its knees drawn up to its chin, completely still. It does not breathe; the air here is too heavy with petroleum mist and ozone to support true lungs. Instead, its flanks expand and contract with a dry, wooden creak that matches the stroke of the sump far below.

It has no companions. Occasionally it reaches down into a recess with a long, twig-like arm and removes a pebble that has fallen from the high scree, holding the grit in its palm for a long moment before dropping it down the exhaust clefts, where it is instantly reduced to powder by the reciprocating stone. 

It does not sleep, because the machine does not idle. If it paused, if it miscalculated the swing of the slate cams by the width of a fingernail, the gorge would take it back without a sound—shearing it into the grey, graphite slurry that keeps the iron from screaming against the stone, adding another fraction of an inch to the clearance that the water and the oil have preserved since the rift first cracked.
