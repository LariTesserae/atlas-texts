At the base of the machine’s brick-and-timber bedding, where the run-off gathers into shallow, scalding pools before sinking through the basalt grit, life organizes itself entirely around the cycle of the beam.

The velvet-black beetles are only the most visible tier of an invertebrate population whose blood is calibrated to the heat of an engine room. They are hornless, broad-backed scarabs with soft, felted elytra that soak up the mist until they are heavy as drowned moles. Their grubs—pale, tallow-white cylinders the thickness of a thumb—do not live in the earth; they bore directly into the saturated, rotting pine balks that anchor the pump’s foot-plates. They feed on timber that has been pickled for decades in animal grease and sulfurous steam, chewing through wood so sodden it can be gouged with a fingernail. Because the grubs excrete a dense, waxy frass that resists mold, the boreholes behind the cylinder blocks stay dry and warm, becoming tiny, pressurized hives where the pupae hang, gently trembling in time with the forty-second percussive stroke from above.

Between the boiler-iron and the rock face, where the water sheets down at near-boiling temperatures, there are no plants, only ribbons of an elastic, liver-colored slime that is neither algae nor fungus. It crawls across the quartzite cleavage planes in sheets three inches thick, feeding on the manganese precipitates and the aerosolized lard blown from the valve-vent. 

Feeding on this slime are the cleft’s true natives: a colony of spade-headed, blind efts. They are no longer than a pencil, translucent pink like fresh tongue-meat, with external gills that wave like tufts of boiled wool in the thermal wash. They have no eyes to burn in the noon sun; they navigate the sheer walls by clinging with glandular belly-pads that produce an adhesive mucus. They are essentially creatures of the subterranean aquifer, spat out of the mountain's torn belly along with the hot water. In the light, they remain tucked into the darkest fissures behind the iron casings, coming out only during the rhythmic five-second lull after the steam-valve vents—that sudden, breathless instant when the mist settles and the chasm is briefly quiet. When the machine rocks back and groans, they flatten themselves against the stone, letting the deep vibration rattle their ribs without dislodging them.

Higher up, where the air cools slightly and the ferns grow heavy enough to form a suspended floor between the girders, there are warm-blooded things that have drifted down from the arctic wastes above and stayed, ruined for the cold.

They were once mountain voles—stout, gray, woolly creatures built for sub-zero scree and short summers. Here, generation after generation in eighty-degree dampness has unmade them. Their fur has sloughed away into a thin, grease-matted bristle through which their wrinkled, mottled skin shows raw and pink. Their legs have lengthened; their claws have hooked into sharp, semi-prehensile curved picks that allow them to scuttle upside-down along the pump’s rivet rows and cross-braces. They nest inside the box girders of the great walking beam itself. It is a violent nursery: thirty feet up, rising and dipping twelve feet with every agonizing stroke of the counterweight. The young are born blind and bald in the rocking hollow of the iron, cradled by the rhythmic, oceanic heave that tilts their world toward the sky, then drops it toward the black gravel, over and over, every forty seconds of their lives.

These "iron-rats" do not eat seeds; there are none. They harvest the thick, starch-heavy croziers of the oar-ferns, stripping them with their teeth, and they hunt the velvet beetles, biting the heads off to lap the fatty grease stored within their thoracic cavities. Their claws are perpetually blackened with cylinder oil; their musk smells faintly of scorched bacon and wet soot.

And there is a hand that oils the trunnions.

It is not an engineer, not anymore, and it does not maintain the machine out of duty to a ledger or a mine below. It lives in the dry triangular pocket formed where an overhanging slab of quartzite shelters the high-pressure cylinder. The shelter is furnished with nothing: a mound of dried fern fronds that have rotted into a mattress of yellow peat, three gallon-tins of tallow salvaged from forgotten stores down-chasm, and an iron pry-bar worn smooth at the balance.

The creature was a man, though the cleft has stripped him of the need for speech or clothing. He wears only a pair of trousers cut off at the knee, impregnated so thoroughly with crankcase grease and paraffin that the fabric has become a stiff, flexible hide, waterproof and self-supporting. His skin where it meets the mist is not pale, but stained an uneven mahogany by the iron-water and the sun that spikes through at midday. His hair is cropped short with a broken utility blade, his beard gummed into two coarse braids tucked into his waistband to keep it from the gears.

He is stone-deaf. The forty-foot beam took his ears years ago, flattening his world until sound is entirely tactile: the initial *click* of the valve trip in the small of his back, the deep iron *thrum* in his teeth, the wet *shudder* through the soles of his bare feet as the counterweights seat themselves. 

His life is small and completely mechanical. If he sleeps, it is during the intervals when the pump's exhaust creates a steady, drumming heat-blanket over his shelf. His work consists entirely of five movements: he scrapes the yellow sulfur-crust from the bronze slide-guides with a chisel; he scoops tallow from the tins with a flat pine paddle and packs it into the brass lubricator caps before they run dry and squeal; he pulls the dead ferns away from the exhaust ports so the valves do not choke; he catches the fat beetles that crawl near his bedding and eats their bellies raw, wiping the green fat from his lips with the back of a calloused, black-rimmed hand.

He understands what the animals understand: the machine is the only thing standing between this cleft and the sky. Above the lip of the rock, the frost is already forming on the scree, two hundred feet over his head, where the thin air smells of nothing at all and kills by midnight. Down here, the piston rises. The bronze trunnion strains. A jet of hot, greasy water shoots twenty feet across the gravel, and the air stays wet, foul, and eighty degrees. He sits with his shoulder pressed hard against the cylinder jacket, warming his bones against the iron boiler-plate, waiting for the counterweight to fall again.
