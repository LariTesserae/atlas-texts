To look closely into the floor is to lose the sense of individuals entirely, at least at first. What presents itself to the eye is a single, continental crust of jointed plate that buckles, settles, and crawls within its own borders. But if you watch a single span of iron—say, the lip of a disused drainage gutter where the metal has been rounded into a bolster as smooth as a horse’s tooth—the carpet resolves into four or five distinct manners of existing.

Nearest the iron itself, in the crushed dark beneath the top layers, are the *planires*—the broad, flat grazers that never see the grey light from the grating above. They are nearly two-dimensional, no thicker than the blade of an oil-spatula, five to eight inches across and composed of eleven overlapping flanges of dull, graphite-colored tergite. They move by the action of sixty or seventy pairs of hooked, microscopic points on their ventral side, sliding over the bare iron like wet suction cups, though they are bone-dry. Their entire underside is a single rasper: a wide, horn-like plate that vibrates at an imperceptible frequency against the substrate. They do not eat metal, but they clean it of the last trace of grease-soot, dead shell-flour, and the dry, yellowish grease that has seeped into the crystalline grain of the iron over eighty years of compression. Where a planire has crawled, the steel is left with an oily, gunmetal sheen, utterly sterile, reflecting the sky above like a smear of mercury.

These flat ones do not run. When the cam thuds overhead every eight seconds, they do not flinch by leaping; instead, their flanges clamp downward with an audible *tock*, vacuuming themselves against the smooth metal so tightly that no predator can get a pick beneath them. Over their backs crawl the second rank: the *hoppers* or *shale-seeders*.

The seeders are the pumpkin-seed-sized bulk of the population. They have the rounded, polished contours of coffee beans, clad in an enamel-like shell that is not chitin in the normal sense, but an amalgam of silicate dust, hardened cerumen, and shed cuticle pounded together by friction. Their legs are kept folded beneath their skirts. They do not walk so much as they are tumbled by the movement of their neighbors; they are a fluid, a dry grain that fills every void. They subsist on the powdery exuviae—the shed skins of the larger crawlers—which they crush in their spherical, revolving gizzards until it reaches the consistency of chimney-sweep’s soot. 

Because they cannot retain heat individually in the drafty chill of the pit, they generate a dull, communal warmth through the sheer mechanics of their crowd: millions of polished hulls grinding against one another generate a friction that can be felt through the soles of an observer’s boots as a faint, oily fever rising through the grating. In the corners where the drifts are four inches deep, the lower inches are warm—not with the wet heat of compost, but the dry, static heat of an electric motor running inside a wool sack.

Above them, operating in the open air like long-legged wading birds upon an expanse of grey shingle, are the *striders*. These are the length of an awl, entirely leg, their bodies reduced to an elongated thorax no thicker than a quill, colored like cold lard with three bands of black iron-scab. Their limbs are three-jointed, tipped with needle points of transparent amber that never touch the iron; they walk exclusively upon the smooth, curved backs of the seeders below, stepping with a delicate, mechanical precision that seems almost polite. 

The striders have no jaws for biting. Instead, their heads terminate in a pair of hardened, shear-bladed forceps that face downward. They are hydraulic in their movements: they pause, perfectly rigid, while three turns of the overhead engine pass—twenty-four seconds of absolute petrification—and then, precisely on the fourth shudder, their long legs snap straight, driving the forceps down into the gaps between the seeders. They do not hunt the seeders, who are too hard and offer no liquid; they fish for the *marrows*.

The marrows are the larval form of the great wall-crawlers, though they resemble nothing so much as three-inch sections of greasy hemp rope. They inhabit the seams where the concrete foundation has separated from the cast-iron sills. Having no shells yet, only a tough, parchment-like hide coated in an unctuous, white tallow that smells faintly of mutton fat and cold oil, they are the only reservoirs of free moisture in the chamber. A marrow contains perhaps two thimbles of a thick, grey, lymphatic gel that does not evaporate even in this draft. To the predators, they are wells. 

When a strider’s forceps find a marrow, there is no struggle. The strider simply locks its shears into the segment behind the head and stands immovable, letting the cam’s vibration drive its hollow proboscis deeper into the grub’s core. The extraction takes three engine cycles. By the time the strider lifts its stilts again, the grub is a flat, dry ribbon of white skin, already stiffening, already beginning to be dragged beneath the seeders where it will be pulverized by afternoon.

The largest of the pit’s inhabitants live entirely upon the perpendiculars. They are the *iron-plane beetles*—creatures the size of a cobbler’s last, heavy, silent, and ancient-looking. Their shells are not smooth like the others; they are pitted and encrusted with the very slag they live among. Many carry long, dead stalactites of dried engine-tallow upon their backs like miniature mountain ranges, built up over years of scraping beneath the cold pillow blocks where the overhead shafts enter the walls. 

These plane-beetles move at the rate of an inch an hour. Their front limbs are broad, scoop-shaped spades of thick horn, edged with a row of teeth like an excavator’s bucket. They rasp methodically against the iron, paring away the old grease-crusts that have turned hard as slate over fifty winters. Their jaws are not paired side-to-side, but work vertically: an upper anvil of horn and a lower hammer of hardened bone that beats a slow, rhythmic tattoo against the steel, pulverizing the grease into small, oily pellets that they sweep into their gullets with four feathered palps.

They are so heavy that when they lose their footing on the greasy vertical iron, their fall through the floor-carpet produces a sound like a flat stone dropped into a bin of wheat. They do not turn over easily; they will lie on their domed backs for days, their twelve legs waving in a slow, cyclical rowing motion that matches the eight-second engine thump, until the blind shifting of the millions of small hoppers beneath them gradually floats them right-side-up, like a ship righted by an incoming tide of pebbles.

There is no mating season; there is only the *hollowing*. 

When an iron-plane beetle reaches the end of its decades—and some appear, by the depth of the graphite rings upon their shields, to have been crawling when the overhead sorting house still had glass in its sashes—its inner tissues do not break down into liquid. They turn to chalk. The animal ceases to feed; it clamps its spades into an iron seam, lowers its forehead until it touches the rivet-line, and waits. The hoppers immediately begin to clean its joints. They work their way under the edges of the elytra, clearing away the dry pleura, entering the cavities of the limbs, polishing the empty interior until the beetle is nothing but a hollow cathedral of cold horn, still pegged firmly to the masonry. 

Into this empty shell, the striders deposit their eggs—dry, oblong seeds of dull amber that need neither warmth nor damp, only the shelter of the old hull against the grinding pressure of the floor-drifts. When the young emerge, they are already stiff, already dry, their legs already tipped with the amber needles that will let them walk upon the living floor without ever touching the metal that is slowly wearing the room away to dust.

Above them all, the cam goes on falling: *clunk-shudder*. 

The million small backs click against each other, the wave of dry sound travels forty paces to the sump-end of the pit, strikes the dead iron of the bulkhead, and dies into the grey light. None of them look up at the hook hanging from the crane rail. None of them have ever seen the sky through the grating as anything more than the source of the coolness that keeps their shells from splitting.
