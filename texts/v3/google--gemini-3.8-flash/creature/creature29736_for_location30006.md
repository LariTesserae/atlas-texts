The creatures that move along the horizontal branches of the grafted canopy have no need for claws that hook or cling; their pads are wide and spatulate, like the undersides of rising dough. 

Foremost among them are the fleece-beasts, whom an outside eye might mistake for otters until they turn their faces, which are flat, jawless, and dominated by a pair of pale, mobile nostrils shaped like the cross-section of a split reed. Their fur does not grow in hairs, but in dense, tubular filaments that trap bubbles of the plateau’s greasy warmth, so that even when drenched, they feel fever-hot to the touch. They live by weight and patience. An adult will lie across a branch-graft for three daylight shifts, its four stubby limbs dangling into the jade fog, chewing at the wet rind of an ox-cart fungus with a rasping plate made of yellowed keratin. They do not fight for territory; their ribcages are so broad and their movements so oil-slack that two meeting upon a narrow highway of bark will simply press their bellies together and roll over one another like two sacks of wet flour, their oily pelts sliding without friction until they have swapped places and resume their progress in opposite directions.

Farther down, in the layer where the mist is tinted brown by the breath of the rotting lower strata, the slugs are the architects of the silence. They grow as long as a calf’s thigh, white-bellied and slate-backed, their hides patterned with the fine, cracked reticulation of dry river silt. They are cold-blooded, the only truly cold things on the tableland. As they move, their heavy, muscled skirts deposit a mucus so clear and viscous that it sets within hours into an amber-like track that lasts for decades before the moss can swallow it. These slug-roads are the closest thing the plateau has to stone: hard, slick causeways across the give of the moss-sponge, used by everything that walks because they do not yield beneath a step and do not squelch.

Where there is a basin of tannic water, there are the breathers. They are lung-fishes that have ceased to swim, having become flat, disc-shaped things the color of stewed tea. They lie stacked three or four deep in the black, inch-deep silt, their gills fluttering against the moss-fiber like damp silk. When the water in a terrace thins to a glaze during the warm mid-afternoons, they do not dry; their skin secretes an alkaline sweat that curdles the mist around them into a private, jelly-like cowl. They have small, milky eyes that do not see form, only the passage of the medusae overhead, and when an aerial bell dies and drops from the fog like a discarded lung, the breathers rise beneath it in a slow, communal boil, dismantling the pale jelly with mouths that are nothing more than round, toothless puckers of wet skin.

And then there are those who drove the pegs.

They are few, and they do not live in bands large enough to leave a trail of refuse. If they have a name for themselves, it is lost in the damp, for their voice is not made for distance; sound here is an offense against the moss, which eats syllables before they can reach the second ear. 

They are small-bodied—barely four feet when standing erect, though they rarely straighten their spines entirely, preferring the low, three-pointed gait that keeps a hand always in contact with the sponge. Their skin has the gray-amber tone of lard that has sat in a cupboard, completely free of hair except for a thick, greasy whorl at the crown of the head that hangs down the neck like a horse’s mane, braided with the dried fibers of inner cork-bark. Because they live in air that is always seventy-four degrees and saturated to the limit of vapor, they wear no hide or cloth. Their bodies are mapped with pale, puckered calluses where the hip and shoulder press against the wood during sleep, and their feet have no distinct arches: the sole is a thick, square cushion of fat and gristle that spreads six inches wide when loaded, whispering through the moss like the foot of an elephant.

They are not hunters in any sense that requires pursuit. They do not run. Their hearts beat twenty-two times in a minute. 

When a maker needs to feed, it crawls to the edge of a root-knee and sets its chin upon the bark. It will wait there through the ambering of two skies until one of the white crayfish—heavy as a turnip and blind as lard—creeps out of a water-hollow to browse the root-moss. Then the maker’s hand, which has rested beside the hole so long that the water-beads have formed continuous necklaces across its knuckles, simply closes. The fingers do not have nails; they end in broad, spade-like studs of horn that can pry a crayfish shell open with a single twist of the wrist. They eat the meat raw, sucking it out with a quiet, whistling intake of breath that sounds like air entering an open reed.

Their dwellings are not built; they are carved into the trees while the trees are still small, two centuries before the hollow is occupied. 

A maker will find a young cork-graft where three taproots knot together above a hollow of dry peat. With a chisel shaped from the leg-bone of a fleece-beast, ground down against a river-cobble that may have belonged to its great-grandmother, the maker removes a thumbnail of bark each day. It does not kill the wood; it guides the scar-tissue. Over seventy years, the tree grows around the absent space, swelling outward like an eyelid around an empty socket, until a cell is formed: three feet wide, four feet long, lined with a smooth, living skin of amber sap that never rots and never dries completely. 

Inside these hollows, they sleep curled upon their left sides, their knees tucked into their chests, their long manes wrapped over their faces to filter the pollen of the shelf-fungi, which causes the lungs to weep if breathed too raw. In the wall of each chamber, an inch above the floor, is a single peg made of ironwood or petrified bog-pine. This peg is not a peg for hanging; it is a hearing-post. The sleeper rests the bone behind its ear against the dark wood. Through it, the entire plateau speaks: the five-mile-distant shift of an old peat-shelf sinking into the shale; the heavy, plodding shuffle of a herd of fleece-beasts crossing an aerial bridge; the subterranean drumming that occurs when one of the continental gas-pockets deep within the shale burps its lukewarm breath into the root-matrix.

They communicate across the waste not by shouting, but by these pegs. A maker sitting at the mouth of its root-hole will take a rounded piece of petrified wood and tap once—a dry, wood-on-wood *tock* that has no air-travel whatsoever. But ten miles away, a child with her chin pressed to the floor of her nursery-hollow will feel the tap in her molars. There is a whole geography of timber: some trees conduct with a high, bell-like ring that means the moss above them is thin and perilous; others have a soft, muffled thud that indicates their heartwood is dead and can be inhabited without the tree trying to fill the chamber with resin.

Their most sacred possessions—if they understand such a thing—are the stones.

To a people who have never known a pebble that did not come from an ancestor’s sack, stone is an unnatural miracle. It does not yield; it does not sweat; it does not rot. The river-washed cobbles that sit in rings upon the swells are visited like shrines. A maker will travel for three moon-cycles, guided only by the alignment of the peg-lines driven into the peat, simply to sit by a gray granite stone the size of an ostrich egg. It will lay both palms upon the rock, marveling at its absolute, mineral refusal to become part of the plateau. They lick the stones to taste the absence of acid; they rub their foreheads against them until the stone is greasy with human oil. When a maker feels its chest growing heavy with the white lung-rot that eventually takes all of them in their sixth or seventh decade, it does not lie down in its own hollow. It crawls to the stone-ring.

There, it lies down in the shallow trough between the cobbles. The slugs find it first, drawn by the salt of its sweat. Within three shifts of the milky sun, the slug-trails have covered the hands and ankles in a clear, drying glaze. Then the moss-fingers—the fine, hair-like protonema of the giant peat-mosses—creep into the nostrils and between the toes. 

The makers do not bury their dead because the ground has no shovel-depth; it is all sinew and heartwood. Instead, they watch from the high canopy walkways as the body becomes a mound, then a knoll, then an identical swell of green velvet upon the endless floor, until the only sign that a person once lay there is that the shelf-fungus growing from that particular spot has an edge of pale, bone-colored yellow along its rim, and the sap that drips from it tastes, for a season or two, faintly of salt.
