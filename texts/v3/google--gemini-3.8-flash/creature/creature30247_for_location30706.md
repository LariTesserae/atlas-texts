To move without water is to live inside an engine that has never known oil. The things that walk, slide, and bore through the marrow-wood rift do not have joints of slick cartilage; their articulations are ball-and-socket hinges of cold, natural horn, ground to an optical tolerance and dusted with micro-particulate graphite that the creatures sweat from dry pores.

Static is their god and their executioner. In an atmosphere so packed with resinous dust and airborne volatile terpenes, a single jump of blue voltage—a scuff of dry claw on dry flake—would detonate horizons in a millisecond. Therefore, the fauna is built entirely of anti-spark. Nothing possesses coarse fur; nothing rubs dry hair on dry hair. They are coated in conductive slates of shellac, thin films of waxy grease that never evaporate because they were never wet, and trailing plumages of carbonized fiber that brush behind them like wire brooms, constantly grounding them against the calcified veins of the rift floor.

The smallest things are the **Chalk-Tickers**. They are not insects, though they have eight or twelve legs that move in rigid, escapement-wheel rhythms. Their bodies are flattened buttons of hard, sun-bleached pith, no heavier than dried leaves. They do not feed on sap; they graze the microscopic frost of crystalline sugars that exude from the wood as it splits under tension. When a trunk snaps, they do not run; they turn their broad, concave backs toward the fracture, catching the supersonic shockwave like sails, tossed thousands of feet across the gorge to land right-side-up with the hollow *tock* of a chess piece dropping on an empty crate. Their mouths are tiny, counter-revolving augers of pure quartz that drill into the fibers not for fluid, but to scrape out dry nitrogen-meal, packing their bellies until they are as hard and dense as billiard balls.

Then there are the **Galleon-Worms**—though among the rift-crawlers they are called *The Augers*. They have no eyes; what would they look at? The glare from above does not pierce the deep pith-strata, and the air is an acoustic sledgehammer. They see through four massive, cantilevered balance-horns that extend thirty cubits ahead of their primary bore. These horns are tuned to distinct frequencies of stress-cracking: they can feel the difference between wood that is green with compressed vapor and wood that is dry-rotted and hollow two miles deep.

An Auger does not crawl with muscle. Its body is an accordion of linked, ironwood-thick segments, each one housing an internal bellows that fills with the hot, subterranean gas-belches of the rift. It expands its forward segments, anchors them into the marrow-wood with hydraulically thrown anchor-spikes made of mineralized tooth, and then exhausts its rear, hauling hundreds of tons of segmented carcass forward with a dry, screaming shudder that sounds like an iron bridge collapsing in slow motion. Behind it, it leaves no slime, only a bore-hole fifty feet wide, packed with warm, fragrant excelsior that smells of ground cloves and parched sawdust. In its wake follow the scavengers: paper-wasps with six-foot wing-spans of cured amber who immediately harvest the sawdust to paste together towering, cellular chimneys that act as natural bellows, siphoning the high-pressure gas off the Auger’s wake before it can ignite.

The mantises mentioned by the hunters are the rift’s silence-seekers. They are known as **Striders of the Pale**. Because the floor is a mechanical roar that ruptures human eardrums in minutes, these creatures have evolved to live entirely within the white daylight of the ceiling rafters. They are twenty feet tall, yet weigh less than an adolescent boy, constructed entirely of air-filled tubes of paper-thin chitin braced internally with geometric trusses of spun dry-silk.

They do not fly by flapping—to flap would produce too much friction. They unfold immense, ribbed sails of translucent skin, catch the permanent furnace-drafts that boil out of the rift’s bottomless cracks, and hang suspended against the mineral veins. They hunt by dropping. A Strider will fold its legs flush against its needle-like body, falling like a plumb-line through three thousand feet of screaming air, only opening its braking-fans five paces above a target. Its claws do not grasp; they are spring-loaded guillotine blades held under tension by dried sinews that it tightens over hours of rest. When it releases the ratchet, the blades snap shut with a sound like a rifle crack, severing the carapaces of the mirror-beetles and harvesting only the warm, white lard from their metabolic glands—the only substance in the rift that mimics grease.

And the people—or the things that occupy the cages hung from the galls.

They are not descendants of any race that remembers a sky with clouds. If their ancestors had names for rain, those names have been sanded down by centuries of throat-grating dust until only clicking consonants remain. Their lungs are five times the size of an ordinary man’s, barrel-chested and rigid, functioning like bellows that draw the heavy, aromatic air through three separate filters of woven hair-fiber fitted into their nostrils.

They do not speak with voice. Between the deafening thrum of the wood-cracking and the absolute deadness of the desiccated air, the voice carries only three paces before being shredded. They communicate through **knuckle-strike** and **bone-lash**. Every hunter carries a dry dog-wood baton; they communicate across their vibrating bridges by tapping rhythmic codes into the living tension-lines of their hamlets. A message travels fifty miles in fifteen seconds through the taught, petrified boughs: *Auger coming; grease-harvest; cut the rungs.*

Their skin is not human skin. It is cured, like the hide of a boot that has spent fifty years in a salt-cellar. It does not sweat; to sweat would mean death, for water does not exist to replace it. What moisture they possess is sealed within an internal, closed-loop lymph system, thick and viscous as mineral turpentine, that moves sluggishly through hardened veins. They do not drink; they suck the **Gall-Marrows**—fist-sized nodules that form where the cathedrals of wood cross each other under extreme pressure. Inside these galls, protected from the baking air by walls three fingers thick, the wood condenses a bitter, white fat that is ninety-nine percent lipid and heavy hydrocarbons. They chew it until their jaws ache, extracting the hydrogen to run their dry, hot bodies, spitting the woody fibers out to be used as bow-strings or cage-bindings.

Their greatest terror is not the beasts, nor the falls into the splintered gulfs where the wood-shards would skewer them like meat on a fork. It is the **White Bloom**.

Once every few seasons, the ceiling’s white veins pulse with an irregular, blue-tinged brightness, and the marrow-wood enters its rut. The stalks don't grow twenty paces an hour; they grow forty. The hanging hamlets are torn apart by their own anchors as the wood surges beneath them. The rungs of their ladders split into barbed fans; the bridges snap their moorings under sudden, monstrous tension. During the White Bloom, the people cannot touch the wood. They climb into silk slung-nests that hang by hair-lines from the non-living rock of the ceiling, pulling their legs up like spiders, dangling in the roaring gale of spores while the forest below them devours itself, breaks its own ribs in its frenzy to reach the light, and crashes down in miles-long tumbles of ivory ruin.

Down there, in the pitch-dry foundations that never see the light, something else lives—something that has never grown a shell, because it does not fear the fall.

The hunters look down through the horn slats of their floors and see only the **Sifters**. They are vast, formless hills of gray hair—not mammal hair, but billions of microscopic, flexible silica threads that roll across the shattered bottom like dry tumbleweeds miles wide. They have no brains, no hearts. They are colonies of mindless, living burrs that feed by tumbling over the dead boughs, grinding the splintered cathedrals to pure powder between their bristling filaments. They absorb the aromatic dust by simple contact, leaving behind only paths of polished, white-hot bed-rock that immediately crack open, hissing out fresh gas, to let the next generation of iron-stalks detonate upward toward the white ceiling once again.

The rift does not want to be quiet. It is an engine of pure leverage, bone-colored and snapping, that has forgotten what it means to be soft, or cool, or still.
