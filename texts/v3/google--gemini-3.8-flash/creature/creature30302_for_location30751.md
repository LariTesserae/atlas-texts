The mid-water does not belong to the eels alone. 

Between the flagstones and the tallow-scummed surface, where the cold alkalinity is most biting, move the sump-dogs. They are not dogs, nor otters, though they possess the long, supple spine of mustelids; they are the get of old terriers and wharf-curs that followed rat-runs down the coal-chutes eighty years ago and found nothing to kill that could not swim. Over generations, the dark and the mineral-bath have peeled away their coats, leaving a skin like boiled pork—tough, slate-colored, and permanently puckered by damp. Their paws have spread into broad, spongy pads with skin bridging the toes, silent as chamois leather on the drowned flags. They have no use for barking; their lungs are small and slow, conserving air for thirty-minute crouches behind the submerged plinths. 

When a sump-dog hunts, it does not chase. It lies across an iron axle like a dropped coat, its blunt, pink muzzle resting just above the waterline, waiting for the walking-beam’s southern tilt. When the heavy iron groans and shifts, the disturbance flushes the white eels from the spokes. Then the dog slips into the water without a splash—a dull, gray ripple—and takes them by the middle, swallowing them head-first and whole so the needle-teeth do not tear its throat.

Above them, where the iron ribs spring from the walls, lives the matter of their nests. 

In the dry recesses of the beam-housings—inside the hollow cast-iron bedplates where the foundation bolts are thick as men’s thighs—there are beds made of eighty years’ accumulation of greasy boiler-lagging, chewed starling wings, and dry hemp. Here the dogs sleep piled three and four deep to keep the blood warm. Their body heat is slight—scarcely more than that of a hibernating toad—and their dreams are soundless, marked only by the occasional twitch of a webbed foot against the rust-crust.

Yet they are not the masters of the Nave. They give a wide berth to the middle bay, where the dry bulkhead meets the water.

There is a man who lives on the brick steps, though to call him a man is to use a word that belongs to the daylight above the perforated iron plates. 

If he had a name when he came down, it has dissolved like the lime on the bolts. He has been here long enough that his clothes have ceased to be textiles; they are an armoring of tallow, coal-silt, and boiled leather, stiff as harness-board and smelling faintly of sheep-grease and vinegar. He does not wear shoes. The soles of his feet are yellow and horny, two inches broader than a city man’s, the toes splayed wide to grip the grease-filmed slime of the submerged flags without sliding.

He does not carry a light. Light would be an injury to eyes that have widened until the irises are merely thin, pale rings around pupils the size of dried sloes. He navigates by the church-click of the greasy starlings and the pressure of the air against his ears. He knows within a hair’s breadth when the forty-minute *thung* is due; five minutes before the water rises by its coin-thickness, he shifts his seat from the second brick step to the third, tucking his knees beneath his grease-crusted smock, his chin resting on his wrists.

His primary work—the thing that keeps him from rotting into the flagstones like an unrecovered sack—is the tallow-cup on the single working walking-beam.

Twice every turn of the light above, when the columns of leaden rinse through the coal-chutes fade from ash-gray into the dead pitch of night, he wades out. The water comes to his hips, parting around his leather coat with a faint, oily hiss. He carries a scraper made from a barrel-hoop and a horn vessel filled with grease he renders from the dead river-lice and the thick fat-crusts scraped from the overflow conduits. 

He climbs the rusted ladder of the southern stela with an ape’s ease, his broad, callous-padded toes hooking into the iron rungs. At the gudgeon-pin—the four-foot pivot upon which the thirty-ton beam balances—he sits astride the cold iron. For two hours, moving with a slowness that mimics the engine itself, he works the paste into the dry hemp packing. He does not use grease to make it run fast; he greases it so it will not scream. To him, the Nave is a soundbox, and a squeal from a dry bearing would be an agony that could not escape the brick vault for days.

While he sits there, the birds settle on his shoulders. 

They do not fear him any more than they fear the stationary balance-wheel. They pick at the crusts of tallow on the seams of his leather coat, and occasionally, with an ancient, domestic familiarity that suggests an arrangement older than his own memory, he reaches up with two fingers—blackened to the knuckle by graphite—and gently strokes the gray, webbed feathers along their necks. When he does this, his throat produces the same dry, clicking percussion as their own: a rattle of tongue against pallet, hollow as dice in a wooden cup.

Below him, in the vertical well beneath the cylinder—a shaft twelve feet wide that drops straight into the coal measures, drowned sixty feet deep in stagnant black liquor—lives the oldest thing in the Nave.

It has never come to the surface, but the man knows its girth by the displaced water when it turns in the sump. It is perhaps forty feet long: an enormous, blanched conger or lung-fish, blind as lard, that came up the subterranean river-flues during the great inundation of the mid-century and could not find its way back past the silted flap-valves. It lives on whatever drops down the cylinder well: the dogs that die in their sleep, the carcasses of drowned cats that wash through the road-grates after autumn rains, and the endless, silent drift of white lice that fall from the balance-wheel like winter meal.

When the great fish stirs in the deep shaft, the water on the terrace does not merely rise by a coin; it swells in a long, greasy heave that lifts the dead scum two inches up the vitrified walls. 

The man waits for that heave. When it comes, he leans down from the walking-beam and lowers a long hazel rod, tipped with an iron crook that has been rubbed smooth with starling-fat. He dips it into the well-mouth, feels the thick, slow drag of water that has not seen an atmosphere for fifty winters, and waits for the crook to touch the gray flank below.

He does not strike. He merely holds the iron there, feeling the deep, cold vibration of the creature’s heart—a pump slower even than the walking-beam, striking once every hundred heartbeats of his own. 

For ten minutes, while the gray light through the road-slots dies completely, iron, beast, and man remain joined by the rod: three things whose engines were built for an era of steam and thunder, now brought down to the same cool, alkaline crawl, waiting together for the boilers to be relit, or for the brickwork to finally fall in.
