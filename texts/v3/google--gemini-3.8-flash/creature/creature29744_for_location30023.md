To see them first is usually to misjudge their speed, because they do not seem to be swimming so much as falling or settling, like the larch needles, along the wicker lanes. 

They are people, though the water has altered the terms of their humanity. Their ribcages are enormous—barrel-shaped and flared at the base like upturned iron kettles—giving them an almost neckless, hunched silhouette. Beneath their skin lies a thick, dense mantle of pale fat, not soft like lowland lard, but fibrous and firm as salt pork, insulating them against the constant near-freezing chill of the tarn. Because of this blubber, their bodies are naturally buoyant; without their gear, they would bob helplessly to the ceiling of the cirque, bursting through into the thin alpine air.

To counter this, their clothes are instruments of gravity. They do not wear spun cloth, which would drag and shred in the currents, but harness-aprons of stiff, scraped horsehide sewn with copper wire and studded with lead-shot and river-ground slate. A child here learns to crawl by pulling themselves hand-over-hand along the braided sedge ropes, weighted down by a pair of slate rings strapped to the arches of their feet.

Their faces are broad, flattened, and oiled. Before leaving the captive air of their chambers, they smear their cheeks, temples, and eyelids with rancid mutton tallow infused with pine pitch, creating a second, waterproof skin that shields their flesh from the biting cold. Their eyes are striking: the corneas are flattened and the irises a pale, milky amber, ringed with heavy nictitating membranes that flicker across the eye when they turn toward the hard, vertical shafts of mountain light. They do not squint against the brightness; they adjust their sight as an owl does, their pupils contracting to precise needle-points in the cathedral sun of the sunken plazas.

They are called "half-gilled," but this is an upland misnomer. They do not possess the feathered fronds of fish. Instead, behind the hinge of the lower jaw, their necks are grooved with deep, vascularized slits—pharyngeal pockets lined with blood-engorged, velvet-like lamellae. When resting or doing light work, they can draw mountain water through the nose and force it out through these neck-slits, extracting just enough dissolved oxygen from the glacial flow to stretch an air-breath for an hour or more. Their heart rates are unnervingly slow; a diver clinging to an ironwood piling to dress a timber might have a pulse of eight beats a minute, his blood thick, dark, and syrupy with specialized myoglobin that hoards heat and gas.

Yet they remain bound to the sky. 

The air-reeds are the city's lifelines, and to watch the people breathe is to understand the real tension of their lives. Scattered along the wicker walkways are the "wells"—inverted bells of woven willow sealed with boiled grease. Inside these bells, the water level is depressed by bubbles of trapped mountain air, piped down through long, flexible siphons made of stitched goat-intestine that drift up toward the hidden surface like translucent eels. 

A diver will work with patient, agonizing slowness at a mortise joint, tap a chisel with a stone hammer until the cold begins to stiffen his fingers, and then glide deliberately up toward a bell. He ducks his head beneath the greasy rim, rising into the dark dome. 

Inside, the world changes instantly. The deafening underwater clatter of the cirque vanishes, replaced by a hollow, wet silence broken only by the sound of his own panting. The air inside the bells is not fresh; it is warm, thick, and rank with the stench of decaying moss-grease, sweat, and exhaled moisture. The diver will cling to the wooden rim, his neck-slits sealing tightly like wet seams, and drag the foul, precious air deep into his massive lungs with ragged, shuddering gasps. Here they cough, hawk up brine, speak a few sharp, clicking words to whoever shares the dome, and warm their numb hands against each other’s tallow-slick shoulders before slipping back under the green water.

Their homes are larger versions of these bells: inverted cradles lashed to the living ironwood trunks, anchored against their own tremendous buoyancy by tons of slate slabs. To enter a home is to breach a ceiling of dark water from below. Inside, they live out of the wet: sleeping on racks of dried sedge, cooking over tiny, carefully vented peat-braziers whose smoke is scrubbed through water-traps before it can poison the captive air pocket. Because fire consumes oxygen, it is treated with religious dread; food is rarely cooked through, but rather cured in cold brine, fermented in stone jars, or eaten raw and warm from the net.

Because their world is an echo chamber, speech as the dry world knows it does not exist in the open lanes. In the water, human voices carry as unintelligible, distorted shrieks that can disorient or deafen. Instead, they are masters of bone-conducted noise. 

They wear flat rings of caribou horn on their thumbs, with which they strike rhythmic, sharp *clacks* against the slate weights on their hips. A mother does not shout for a child; she taps three times on a structural piling with a river stone, and the vibration travels through the dense water and through the child’s skull bones, humming directly into the inner ear. They speak a rich, tactile sign-language with their greased hands, the fingers flashing white against the dark peat-bed in the sun-shafts. When they do "sing"—often during the long alpine twilights when the light dims to a bruised violet—they gather along the rope-walks, press their foreheads against the resonant larch-beams of the public storehouses, and hum into the wood. The combined low-frequency vibration passes through the city’s timbers and down into the moss carpet, a deep, organ-like drone that can be felt in the marrow of every living thing in the tarn.

Their relationship with the other creatures of the deep basin is neither pastoral nor predatory; it is communal. The great schools of high-lake char, with their pale bellies and milky eyes, treat the people as structural features of the reef. They gather around the tallow-greased divers, gently picking away dead skin and parasite-larvae with soft, toothless nips. The divers tolerate this patiently, occasionally reaching out with an oiled hand to pin a sluggish, fat-bellied fish against the wickerwork with a single, practiced flex of the fingers.

Beneath the city, in the cold, lightless gloom where the anchor-cables disappear into the peat-reef, live the older ones. 

Age does something strange to them. After decades in the heavy water, their skin thickens into an ashen, lichenous rind, and the circulation in their extremities slows so severely that they lose the ability to dwell for long in the dry air of the cradles. They leave the upper tiers and descend to the peat floor, living in flooded wicker pens pinned directly to the moss. They move with such glacial patience that blind crabs use their leather aprons as hunting perches, and silt settles on their wide, slate-hung shoulders. 

These elders are the city's balancers. They spend days lying face-down upon the green peat-reef, testing the tension of the rawhide lashing with their thickened fingers, listening with their cheeks pressed against the dark mud for the shifting of the mountain's bones, or carving the soft, compressed lignite into new weights for children yet unborn. They do not fear sinking. In the end, when their slow hearts finally miss the eighth beat and do not take the ninth, they are not brought to the surface. Their slate weights are simply cinched tighter, and their kin push them gently into the living moss-mat, where the cold arrests all rot, and their bodies slowly harden into the very geology that keeps the city from flying toward the sky.
