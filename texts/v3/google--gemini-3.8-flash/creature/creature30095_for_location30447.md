To survive where wood behaves like an uncapped well, an organism must possess either no structure at all, or a structure that can out-split the grain. 

The first things visible—once the eye learns to ignore the grand, sickening sweep of the colonnades—are the ones wedged directly into the wounds. 

Wherever an eight-story pillar groans and bursts under its internal sap-head, pale, oblong bulks force themselves into the tear before the sap can crust into amber. These are the *calkers*, though they bear no marks of manufacture. They are beasts of gristle and slick, wet tendon, shaped like fat, headless keystones, with hide as tough and hairless as an inflated bladder. They have no limbs to speak of; instead, their flanks are ribbed with broad, overlapping plates of hard, cornified keratin that act like the threads of a screw. 

A calker does not walk. It waits for an explosion of wood, drops into the fresh yellow fracture from above, and worms its way downward into the vascular churn. It lives entirely on the blinding, high-pressure rush of white cambial milk, opening a wide, circular sucker at its base to mate directly with the plant’s torn capillaries. In doing so, it serves as a biological pressure-relief valve. The sheer hydraulic thrust that would blow a room into kindling is absorbed by the calker’s rubbery mass; it swells, creaking like a leather wineskin, sweating out a foul, grease-like tallow that lubricates the grinding surfaces of the tree. When the split inevitably closes—when the tree heals with the blind ferocity of hot glass—the calker is slowly, inexorably crushed to death, but not before its belly has hardened into a knot of dense, flinty wax that will stay wedged in the heartwood for decades, leaving an oily knot in the grain around which new avenues will creep.

Higher up, along the conveyor-belt avenues of creeping ironwood, there are things that never stop walking. 

They are tall, incredibly lean bipeds, roughly twice the height of a man, with limbs that look less like flesh than peeled willow whips wrapped in dried caul. Their feet are not broad, for a broad surface provides a grip for the floor-runners; instead, they terminate in single, hardened stilettos of solid bone, tipped with a ring of tiny, continually secreting nozzles that weep a caustic, sulfurous bile. Everywhere they step, the predatory floor-vines curl back, chemically scalded. 

These are the *striders*. They have no eyes; the glare off the chartreuse canopies would blind them within a morning. In place of eyes, their skulls flare into wide, horizontal sounding-boards of hollow, acoustic bone, ribbed like fungal gills. Through these, they read the city through its stress: they hear the microscopic pinging of cell-walls shearing three leagues away; they can tell by the pitch of a lintel’s whine whether the arcade beneath them has ten hours or ten seconds before it buckles into matchwood. 

The striders live in a state of rhythmic, staccato panic. They cannot sleep lying down; to lay an ear against the wood would be to have one’s skull shattered by the hydraulic artillery. Instead, they sleep standing, balanced on their stilettos in the momentary dead-centers of the great moving plazas, swaying like pendulums against the wind of the sap-vents. Their long, multi-jointed fingers are tipped with chisel-nails of grown iron, with which they pry open the newly woven wicker ceilings to feed on the thick, clear grubs that hatch within the air-cells—blind, squirming larvae the size of thighs, whose guts are nothing more than filtered mint-oil and pure, compressed glucose.

And then there are the tenants who think of themselves as people.

They are small, heavily muscled, and barrel-chested, shaped to endure atmospheric pressures that would turn an ordinary man’s lungs to water. Their skin is not pale, but the color of cured hide that has been soaked in brine and then smoked over green leaves—a greasy, yellow-brown that does not blister under the alkaline sweat dripping from the leaves above. 

They do not speak. The city has drowned language in the catastrophic din of its own construction. Their ears are small, cauliflowered slits, plugged permanently with plugs of amber gum; they communicate instead through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, thumping the heartwood with heavy wooden mallets to send coded tremors along the grain. 

These people do not build; to build here would be an obscenity, a waste of fuel. They are *diverters*. They carry no possessions that cannot be slung across the shoulders in a sling of raw hemp, for a home here is an afternoon’s lodging that will be crushed into a solid mass by dusk. Their sole tools are wedges—wedges of river-stone, which they prize above any metal, because river-stone does not rot, bend, or fuse with cambium. 

When a family enters a newly formed banyan-citadel, they do not clear a room. They *jam* it. They drive stone wedges into the tension-corners of the osier walls, interrupting the growth-pulse just enough to create a pocket of still air. Within this pocket—where the wood writhes against the stone with a muted, frustrated squeal—they spend their short domesticities. 

They eat the sap by hanging wicker traps into the geysers, letting the foam settle into a stiff, bitter curd that tastes of iron and sugar. They drink from the condensation that bleeds through the underside of the broadleaf roofs, gathering it in bladder-bags before the oily film makes it unpalatable. They mate with the swift, desperate economy of animals whose nests are on fire. 

Their children are born with legs already bowed and knees double-jointed, able to drop from a collapsing arch into the canopy of an arcade ten fathoms below without breaking their ankles. These young are taught to run before they can crawl, because the floor beneath them is not a floor: it is a mouth that does not know it is hungry, a surface that will absorb a motionless child into its grain in the space of a single midday sleep, leaving only the tiny, calcified shadow of a skeleton embedded three inches beneath the ironwood tiles.

When the tenants die, there are no burials. To leave a body is to let it be dissolved by the floor-runners within three changes of the sun, turned into fuel to drive another column toward the clouds. Instead, the kin take the dead to the collisions—the grinding front lines where two avenues run into one another. 

There, where the walls are heaving skyward and the wood is flaking into keen, yellow blades, they thrust the corpse between the meeting trunks. They watch without grief as the city claims the meat, watching the bones crack like dry kindling as the two living timbers weld together over the chest, sealing the dead into the imperial fabric, turning a brother or an ancestor into five inches of extra thickness inside a pillar that will support an empty balcony for another forty hours.
