To live here is not a matter of finding food, but of surviving the clearances. 

Because the landscape operates on tolerances—wood grinding against wood, fibers drawn taut under hydraulic pressure, burls plunging through chutes—the creatures that inhabit this basin are defined primarily by their cross-sections. They are organisms built to exist between moving parts, or inside the fluid lines, or in the brief, predictable pauses of a stroke.

In the narrow, wet gaps where the massive horizontal trunks slide over one another, there are the *shims*. They are not arthropods, though they possess a slick, segmented exterior like water-soaked horn. Flattened to the thickness of a knife-blade and broader than a man’s spread hand, they are flexible, disc-like animals that have abandoned internal bone entirely in favor of dense, hydrostatic cartilage. They grease themselves. Their flanks continually weep a thick, cold lipid that mixes with the liverworts to keep the contact faces of the trunks from galling. When a thirty-ton ribbed bough shifts suddenly under a surge of sap, pinning a shim against the peat, the animal does not crush; its fluids displace smoothly into its perimeter, flattening it further, until the heave reverses and it re-inflates with a soft, internal draw. They feed directly on the shear-paste—the warm, fibrous slurry ground into paste between the sliding barks—lapping it up with broad, rasping flaps that leave the wood polished to a mirror finish.

Deeper down, beneath the hanging curtains of beard-lichen, the green meal that settles from the pod-teeth does not rot. The cold is too clean, the air too biting. Instead, it is processed by herds of things that look like heavy, leather sacks.

These are the *sump-feeders*, blind and stub-legged, their hides colored the dull purple-gray of an old bruise. They move with an agonized slowness along the bottom of the damp chutes, shovel-headed and neckless, grazing the drifted pulp before it can mat and sour. Because they cannot hear the levers above them, they live entirely through mechanoreception. Their undersides are fringed with stiff, bristle-thick vibrissae that read the tension in the peat floor. They know the rhythm of the winching tendrils three minutes before the release; when the peat begins to sing with that particular rising tightness, the herd drops flat, tucking their soft sensory faces beneath their heavy, spade-like rostrums, letting the descending burls skip over their sloped, rubbery backs. A burl will glance off a sump-feeder’s hide with a dull, wet *whuff*, leaving an indentation that slowly pops back into shape like cold lard.

Then there is the matter of the sap.

The conduits that swell and pulse beneath the fleece are pressurized far beyond the threshold of animal blood. To tap them is dangerous; a breach can spray a hard, bitter jet cold enough to freeze flesh and dense enough to punch through muscle. Yet there are parasites that exist solely to harness that head of pressure. 

Clinging to the seams of the great iron flumes are the *plugs*. At first glance, they appear to be knot-holes or lignified tumors, but they are alive—spindle-shaped creatures anchored into the wood by corkscrew mouthparts of blackened enamel. They do not pump; they have no hearts. They rely on the tree’s own violent turgor to drive sap through their gut tubes, filtering out sugars and cold-resistant alcohols through complex, spiraling internal gills. When a conduit’s pressure peaks, the plugs distend, their translucent gray flanks swelling until they look like glass floats, sweating drops of dark, concentrated amber through their skin. If one is torn loose by a thrashing tendril, it does not bleed; it leaves a hissing knot that the tree’s cambium instantly clots and seals over with a rapid, blistering growth of scar-wood.

Above this wet, deafeningly quiet mechanical sprawl, the canopy of dead wood remains the only realm of stillness. Here, the air is not humid with sap-mist, but bone-dry and scouring. 

The fist-sized beetles observed on those gray, frozen crowns are not solitary by accident; they are the terminal phase of a creature that spends forty years underground. In its larval state, it is a white, oil-rich auger, boring through the heartwood of the winching burls, surviving the violent shocks of the drops by floating in an internal ballast of dense, paraffin-like gel. It emerges only when the burl finally fractures or locks up from accumulated scar tissue. 

When it crawls out onto the dead crown, it transforms into an animal that does almost nothing at all. Its pale, frost-crusted carapace is so thick it accounts for three-quarters of its weight, a shield against the slate-blue mountain air and the predatory drafts that spill over the rim. It does not eat; its mouth is fused shut into a blunt rostrum. It simply clings to the gray bark with hooked, ratcheting tarsi that cannot be prised loose even by gale winds, facing directly into the airflow. 

These beetles are seismographs. Through the dead timber, which conducts the silent, subterranean shuddering of the factory floor with crystalline clarity, they feel the entire basin’s operation. They are waiting for a stoppage. 

Because the landscape is an uncontained, mindless equilibrium, it occasionally trips its own gears. A winch cable will knot; two pods will jam on a foreign knot and stall, their hydraulic pressure climbing toward a catastrophic burst. When a section of the machinery freezes, the vibration in the dead crown alters, changing pitch from a low, rhythmic thrum to a brittle, stationary hum.

Only then do the beetles move. 

Slowly, their frost-locked joints cracking with tiny, dry snaps, dozens of them will turn in unison toward the stalled sector. They do not fly—they allow themselves to fall. They release their grip and plunge into the curtains of beard-lichen, bouncing through the fibrous tapestries, falling silently toward the jammed machinery. They land among the grinding teeth and the swollen, unvented galls, where they wedge their impenetrable, triangular bodies into the shear-points. 

They do not clear the jam; they are the fulcrum. Their shells are hard enough to crack the wooden teeth or snap the wooden dogs that hold the levers, sacrificing their bodies to release the accumulated pressure in a sudden, silent spray of splinters and pale green froth. The machine lunges forward again, the tendril hauls, the burl drops, and the beetle’s crushed fragments—its tallow, its enamel, its mineral-heavy dust—are swept into the green meal, carried down the chutes to feed the blind things waiting in the dark below.
