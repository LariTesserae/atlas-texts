To find what lives here, one must look where the heat collects, for in an atmosphere that refuses to freeze yet never warms, life does not spread itself out; it burrows into its own fermentation.

The giant moss mounds are hollow. 

Within their twenty-foot domes of pale, waxy green, the interior decay generates a steady, swampy fever—a humid eighty degrees that smells of boiled vinegar and wet wool. It is inside these mounds, and within the windowless lower cells of the basalt bastions where the condensation runs in hot, mineral-choked rivulets down the dry-stacked seams, that the builders’ descendants remain.

They are men, though the plateau has spent uncounted epochs paring away the accidents of their form until only the mechanics of survival remain. 

They are remarkably short, rarely standing more than four feet tall, but their torsos are barrel-shaped and immense, built like diving bells to house lungs that can harvest the thin, iron-scented air. Their skin has no pigment to speak of; it is the color of skimmed milk, faintly veined with grey, and completely hairless. Because there is no sun to burn them and no shadow to hide in, their skin has thickened into a tough, water-repellent rind that never dries. It sheds the omnipresent grease-mist in beads, giving them the dull sheen of peeled turnips or dressed tallow.

Their skeletons are what reveal them as creatures of the seams. To withstand the violent shifts in gravity—where a single stride across an adze-notched curb can double the mass of blood and bone—their skeletal systems have undergone a profound pachyostosis. Their ribs are wide, flat plates that overlap like armor; their femurs are as thick as ship-timbers, lacking the hollow marrow-cavities of lowland folk and filled instead with a dense, spongy cancellous bone that stores native iron. 

Because of this weight, they do not walk with an upright swing. They shuffle. Their feet are broad, square spades of flesh, entirely without arches, the soles padded with a three-inch layer of calloused, yellowed horn that feels no pain from the chert flakes or cold stone. They walk with their toes splayed wide, their knees permanently bent, dragging their heels along the flagstones. 

Watch them cross a threshold, and you see the purpose of this gait. They do not look ahead; they feel downward. Before an elder passes from the level terrace into a street where the perspective shears, he extends one grey, blunt foot, sweeping it back and forth over the basalt until his heel catches the carved adze-notch. He seats his weight into the groove, pauses, exhales a long plume of white steam, and lets his inner ear settle. 

Their ears do not contain the delicate, fluid-filled loops of normal human anatomy. Their vestibular systems have calcified into heavy, stone-like otoliths—tiny pebbles of native copper and aragonite that roll through thick, gelatinous chambers. When they cross a seam where verticality tilts ninety degrees, their bodies do not panic; they simply list into the new angle of the world, leaning against the updraft as if it were a solid embankment of sod, their balance maintained by the slow, gravitational drag of the stones in their heads.

Nothing eats the lush, rubbery plants of the tundra because the plants are not edible to any stomach that requires starch or sugar. But inside the moss mounds, where the generational rot reaches its peak, the vegetation breaks down into a thick, grey curd—a bacterial lather that smells of lye. 

This the people eat. They scoop it out of the rotting hearts of the mounds with shallow spoons carved from dense, yellowed thigh-bones—the bones of their own dead, for there are no other large animals on the plateau to yield such material. They work the paste between flat, peg-like teeth that have no enamel, only a hard, ivory dentine stained black by the water. They require very little; their metabolisms are slow, cold, and reptilian, their hearts beating perhaps twenty times a minute as they crouch in the fermenting dark.

They possess language, but it is rarely spent in the open air, where the wind shears sound into fragments. 

When they speak, it is an interior vibration: a low, resonant chest-hum that passes through the flagstones and the stacked basalt boulders better than it passes through the atmosphere. Two of them can sit thirty paces apart on a sunken avenue, appearing completely motionless and mute, while their rib cages drum against the stone, exchanging directions on the sliding of the city blocks or the thinning of a moss mound’s core.

Their relation to perspective is matter-of-fact, almost administrative. 

A mother sitting on a curb may reach out to her child who has stepped two yards across the gutter. In the child’s eyes, the mother has swelled until her chest is a wall of white flesh that blots out the sky; to the mother, the child is a pale insect, no larger than a bee, clinging to the edge of a flagstone that now resembles a vast, slate cliff. Yet the mother does not scream. She lowers a single, gigantic finger—its whorls thick as rope—into the gutter. The miniature child climbs onto the nail. With a slow, steady haul that strains the muscles of her thick neck as the child’s mass re-multiplies across the threshold, she brings the infant back to her breast, where it snaps back into its natural four-foot proportion with a wet, audible pop of displaced air.

They do not make art, unless the notches in the stone can be called art. Their creative impulse is entirely absorbed by maintenance. With heavy wedges of cold-hammered native copper, they walk the margins of their sliding cities, chipping away at the ferns that threaten to jam the joints where basalt raft meets basalt raft. They clear the drainage grooves so the condensed grease does not freeze in the deep cracks, for if ice were to form and wedge apart the cyclopean foundations, the seam-geometries would shudder, buckle, and throw whole districts out into the void.

And occasionally, when an elder’s otoliths grow too heavy and their dense, iron-logged legs can no longer drag themselves through the warm curd of the mounds, they go down the great stairs at the edge of the circular town.

They do not walk down them in sorrow. They strip away the stiff skirts of waxed fern-fiber they wear against the wind. Naked, white, glistening with condensation, they shuffle down the basalt steps that have been worn concave by millions of feet before them. 

When they reach the place where the steps cease to lead downward and begin their path into the open sky, they do not hesitate. They step out. 

The air holds them. To them, the clouds blowing between their knees are not vapor, but a floor of sufficient density to support a creature whose relationship with down has been negotiated through five hundred thousand years of stone-cutting. 

They walk out into the bright, shadowless emptiness, climbing the sky that leads back to the sky. Below them, framed between their calloused toes, the plateau turns like an enormous, moss-slicked wheel, its cities inching across the slate rock like snails on a wet tombstone. They keep climbing until the perspective folds one final time, and they diminish not into distance, but into the identical city that sits above the one they left—stepping off the air and onto the identical worn stairs of the acropolis, their weight returning, their lungs pulling in the bitter iron mist, ready to begin the long, barefoot transit all over again.
