To cross the floor at a walk is impossible; the grain rejects the clumsy friction of a heel. Those who belong here do not step. They plane.

They are called the Skiffs, though there is nothing manufactured in their gristle. In silhouette they suggest long-legged greyhounds that have been drawn out through an iron ring, flattened horizontally until their ribcages are no thicker than a butcher’s board. Their undersides are entirely devoid of hair or skin, covered instead in a seamless, sweating sole of pale yellow horn that secretes an oil identical to the floor’s own sap. When they run, they do not push against the decking; they drop their sternums against the racing film of water and slide. 

A Skiff travels at the speed of a low-flying falcon, silent except for the high, dry *shuck* of parted moisture. They steer with tiny, vestigial claws set into the rims of their haunches, dipping a claw into the lignin grain to arrest momentum or carve a ninety-degree turn toward a gutter without breaking bone. Their heads are long, sightless wedges, blind because there is nothing to look at across the flats but the blank glare of steam; instead, the skin of their snouts is crowded with nerve-pores that taste the pH of the water they hydroplane over. They know an archway has aborted two hundred yards away because the wash suddenly turns bitter with dissolved resin; they bank into the spray to catch the warm, curdled foam before it clears through the municipal drains.

They are not solitary, though they run in loose, wide spreads to avoid tripping one another’s wakes. When two Skiffs cross paths at eighty miles an hour, their flanks pass within inches—a momentary blur of wet leather and translucent ribs—without a sound. They do not sleep on the floor; to stop sliding in the basin is to risk being adhered to the vascular mat, which is always seeking fresh cambium to graft into its lower layers. 

For stillness, one must climb. 

High up the flanks of the shifting iron-wood towers, above the hiss of the deck gutters, live the things that the mantis-eels fear. The mantids are merely the gluttons of the lower trunks, gorging on structural waste until their bellies drag like jars of marmalade. But higher, where the towers knot themselves into tight, dense burls that resist the self-dismantling spasms for weeks at a time, dwell the Glaziers.

The Glaziers might once have had four limbs, but age and the logic of the wood have braided them into something more like living splints. They are heavy-bodied, encased in thick, slate-coloured carapaces that look like segments of wet stovepipe, bolted together by fleshy, ringed joints. They have no faces, only a broad, downturned maw surrounded by three pairs of short, spade-like appendages tipped with serrated zinc. 

A Glazier does not wander. It chooses a newly grown archway, climbs into the crook of a cambium vault while it is still soft and steaming, and drives its zinc appendages into the structural veins. It does not drink to feed itself alone; it acts as a throttle. By filtering the flow of mineral-rich liquor through its own spongy liver, the Glazier can chemically lie to the tower. It spits an acrid, milky wax into the xylem tubes that convinces the local wood it is under immense physical load. Under this chemical delusion, the tower will not starve the archway; instead, it reinforces it, sending thicker cords of grain to bundle around the Glazier’s shell, anchoring it into the living masonry.

There the Glazier sits for months, half-embedded in the architecture, a living keystone. Its back grows a velvet moss of orange luciferin bladders that pulse in rhythm with its gut. When a Glazier finally dies, its wax turns thin and sweet. The tower realizes its mistake within minutes. The wood turns grey, curdles, and sloughs off in a great, foul-smelling avalanche that carries the dead tenant down to the floor, where the Skiffs are already waiting for the splash.

Between the skittering surface and the fixed towers, there is an intermediate presence—the things that attend the central depression.

They are the oldest things here, taller than the stump-teeth of the ruined council ring, and they are the only things that seem to have inherited the basin’s ancient sense of decorum. They have no names that carry through the steam, but they are shaped roughly like tall, narrow steles of dry, pressed pith, ten to twelve feet in height, tapering toward the crown like old willow hampers. They have no legs. They stand upon thick, fibrous skirts that spread out across the slick floor, their fringes working deep into the sub-molecular grooves of the lignin.

They do not slide, and they do not climb. They root, but they root *temporarily*. 

When the floor begins its deep, pulmonary heave—the great, pneumatic inhalation that pulls the surface water flat before the ripple—these Stele-creatures do not resist the motion. They loosen their skirts. As the wood bulges upward, they are lifted like dry buoys on an ocean swell. In the brief five seconds while the water is held in those tiny, spitting pyramids, suspended by harmonic tension, the Steles open.

Vertical seams down their parchment flanks part with a sound like tearing dry silk. Inside them, arranged in neat, vertical ranks like pipes in an organ, are hundreds of thin, dark ribs made of polished jet or ancient bone. Through these open ribs, they inhale the standing steam. It is not an act of breathing, but of sorting: their interiors are lined with sticky, hair-like cilia that comb the air for the metallic spores shed by the chitinous birds, the airborne copper flakes, and the drifting pollen of the towers’ short-lived flowers.

When the floor exhales and flattens out, and the water roars back into the gutters, the Steles snap their seams shut with a wet, wooden clap that echoes against the containment field above. They settle back down onto their fringes, six inches to the left or right of where they were before, slightly rotated, facing a new vector of the empty nave. 

They appear to be waiting for an assembly that has been delayed by several hundred thousand seasons. They do not communicate with the Skiffs, who veer around their fibrous skirts like water around bridge piers, nor do they look up at the screeching flocks that circle in the hot mist. But sometimes, when the towers undergo a particularly violent convulsion—when three galleries fall at once and the whistle of the xylem vents rises to an ear-splitting scream—one of the Steles will vibrate its internal ribs. It produces a deep, wooden drone, pure and civic, like the chime of a wet finger circling the rim of a massive cedar tub. 

The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through the horn underfoot. For miles through the vascular mat, the grain stiffens in response to the note. The racing sheets of water slow down, just for an instant, thickening into heavy, amber syrup, before the municipal hum resumes its work, and the vast, wet floor empties itself once more.
