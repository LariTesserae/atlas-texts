To watch them is to realize that the forearm-sized shingles are not merely inhabitants of the vault; they are the room’s second geology, a softer strata of slate that crawls over the harder basalt. 

Seen close, where the dull infrared radiance maps their contours, an individual plate-beast possesses none of the wet mechanics of terrestrial vermin. Its body is flat, scarcely thicker than a dry ledger, its edges bevelled like split flagstone so that the creature before it can slide effortlessly over its back without catching. The horn of its shell is not chitin, nor turtle-scale, but a fibrous composite of compacted silicate and keratin, layered like micaceous schist. Scratch it, and it does not bleed; it yields a faint smell of burnt hair and gypsum, leaving a white chalk-line that the animal’s neighboring plates will rub smooth again within an hour.

Underneath, where their bellies graze the hot rock, they are pale—not the sickly white of cave grubs, but the scorched cream of calcined bone. Here, along the keel of the thorax, are the five pairs of vent-slits. They do not expand and contract like lungs; they are passive louvers lined with a dense felt of stiff, mineral-hungry hairs. When the hot updrafts blow, these louvers dilate, vibrating faintly in the draft with a dry, musical drone too low for human ears to catch—a collective, subterranean pitch that sets the very floor vibrating against the bedrock.

Between the vent-slits, their legs work in a continuous, hypnotic ripple. The ten limbs are short, thick as thumbs, their joints protected by sliding cup-and-socket plates of dry bone. The black, rubbery pads upon which they tread are formed of a dense, cross-linked tallow—a concentrated lipid produced by their mineral digestion, heat-cured until it has the resilience of vulcanized gum. These pads do not sweat, but they leave behind a microscopically thin trace of sulfurous wax upon the rock. Over centuries, this wax has filled every pit and fissure in the basalt, polishing the chapel floor to the slick, mirror-dark finish of an obsidian road.

They do not mate in the open. The pressing multitude allows no ceremony, no territory. 

Instead, reproduction is an ongoing, quiet dispersal. In the deeper, cooler crevices near the masonry bases—where the heat drops from kiln-fire to the warmth of baking bread—the female plates shed their brood. They do not produce eggs; liquid yolk would boil and rupture in this air. What drops from between their rear plates are dry, gray granules the size of coriander seeds, hard as dried river-silt. 

These seed-cysts are rolled into the masonry sockets and the spaces behind the limestone blocks by the thousands, packed tight by the scraping movement of passing adults. There they lie dormant, baking, until a pulse of sulfur-gas of sufficient heat and density shocks them open. 

The hatchlings do not crawl out; they emerge as paper-thin shavings, barely an inch long, completely transparent, like leaves of mica peeled from a rock. They are too frail to fight the main floor’s press. If they slipped beneath the adult carpet, they would be ground to flour in a matter of hours. So they climb. 

Using microscopic, needle-sharp hooks at the tips of their rubber pads, the young scale the vertical walls into the lintel-shadows and the ribbed ceiling, living upon the ceiling’s skin as an inverted ceiling itself. They do not filter the air yet; their vent-slits are still fused shut. Instead, they feed upon the crust of dead air that clings to the roof: the mineral smoke, the sloughed horn-powder that rises on the heat columns, and the dead, papery cast-skins of their parents that drift up on the thermals and catch upon the ceiling's volcanic spurs. They are scavengers of the sky in a room without an atmosphere. Only when their plates thicken and take on the dark, iron-slate hue of the adults do they fall—releasing their grip deliberately to drop through the dark, clattering into the living carpet to take their place in the slow rotation.

Yet the shingles are not alone. Where there is a host so dense that it forms a continuous surface, smaller lives find purchase in the seams.

In the narrow gutters between the overlapping dorsal plates of the adults lives a secondary fauna: the *sieve-mites*. They are no larger than a grain of coarse salt, fashioned from the same glassy, vitreous horn, with legs like spun wire. Hundreds of them inhabit the margins of a single adult shingle. They are not parasites—the adult possesses no blood to drink, no soft tissue to pierce—but grooms. They spend their lives crawling through the fibrous vent-louvers, clipping away accumulated crusts of lithium salt and iron clinker that would otherwise suffocate the host. In return, they harvest the waxy tallow the plate-beast excretes around its breathing slits, spinning it into brittle, hair-fine shelters that anchor them to the beast's armor against the friction of the swarm. When an adult plate is overturned or dies, the sieve-mites do not scatter; they huddle into the breathing chambers of their dead home, feeding on its internal grease until a new adult wedges itself against the corpse and they can step across the gap.

Deeper still, in the narrowest fractures where the basalt chimney meets the roots of the mountain, the swarm changes character. 

Down in the throat of the vent, where the draft is hot enough to soften lead, the plates can no longer turn or shift. Movement here is fatal; the kinetic friction would ignite the sulfurous dust in their plates. In these lower depths lie the *anchors*.

These are the immense elders of the lineage—monstrous, ancient individuals three paces long, wedged immovable into the cracks of the rock. They have ceased to crawl. Over centuries, the heat has vitrified their lowest plates, fusing their horn directly to the basalt walls in a seamless weld of biologic rock. Their bellies have expanded into immense, chambered bellows that pulse with the slow, tectonic rhythm of the chimney’s gas-sighs. Their dorsal plates have grown thick, rounded, and nodular, shaped by the passage of thousands of their smaller kin who use their backs as staircases to climb out of the furnace. 

These anchors are no longer merely animals; they have become organs of the cleft. Through their vast, calcified vent-pipes, they gulp the raw, unrefined thermal vapor from the deep earth, cracking the heavy metal gasses in their boiling guts and venting a cooler, filtered cloud of mineral ash from their flanks into the upper chapel. It is this processed dust that feeds the shifting sea above them. They are the hearthstones upon which the entire crawling pyramid rests, blind and calcified, buried under fifty layers of their own descendants, enduring an immortal, petrified digestion in the pitch dark.

And around the masonry—the square-cut stones, the dead terrace—a stranger phenomenon occurs.

The things avoid the sharp angles where the limestone lintels meet the volcanic ash. They have worn the stone down, yes, but they do not lodge there. In the gaps between the human-quarried blocks, where the ancient mortar has turned to yellow dust, there are long, flat tubes made of cemented mineral flakes, thick as a man’s wrist, that wind between the joints of the dry-stacked wall.

These are the galleries of something that does not move with the swarm. 

Inside these tubes lives a creature that has never touched the heat-draft directly: long, segmented, soft-bodied despite the dryness, shielded only by a skin of tough, parchment-like leather. They have no legs, only rings of stiff horn-bristles that allow them to pull themselves through the masonry tubes like giant, desiccated earthworms. They do not feed on the ash. Their heads are equipped with three curved, horn chisels that work against the ancient limestone, slowly, grain by grain, mining the lime and the old calcium of the foundation blocks.

They are the stone-eaters. They do not care for the swarm, save for this: when an adult shingle falls into a crevice beside the terrace and dies, the stone-eaters reach out with dry, grasping mouthparts, seize the empty shell, and drag it into their wall-tubes. They do not eat the horn. They use the flat plates to shingle their tunnels, lining the limestone fissures with the armor of the dead to prevent the crushing weight of the basalt swarm from caving in their gallery walls.

Century by century, the masonry is disappearing into their bellies, converted into a fine, alkaline powder that they excrete out into the chapel floor, while the carapaces of the swarm take the place of the limestone, slowly rebuilding the ancient watch-cell into a structure made entirely of living and dead shells.

There is no sound of mourning here, no hunger in the mammalian sense, no sleep. A plate-beast does not know that there is a world beyond the basalt throat where the air is cool and wet, where water falls from the sky like poison, dissolving the salt and cracking the horn. For them, the universe is only this dry, vertical chapel, the measured pulse of the gas-hearth below, the velvet slide of their neighbors’ shells against their own, and the eternal, quiet labor of holding back the cold.
