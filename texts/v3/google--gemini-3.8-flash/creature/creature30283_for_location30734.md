The people who inhabit the hanging tiers have the dry, light-boned bodies of birds that have forgotten how to migrate. 

They are small-statured, rarely exceeding five cubits, with calves shaped like twisted cedar knots from a lifetime of living on a pitch. Because the rock face has no horizon, their eyes do not scan side to side; they look up and down with an unsettling, bird-like tilt of the head. Their skin is not moist or sweat-glossed. Under the constant wick of the chimney draft, perspiration evaporates before it can bead, leaving behind a fine, saline crust that they do not wash—for washing with water is a concept as alien to them as sailing on a sea—but scrape off with curved ribs of desert horn, rubbing their limbs afterward with tallow rendered from cricket meal and the wax of the spindle-tree. This gives them a faint, amber gleam in the twilight, like figures turned from boxwood, smelling faintly of old apiaries and sun-baked marrow.

They call themselves the *Oun*, though they have little need for a name when there is no one else within forty leagues of hard plateau to hear it. 

To an outsider, their speech would sound less like human language and more like the mountain settling. They speak from the back of the palate, conserving the moisture of their tongues, relying on sharp, percussive clicks and hollow glottal stops that carry down the stone tiers without requiring the chest to pump out a great waste of air. When an elder speaks to a child three terraces above, he does not shout; he clicks his fingernails against a disc of slate, or whistles through a dry marrow-bone with an aperture drilled for three distinct notes. 

Their clothes are stiff, rattle-voiced things. They weave the split rinds of spindle-succulents with the beard-lichen that hangs from the roof, producing heavy, tabard-like vests that do not drape so much as form an articulated shell around the torso. These garments are never dyed—pigment is for bone and stone—but they are deeply burnished by friction against the rock walls. Along the hems, the women tie the wing-cases of the large, slate-colored ground beetles that breed in the lower masonry; as an Oun walks the shale walkways, their skirts make the dry, clicking murmur of pebbles shifting in an undertow.

The children are the most astonishing part of the pocket’s ecology. Up until their seventh or eighth year, they are tethered to the roof timbers by long, braided sinew cords looped around their waists. They run on the stone parapets on all fours with an agility that rivals the long-legged lizards, chasing the chalk-winged moths that hatch in the twilight vents. A child will snatch a moth out of the air, pinch off the chalky wings with two fingers, and pop the fat, greasy abdomen between their teeth like a berry before its tether jerks tight at the terrace edge. They do not fear the fall; they treat the four-thousand-cubit drop beyond the fissure as a wall that merely happens to be invisible.

At the base of the tiers, where the cave wall curls under to form the floor of the belly, live the *drawers*. These are the families who hold the bone-hooks. Their houses are not stacked high; they are sunken, carved into the talus slopes that choke the lower vents. 

The drawers do not climb. Their work is down in the cracks where the thermal drafts smell of sulphur and damp clay. Here, the great burrowing frogs—beasts the size of sheep, blind and wrinkled like ancient water-logged sacks—lie wedged in the narrow basalt slips, absorbing the vapor that rises from the deep crust. A drawer knows the placement of every hook by the sound the draft makes whistling through the lines. When a line goes taut, three men haul on a braided raw-hide winch, bringing the pale, sluggish thing up out of the black. 

They do not kill the frog. To kill a frog is to spill its life-wealth into the thirsty dust. Instead, they pierce the throat-bladder with a cannula of hollow martin-bone, drawing off two, sometimes three gourds of a thick, alkaline water that tastes of slate and copper, but which is sweet enough to sustain a family for half a moon. The frog is then smeared with bitter grease to heal the puncture and lowered back into the heat, left to swell again like a sponge in the deep dark.

Between the tiers, the fauna does not merely share the space; it works it. The cliff-mice are not vermin; they are herded. The Oun build small, box-like runs of dry shale along the lintels of their houses, providing the mice with husks of the spindle-seed in exchange for their dung, which is swept every dawn into woven bins. Dried and crushed with wild resin, this dung forms the mortar that keeps their city from sliding into the abyss. It is the cement of the mountain: odourless, hard as iron, and impervious to the eternal scuff of bare soles.

High above, in the light-slot, the martins are the keepers of the calendar. In the cool hours before the sun-dial strikes the floor, they leave the cave in a single, shrieking funnel, shooting up into the high plateau winds to hunt the midges that rise off the salt plains miles away. When the martins return, their crops are full of moisture-laden aerial plankton. The older boys climb into the roof vaults with long poles tipped with loop-snares, plucking the birds from their mud-bottle nests to stroke their throats until they regurgitate the damp mash of foreign insects into shallow basalt platters. This grey, moist paste is fed to infants and the dying; it is the only taste of the wider, wetter world the Oun ever receive—the distilled essence of rains that fell twenty leagues away, brought to them on the feathers of a creature that lives between two skies.

There is an absolute economy of movement in the town. During the long hours when the slot is dark and only the copper twilight illuminates the ledges, the people sit with their knees tucked into their chests, their chins resting on their knees, reducing their surface area to prevent moisture loss. They appear almost dead—rows of dried, polished wooden statues resting along the balconies, their braided hair greased flat against their skulls. 

Only their hands move. A woman will sit for four hours without shifting her hips, her fingers rapidly splitting reed-fibers, twisting them against her thigh, feeding the line into a basket that grows between her feet with the slow, geometric inevitability of a hornet’s comb. 

Then, the slot turns.

The sun strikes the communal floor. 

It does not arrive gently; it hits like a brass bell struck by an iron hammer. The light is so fierce, so hot, that the ambient twilight is instantly extinguished, replaced by a blinding, vertical pillar of white and gold.

In that instant, the stillness shatters. The statues stand. 

The entire population drops from the ladders—not slowly, but sliding down the guide poles with their feet clamped to the wood, landing on the floor with a rhythmic, wooden thud that sets the cliff-mice scurrying into their runs. 

For the sixty minutes that the pillar of sun remains on the threshing rock, life becomes a frenzy. This is the only hour of true sight they have. The needle-women bring their bone awls to the edge of the light to mend the fine-mesh lizard nets where the knots require absolute precision. The old men drag out great slabs of fibrous spindle-wood, working them with chert adzes while they can see the grain, their blades chipping off dry flakes that are caught in baskets before they touch the ground—not a splinter is lost, for all wood here is fuel for the slow-smoldering resin-pits that bake the shale.

Young men strip off their fiber tunics and stand directly in the beam, letting the sun scorch their backs until their skin turns the color of roasted chestnuts, baking out the mites that nest in the plant fiber of their clothes. They laugh then—a strange, dry barking that doesn't use the throat—and throw handfuls of dried crickets into the light to watch the martins dive through the beam, their dark bodies flashing like polished knives as they snatch the food out of the air.

And then, as suddenly as it broke through, the dial begins to slip. 

The beam climbs the eastern wall of the pocket, illuminating tier twelve, tier eight, tier three, like a finger pointing out each household in turn. As the light leaves each balcony, the people on it drop back down into their crouch. The laughter dies. The clicking of the tools slows, settles, and returns to the rhythmic, hypnotic scrape of chert on bone.

By the time the sun has cleared the lip of the slot above, leaving only the blue, cold drift of the upper atmosphere overhead, the town is silent again. The chimney draft sighs through the spindle-trees, their waxy leaves rattling like dry bones in a sack. 

Down in the lower shadows, a frog-line twitches. A bell of dried horn rings once. A man rises from the shale without a word, picks up his bone hook, and steps down into the dark, following the mountain’s breath down to where the water hides beneath the stone.
