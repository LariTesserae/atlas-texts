To live in the Sub-Sieve is to be shaped by three immovable conditions: the acoustic weight of the machine above, the absolute reliability of the forty-second thermal pulse, and the total absence of soil. Nothing here burrows; there is nothing soft enough to give way before a claw or a snout. Everything that exists must either grip the alloy, float upon the hot convection plumes, or wedge itself into the microscopic tolerances where the plates grind together.

At the lowest level of mobility are the *calcite-leeches*—though they are not annelids at all, but flattened, soft-bodied molluscoid automata whose organic tissues have long since incorporated the zinc and nickel salts of the drainage gutters. They grow in long, dull ribbons along the lip of the central trench, rarely thicker than a coin, their dorsal sides crusted with a jagged rind of grey mineral scale that makes them indistinguishable from the edge of the casing. They do not hunt. They lie with their ventral membranes spread flat against the molecularly repellent tiles, using the slight, persistent surface tension of the alkaline runoff to draw fluid across their digestive plates. When the twenty-hertz hum deepens—when some colossal generator miles above shifts its load—they shudder in unison, their edges curling like scorched paper, drinking the electrostatic spillover that hums through the vitreous floor.

Higher up, where the walls tilt back into the stepped terraces of the inverted amphitheater, the myriapods mentioned in the oldest records are not solitary; they are the cattle of the void. 

They are known to graze in slow, clattering herds of forty or fifty, their milk-white segments overlapping with the precision of armor-plate. Because they lack eyes, their universe is an intricate map of tension and resonance. Through the pads of their dozens of needle-like tarsi, they read the stress-fields in the alloy slabs: they know which plates are bearing the weight of the city’s water-reservoirs, which are light and dry, and which are under the shear-stress that precedes a seismic venting. They feed by scraping away the jade-colored chemiluminescent moss with hardened, diamond-grit radulae, leaving behind pale, zigzagging trails that remain bare for decades. When the regional balance-valves shriek, the myriapods do not merely hide; they interlock. Two dozen of them will coil together into a single, seamless hemisphere of chitin and silica, their flat heads tucked inward, presenting a smooth, deflective dome to the descending wall of scalding mist.

Feeding on them are the things that cling to the underbellies of the high struts. 

They are rarely seen until the forty-second vent cycle begins. In the three seconds before the mist erupts, when the air goes momentarily dead and the pressure drops enough to pop the ears, they drop from the ribs sixty yards above. They do not fly so much as they fall with intent. Their bodies are long, pale, and narrow—the thickness of an arm, but stretched to six or seven feet—ribbed with external pneumatic bladders that inflate with the hot, rising steam. Their wings are not feathers or skin, but broad, translucent panes of a mica-like secretion that catches the upward thermal gusts. 

As they glide across the void, they trailing long, dangling arrays of prehensile filaments, wet with an adhesive grease that smells faintly of scorched engine oil. These filaments comb the air for the steam-flies—fragile, ephemeral things that hatch, mate, and die in the sixty seconds between one mist-burst and the next—but their true target is the myriapod herds. A glider will bank hard against the draft of a hot gutter, slam its ceramic talons into the seam beside a grazing myriapod, and pry it from the wall with a sharp, levering wrench of its entire chassis before the mist can drown them both. They feed while anchored upside down by their hind-spurs, their pale faces split by vertical, scissor-like mouthparts that make a steady, wet crunching sound that is drowned utterly by the twenty-hertz drone.

Yet there is something else here—something that does not belong entirely to the fauna of the drainage system.

In the blind corners where the eastern buttress meets the basalt foundations, where the drip-gutters have built up massive, rust-streaked mounds of calcified tufa, lives the remnant of the maintenance lineage: the *Tallow-Cloaks*. 

Whether they were once human, or the manufactured serfs of the builders, or some desperate offshoot that crawled down the drainage conduits during a forgotten war, cannot be told from their shapes. They are bipeds, but their proportions have surrendered to the architecture. Their shins are long and bowed, capped with broad, splayed feet whose soles are calloused into thick, rubbery pads that can find purchase on the slick, sweat-wet alloy without slipping. They have no body hair; their skin is the color of lard kept in an engine room, translucent enough that the dark, sluggish veins can be seen branching through the torso like soot-lines in marble.

They have lost the use of ears. The tympanic membranes have thickened into flat discs of fibrous horn to protect their brains from the shattering volume of the balance-valves. Instead, they perceive the void through their jaws. 

A Tallow-Cloak resting against the wall does not sit; it crouches with its chin pressed firmly into the vitreous stone, its mouth slightly open, its teeth conducting the vibration of the machine-city directly into the base of its skull. In this way, they "hear" what is happening miles above. They know when a tram-line is stalled on the Third Tier; they know when the cooling-towers of the Seventh are choked with silt; they know when an elevator cable is fraying by the harmonic shudder it sends down the vaulted ribs.

They live in the pockets behind the dormant monitoring pods—spaces no larger than a bunk, lined with the dry, harvested felts of bracket-fungus. They wear nothing but a thick, smeared layer of synthetic grease mixed with crushed calcite, which protects their skin from the alkaline sting of the vents and the razor-fine mineral spray that hangs permanently in the air. 

Their hands are extraordinary: the fingers are long, spatula-tipped, and possess a second thumb where the little finger should be, giving them an iron, vice-like grip that allows them to hang from the horizontal seams for hours without fatigue. They use tools made of scavenged alloy pins, ground against the black river-stones until they are shaped into chisels and scrapers. 

With these, they perform a kind of slow, blind liturgy that has outlived its purpose. They move across the face of the great buttresses, methodically clearing the calcite buildup from the ancient weep-holes. They pry loose the dead shells of the myriapods before they can jam the drain-hatches. They scrape the corrosive black lichen away from the high-amperage bus casings, rolling it into sticky, pungent balls which they store in the hollows of the ribs to ferment into a greasy, edible paste. 

They do not seem to have a language of sounds. When two of them meet on a narrow ledge seventy feet above the floor, they do not look at each other—their eyes are small, milky spheres that register only the pale jade pulse of the moss and the red warning-glow of the coolant lines. Instead, they press their foreheads together for several seconds, their skulls vibrating in common against the air, sharing the bone-shiver of the city like two tuning forks struck by the same hammer. Then they part, moving with the slow, terrifying sureness of creatures that do not know what falling is, their pale limbs disappearing into the warm, fever-thick mist as the valve forty yards above begins its forty-second hiss.
