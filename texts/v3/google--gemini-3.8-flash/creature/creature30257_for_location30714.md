They do not stand at full height unless they are climbing. The low ceilings of the shingle tiers, the overhanging sedge eaves, and the perpetual drip-line from the upper cliffs have shaped their posture over generations into a soft, forward-tilting crouch—a gait that is less about haste than about keeping their centers of gravity flush with the slate.

To look at them is to see the rift’s materials repeated in muscle and hide. They are compact, heavy-chested people, wide through the clavicle and ribcage to house the large lungs demanded by the cold, dense air. Their limbs are short and thick, the joints swollen with dense cartilege, but their hands are startlingly long: spatulate, hard-tipped fingers with flat, horn-thick nails that never grow past the quick, worn blunt by a lifetime of worrying fractured stone into place. There is no tremor in their grip. A man or woman can hang by three fingers from a damp bedding plane for minutes without shifting their breath, waiting for a downdraft to pass before hauling a basket of blue clay upward.

Their skin is not pale in the way of cave-dwellers; it has the translucent, grayish-pink cast of a river trout’s belly, heavily marbled with blue veins close to the surface. It is extraordinarily thick and constantly slicked with a yellow, cold-rendered fat they press from the bellies of cliff-ducks and the autumn char. The water does not soak them; it beads on their forearms and the napes of their necks, running off in clear, quivering tracks as if they were greased stones. Hair is worn short, cropped against the scalp with sharpened river-flints, or braided tight into scalp-rows that are plastered down with marrow-paste so that falling grit and condensation find nothing to catch on.

Because stone and rushing water conduct vibrations with terrifying fidelity, they are people of profound acoustic restraint. Their voices exist almost entirely below the register of the water’s hiss. They speak in the chest: low, resonant hums, glottal clicks of the tongue behind the upper front teeth, and a dry, rustling sign language formed by the knuckles against the thigh or the flat of a wall. A mother does not call to a child across a flume; she strikes the slate pavement twice with the heel of her bare foot, and the child, feeling the jolt through the soles of its feet ten yards away, drops flat against the moss.

Clothing is spare, functional, and intensely layered. They wear no woven cloth—the air is far too wet for wool or linen, which would rot within a season. Instead, their garments are constructed from the tanned skins of the slate-gray water-voles, sewn flesh-side out with split sinew, and heavy tunics made from the oiled, feather-on skins of the cliff-nesting ducks. These tunics are heavy, oily, and reek pungently of tallow, wild allium, and river mud, but beneath them, a body remains as dry and warm as a fledgling in a down nest. On their feet they wear nothing. Bare soles, calloused until they are like soles of cured elk-hide, offer the only reliable purchase on the moss-greased steps and wet shingle ledges.

Their day is governed not by sunrise or sunset, but by the movement of the white daylight bar across the rift floor. When the high beam touches the slate, the settlement turns inside out. 

From low, round doorways draped with water-rat pelts, they spill onto the terraces in complete silence. They move through the flumes with an unhurried, collective rhythm. A trio of elder women, their backs permanently arched into the curve of the cliffside, squat at the edge of a surging stone trough. Between their knees are baskets woven from split alder and peeled willow. With practiced, mechanical flicks of their wrists, they harvest cold-water cresses and the pale, tender growth-tips of the sphagnum, tossing them into birch-bark panniers while their eyes scan the water for the pale bellies of migrating fry. 

Nearby, two young men work a fractured buttress that has begun to weep gray silt—a sign that the mountain above has shifted slightly in the night. They work without tools of bronze or iron. One wedges a dried pine peg into the fracture; the other pours water over it from a slate scoop. In an hour, the wood will swell and cleave the stone cleanly along its bedding plane, yielding three flat, perfect slabs of building shingle that they will catch before they hit the flume. They do not speak. One taps the wall with an antler haft; the other leans his weight instantly into the lever.

Inside the dwellings, where the roofs overlap so tightly that the sky is entirely lost, the air is thick, warm, and remarkably still. The stone walls, two feet thick and chinked with moss that continues to grow inward, keep the rushing cold of the chasm at bay. Small oil lamps—shallow saucers of chipped slate filled with fat and wicked with rolled liverwort fiber—burn with tiny, smokeless blue flames in wall niches. In this dim, yellow-gray clarity, the domestic life of the rift unfolds in heaps of down-filled sedge.

Children are carried on the chest or hip until they can grip slate with their toes; they are round-bellied, quiet, and watchful, their eyelids ringed with thick, pale lashes that shed the cold mist. They are taught stone-sense before they can speak: which shale will shatter under weight, which green moss is slippery as fat, and which black moss will hold a foot like dry hide. An elder sits in the corner of a shared hearth-bench, using a bone bodkin to mend a gill-net woven from horsehair and nettle bast, his gnarled toes holding the anchor-knot against the floor while his thumbs work the loops with blind, rhythmic precision.

They eat cold, or very near to it. Fires are rare, small, and built only in communal draft-chimneys that vent directly into the high downdrafts, fed with dry root-turf harvested from the high meadows before the earth cracked. Mostly, their food is cured by the cold water itself: char fillets buried in wet, salted clay until they are pungent and soft as tallow; bitter, peppery greens ground with crushed snail shells for lime; and the dense, oily eggs of the swifts, gathered by climbers who ascend the sheer, hundred-foot facets using nothing more than bone pegs and the natural step-fractures of the slate.

There is no sense among them of being trapped in the wound. To them, the chasm is not an abyss, but an immense, sheltering cradle that keeps the vast, predatory winds of the open tundra from reaching their roofs. The rock provides the floor, the walls, and the roof; the water brings the fish and carries away the waste; the ducks return every spring to fill the clefts with meat and oil. They live wedged between the jaws of the earth with the fierce, unblinking patience of lichens, measuring the passage of time only by the thickness of the moss on their lintels and the slow, millimeter-by-millimeter deepening of the flumes worn into the slate beneath their bare heels.
