The first things to notice are not the creatures that move, but the ones that have chosen to resemble the joinery. 

Along the seam of every fourth or fifth boiler plate, where the iron lap-joint has swollen into a jagged lip of rust, there are rows of chitons the size of an engineer’s palm. Their shells are not lime or nacre, but an agglomeration of iron filings, carbon slag, and sulfur-rich grit held together by a tough, horn-like matrix. They have aligned themselves precisely with the rivet lines. To a passing knuckle, they feel like cold, slightly give-prone metal; to the chiton, the rivet head is not an obstacle, but an electrode. They do not graze the ivy; they feed upon the galvanic scum that coats the cathodic patches of the iron. If one is pried away—an act that requires an iron chisel and leaves a clean, silver-gray scar on the metal—its underside reveals a foot of pale, grey-blue meat and a mouth like a circular file, continually rasping the rust back down to the unoxidized ore. They move perhaps a foot every twenty years, following the slow drift of the electrical gradients toward the submerged dynamos.

Where the tenements lean into the muck, their parlors and bedchambers have become stagnant holding pens for a species of blind, paunchy crustacean descended from cellar-dwelling woodlice. Over centuries in the tepid brine, they have lost their segments to a single, greasy carapace of uncalcified chitin, semitransparent and yellowed like old drafting linen. They are as large as mutton hams. They do not swim; they hang suspended in the curtains of black water-ivy by four hooked limbs, their bellies turned toward the warmth of the buried steam trunks. They have no eyes, only twin pairs of antennae that trail three feet into the amber soup, sensitive to the change of pressure that occurs when a gas bubble breaks five rooms away. They live on the velvet-mold and the tallow-sheened dead-fall that sifts continuously from the upper floorboards. When one dies, it does not float; its tissues are as dense as the bitter water around it, and it simply sways where it hangs until the smaller amphipods have hollowed it out from within, leaving a hollow bladder of yellow skin that the ribbon-ferns root through.

Deeper in the mass, within the hollow fireboxes and the clearance spaces of the large compound engines, lives something that has developed an appetite for the lubricants.

In the nineteenth century, the tallow used to grease the crosshead guides and pack the stuffing boxes was animal fat, tallow boiled down from sheep and ox, mixed with heavy paraffin and graphite. Centuries later, that fat has not dispersed; it has settled into thick, rancid curds within the cold sumps and the lower chambers of the steam chests. Feeding on this deposit is a creature shaped like an enormous, flattened leech, eight or nine feet in length, with skin the color of lard that has been dropped in soot. It has no mouthparts to speak of; instead, its ventral surface is a porous, glandular pad that secretes an alkaline bile, liquefying the cold grease beneath it so that it can be absorbed through the skin. These things are almost completely motionless. They drape themselves over the crank-webs like discarded oilcloths, their edges rippling in the five-hour thermal surge from the heat-exchangers. They have no natural predators, for their tissues are so thoroughly impregnated with petroleum waxes and heavy metallic salts that nothing with a nervous system could endure eating them.

And yet, there are things with nervous systems here—systems tuned with terrifying specificity to the mechanical environment.

In the long horizontal runs of the steam distribution mains—pipes four feet across, now flooded and lined with a thick fur of copper-green algae—dwell the sleepers. They are vertebrate, though only just: neotenic, lungless salamanders that have reached the proportions of men, but without the armature of a man. Their limbs are withered, four-fingered paddles that serve only to hook into the open bolt-holes of the pipe flanges. Their skin is smooth, dead-white, and so thin that the faint, lavender-tinted pulse of their dorsal vessels is visible through it, rhythmically answering the sixty-cycle hum of the drowned transformers. 

These creatures do not breathe air; their external gills are vast, branched plumes of deep carmine that spread out across the diameter of the pipes like soot-catchers, straining the warm, saline broth for microscopic larvae and the oily spores of the horsetails. They are almost entirely cataleptic. They can hang by one finger from a valve stem for a month, their long, pale snouts pointing upstream toward the heat sources, their chins resting on the silt. Their eyes have long since disappeared behind thick, pinkish lids that have fused with the bone beneath, but their whole bodies are an ear. They listen. They know the voice of every pipe in the ward. If a rivet shears in the third tier, two hundred yards away, a ripple runs through the fringe of their gills; if the galvanic voltage drops by half a volt, they twitch their pale tails once, twice, and shift six inches closer to the bronze valve-casings where the current leaks sweetest into the mud.

There are rumors, held only by the structure itself, of the things that inhabit the pockets.

Where the brick vaults have held against the crushing weight of the water, small domes of trapped atmosphere remain. It is not air that could support lung-breathing life: it is an oily, breathless vapor of marsh gas, coal gas, and the breath of things that have lived in the dark so long they have forgotten the sky. 

In these upside-down lakes of gas, hanging between the flat black surface of the water and the sweating brick ceilings, live the descendants of the tenement rats. They are no longer rats. They have become nearly naked, their skin puckered and blubbery, their feet webbed with tough, black membrane like old gum boots. They cannot leave the pockets for long, but they do not need to. They dive through the water down into the drowned sculleries and larders, their long, flattened tails sculling through the soup with an oily ease, gathering the tubers of the water-ivy and the fat, white snails that cling to the iron bed-frames. They bring these things back up into the dry vaulting, where they crouch in the foul-smelling condensation, cracking the shells with teeth that have grown broad and chisel-shaped like those of beavers. They are blind, their ears are simple pinholes sealed with oily sebum, and they speak to one another only by clicking their teeth against the iron beams that pierce the gas pockets—a dull, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* that carries down through the framework of the borough, mingling with the shearing of the rivets and the humming of the salt.

None of these creatures are intruders; none of them remember an edge to this world. To them, the iron is not a construction; it is the geology. The pipes are the roots of the world, the flywheels are its frozen suns, and the warm, bitter broth is the only sky that has ever been.
