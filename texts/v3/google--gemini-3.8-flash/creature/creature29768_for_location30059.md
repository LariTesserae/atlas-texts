The things that move across the terraces did not evolve for this alloy, but they are learning it with a swift, desperate grace.

The tall, eyeless striders are the most visible, though they are fewer than the vastness suggests. Seen at a distance of half a mile, one looks like a loose constellation of bone needles drawn slowly across the slate-dark ground. Up close, their pale skin has the look of peeled turnips, faintly damp and translucent enough that the sluggish, blue-black circulation of their lymph can be traced up through their towering, three-jointed legs. They are entirely blind, possessing no sockets or vestigial pits; instead, their long, tapering snouts are split by three horizontal clefts that flare and shut like gills, tasting the air for thermal plumes and the sharp, alkaline sting of the copper ferns.

They are awkward on the horizontal flats. Their footpads, tipped with single, soft calcifications like worn ivory thimbles, were meant for deep swamp silt or subterranean scree. On the atomic perfection of the black metamaterial, they find almost no purchase. They walk with high, tentative lifts of their knees, their small, pouch-like torsos swaying between their hips like pendulums, feeling for the faint, microscopic grain left by the cold-pour process. When they reach an edge—where an esplanade drops sheerly into a two-hundred-meter canyon—they do not hesitate. They descend. Their long, spatulate toes wedge into the microscopic tolerances where the vegetation has prised a foothold, picking their way down a vertical surface with the same maddening, dreamlike slowness they use on the level ground.

They do not graze on the fronds themselves. Instead, their split snouts press against the stems to suck the concentrated runoff—water that has gathered heavy trace minerals by washing over the unaged stone. When the twenty-minute vibration runs through the terrace, the striders freeze. They drop their bellies flush to the cold alloy, splaying their four stilted limbs until they look like pale, flattened spiders. They wait through the tooth-rattling hum, shuddering slightly as the cloud of falling droplets batters their hairless flanks, and only rise when the floor is dead-still again.

Beneath the viaducts and along the undersides of the cantilevers, life is denser, quieter, and flatter.

Adhered to the sheer ceilings are colonies of wide, leather-textured discs—some no larger than a coin, others broad as wagon wheels. They have no faces, no limbs, only a ring of wet, muscular tissue that creates a vacuum against the seamless metamaterial. They are color-matched to the architecture: an absolute, matte charcoal that drinks the weak daylight. When the condensation gathers and falls, these discs do not drop. They loosen their grip along one crescent of their circumference, allowing the sheet of lukewarm drainage to slip under their bodies, filtering the microscopic spores and atmospheric soot through comb-like fringes before re-sealing themselves with an audible, rubbery *clack*.

Occasionally, one will migrate. It does so by peeling an edge loose, rolling its entire mass forward like a tongue, and sticking down another section, leaving behind a pale, temporary ring of sterile mucus that the rain washes away in minutes. 

Higher up, where the hollow rings of black glass frame the bruised wool of the sky, the air belongs to the gliders. 

They are scarcely more than membranes of clear cartilage, forty centimeters wide, thin as onionskin and completely colorless. They possess no wings that flap; they are kites of living cellophane. They ride the perpetual thermal chimneys that bleed from the tops of the towers, circling the apertures where the humming frequency is most intense. The vibration seems to agitate their reproductive cycles; during the height of the midday heat, hundreds of them can be seen drifting inside the open circle of a single ring, tumbling through the void like transparent ash. They feed on the spore-smoke released by the copper ferns, diving with a soft, paper-like flutter toward the monoliths’ corners to brush their ventral hairs against the vegetation before sweeping back out into the empty air.

They have predators, though few. In the deep, overhung recesses where two slabs meet at a true right angle, long, whip-thin organisms suspend themselves on tethers of hardened bile. They have the gray luster of lead shot. When a glider drifts too close to the dark vertical face, one of these whips snaps outward—a sound like a wet towel flicked against stone—and the glider is jerked into the shadow, folded into a knot, and digested against the warm wall without a sound.

Yet none of these things explain the architecture. 

The striders, the discs, the pale gliders—they are squatters, opportunists spilled over from the surrounding lowlands or shaken loose from the broken strata when the basin was hollowed. They are too small for these concourses. A being that requires an esplanade five hundred meters wide does not hop on thin stilts or slide on grease.

The true inheritors of the city, if they are inheritors at all, are solitary to the point of unreality.

Sometimes, at the distant intersection of two immense terraces, miles out into the basin where the fog hugs the floor, a bulk will be visible that does not belong to the geometry. It does not look like a machine, nor does it look entirely like an animal. It is low, long-slung, the color of wet river slate, perhaps thirty meters from its blunt, wedge-shaped prow to its broad, undivided tail. It does not walk; it shifts. It moves along the mathematical water-channels with a heavy, hydraulic patience, its belly resting directly in the lukewarm trickle, drawing the drainage into wide lateral vents along its flank and exhaling it behind as a pale, odorless mist.

It possesses no eyes, no appendages capable of holding a tool, and no visible voice. But it stays within the lines. It follows the precise, carved ruts of the city as if guided by an iron rail, never straying onto the open terraces, never disturbing the thick mats of succulent groundcover. When it encounters another channel crossing its path, it will pause for hours, perfectly motionless, letting the ferns grow over its blunt snout until some internal clock or unseen current clears the way. 

There are perhaps only four or five of these great slugs in the entire forty-kilometer basin. They do not seek one another. When their paths would bring them within a league of each other, one will simply back away—reversing with the same smooth, heavy glide through the concrete groove, retreating into the endless, doorless canyons of the interior until the distance between them is restored.

And deep beneath the slate-gray bridge that hovers above the mud, in the dark depression where the purple-veined vines have begun to braid together, something else is breathing. 

It does not come up onto the alloys. It lives in the churned, sulfurous slurry where the foundations were sheared into the earth. It is immense enough that when the twenty-minute purge cycle finishes and the mist settles, you can hear its lungs—not the rattle of a beast, but the slow, wet heave of several tons of soft tissue drawing air through thousands of damp, hair-lined vents in the mud. It smells of river-silt, crushed leaves, and the sweet, electric tang of a machine that has run hot for ten minutes and will not be turned off again for ten thousand years.
