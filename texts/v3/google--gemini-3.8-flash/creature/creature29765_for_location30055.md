The first thing to understand about what lives in the basin is that metabolism here is not an engine of combustion, but of accommodation. Standard digestion—the fierce, hot breaking of chemical bonds to scavenge calories—is too loud a process for a place where the local vacuum is already precarious. A creature that burned food the way a wolf or a marsh-hare does would find its cellular membranes sheared apart by the ambient metric stutter within a dozen generations. 

Instead, the living things have the quality of things left out in the rain until they have taken on the rain’s exact temperature and composition. They are thick-walled, cold, and predominantly hydraulic.

In the shallows between the slate hummocks, where the water clings like a skin of cold tallow to the beds of dark liverwort, there are **Loom-leeches**. They are not annelids, though they share the form: flat, pale ribbons the width of a spade-blade and up to three fathoms long, entirely blind, with margins that do not end cleanly against the water. If you look directly at their flanks, your vision slips, as though greasy fingers had smudged the lens of your eye; they maintain an internal weak-charge differential that bends incoming light slightly backward along its own path. They feed by lying across the breaks where the translucent, light-trapped reeds have snapped. When a reed breaks and sheds its low, humming interference pattern, the leech draws that frequency into its ventral ridge, shuddering with a slow, peristaltic ecstasy as it drinks the coherence out of the glow. When it finishes feeding, the bruised violet light has gone dull, reduced to a heap of wet, grey ash that smells faintly of boiled tin, and the leech itself is stiff, heavy, and resonant as a tuning fork for days.

Further down in the iron-stained channels, the long, pale things mentioned by travelers move with an eerie, unhurried symmetry. The bog-cutters call them **Chime-strays** or simply **Carcasses**, though they are entirely alive. They have no skulls; their nervous systems are dispersed along fifty or sixty pairs of thin, hoop-like ribs made of a flexible, vitreous phosphate that never calcified into true bone. These ribs are connected by sheets of muscle so clear you can see the brown river-silt washing through their hollow interiors. 

A Chime-stray does not hunt by pursuit. It positions itself in the reverse-running channels, hooking its stern-most ribs into the peat overhangs, and lets the water run through its open basket. Because the water in these cuts is metric-dense and heavy with floating lead-salts, the creature acts as a living weir. Its ribs vibrate in sympathy with the deep, tearing roar of the bedrock. Small things—blind, three-lobed swimming disks that are neither fish nor trilobite, but curdled knots of self-replicating slime—are stunned by the rib-frequencies and drift into the basket, where they are enveloped not by stomach acid, but by a cold, clear lymph that dissolves them into their constituent isotopes. A Chime-stray killed by a falling slate ledge does not rot; it simply de-coheres over a month, its ribs loosening and turning into curved rods of cloudy quartz that litter the beds of the channels.

In the mid-air, beneath the shelf of grey vapor, the sickle-wings drift. Seen closer, they are not birds, nor bats, nor any lineage that ever knew feathers. They are called **Drift-veils**. Their wings are single, continuous spans of leathery cartilage, black as wet boot-leather on the upper surface to catch what little diffuse radiation filters through the overcast, and pearlescent below. They possess no true heart. Instead, along their dorsal keels are clusters of small, nodular voids—vesicles where the local vacuum ground-state has been drawn down to zero. They do not flap because they have no need of lift; they are literally repelled by the mass of the basin floor. 

The Drift-veils steer by subtly flexing their ribs to alter the orientation of these voids, sliding sideways down gradients of metric pressure. They feed on the nodules of pearlescent water that swell out of the empty air at throat-height. A Drift-veil will coast along the reed-tops with its wide, jawless mouth agape—a slit lined with fine, horn-like combs that harvest the heavy water and the suspended micro-phenomena before they can tear downward into the mud. When a metric *thwip* snaps through the air, sending its cold mist upward, the Drift-veils tilt toward the plume like iron filings toward a lodestone, their wing-surfaces shuddering with a faint, metallic drum-roll as they drink the sudden release of tension.

Yet there are things here that have minds—or something that serves the office of a mind.

On the higher slate hummocks, where the ground is somewhat shielded from the upward-creeping water by thick shields of oily moss, live the **Tallow-gangers**. They are perhaps eight feet tall when they stand, though they rarely do; they move on all fours with the liquid, disjointed roll of very large hyenas, their joints doubling both ways without resistance. They are roughly anthropomorphic only in the sense that they possess four limbs and an upright torso, but their hide is seamless, like the skin of a boiled pig that has chilled in lard, grey-white and completely hairless. 

A Tallow-ganger has no eyes, no ears, and no nostrils. Its head is a blunt, wedge-shaped prow of solid cartilage, grooved like a walnut shell with deep, weeping runnels that channel condensation down toward its mouth—a vertical, lipless fissure between its collarbones that opens only to swallow the thick, iridescent skins of heavy metals that skim the stagnant pools. 

They are tool-users, though their tools would not be recognized by an engineer. A Tallow-ganger will spend forty years sitting on the curve of a single slate hummock, holding a long, faceted rod of altered quartz into the flow of an upside-down cataract. They do not sharpen the rods; they are *tuning* them. They feel the vibration of the weak-force anomalies through the pads of their broad, three-toed hands, which have the texture of damp cork. By turning the stone a fraction of a millimeter each season, they learn to redirect the micro-pockets of metric tension. A traveler watching from a distance might see a Tallow-ganger make a small, precise adjustment with its stone rod, and fifty yards away, a boiling cataract will suddenly quiet into a smooth, silent sheet of falling ice, or a patch of coherent reeds will shift its hum from bruised violet to a long, low amber chord that causes the peat beneath one's boots to settle by three inches.

It is impossible to tell how many Tallow-gangers there are. They do not congregate, nor do they fight. When two meet in the narrow sheep-paths between the liverwort banks, they press their flat, wedge-shaped heads together for hours at a time, perfectly stationary under the dripping sky, their cold hides sweating identical beads of grease, while between their brows the air crackles with tiny, sparkless discharges that smell of wet slate and turned earth. They may be remembering the thing that altered this place; they may be the altered thing’s remaining senses, grown stupid and biological over ten million wet years, forgetting why they were made to listen, but unable to stop.

And beneath all of them, deeper than the peat, down in the drowned galleries where the limestone has been ground into something like grey grease, there is the **Slump**. 

It is not an animal, nor is it a plant, though it has veins of dark, uncoagulated mercury that pulse twice or thrice in a day. The Slump is a colonial residue—a living layer of sentient, organic sediment three to ten feet thick that underlies the entire southern sweep of the basin. The reeds root in it; the Chime-strays lay their vitreous eggs in its soft, cold pockets. It possesses no consciousness that could be spoken to, but it has a massive, slow inertia. When the atmospheric shear becomes too great, and the air threatens to split along a metric fault, the Slump tenses. You can feel it through the soles of your feet miles away: a sudden, terrifying rigidity in the ground, the muck turning hard as basalt underfoot for the space of five breaths, holding the valley together by sheer structural will until the fault passes and the mud resumes its wet, oily slackness.

Nothing here dies easily, because death requires an unambiguous boundary between an organism and its surroundings, and here the surroundings are always intruding upon the flesh. When an inhabitant becomes old or damaged—when a Drift-veil tears its membrane on a light-stalk, or a Tallow-ganger’s joints finally stiffen with lead-crust—it does not perish of organ failure. It simply ceases to maintain its phase-state. Its margins soften; the water takes it; the light that was bound in its tissues unspools with a long, lonely *whick* that leaves a momentary smell of winter ozone in the mist; and the grey basin, roaring and dripping beneath its permanent ceiling of cloud, takes another grain of weight into its ancient, altered belly.
