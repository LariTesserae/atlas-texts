To understand what lives here, one must first discard the habit of looking for eyes, lungs, and feet, and instead look for surfaces that drink.

At the lowest tier of existence in the pocket, life does not walk upon the rock; it is the rock’s outermost, unfinished thought. The rust-colored biofilm that clings to the weeping depressions is not merely a coat of paint, but an organ three millimeters thick, spongy and corrugated like the underside of a tongue. It is an intricate federation of iron-oxidizing and sulfur-metabolizing bacteria, billions of them bound in an extracellular slime of their own weeping. Because there is no sunlight to synthesize and no organic debris raining down from a forest floor, they make their bodies directly out of the mountain’s hot, venting interior: they eat the ferrous iron dissolved in the lukewarm seepage, stripped atom by atom from the basalt miles below, and exhale hematite. 

Where this slime thickens, it forms miniature terraced rims—scalloped lips of biogenic stone no larger than fingernail clippings, holding back thimblefuls of warm, sour water. In these tiny impoundments, the biofilm grows vertically, rising in soft, gelatinous filaments that mimic the gypsum needles beside them. At their tips, they bud into pale, velvet knobs that drift loose and float on the surface tension, riding the perpetual, unmoving condensation like rafts of living grease.

And where there is organic carbon—that solitary, catastrophic intrusion of the outside world—a different life has taken hold.

The block of yew, wedged centuries ago into the crack to keep the stone from healing shut, has become a slowly dying heart for an entirely specialized fungal empire. Beneath the glittering sheath of calcite that encrusts the hemp rope, a pale, anemic mycelium has invaded the wood’s tight grain. It does not fruit into mushrooms; there is no wind to take spores, no open air to warrant a cap. Instead, it lives as a subterranean creeping skin, an ivory felt that works between the annual rings, digesting the ancient lignin in an atmosphere devoid of oxygen. 

Where the yew is softest, turning to that dark, bruised plum, the fungus produces a steady, faint exudate: tiny, clear beads of metabolic water that push their way through the mineral crust. To the creatures of the hollow, this calcifying wedge is not an artifact of a tool-making human; it is a hill of endless, fermenting sugar, a sole carbohydrate mountain whose scent—faintly alcoholic, warm, and bitter as crushed laurel leaves—diffuses through the suffocating humidity.

Drawn to this wooden hill, and to the rust-mats that fringe the warm pools, are the mobile ones.

The eyeless amphipod is not an anomaly, nor is it alone. It belongs to a population of perhaps four dozen individuals that inhabit this cart-bed-sized world—a number kept strictly stable by the precise calories leaked from the wood and the stone. It is a creature pared down to an austere mechanical perfection. Its carapace has surrendered all pigment, polished by moisture to the milky lucidity of river ice, so that through its sides one can watch the faint, pulsing rhythm of its dorsal vessel pumping hemolymph. 

Because it cannot see, its universe is an astonishingly detailed map of touch, chemistry, and vibration. Its two pairs of antennae—each segment ringed with micro-setae so fine they can register the turbulence caused by a falling drop three feet away—are kept perpetually extended, testing the water films that coat every surface. It does not swim through open water, for there are no lakes here, only films and pools an inch deep. It walks. It has modified its pleopods into delicate, grasping stilts, stepping across the tips of the aragonite needles with the meticulous precision of a clockwork automaton. 

When two of them meet upon a curtain of hollow stalactite straws, there is no panic, no sudden flight. Flight would mean snapping a mineral lattice, becoming tangled in a falling ruin of crystal shards, or dropping into a dry crevice where the gills would dry in the 85-degree heat. Instead, they pause, their thread-like antennae weaving through the space between them in an intricate, reciprocal ballet. They stroke each other’s carapaces, reading sex, age, and nutritional status from the chemical signature of the slime they have each grazed upon, then move laterally past one another, their legs never touching the same needle twice.

Their eggs—scant, usually only two or three held within the female’s ventral brood pouch—take months to hatch in the unchanging warmth. When the juveniles emerge, they are not larvae; they are dust-sized replicas of the adults, small enough to inhabit the interior lumen of a single soda-straw stalactite. In those hollow, grass-thick tubes, where the warm water creeps toward its falling point, the young live safe from the larger crawlers, grazing on the inner walls where the bacteria form a pink, microscopic fur.

Yet the amphipod is not the summit of this pocket’s ecology.

Near the base of the four scraped tally marks, where the tuff was bruised by the long-dead hunter’s chert scraper, the rock has softened into a grey, clay-like slurry. Living within this muck is something that does not step, but glides: a troglobitic flatworm, colorless and flattened to the thickness of a wet cigarette paper. It has no segments, no shell, and no specialized respiratory organs; it breathes through its skin, absorbing the hot moisture of the air directly.

This flatworm moves over the botryoidal chalcedony like a slow drop of wax, driven by microscopic cilia that beat through a trail of clear mucus. It is the hollow’s quiet, blind butcher. It hunts by smelling the chemical wake left by the amphipods on the crystal bristles. When it encounters one that has paused too long—or one that has become stuck, for to remain still in this room for more than a few days is to allow the precipitating aragonite to begin cementing one’s legs to the floor—the worm flows over it. It extends a muscular, tube-like pharynx from the center of its belly, finding the soft seams between the crustacean’s abdominal plates, dissolving the flesh from within using digestive enzymes that leave the white outer shell intact.

Scattered throughout the crystal forest, one can find these empty carapaces. They do not decay. Instead, the water dripping through the grass-stem stalactites coats them in calcium carbonate. Within decades, they become white, ghostly monuments: tiny, hollow crustaceans made entirely of stone, welded forever to the needles they once grazed upon, indistinguishable from the mineral world that spawned them.

And deeper still, inside the tiny, capillary-sized cracks where even the amphipods cannot reach—where the basalt is split by pressures from the mountain’s shifting hips—there is an invisible, teeming drift. 

Nematodes no wider than a hair, their bodies consisting of a few hundred cells, swim through the continuous water sleeve that blankets the rock. They live lives of intense, microscopic violence within the film, hunting single-celled protozoa that tumble down through the dark in the slow geothermal sweat. They know neither up nor down, only the drag of the water’s surface tension against their sides and the chemical tang of the iron seam.

All of them—bacteria, fungus, worm, amphipod, and nematode—live under an absolute sovereign: the equilibrium. 

They have no seasonal cycle; there is no spring to cue breeding, no winter to starve them into torpor. Their clocks are not measured by the turning of the earth or the rising of a sun they have never known, but by the relentless, identical deposition of crystal faces. They live at the speed of mineralization. 

The ancient wedge of yew is shrinking, eaten by the white mycelium from within and bound by lime from without, growing narrower by the thickness of a leaf every hundred years. The chert flake in the lime-water pool is slowly disappearing beneath a white scab of travertine. The four tallies carved by touch into the wall are filling in, their rough edges smoothed over by the budding globes of new chalcedony.

In time—perhaps a thousand years, perhaps five—the crystal straws will meet the floor, the needles of aragonite will cross and knit into a dense, solid tooth of stone, and the pocket will close entirely. When it does, the water will be forced into some other, newer split in the crown, and the living things will not die with a gasp; they will simply be embedded, layer by layer, cell by cell, their hollow forms preserved in the white heart of the mountain, waiting for some unimaginable future tectonic fracture to break them open to the air again.
