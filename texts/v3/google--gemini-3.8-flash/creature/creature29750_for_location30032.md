The things that dwell here are not made of flesh that can be torn, because there is nothing in this world soft enough to survive being cut. 

If you were to watch the fracture planes long enough, your eyes adjusting to the dull, furnace-door infrared that bleeds from the fresh breaks, you would first notice the smaller things—the ones that do not walk, but slide. 

They exist in two dimensions. They are broad, wafer-thin sheets of flexible horn and mica-bright carapace, no thicker than a playing card and often several feet across. They have no bellies, no underbellies to expose. They navigate the razor-edges not by stepping over them, but by slotting directly into the stone’s cleavages. When a gust of hot zinc-smelling air screams up a vertical chimney, these creatures—the *shear-flecks*—tilt their edges into the draft with a dry, metallic rattle. They eat the sulfurous skin off the rock, shaving it away with mouthparts made of fused iron-silicate, leaving clean, pale tracks behind them. They have never known a drop of grease or water; their joints do not pivot on fluid, but slide along dry, graphite-dusted grooves in their own shells. When they die, they do not rot; they simply dry out a fraction more, turn brittle, and shatter into flakes of black dust that the next updraft carries into the gulf.

Above them, occupying the spines and the leaning slabs, are the ones who lift the stones.

They are few, separated by vast gulfs of silence and dropping glass, but they are as permanent as the pressure that heaves the peaks skyward. In shape, they are heavy, low-slung wedges, designed entirely to lower a center of mass against a world that is perpetually tilting. They do not have legs like pillars; they have great, splayed lever-limbs—four or six jointed spars capped not with pads or claws, but with deep, grooved shoes of dense, self-annealing keratin that slot over razor-crests like a carpenter’s plane fitting a timber. They do not walk so much as they *shimmy* along the knives of basalt, anchoring themselves by gripping opposing bevels of the fracture.

Their bodies are armored in overlapping shingle-plates of dull, leaden slate. These plates are not static; they breathe with an agonizing slowness, lifting slightly on the intake to expose dark, dry thermal gills that drink in the baking heat of the crust, then clamping shut with a hard, hollow *tock* when a cold vacuum draft sweeps down from the starless black above.

They are completely blind to light in the hydraulic sense—they have no lenses, no wet humors to boil or freeze in the thin air. Instead, their faces are blunt, horn-sheathed battering rams fitted with rows of sunken pits that read the world in thermal stress. To them, the landscape is a shifting map of temperature and tension. They can “see” the fault line five miles below because it is warm; they can see the structural failure of a spire three minutes before it happens because the rock along the shear-plane heats up under the agony of friction, glowing bright to their senses before it ever snaps.

This is why they build.

The stone structures—the roofless dry-stacked basalt pens, the tapering plinths—are not houses, and they are not temples. They are braces, counterweights, and acoustic organs.

Watch one at work: it finds a needle of black glass that has just punched its way out of the abyss. The spire is ringing with an unbearable, high-frequency vibration—a sound that, to the builder, is a blinding shriek of stress. Working without haste, moving with the slow, terrifying leverage of a hydraulic jack, the creature begins to quarry. It does not hit the rock; it wedges its chiseled forearm into a cooling seam, leans its entire tectonic weight against it, and waits. The rock snaps on cue, yielding a perfect, flat-faced block. 

With its front levers, which are scarred and pitted from centuries of dry friction, it lifts the block. It balances it. It stacks it. It builds not to shelter itself from weather that does not exist, but to load the stone. A stacked wall adds hundreds of tons of dead weight to an unstable fault-block, pinning it, damping its vibration, tuning the pitch of the mountain down from a scream to a tolerable hum. 

The plinths, with their flat, empty basins balanced on needle-sharp summits, are instruments of calibration. When the wind catches the vertical fins and plays them like reeds, the creature climbs to the basin and sits inside it, curling its slate-armored bulk into the depression. By shifting its weight an inch to the left or right, it changes the harmonic resonance of the entire spire, preventing the wind from matching the rock’s natural frequency and shattering the needle beneath it.

They are the mountain’s nervous system, forever shoring up an edifice that cannot bear its own birth.

They have no language of mouths, for they have no lungs to push wet air past wet cords. Their speech is percussive, delivered straight into the spine of the basalt. When one of them strikes a plinth with its dense, calcified tail, the strike produces that clean, dry *clack* that carries for forty miles through the solid bedrock. Another, perched on a tilting horn three ridges away, feels the pulse through its foot-grooves, calculates the vector of the impact, and strikes back in answer—an ancient, mathematical dialogue about where the crust is thin, where the heat is dying, and which fin of stone is slated to die next.

They have no fear of the falls. When a ridge shears beneath one of them, it does not thrash or cry out. It draws its lever-limbs inward, locks its overlapping plates into a single, dense rhomboid, and rides the avalanche of razor-shards into the ink-black floor of the world. Miles below, in the dark where the broken spires collect like heaps of discarded iron needles, it will either lie shattered—its dry, sulfurous interior exposed to cool into mineral salt—or it will find its footing in the rubble, smell the zinc on a newly opened thermal vent, and begin the long, agonizingly patient climb back toward the sky, searching for a blade of rock that needs to be weighted against the void.
