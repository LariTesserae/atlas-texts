The gap beneath the floor is three feet high, unvarying, and runs the entire three thousand miles. 

It is here, in the cold drafts between the lilac underside of the deck and the vacuum tubes of the flue system, that the smaller things run. They are not warm. If you placed a palm against them, they would register as room-temperature horn or cold gutta-percha, perhaps ticking slightly like a pocket watch that has been dropped in sand.

Most numerous are the *clearing-spiders*, though they have ten legs rather than eight, arranged in two radial tiers so that five legs may walk while five are raised, feeling along the lower ribbing of the steel plates for burrs or uneven wear. Their bodies are flattened disks of dried cowrie-shell and dark, unvarnished leather, no thicker than an architectural drafting ruler. They have no eyes; in the shadowless, magnesium-flooded world above, eyes are an extravagance easily blinded, and down here, between the joists, there is nothing to see anyway. Instead, they navigate by the tension of the wires. Every clearance in the basin is strung with guitar-gauge piano wire that runs through eyelets in the spiders’ leg-joints. As an overhead gantry moves twenty miles away, the wire tightens by three microns; the spiders feel the note shift from a dry B-flat to a sharp C, and they alter their crawl accordingly, scattering ahead of the dropped quadrants.

They live on the powdered graphite that sloughs off the runners. They do not digest it with enzymes; their bellies are dry ball-mills—two hollow hemispheres of fired porcelain containing three loose, tumbled agates. As the spider runs, the motion of its legs churns the agates against one another, pulverizing the graphite into an impalpable grease which their lower vents exude along the tracks behind them. They are self-propelling bearings. When two meet, they do not fight or court with display; they interlock their perimeter gears, trade three drops of dry shellac through a hollow rostrum, and spin each other until their spring-tensions equalize, after which they disengage and run on.

Above the deck, where the liverwort-paper spans the die-cast brackets, lives the slower, heavier population.

They are difficult to distinguish from the furniture of the plain because they share its palette: the color of manila card, old boxwood, and dried bladder. They have the long, slow geometry of folding surveying rules. One of them, resting among the corrugated brass thistles, looks like an abandoned tripod of pale pear-wood whose joints have been reinforced with neat, hand-stitched linen thread. 

These are the *gaugers*. Their metabolisms are so sluggish that their lives are measured not in heartbeats—they have no heart, only an electrostatic bellows made of pleated goatskin that expands once every twenty minutes—but in the forty-day cycle of the basin’s charge. During the intake phase, while the cold deepens and the sky pours its steady, white glare downward, the gaugers stand completely rigid on their three pointed stilts. Their long, spatulate heads, capped with polished calcite prisms, tilt upward toward the glass roof at an angle that does not deviate by a second of arc for two weeks.

They are reading the thickness of the sky.

To a gauger, the magnesium overcast is not a ceiling; it is a text ruled with fine, parallel lines that shift as the glass panels expand and contract under the pull of the continent-wide magnets below. The gauger’s brain is a continuous scroll of wax-impregnated ribbon, eighteen inches wide, that moves between two lignum vitae rollers inside its thorax. As the light pulses, needles of ground sapphire prick the ribbon, recording the clearance between the six-mile trusses and the deck. They do not know they are recording this; they simply feel an intolerable dry itch in their neck-springs whenever the glass settles unevenly, an itch that is relieved only when the prism rotates by a fraction of a millimeter and allows the sapphire point to bite.

When the discharge comes, they fold. Their limbs, constructed of hollow bone-china sleeves fitted over core-rods of seasoned hornbeam, collapse inward along pre-scored bevels with a sound like a surveyor packing his instruments into a leather case. They lower themselves into the spindle-grass, pressing their bellies against the lilac-colored deck to receive the ground-wave. 

They do not bleed if crushed; if a gantry rail descends upon a gauger, it shatters with the dry, hollow *pop* of an old dry-plate negative. The interior is clean: pale sawdust, three yards of intricately punched parchment ribbon, and small, dry chambers lined with the velvet scraped from the antlers of reindeer, perfectly preserved by the zero-humidity air, serving as acoustic dampeners for their gear-trains.

There are things that fly, though "flying" is an imprecise word for travel through an atmosphere so thin and cold that lift must be bought with frantic surface area.

They are the *kites*. They have no muscles in their wings; their wings are kites in truth—hexagonal frames of shaved split-willow covered in the thinnest obtainable capacitor tissue, stretched so tight that when a dead seed from a wild-linen loom strikes them, they ring like finger cymbals. A kite has a span of twenty feet but weighs less than three ounces. It hangs permanently in the six-mile space between the thistle-tops and the glass rafters, never descending below the draft line of the gantry cranes.

The kites have no legs. Their bodies are merely dried reed-pipes containing an array of tuned copper reeds. They do not steer by flapping; they alter their flight by warping their frames through the contraction of hygroscopic gut-strings that react to the static charge of the air. When a dry cold-front swings south—a wall of ten million volts sliding across the iron decks without a cloud to mark it—the gut-strings snap taut, the wing twists four degrees, and the kite leans into the vacuum behind the front, riding the dry thermal for five hundred miles without dropping an inch.

They eat the needle-punch beetles. They do not strike from above with talons; they simply drift down like falling paper until their trailing tethers—long, sticky threads of raw, unspun silk weighted with lead shot the size of mustard seeds—snag a beetle as it sews. The kite cannot reel the thread in; instead, the beetle’s own frantic, clockwork vibrations travel up the line like the hum of an electric fence. The vibration reaches the kite’s core, where it strikes a dry, ratcheted escapement that slowly winds the thread around the kite’s main axle, drawing the beetle upward inch by inch through the motionless daylight.

By the time the beetle reaches the kite, the vibration has shaken the beetle’s joints loose. Its traced-cloth wings have dropped away; its brass needle has fallen into the spindle-grass below. The kite’s mouth is an unthreaded steel die that fits the beetle’s bolt-like body precisely. It screws the prey into its own breast-plate with three slow, mechanical twists, incorporating the beetle’s zinc casing into its own ballast and dropping the empty carapace down into the combs.

And there are the keepers—though to call them keepers implies they own or understand the works, when in truth they are simply the only things here that have retained the memory of hands.

They are tall, twice the height of a man, but extraordinarily narrow, as though they have spent ten thousand generations squeezing through the inspection gaps between high-voltage switchboards. Their garments are not woven from cloth, for there is no thread here that is soft enough; they wear suits made of overlapping scales of oil-paper, each scale stamped with a serial number in faded red lithographer’s ink. The scales rustle dryly with every movement, producing the sound of wind in a winter bamboo grove.

Their faces are obscured behind masks of thin, unlacquered horn, curved to fit the skull and pierced with thousands of pin-holes instead of eye-slits. Through these sieves of vision they see the basin not as forms, but as gradients of static potential. They carry long ash-wood poles tipped with bone hooks, with which they reach into the reciprocating looms to flick away the carcasses of kites that have drifted into the shuttles, or to realign a drive-rod that has slipped from its graphite socket.

They do not speak. The dry air has long since withered their vocal chords into hard, resinous ridges that can produce only a dry, whistling click when they exhale. Instead, they communicate through their boots.

The soles of their boots are four inches thick, carved from solid blocks of dense, unyielding boxwood, banded with iron. When two keepers meet at a gantry intersection, they stand six feet apart, their paper skirts rustling in the downdraft, and they stamp. 

*Clack.*

The blow travels through the cold-rolled plate, through the runners, through the lilac patina, into the foundation three miles below.

*Clack-clack.*

The response comes not through the air, but up through the bones of the other’s legs—a sharp, percussive vibration that rattles their teeth. They will stand in the shadowless white noon for three hours, their wooden feet striking the floor in complex, syncopated measures, trading clearances, wear-tolerances, and the coordinates of rusted pins thirty leagues to the west. Their messages are entirely technical: they have no words for *sorrow*, or *distance*, or *white*, because here distance is simply a measure of chain-links, and there is no other color to compare the light against.

When a keeper dies, they do not bury him; the steel floor cannot be dug, and the flues are meant only for flakes of size four or smaller. 

The others gather. They strip the dead one of his paper scales, stacking them neatly by serial number to be re-glued onto the skin of the next generation. They remove the mask of horn and set it upon a thistle-stalk to season in the ozone. Then they lay the body—which is entirely bloodless now, a light, stiff armature of dry sinew and chalky, de-calcified bone that weighs no more than an armful of dried elderberry twigs—directly upon the path of the wild-linen loom.

They stand back, their wooden boots motionless upon the iron.

The great ash shuttle, thirty feet long and tipped with chisel-steel, comes sliding down its graphite bed with that vast, library-hush whisper. It does not pause. It strikes the body and presses it into the open shed of the warp, where fifty thousand strands of cold, carded zinc-fiber cross over it, under it, and through it, beating it into the weave.

Within an hour, the keeper has become three square yards of high-tensile fencing, indistinguishable from the rest of the floor, perfectly flat, lilac-painted, over which the spindle-grass slowly begins to creep on its thirteen-hour gears, turning its broad leaves toward the six-mile glass to take the charge once more.
