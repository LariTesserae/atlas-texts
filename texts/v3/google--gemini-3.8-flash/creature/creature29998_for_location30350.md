To find them, one must discard the expectation of breath. 

There is no lung-work here, no wet pump of blood, no nervous flicker of muscle. In an atmosphere so clear that a horizon twenty miles distant appears close enough to touch, life does not advertise itself with agitation. Motion is expensive; it creates friction, and friction produces heat that cannot easily be shed into dry, thin air. 

Life here lives within the tolerances.

Deep within the groves of brass and spun basalt, where the umbrellas of foil overlap to cast razor-edged, violet-black shadows, the understory is inhabited by creatures that resemble less the animals of an earthly forest and more the discarded, precision-stamped internal components of an astronomical clock.

Clinging to the conduit-ribbed reeds are the *tines*. They are long, slender organisms composed of laminated leaf-springs—alternating wafers of virgin silver and cold-drawn molybdenum, bound together with a clear, vitreous silicate. They have no heads, no eyes, and no appendages for grasping. Instead, their bodies are shaped like tuning forks, three to four feet in length, their pointed bases driven deep into the seams of the hematite floor where the roots of the groves bleed residual charge. 

The tines do not eat. They are bimetallic thermocouples. One arm of the fork extends outward into the arc-lamp blaze of the noon sun; the other remains strictly within the zero-degree shadow of the canopy. The steep thermal gradient between the two arms generates an internal electromotive force—a quiet, ceaseless voltage that circulates through their micro-crystalline lattices. 

They communicate, if the term applies, through sympathetic resonance. When the plateau shudders or emits its solitary, five-mile expansion *ping*, the tines take up the vibration. They hum, their polished edges shimmering so rapidly they turn into silver blurs, shedding microscopically fine scales of dry graphite dust. To an observer standing motionless beside them, their presence would register only as a faint, high-frequency singing, like the whine of high-tension wires in winter, and a clean, sharp smell of ozone.

Lower down, moving through the mica mulch, are the *planers*.

They are the grazers of this floor, though they ingest nothing. In outline, a planer is a heavy, bevel-edged bar of dull specularite iron, five or six inches thick and perhaps three paces long, rounded at the fore like the runner of an arctic sledge. They possess no legs. Their locomotion is an act of solid-state hydraulics: along their polished bellies run thousands of microscopic chambers filled with a low-melting-point eutectic alloy of bismuth and tin. 

As the sun tracks across the sky, subtle shifts in radiation warm the planer’s lead edge. The metal inside melts, expands, and alters the center of gravity; the trailing edge, remaining cool, contracts. Inch by inch, with the glacial, terrifying patience of a vernier scale settling into alignment, the creature drags itself across the flat bronze plates of the plateau. 

Its function is purely capacitive. As it creeps over the bare metal, its immense weight and mirror-lapped underside scour away the film of vapor-deposited zinc and free sulfur that accumulates on the tableland. It shears the surfaces clean, preserving the electrical continuity of the plain. Behind a planer, the floor does not show a footprint, but a path of raw, blinding luster, so bright that the sun reflecting off it can crack the mica overhead.

Where there are grazers, there are those that quarry them.

On the high perimeter of the groves, perched upon basalt pillars whose stepped cooling fins give them the look of industrial substations, sit the *wedges*. 

A wedge does not stalk. It is a solitary block of dark, case-hardened titanium-carbide, heavy as an anvil, poised on three needle-fine points of synthetic corundum. It can remain motionless for decades, indistinguishable from a fallen block of furnace-slag. It possesses no sensory organs save for a row of narrow, horizontal slits along its prow, inside of which are suspended arrays of microscopic quartz crystals—piezoelectric sensors tuned to the slightest shift in ground-current.

The wedge hunts by interruption. It waits until a planer, or a wandering cluster of smaller, needle-like scavengers, crosses a critical galvanic line—a subterranean copper spiral or an active silver vein. When the target shorts the local circuit, altering the hum of the plateau by a fraction of a cycle, the wedge acts.

Its movement is explosive, but it is not muscular. A sudden internal discharge of stored electrical potential vaporizes a droplet of liquid sodium sealed within its central core. The instantaneous expansion snaps a latch of brittle spring-steel. The wedge throws itself down from its perch with a flat, deafening *thrum*, striking the shelf below with the force of an unexploded shell.

It does not crush to consume flesh. It lands directly across the conductive path of its prey, bonding to the floor with an instantaneous, high-amperage arc-weld. It acts as an absolute ground. In a fraction of a second, the entire thermal and electrical charge of the planer is siphoned into the cold, dense mass of the wedge. The planer’s internal eutectic freezing, its laminated lattices buckling under the sudden loss of potential, it dies instantly by becoming cold. 

The wedge then remains there, clamped to the carcass, slowly radiating the stolen heat into the sky through broad, black-anodized cooling vanes that fold out from its flank like the wings of a dead beetle, until the charge has equalized and the seasonal drift of the sun allows it to fracture its own weld and reset its spring.

Farther out, on the dead, mirror-bright voids between the groves, where the scale expands and the horizon seems to drop off the edge of the world, there are the *sleds*.

They are the largest things that live on the tableland. From a distance of miles, one might mistake them for low, faceted hills of burnished lead, three hundred paces long, moving across the hematite flats so slowly that their progress can only be measured against the distant basalt towers. 

A sled has no interior organs in the biological sense; it is a walking crystal lattice, an immense, self-organizing macromolecule of native antimony and tellurium. It lives entirely off the Peltier effect, harvesting the vast temperature differential between its scorched, sun-baked upper terraces and the ice-cold shadow it casts beneath its own belly.

The sleds never enter the groves. The brass trees would tangle their edges; the carbon tubs would choke their vents. They belong exclusively to the void. Beneath their hulls, thousands of heavy, self-lubricating graphite pads cycle in an endless, slow-motion caterpillar crawl, their movement accompanied by a sound like millstones grinding dry grain—a low, rhythmic crunch that travels through the rock for leagues.

Birds do not fly here, for the air gives no purchase to feathers and carries no thermals of moist air. But hovering above the sleds, suspended three inches above the incandescent floor, are the *foils*.

They are sheets of native gold and beaten copper, no thicker than an oil slick on water, several yards square. They have no mass to speak of; they are held aloft by pure diamagnetic repulsion, floating on the invisible, braided magnetic fields that leak upward from the plateau’s twisted copper veins. They drift like fallen sails, fluttering slightly when the expansion *pings* strike the air, tethered to the sleds by invisible lines of electrostatic tension. They are the eyes of the great sleds—not that they see, but that their subtle ripples and drift-angles map the magnetic contours of the tableland, guiding the massive hulls toward the richest galvanic lodes.

Death here is clean, structural, and dry. 

Nothing rots. Without bacteria, without moisture, without chemistry that yields to decay, a dead thing is simply a machine that has suffered work-hardening. Over centuries of microscopic flexing, the crystal planes of the tines and the wedges become brittle. Slip-planes form in their metals; dislocations migrate to the surface. 

At last, under the stress of an ordinary noon, a fatigue limit is exceeded. 

There is no struggle. The organism simply parts along a cleavage plane. A tine splits into two identical, inert strips of silver and molybdenum with a tiny, musical *clink*; a planer fractures across its middle, releasing a spoonful of silvery bismuth that instantly rolls into perfect, non-wetting spheres on the hot floor and cools into gray beads.

The grove does not consume them. Instead, the relentless abiotic chemistry of the plateau goes to work. The dry graphite dust settling from the canopies slowly silts over the fragments; the vapor-phase zinc coats them in a uniform, sparkling frost. Within a thousand years, the corpse is integrated into the pavement, its geometry incorporated into the unchiseled strata of the floor, waiting to serve as the conductive footing for a new seedling of brass-foil, which will drill its lead taproot down through the old carcass and begin, again, the long, motionless hum of living.
