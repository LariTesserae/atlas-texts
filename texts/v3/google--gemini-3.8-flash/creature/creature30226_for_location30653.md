At the base of the food web, in the grease-filled gutters between the pipe-reeds, lives what cannot afford to be crushed: the caulkers.

They are small, flat, and hexagonal, about the size of a shovel-blade, scuttling on eight pairs of jointed, copper-tipped pincer-legs. Their carapaces are not solid plates but laminated stacks of vulcanized horn that can flex under three tons of static load without fracturing. They are the maintenance crew of the shale beds. Where the great walking-beams of the behemoths pound down, cracking the basalt and squeezing veins of amber grease up from the compressed mulch, the caulkers arrive in swarms. They do not eat the grease directly; they graze on the film of dry-cell mold that blooms across it within seconds of exposure to the raw ultraviolet. 

To feed, a caulker locks its body to the stone with a central vacuum cup—a rubbery sphincter that pulls thirty pounds of negative pressure—and extrudes a pair of spinning, serrated scrapers. The sound of a thousand caulkers at work is a dry, whispering rustle, like heavy sand sliding down a tin roof. When a herd shifts its collective weight, a caulker simply draws in its limbs, burrows two inches into the needle-talus, and flattens out, letting the locomotive-scale feet pass overhead. If an axle-leg clips one at an angle, the caulker does not split; it deforms with the dull *tunk* of a lead ingot, then slowly levers its bent skirts back into true using its own spring-tempered undercarriage.

Higher up, wedged into the actual anatomy of the behemoths, are the tallow-leeches. 

A tallow-leech is essentially an eighteen-inch syringe of ribbed, cartilage-reinforced leather. It lacks eyes, fins, or true limbs. Its entire existence is dedicated to the soft, oiled seals that bridge the gap between a behemoth’s chitinous plates—the ball-sockets of the hips, the neck-gimbals, the heavy sliding sleeves of the knee-pistons. The leech seeks out the warm, paraffin-scented seepage where an animal’s joint-linings have worn thin from the endless, grinding migration. 

Using an array of four counter-rotating borers made of dense, diamond-impregnated enamel, the leech cuts a neat, three-millimeter hole through the beast’s external gasket. It does not drink blood; blood would freeze in its gut at forty below. It siphons the hot, mineralized synovial oil directly from the hydraulic envelope. The host beast feels nothing—its central nervous system is an insulated, slow-pulsing bundle of fat-sheathed galvanic cords that only registers catastrophic loss of pressure. A large bull-engine can carry four hundred tallow-leeches without a drop in gait, though in deep winter, when the leeches grow fat and stiff with cold lard, their protruding hind ends form a fringe of pale, trembling tubes that whistle like panpipes whenever the beast vents its lateral steam-ports.

Then there are the shear-crows, the scavengers who follow the knocking of the herd.

They are not birds, though they have the silhouette of storks that have been stripped down to the structural trusswork. Their legs are dry, hollow struts of blackened ironwood, ten feet tall, ending in broad, self-cleaning tripods that distribute their weight across the treacherous scree. Their heads are massive, unbalanced wedges—solid masses of horn-sheathed bone shaped like cold chisels, operated by heavy, counter-weighted tendons that run the full length of their backs like external stays.

A shear-crow does not wait for natural rot, because rot does not happen here. When a behemoth misses a stride—when an intake valve fouls with grit, or an internal bronze-ring cracks and the steam chamber loses its seal—the creature slowly tilts, its rhythmic strokes slowing from one beat every forty seconds to one every four minutes, until the walking beams freeze in half-extension. The animal stands there, a paralyzed tower of plate and muscle, its eyes (two thick, cloudy globes of crystalline quartz set deep in iron-rimmed pits) slowly dulling.

The shear-crows gather within an hour. They move with an unnatural, rocking gait, tilting their heads toward the sky to gain potential energy, then letting the chisel-beaks drop by gravity alone. *Tock. Tock. Tock.* They strike precisely at the pin-joints and the seams where the dorsal plates overlap. They do not peck; they wedge. Two shear-crows will insert their lower mandibles under a plate edge, brace their tripod feet against the frozen talus, and expand their jaw-muscles—hydraulic bladders that inflate with pressurized air drawn through nasal bellows—until the three-inch-thick carapace plate pops from its flesh-pegs with the report of a field gun.

Only then do the siphon-crawlers descend into the opened chest. These are long, blind, pale things that look like lengths of corrugated garden hose, sliding down through the shattered ribs to harvest the deep, warm visceral organs: the rubbery lung-bladders, the heavy iron-metabolizing liver-pancreas that still clicks with residual chemical heat, and the thick, fibrous belts of cardiac tendon that will remain elastic for weeks.

In the dark beneath the stone, living inside the mountain's pressurized plumbing, are the pipe-grubs.

They are the architects of the subterranean water-knock. A pipe-grub is a creature of pressure; it cannot exist at standard atmospheric weight. Shaped like a massive, five-foot segmented slug clad in greasy, overlapping iron rings, it lives inside the tectonic cracks where geothermal steam meets glacial melt. The pipe-grub feeds on the mineral crust that deposits along these fissures—pyrite, sulfur, silicates. 

As it crawls, its belly-glands exude a dense, vulcanizing latex that cures instantly against the wet stone, turning jagged fractures into smooth, high-pressure conduits. The grubs form living check-valves. When the hydraulic pressure from a high-altitude reservoir surges, thousands of pipe-grubs wedge themselves against narrowing rock-throats, their muscular bodies inflating to block the flow, building up enormous potential heads until they release in unison. They are the reason the valley floors throb underfoot; the cordillera does not have a water table, it has an arterial system driven by the rhythmic, collective spasms of these subterranean gaskets.

And high above the scree, drifting where the air is too thin for liquid water to exist, are the grand bellows—the aerostats.

From below, they look like ribbed sheets of translucent mica, miles across, but close to, they are complex, slow-cycling sponges of rigid air-cells. They have no faces, no brains in the vertebrate sense, only a vast, flat ring of nerve-ganglia that measures atmospheric shear and solar flare. Their bodies are lightened by thousands of internal combustion chambers—small, bladder-like pockets lined with platinum-catalyst moss.

As the aerostat drifts through the high banners of dry ice and mountain frost, its trailing tentacles—long, jointed combs of fibrous pipe-reed that hang thousands of feet down into the thermal updrafts—harvest ambient hydrogen and trace hydrocarbons from the volcanic plumes. Inside the bladders, tiny, rhythmic sparks, generated by piezoelectric crystals in the muscle-hinges, ignite micro-charges of gas. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* Every six miles of travel, an aerostat flashes pale violet from within, a cold, chemical lightning that illuminates the lattice of its skeletal trusses.

They are an ecosystem unto themselves. The upper surface of an aerostat, baking in the raw, sterilizing light of the sun, is a dry plain of salt-lichen and flat, scissor-tailed sky-mites that graze on the frost that sublimes against the leading edge. The sky-mites never touch the earth; if one falls, the descent takes three hours, its delicate, stamped-tin wings whistling until the dense air of the low valleys crushes its thin air-chambers like paper cups.

Down in the saddles, the behemoths pay no mind to what is above or below. 

A single bull stands sixty feet at the shoulder. His head is a solid, battering block of composite plate, completely devoid of mouth-parts in the mammalian sense; between his front walking-beams, where an animal’s throat should be, is a revolving, conical hopper lined with case-hardened tooth-plates. He does not graze selectively. He moves forward at four inches an hour, his hopper turning with a heavy, wet *churr*, eating the pipe-reeds, the iron-moss, the slate beneath it, and any caulkers too slow to clear the track.

Inside his vast gut, the rock is crushed by massive, internal stone spheres that roll against one another in a muscular mill-race, turning basalt into fine flour to extract the microscopic veins of native iron and sulfur. His body temperature is high—nearly one hundred and forty degrees—maintained by the ceaseless friction of his internal works. The grease that lubricates his ball-sockets is filtered through an abdominal maze of felted hair and charcoal membranes, pumped back to the neck-gimbals by the rhythmic sway of his huge, wedge-shaped rump.

He has no voice. When he must challenge another bull for space in the narrow saddle, he does not roar; he alters the cycle of his steam-exhaust. He closes his lateral relief valves, allowing the pressure in his dorsal reservoirs to climb toward the bursting point. His shell begins to tick—a rapid, brittle sound like a watch spring being over-wound. The metal-sheathed skin between his plates bulges, glossy and black. 

The two giants will approach with a terrifying, hydraulic slowness, their walking-beams groaning as the oil thins under the friction of maximum torque. They press their flat, chisel-shaped foreheads together. There is no impact, no charge. There is only the long, agonizing application of horsepower. 

The shale beneath their pads crumbles into blue dust. The parasitic lever-crabs on their shoulders scurry wildly between the rivets of their carapaces, their escapement-mechanisms clicking in a frenzy as the shell plates flex and grooved edges bite into grooved edges. They will stand there for three days, forehead to forehead, vents screaming with jets of dry, white steam that burn the frost off the crags for fifty yards around, until the piston-seals of the weaker bull begin to weep amber lard—and with a long, whistling groan, his hydraulic pressure fails, his hind quarters drop into the talus, and the herd shoves him aside, grinding his shell into the chute without a backward glance.
