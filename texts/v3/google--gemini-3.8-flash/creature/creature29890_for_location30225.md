Between the overlapping bellies of the *kivva*, where the heat of their slow blood is trapped against the freezing syenite, there is no empty space. To pull two of them apart—were their amber-tipped ribs not locked into the rock with sixty pounds of leverage apiece—would sound like tearing wet parchment from a tub of lard.

In that smothered, body-warm dark live the things that cannot endure the wind outside.

Most numerous are the *nits*, though they bear little resemblance to insects. They are flat, blind crustaceans the color of blanched parsnips, broad as a man’s palm and no thicker than a fingernail. They have abandoned swimming paddles for dozens of paired, hook-ended needles that comb continuously through the kivva’s grease. They do not bite through the hide; they do not need to. They feed exclusively on the grey, curd-like smegma that accumulates around the hinges of the kivva’s rib-plates, and on the sheets of sloughed dead epidermis that peel off in the damp like boiled onion skin.

Where three or four kivva overlap, the heat rises to nearly eighty degrees, and here the nits congregate in tight, sliding mills. If an adult kivva takes its deep, shuddering inhalation, the rock groans, the rib-plates expand with a wet, sucking click, and thousands of these flat white plates shift simultaneously, a dry, scuttling rustle beneath the colossal rhythm of the lungs. They serve the host by keeping the breathing-slits clean; a kivva whose nits have died from an intrusion of sub-zero wind will slowly choke on its own tallow, its spiracles gummed shut by a rubbery, pale plug of oxidized fat.

Higher up, where the cleft narrows to three paces and the frost forms long, needle-like rimes along the rock ceiling, lives another tenant: the *skimmer*. 

These are birds only by ancestry. They have the wingspan of a raven, but their flight feathers have degenerated into oily, black quills that look like the bristles of a chimney sweep. They never fly; they cannot open their wings in the slot. Instead, they use them as crutches and snowshoes, levering themselves across the vibrating floor of kivva-backs with a frantic, limping gait. 

Their eyes are vestigial, sealed under a thick, translucent fold of skin that protects them from the ammonia gas, but their bills are eight inches of curved, flexible horn, pliable as whalebone. With these, they probe the deep fissures between the kivva bodies, searching for the soft brood-pouches that form on the flank of the females during the height of the summer jet stream. 

When a skimmer finds a gravid kivva, it does not kill it. It inserts its soft bill between the rib-bars, locating the small, jelly-encased embryos that cling to the mother’s lateral artery, and sucks them out one by one, like marrow from a split bone. The kivva does not flinch; its nervous system is calibrated to the crushing weight of rock, not the delicate theft of its seed. The skimmer’s nest is nothing more than a shallow depression in the grease-mound of an ancient, dead adult whose skeleton has calcified into the rock-face, lined with its own moulted quills and the stiffened husks of desiccated nits.

Then there is the drift of life that begins at the bottom, in the sump.

Down where the elk-horn wedges remain driven into the syenite, the floor is not stone. It is a deep, cold moraine of *adipocere*—grave-wax—formed from five centuries of dead kivva that slipped their moorings and fell into the dark. In this cold, grey tallow, which has the consistency of cold soap and smells of brine and wet wool, lives the *gullet-worm*.

They are thick as a wrestler’s forearm, slate-grey, and completely boneless. They do not have mouths in the ordinary sense; their anterior ends are simply rounded muscular bulbs that ingest the waxy floor by peristalsis, extracting the high-energy lipids that have settled out of the carcasses above. They are so slow that their movements can only be measured over days. A worm will spend half a year burrowing through the remains of a single kivva that fell during the great shake of fifty winters ago, turning the grey tallow into a fine, crumbly grease that eventually washes down the bottom drainage into the mountain’s subterranean aquifers.

Sometimes, when the windward storms are particularly fierce, a high-altitude hawk or a snow-pigeon is sucked out of the jet stream and hurled into the cleft by the vortex. If the impact against the glass-sharp syenite does not kill the bird instantly, the atmosphere does. Within twenty paces of the rift's mouth, the temperature plunges, then suddenly spikes into the foul, oxygen-starved vapor of the colony. The bird flutters, blinds itself against the greasy ribs, and falls into the carpet.

It does not touch the floor. The kivva do not eat meat—their mouths are only bristles for mountain-dust and airborne mites—but the nits do. Before the bird has cooled to the temperature of the cleft, its feathers are being methodically clipped away by thousands of serrated claws, harvested to line the deeper breeding-chambers between the rocks, while its flesh is dissolved into the general suet of the wall.

And deeper still, behind the bodies, in the microscopic joints where the fault-line continues its millimeter-per-century grind into the heart of the horn, are the things left by the hunters.

They are not alive, but they host life. The dried elk-horn wedges, hammered into the stone when the cleft was open to the sky, have absorbed the ammonia and grease for hundreds of years. From their porous cores grows a lichen found nowhere else on the globe: *Caloplaca cadaveris*. It does not produce spores; it spreads by sending brittle, blood-red hyphae through the grease-films from one wedge to the next, following the old safety lines left by men who have been dust for ten dynasties. 

In the total dark of the fissure, the lichen produces a faint, cold bioluminescence—a dull, bruised yellow that does not travel three inches through the dense, hanging mist of suet. Nothing uses the light. The kivva have no eyes; the nits have no eyes; the worms are blind as the stone they crawl upon. The glow illuminates only the wet, segmented sides of the middle tier, where the ribs rise and fall together in that monstrous, thirty-second gasp, lifting sixty tons of greasy meat an inch away from the rock, and then settling it back against the cold mountain with a soft, collective sigh that sounds like the falling of a wave on mud.
