Where an ear cannot survive, the body becomes a receiver of lower octaves.

The few things that live in the open across the ninety-pace flagstones do not run; running requires an airborne balance that the acoustic displacement makes sickening. Instead, they adhere.

Between the accumulator pits and the spray aprons move the *plinth-leeches*, though they are not vermin in the common sense—they are lungless, flattened salamandrids the width of a shovel-blade, slate-colored with bellies stained a greasy, tufa-orange from decades of sliding over iron-lime sediments. Their ribs do not expand. They take what oxygen they need through an external vascular mantle that lies against their backs like wet flannel, kept continuously drenched by the descending mist. They have no eyes to speak of—only paired depressions filled with a cloudy, gelatinous oil that registers the shift between the flat silk of the sky and the dark iron of a passing walking-beam.

They live upon the tallow-scum that gathers in the gutters and the dead crickets that fall from the trusses when the steam pulses spike. Because their flesh is dense and cold, they are nearly invisible to the counterweights; occasionally, a laminated spruce beam descends upon one at the bottom of its five-minute arc, pressing it into the soft sediment of the drain-lip with thousands of tons of slow leverage. The salamander does not burst. Its tissues are fibrous, full of a tough, oily mucus; it yields like lead shot, and when the beam groans upward five minutes later, it remains pressed flat into the stone, its pale mouth still working the warm water for dissolved grease, slowly peeling its flanks from the flags over the course of three days.

Farther back, where the redoubts form dry alleys behind the condenser walls, the population is human, or what the basin permits a human lineage to become after four generations of uninterrupted saturation.

They call themselves the *Trunnion-Tenders*, though they have long forgotten how to loosen a wedge or re-pack an accumulator gland. They do not repair the engines; they are simply the engines’ commensals, living like mites in the folds of an elephant’s hide.

They are small-stature people, barrel-chested to an unnatural degree—not from muscle, but from the immense expansion of the rib cage required to keep the lungs inflated against the continuous hydrostatic pulse of the air. Their clavicles are thick and bowed, like dry drift-oak, and their skin has the bleached, puckered texture of an iron-washer’s thumb, permanently water-logged, smelling faintly of the tallow-vats and the sweet, mineral rot of the liverwort mats.

They have no language of vowels. In the basin, the human voice does not carry thirty inches before it is shredded by the iron-heavy thrumming of the flumes. Instead, they communicate by strike and tooth. They carry short rods of bell-bronze, six inches long, with which they tap the flanges of the cold pipes, sending sharp, metallic pings through the frame of the works to be picked up miles away by family groups leaning their foreheads against the cast iron.

When they must speak face-to-face, they touch. A mother speaks to her child by pressing her lower jaw firmly against the child’s shoulder blade and humming into the bone. The teeth act as bridges; the skull takes the vibration directly from the marrow. It is an intimate, brutal dialect: a vocabulary of jaw-pressures, clicks of the molars, and long, low throat-rumbles that travel through the skeleton like grease through a housing.

Their domesticity is tucked into the dry pockets behind the condenser stacks—cavities eight feet high where the draft of the cooling towers pulls the mist away, creating warm, lime-encrusted caves of stagnant air. Here, they hang their hammocks from the heavy iron stays. The hammocks are woven from the split fibers of water-logged liverwort and the hair of their own dead, which they save religiously, as organic fiber that does not rot in the damp is precious beyond salt.

They eat very little that has ever seen the sun. Their staple is the *pale tongue*, a species of shelf-fungus that grows inside the dead intake pipes where the boiling head has leaked through rotted wood-plugs. It is gray-white, leathery, and tastes of sulfurous tallow, but when shredded into the iron-water and boiled with handfuls of the blind crayfish, it keeps their teeth from dropping out of their spongy gums.

They have a religion, though it has no gods, only clearances.

They believe that the world below the rim—the plains they can see sometimes when the mist thins, flat and blue as a slate floor three thousand meters down—is an unfinished basement that has not yet been filled. They believe the engines are not draining a swamp, but are slowly, with infinite rectitude, laundering the weight of the sky.

Once every generation, when a walking beam’s accumulator leather finally rots through and the stroke slips—falling two inches faster than it should with an impact that shakes the basalt knees of the mountain—an old man or an old woman will climb into the open bronze drum of the delivery manifold. They do not do this as a sacrifice; they do it as packing. They wedge themselves between the worn phosphor bronze and the oak casing, their water-bloated bodies wrapped in tallow-soaked liverwort, to help the cylinder hold its head for another sixty years.

By night, if night can be said to exist beneath that pale, unmoving wool of the high sky, they gather on the catwalks above the great aprons. They sit in rows, their heels hooked over the lower rails, their broad, damp faces turned down toward the foaming sheets of orange runoff. They do not look at each other. They hold each other by the elbows, bone against bone, letting the five-minute heave of the walking beams lift their shoulders and drop them back down, their lungs filling and emptying in time with the accumulator's breath, unable to conceive of an emptiness that does not roar.
