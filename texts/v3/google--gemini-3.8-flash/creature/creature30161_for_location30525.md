At the bottom of the work, where the tallow-cakes are pressed into the mortises, there is an entire ecology that tastes only of animal fat and lime.

The silverfish are the base of it. They do not run like the dry, papery insects of cupboards; they are the length of an adze-haft, clad in overlapping shingles of soft, lead-colored horn that flex with the wetness of the timber. Their legs are short, thick as garlic cloves, tipped with curved hooks of clear amber that allow them to scuttle along the vertical sides of joists without dropping into the draft. They graze the tallow until the fat-seams look like cheese that has been grooved by mice. When a timber cracks under the settling load—that sharp, dry detonation that shivers the floor—they do not scatter; they flatten, pressing their belly-plates against the wood to drink the shock into their meat, then return to their slow, blind scouring.

Following them are the *slicks*, the pale ferrets. They are not pets, nor are they wild; they are tools that have been bred until their spines are like wet ropes. A carpenter keeps two or three in a woven reed kit slung from his groin-strap. Because their eyes are only small, pink pits buried under folds of lard-like skin, they navigate by the draft that blows upward through the seams. They know the geography of the joins better than the men who drive the pins. When a slick is released into a hole where a peg has rotted or split, it disappears into the lower ash-lattice with a sound like wet leather being drawn through an auger-hole. Minutes later, there is a thrashing in the under-joists—a sound muffled by three layers of timber—and the beast crawls back out, its whiskers stiff with cold grease, carrying a silverfish by its neck-plates. The carpenters do not stroke them; they wipe their muzzles with a scrap of burlap, break the slick’s portion off the insect’s tail, and put both back into the dark of the basket.

The people themselves have become shaped by the floor as thoroughly as the ash balks are shaped by the adze.

They are short, thick-chested, and long-waisted, with an extraordinary development of the lower rib cage and the heels. Their feet do not have high arches; they are flat, broad spades of horn, yellowed by contact with the wet river-pitch and scarred by the razor-edges of freshly split oak. They have learned to walk with a peculiar, shuffling roll that never lifts the sole more than a thumb’s width from the terra-cotta. To lift a foot high is to invite a fall, and to fall here is to risk sliding between the unpegged stringers into the draft below.

Because the din of the drop-rams is continuous—a rhythm so vast it feels like an internal pulse rather than a sound—the people are stone-deaf to the air. The small bones of their ears have fused or thickened against the concussion. Yet they are exquisitely sensitive to the timber. They hear through their shins and their teeth. A master joiner does not look at a beam to see if it is true; he sits upon it, pulls his knees to his chin, and presses his brow against the grain. Through the bone of his skull, he can feel the groan of a mortise two hundred yards off that has begun to bind, or the subtle, wet give of a floor-course that has been laid over an uncured spruce strut.

When they speak to one another, it is not with the tongue. For close business, two workers will lean together until their foreheads touch, the chalk on their brows mingling, and hum into each other’s face-bones. The vibrations travel through the skull-seams: a low, grunting rattle that means *pin here*, or a thin, whistling vibration through the teeth that means the timber below them is green and twisting. For distance, there is only the ash-mallet on the framing. A man working at the western perimeter can rap a six-stroke pattern on a girder and know that five miles to the south, another man sitting in the dark over an empty bay has caught the pulse in the soles of his feet, laid down his auger, and reached for the pitch-bucket.

They sleep where the timber is densest, in what they call the *sags*.

These are the places where the weight of five or six tiers of older floors has pressed down upon the posts below, bowing them into shallow, cradle-like depressions. In these troughs, out of the way of the runners who carry the hot tallow and the mortar-hods, the families sling low platforms of woven willow withies between the king-posts.

There are no beds, only greasy bundles of sheep-shorings and old felt wedges that have served their time as shim-stock. They sleep in shifts that match the drop of the counterweights: six hours while the rams are hauled up by the tread-gangs, two hours of stillness while the cables are greased, then the waking shock of the first drop that sets the willow cradles dancing.

Their women give birth sitting over the gaps between the joists, held up under the armpits by two elders while a third waits below on a temporary staging of ash poles to catch the infant before the draft chills it. The children are chalked before they are washed; their first taste is often a smear of cold tallow taken from the mother’s thumb to coat their throats against the lime-dust. By the time a child can crawl, its palms are hard enough to bear the splintered face of green fir. They are tied to the posts with three fathoms of braided horsehair; if a child rolls over the edge of an unfinished course, it hangs there, kicking quietly in the blue-gray air like a spider from a rafter, until someone has an arm free to haul it back up by the cord.

Beneath the working tier—down in the "deads" where the floor was finished three or four seasons back—there is another sort of life, though the carpenters do not speak of it often.

There are those who have slipped through. Not every fall kills; the ash framing is dense, five tiers deep, and a man may tumble through forty feet of struts and rope-lacings, breaking his collarbone or his ribs, to land upon an older, darker floor that has already been roofed over by the new work.

Up above, they do not drop ropes for the fallen. The pace of the hammer-crews cannot be broken for a man with a cracked hip. The fallen remain down there, in the cold, windowless space where the lime has finished sweating and the air is dry, sour, and dead.

Over years, these survivors—the *Gleaners* or the *Under-men*—have made a different kind of existence in the dark tiers. They are thinner, longer-limbed, with skin the color of lard that has been kept in a crock. They move on all fours along the lower joists, their toes prehensile from gripping the split poles. They live on what falls through the seams: the dropped gouges, the scrapings of tallow that work down through the tile-joints, the bits of salt-pork dropped by the tread-crews, and the silverfish that grow too fat to turn around in the mortises.

The Upper-folk know they are there by the sound of their knives. Sometimes, when a crew is boring an auger-hole through a twelve-inch timber, the bit will suddenly spin free into an under-chamber, and from below will come three quick, metallic taps—a knife-blade striking the iron of the tool. It is a sign: *we are beneath you; do not drop the ram here.*

Occasionally, an Under-man will die, and his fellows will wedge his body into a vacant strut-pocket, packing him tight with dry reed-bundles and horsehair so that his drying flesh becomes part of the permanent shim of the world. In this place, nothing is buried in dirt, because there is no dirt; a man simply becomes four inches of packing between an oak joist and a pine header, helping to hold up the children who are walking fifty feet above his head, driving their mallet-pins into the floor that will cover him forever.
