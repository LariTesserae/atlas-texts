To understand what lives in the basin, one must first understand that to live here is to be completely and permanently deaf, or to have never possessed hearing in the first place. The machine's twelve strokes a minute do not simply fill the air; they displace it, hammering through water, wood, and flesh with the blunt mass of an hydraulic ram. Anything with a delicate tympanum would have it driven in within an hour. Consequently, the hollow belongs exclusively to the insensate, the heavily armored, and the thick-boned.

Along the floor, the carabus beetles do not crawl so much as they are carried forward by the twitching rhythm of the timber. Their bodies are broad, flattened discs the color of wet slag, heavy enough to click audibly against the grain if there were any silence to hear it in. They have lived in the shadow of the walking-beam for so many generations that their carapaces have taken on the precise, dull luster of the cast-iron frames. They feed not only on the tallow that drips from the crank journals, but on each other, and on the steady rain of dead swifts that tumble from the eaves sixty feet above. Where a bird hits the root-plank, it does not linger; within twenty minutes it is covered by a mounded, shifting crust of black elytra. They strip the feathers down to the quills, scrape the skin from the breastbone, and leave only a flat, grey stain that the damp timber absorbs into its pores before the afternoon is out.

Down in the mulched pit where the lower third of the twenty-foot flywheel disappears, the friction of the iron through the wet wood-meal generates a sour, continuous heat. Here, protected from the cold daylight by the massive overhang of the counterweights, lives a pale, unsegmented worm—thick as a wrist and nearly two feet long. They exist in thousands within the mulch, blind and translucent, grazing on the iron-filings and carbon dust shaved away by the turning axle. When the flywheel dips and rises, it drags these worms up on its face; some are sheared clean by the clearance of the trough, while others simply cling to the greased rim, riding up into the flat light and down into the mulch again, hour after hour, seemingly indifferent to the speed or the cold.

Higher up, where the timber walls rise toward the overcast square of sky, the swifts dominate the vertical planes. They are not true swifts of the open air, but a degenerate, heavy-shouldered variant that rarely leaves the basin. Their plumage is saturated with wood-tannin and coal-tar until it is water-repellent, stiff, and greasy to the touch. They build their nests directly in the compression cracks where two buttress roots have fused under immense tonnage. Because the vibration of the engine would shake loose any standard clay or mud, their mortar is compounded of regurgitated beetle grease, shredded heartwood fibers, and a thick, curd-like saliva that sets as hard as amber. In the dim pockets behind these nests, where the light never reaches, small, blind pseudoscorpions—pale amber and flat as parchment—hunt the mites that infest the birds' wings.

Yet the floor is not solely the domain of the beetles. There are people here, though "people" is a word that sits awkwardly on them.

There are perhaps seven or eight of them, living in the deep horizontal runnels and clefts where the roots swell out from the base of the walls. They are squat, barrel-chested, and move with a wide, crab-like gait designed to keep their centers of gravity between the slick furrows of the timber. They go entirely barefoot; the skin on their soles is not soft or pale from the damp, but cracked into thick, yellow pads like vulcanized rubber, cross-hatched with deep treads that find grip on the wet saddle-leather surface where a shod foot would instantly slip.

They wear garments made of cured carabus hides, stitched together with frayed lead-wire taken from the intake pipe wrappings, the small, iridescent black plates overlapping like shingles down their thighs to turn the constant condensation. They have no language of the throat. When they communicate, they do so through the floor: one will drop hard onto his heels in a specific cadence, sending a distinct, sharp shudder through the grain that travels thirty yards through the root-mass to be felt in the knees and teeth of another. 

Their entire existence is synchronized with the engine. They do not fight its cadence; they inhabit it. When they walk across the sixty-foot clearing, they do so in a lurching, deliberate meter—taking three rapid strides during the upward swing of the walking-beam, then locking their joints and dropping their weight into the grain during the downstroke, absorbing the concussive *tonk* through bent knees so that it does not shake their organs loose. If an outsider were to watch them from the rim above, they would appear to move like figures in an erratic clockwork, freezing and surging in absolute sympathy with the iron.

These dwellers are not mechanics. They possess no wrenches, no spare parts, no understanding of the head of water that turns the hidden turbines behind the wall. They are attendants in the way that remoras are attendants to a shark. Their primary work is scraping. With chisels fashioned from the rusted stays of ancient, vanished timber-cribbing, they work along the great bronze journal boxes, clearing away the buildup of oxidized tallow and dead beetles that would otherwise pack solid and overheat the bearings. They collect the rendered grease in bladders made of swift-skin, knead it with crushed tannin-bark to cut the grit, and smear it back onto the guides.

They do not leave the basin. To them, the rim sixty feet above—where the overcast daylight breaks flat and cold—is merely an edge where the wood ends and the useless, silent emptiness begins. Down here, the air is solid; it has weight, sound, and a familiar, unvarying temperature that never chills the blood entirely nor ever lets it warm. 

When one of them dies, there is no earth to dig. The floor will not receive a spade; it is harder than white oak, harder than lignum vitae, sealed like an iron deck. The survivors carry the body to the western curve, where the great intake pipe enters the root-wall. There, in the narrow, turbulent sump beneath the intake grate where the water rushes in black and furious from the unseen dark, they wedge the corpse beneath the hemp-wrapped lead. The cold current strips it; the carabus beetles find what remains; and within three strokes of the great beam, the sound has beaten the memory of it into the floor, leaving the basin clear, wet, and perfectly occupied.
