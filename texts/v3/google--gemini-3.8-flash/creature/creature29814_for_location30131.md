To understand the life here, one must first discard the concept of a face. 

There are heads, certainly, but they are not centers of expression or gaze; they are spade-shaped battering rams, wedges for prying wet lichen off iron, or swollen acoustic arrays bulbous with fluid-filled chambers. Where an eye would be, there is only a continuous sweep of thick, pebbled hide or the pink, puckered opening of an ear-pit that drinks in the shuddering thrum of the dynamos.

Consider the largest of them—the ones that congregate along the footings of the hydraulic siphons. The skin of these creatures does not shed water; it drinks it, swelling until their flanks are soft and heavy as soaked peat. They have no necks to speak of. A great, sloped carapace of fibrous bone runs from between their shoulder-clusters down to their wide, flat haunches, ribbed like the hull of a scuttled barge. Beneath this shell, their organs are suspended in heavy, viscous oil that cushions them against the violent ground-shocks of the pump strokes. 

They do not sleep lying down. To sleep, they wedge themselves between the cooling ribs of the iron casings, wedging their blunt noses against the weeping rust where the condensation runs hottest. Their mouths are low, broad slits lined not with teeth, but with revolving bands of rough, horn-plated cartilage that turn like millstones, grinding away the sulfurous sludge and the thick crust of iron-reducing bacteria that coats the steel. They are nearly motionless for days, their digestive tracts humming with a low, chemical heat as they process the metallic paste. When one finally moves, the sound of its departure is the tearing of wet hide from wet metal—a slow, sucking peel.

Their communication is tectonic. A mature bull possess a sternal keel as dense as pig iron, suspended by thick, corded ligaments just millimeters above the rock. When it strikes the stone, it does not make a sound that travels through the air; it sends a sharp, traveling wave through the basalt shelf. Three miles away, standing in the cold mire of a peat-pan, another beast receives the pulse through the soft, oil-filled pads of its feet, the vibration traveling up the dense marrow of its leg bones directly into its inner ear. They talk of shelf-cracks, of water-weight, of the rhythmic cadence of an engine that has begun to slip its timing.

Above them, where the cables sag under their pelts of iron-moss, the air is occupied by things that have no need of earth. 

They are wide, paper-thin, and cold to the touch. Their bodies are essentially single, muscular sheets of greased cartilage, four or five meters across, ribbed with flexible struts of bone that resemble the ribs of an umbrella. They do not flap; the air is so heavy, so laden with warm vapor, that they swim through it as if through an estuary. Along their leading edges runs a ribbon of dense electro-receptive pores, glowing internally with a faint, cold twitch of nerve-fire that responds to the magnetic halos of the high-voltage lines.

These gliders live along the power corridors as if along invisible rivers. They drift blind down the invisible corridors of force that link pylon to pylon, their underside fins trailing in the ionized mist. They feed upon the colonies of pipe-leeches and armored gastropods that crawl along the aerial conduits. When a glider finds a cluster, it stalls in mid-air, wrapping its entire wet, velvet mantle around the iron pipe like a warm shroud. Its mouthpart—a barbed, muscular siphon—works its way beneath the shells of the mollusks, dissolving their foot-pads with warm alkaline bile and vacuuming the meat clean from the iron, leaving behind only the empty, corroded husks still stuck to the rust.

Down in the flumes, where the water cuts through the basalt in smooth, foaming gutters, life is even more stripped of elegance. 

In these black sluices live the scythe-tails: long, pale organisms, blind as peeled tubers, with skin so transparent that the rhythmic churning of their iron-stained guts is visible against the black water. They are entirely sinew and gristle, wedging themselves into the bore-holes of ancient intake pipes where the water pressure would crush a man’s chest. They face into the torrent, their jaws expanded into vast, sieve-like strainers made of modified gill-rakers, collecting the organic detritus that peels from the hanging gardens above—bits of rotting lichen, dead slug-flesh, and flecks of engine grease. They have developed an intense tolerance for industrial hydrocarbons; their livers are enormous, fatty reservoirs of petroleum-sludge that they convert into metabolic heat, keeping their blood well above the temperature of the cold flume-water.

When an exhaust valve blows, the dynamic changes entirely.

The scream of the steam is an acoustic absolute; it obliterates the subtle thumps of the herd and the low hum of the cables. To an ear that can hear a worm turning in the peat, the blast should be agony, but instead it acts like a hormonal trigger. From the deep fissures in the stone, from the tangled under-mats of the calcified ferns, the vent-ticks come spilling out.

They are not insects, though they move with that many-legged, chittering haste. They are heavy, soft-bodied things the size of hogs, armored only in overlapping shingles of flexible horn that rattle like wet shingles as they run. They have no lungs; they breathe through lateral spiracles packed with vascular wool that drinks the superheated vapor directly into their blood. They pile atop the vent-grate in a writhing, heaving pyramid, squealing through their spiracles with high, piping whistles, biting and shoving one another to get closer to the pipe's scald. In that white, roaring column of steam, they shed their outer skins. The old, rot-softened horn splits down the spine, peeling back to reveal fresh, white plates that harden within seconds in the mineral-rich spray, cured like boiled leather by the heat.

Nothing here dies easily. There are no scavengers that wait for rot; the humidity is too high, the bacterial breakdown too ravenous. If an animal weakens—if its sternum cracks against a loose stone or its electro-receptors are scorched by an arc from a sagging cable—it is dismantled while still twitching. 

The things that do this are small, flat, and ubiquitous. They live by the millions in the peat-pans: pale, ribbon-like worms that smell of wet copper and old tallow. They follow the vibrations of distress. When a grazer falters, stumbling as its leg-pads lose their grip on the slime-covered rock, these ribbons emerge from the mud like grease rising on water. They do not have mouths that bite; they secrete an enzyme that turns living flesh into an emulsion, a gray, protein-rich broth that they drink through their skin. Within an hour of a six-ton beast falling, its ribs are picked clean, white and smooth as polished marble, arching up out of the peat like the rafters of an abandoned chapel.

Within a week, the iron-moss has found the bone. Within a month, the roots of the sulfur-ferns have drilled through the marrow, cementing the skeleton to the basalt forever, turning the corpse into just another terrace for the water to fall over, another anchor for the blind, wet world that eats the work of the engines.
