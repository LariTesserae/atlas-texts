To understand what lives in the cistern, one has to begin with the sound, because the creatures themselves have no visual memory of their ancestors or their home. Their world is entirely acoustic and tactile, a cartography of wet, ringing planes. 

When the river-crawlers lie against the baseboards, their pale flanks flatten against the tile until they look less like four-legged beasts and more like split bladders of lard or kneaded dough. Their ribs are wide, flexible struts of cartilage, designed to collapse so that an adult—measuring six feet from snout to the tip of its rudder-like tail—can press its belly flush against the curve of the terracotta. This is not merely for rest; it is an act of hearing. Through the sternum and the flat plates of the pelvic girdle, they read the cistern. They can feel the microscopic drag of a beetle’s legs three hundred feet away across the film of water. They know the weight of an incoming storm above-ground hours before the first pipe begins to spout, sensing the subtle barometric swelling of the air through the high vents, which alters the frequency at which the room hums.

Their skin is entirely devoid of pigment, but it is not smooth. Close to, it has the texture of wet tripe—fine, pale papillae that rise and fall with the movement of their lymphatic fluid. Because there is no mud to bury in, no weed to hide among, their defense is sheer, slippery continuity. They produce an alkaline, non-coagulating mucus that sloughs off continuously into the sheet-flow, washing down toward the bronze drain. This grease coats the tiles in an invisible, microscopic veneer, keeping the earthenware from grinding their bellies to raw meat during their lunges. 

When they breed, they do not lay gelatinous clusters in deep water. In two inches of current, loose eggs would simply be sucked into the bronze throat at the center. Instead, the females produce wide, leathery sheets of eggs—each disk the diameter of a millstone—which they plaster directly to the ceiling tiles with an adhesive secreted from their cloacas. They achieve this by climbing the walls, wedging their spade-toed limbs into the tongue-and-groove seams that have opened slightly with the settling of the vault. For weeks, the high curve of the roof is hung with these pale, trembling blisters, each containing dozens of dark commas suspended in clear jelly. The water that condenses on the ceiling washes over them, keeping them oxygenated.

When the young hatch, they drop. 

They hit the shallow water with the sound of thrown grease, already three inches long, fully formed, and possessing the milk-white skin and wide, mouth-slit of their parents. The adults make no distinction between a drowned field vole washed down a pipe and their own fallen spawn; the race across the tiles begins the instant the water breaks. Only those young that manage to land near the lip of the room, scurrying instantly into the damp shadow behind their parents’ resting flanks, survive the first minutes of life. There, crowded into the seam where the floor curves up to the brick, they browse on the pale, rubbery fungus and the shed skins of their elders until their horn-pads harden.

Above them, the scissors-tailed swifts occupy an entirely separate strata of this artificial cave, yet their lives are mechanically linked to the floor.

These are not the sleek, slate-grey birds of the river valleys. Over generations in the dark, their plumage has bleached to a dull, chalky ash, though their eyes remain large, black, and watery, capable of gathering the thinnest dregs of tallow light from the shafts. They do not migrate. They have no concept of season beyond the rhythm of the floods. They nest in the horizontal fissures where the vault springs from the wall, building their shallow cups out of shed crawler-mucus, dead midges, and the tough, fibrous rootlets that occasionally push through the joints from trees rooted hundreds of feet above in the sunlit soil.

Because their wings are long and their legs are mere vestigial hooks, a swift that touches the floor is dead; its flight feathers become soaked in the mucus-heavy sheet of water, and it is dragged into the nearest crawler’s maw before it can beat its way back into the air. Thus, they fly in perpetual, scything circuits beneath the vault. Their flight is noiseless save for a dry, paper-like flick of wingtips when they bank against the curve of the ceiling. 

They feed on what emerges from the bronze grate. 

For the drain is not an exit only; it is a mouth that leads down into a flooded network of lower siphons, drowned settling-troughs, and horizontal channels that crawl beneath the foundations of the ancient world. Out of that choked plumbing rise the midges—blind, translucent gnats with bodies like threads of spun sugar. They hatch in the millions from the turquoise fur that encrusts the bronze slats, rising through the drain-throat in thick, smoky funnels that twist toward the ceiling vents. The swifts carve through these columns with their beaks gaping, their small, dry calls—a metallic *tink-tink-tink* like needles dropped on glass—lost entirely beneath the heavy, churning thunder of the water drops.

And in the silence between the falling torrents, when the rains above have ceased and the water on the floor thins to a skin no deeper than a leaf, another layer of the room reveals itself.

From the seams between the floor tiles, where the lime mortar was dissolved centuries ago, emerge the flat-worms. They are broad, black, and completely flat—no thicker than wet parchment. They slide over the wet terracotta like gliding shadows, their margins undulating in hypnotic, oily waves. They do not enter the hunt; they are the cleaners of the stone. They graze on the grease left behind by the crawlers, the chalky droppings of the swifts that have dissolved in the film, and the pale fungus that creeps along the edges. Behind them, the dark red earthenware is left naked and raw, scrubbed down to the gritty brick, so that when the crawlers run, their horn-pads bite into the clean, baked clay with a dry, sharp screech that echoes five times over the length of the hall.

Nothing here dies of old age in a state of rest. 

When an old crawler’s heart slows and its whiskers lose their spring, its belly no longer grips the tile with the same hydraulic suction. It begins to drift. The slow, perpetual sheet-flow—that endless two-inch river moving inward from the perimeter—begins to nudge it toward the center. The animal does not fight. It lies with its legs trailing, its pale chin resting on the wet glaze, sliding an inch every dozen heartbeats down the gentle concave incline. 

Its fellows do not attack it while it still draws the air-hiss into its wide lungs. They simply let it pass. It slips toward the bronze disk, where the water gathers its speed, swirling into the turquoise slats with that deep, rattling gurgle. There, the current wedges the dying crawler against the grate. Its white flanks plug several of the slots; the water backs up slightly around its ribs, rising to its nostrils, until with a soft, rubbery lurch, the current folds the soft cartilage of its body, and the suction pulls it piece by piece through the corroded bronze into the drowned labyrinth below.

The slots clear with a loud, sucking gasp. The water lowers again to the height of an ankle. A single drop falls from the vault sixty feet above, hitting the empty metal with a sound like a copper bell, and thirty yards away, a white head turns, its whiskers shivering in the dark, waiting for what the sky will drop next.
