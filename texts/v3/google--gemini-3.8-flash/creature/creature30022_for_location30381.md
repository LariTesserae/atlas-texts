The inhabitants of the fins do not sleep lying flat; to lie horizontal across a stone shelf here is to risk one’s heels being dragged into an updraft while the back of the skull is pressed down with the weight of an anvil. They sleep upright or wedge-folded, their ribs bound tightly to the interior slate lining of their boxes with bands of tallow-rubbed hide, their foreheads pressed against the damp rock to keep their necks from snapping if the plumb-line turns in the dark.

They are small, broad-shouldered people whose joints have thickened like the burrs on pruned willow. Because the light is lilac-white and casts no shade, they have no eyebrows; the brow-ridge has flattened out, and their lids are swollen into thick, hairless folds of pinkish gristle that overlap the corners of the eye to strain out the burning caustics. They see the plateau not as a map of surfaces, but as a shifting weave of tension. Their gaze is never level. They look diagonally, squinting along the seams where the falling sheets of water shear past the climbing ones, watching the trembling spheres of moisture to see which way the air is leaning.

They do not speak with the mouth open. To open the mouth wide in this altitude is to invite the wet, pressurized air to flood the lungs like a siphon. Instead, they communicate in glottal clicks, palate-snaps, and deep, pectoral hums that match the acoustic drone of the hollow limestone fins. A man tucked into a cleft fifty feet above can feel the rumble of a neighbor’s chest through the wet stone before he hears it through the whistle of the wind.

Their clothes are not woven; there is no flax, no fleece, no nettle. Everything they wear is salvaged, re-tanned, and mineralized. It consists of layers of cured pericardium, split belly-gut, and dense hides that have been steeped in the gypsum pools until they are as stiff as dried horn. These garments do not drape; they are articulated cuirasses, laced together with greased thongs and counter-weighted with pierced slates sewn directly into the hems. A traveler moving between fins must constantly change these ballast stones, shifting a three-pound slate from the left hip to the right shoulder as he steps from an eastward-draining face into an ascending braid of weight. If he misjudges the seam, his own limbs will fight him—the lead leg pulled toward the zenith while the trailing heel is anchored to the abyss.

They live on the water itself and the tallow-crust that grows upon the gypsum. 

The upward-falling water is not the same as the water that runs into the deeps. The climbing water is pale, sweet-tasting, and sharp as lye; it leaves a white, chalky silt on the teeth that slowly fossilizes the mouth. The downward water is bitter, thick with dissolved iron and bitter salts, greasy to the tongue. They drink the climber and use the faller to cure their gut. Along the margins where the two currents meet—where the water hesitates, suspended in mid-air in vibrating sheets—there forms a curdled, gray-white scum, neither plant nor mold, but a colony of mineral-fixing slimes that feed on the friction of the opposing gravities. The dwellers scrape this grease from the limestone gills with curved bone blades, pressing it into cakes that dry in the fierce light until they resemble chipped suet. It tastes of river silt and battery-wash, but it keeps their blood thick enough to resist the vacuum of the high passes.

Between the shelters, along the sinew lines that span the fifty-foot gulfs, things move that are neither human nor machine. 

They are the *tether-crawlers*—small, six-legged assemblages made of boiled rib-bones and slate weights, lashed together with twisted sinew that contracts and relaxes as the humidity shifts. The dwellers do not build them so much as set them in tension and release them onto the lines. When a gravity braid shifts, the change in pull alters the balance of the bone limbs, causing the crawler to hitch its way clumsily along the suspended gut, carrying a pierced pot or a packet of grease from one stone coffin to the next. They click as they move, like crabs made of mutton-bones, their tiny slate feet tapping against the wet limestone as they cross the chasms, shivering in the violent lilac glare.

Then there are those who have spent too long in the duplicate places.

When the landscape slips—when the spires double themselves with that concussive, wet *thwack*—the people who are caught in the overlap are not torn apart. They are diluted. There are perhaps three or four such beings living in the deep defiles, individuals who were standing on a fin when it became two fins and then one again. They are wider than they should be; their outlines are blurred by a persistent, chalky vibration, as though two identical bodies were occupying the same skin an eighth of an inch apart. They do not use the sinew lines. They can reach across gaps that are wider than a man’s leap, their fingers finding purchase on handholds that exist only every few seconds. When they move, the light through them splits into those flat, non-prismatic pinks and dull ochres, staining the spray around them. They do not answer the pectoral hums of the box-dwellers; their breath makes a wet, double-whistle, like two clay pipes blown slightly out of tune.

At dusk—or what passes for dusk, when the lilac-white sky hardens into an opaque, frozen violet that burns without warmth—the dwellers retreat into their boxes. They pull the slate lids shut by counter-weighted cords, wedging themselves into the absolute dark of the stone seams. 

Inside the boxes, the world is reduced to two sounds: the catastrophic pipe-organ thrum of the mountain’s wet marrow vibrating through the limestone against their spines, and the high, thin chiming of the little clay pots outside, swinging on their lines, whistling as they empty, telling the sleepers through the thick stone whether they are still right-side-up, or if the mountain has turned its face toward the void while they were closed away.
