To live here is to be an instrument of alignment. 

Because the grain runs true from north to south without deviation, movement in this world is polarized. To travel across the grain, east to west, is an ordeal of friction—a jarring, rhythmic stumbling over microscopic ridges of xylem, where every inch must be bought against the natural orientation of the floor. But to move north or south is to slip along an endless, polished trough. The things that walk here do not have feet in the sense of hooves or paws; they have skids, pads of compressed horn, or rows of hydraulic cilia that secrete their own boundary layer of clear mucus. They do not run; they plane.

The pale, turnip-fleshed crawlers are the slowest of these, but they are not the only ones. Beneath the causeways, where the air is eternally cooled by the spray of the falling curtains, there are long, ribbon-like beings—some forty cubits in length, matching the exact width of the heartwood bands. They are flattened like leeches, but their skin is the translucent, milky green of young pea-pods. They adhere to the underside of the flumes by absolute capillary action, their bellies spread so thin against the wood that you can see the grain continuing unbroken through their bodies. They do not possess guts or mouths; they are living filters, porous as sponges, through which the weeping sweat of the viaducts passes. They strain the water before it falls into the void, absorbing the faint traces of sugars and atmospheric dust that ride the high thermals, leaving the wood beneath them scrubbed to the purity of bone. When a viaduct shudders under a sudden pulse of pressure, a ripple passes through these ribbons from tail to snout—a coordinated, shivering wave that eases the strain on the joints above. They are less like inhabitants and more like living gaskets, keeping the wet timber from tearing its own seams apart under the force of the siphons.

Higher up, along the tension cables where the leather-thick leaves are moored, lives an entirely different lineage. 

They are bipedal, though their bodies are narrow as laths, seemingly pressed between two flat planes. Their limbs are unnaturally long, jointed with double-pivots that allow them to fold their legs flat against their ribs like carpenter’s rules. Their skin is dry, pale, and hairless, with the matte texture of peeled birch bark, and their eyes are protected by permanently drawn, amber-tinted nictitating membranes through which the sun-glare looks cool and brown. 

These are the Calibrators, though they have no name for themselves that can be spoken across the roaring air. The sound of the falling water and the pneumatic hiss of the towers is so total that vocal cords would be useless; their throats have narrowed to simple breathing slits, and they have no ears to speak of. Instead, their collarbones and shins are extraordinarily dense, fused directly to the cartilage of their joints, acting as acoustic receivers. When two of them meet, they do not gesture. They kneel, pressing the flat plates of their foreheads against the timber between them, and speak through their teeth—sending sharp, rhythmic percussions directly through the wood. A conversation between them sounds like two woodpeckers drumming on distant hollow trees, but within the floor itself, the vibration travels for leagues, pure and uncorrupted by the wind.

They carry no stone, no woven cloth, and no metal. Their tools are cut from the hardest, dark heartwood found at the centers of dead pylons—wedges, mallets, and narrow gouges polished with their own waxy saliva. Their work is perpetual, though it never looks like labor. They walk the causeways with a slow, drifting glide, their long toes curled into the micro-fissures of the grain. Every few miles, one will stop, crouch, and press an ear to a mortise joint. 

They are listening for the "thirst" of the wood.

If an expansion joint is dry, it rings with a high, glass-like chime that indicates the cambium is starving. If it is over-pressurized, it thrums with an oily, low-frequency shudder that threatens to split the tensile fibers. When they find an imbalance, they do not dig or chop. They insert slender pegs of dried vine into the seam. The vine drinks the bitter sap that wells between the timbers, swells to four times its dry diameter in seconds, and drives the vast, hundred-ton planks back into their micron-tight tolerances. When the work is done, the peg becomes part of the causeway; the living wood accepts it, knots around it, and smooths over the scar before the sun has moved three hand-spans.

Nothing dies here in the way that things die on the earth below. There is no putrefaction, because there are no bacteria to rot the meat; the tannin that saturates the wood and the water is an absolute antiseptic. When a Calibrator grows old, their tissues do not soften; they stiffen. Their skin turns from birch-white to the amber-yellow of cured resin. Their joints begin to lose their supple slip, catching against the grain. 

When they feel the lignification taking hold of their marrow, they do not wait to become immobile. They walk out along the narrowest suspension cables, out toward the solitary pylons that cradle nothing but the sky. There, in the high, dry wind where the mist cannot reach, they wedge themselves into the deep, vertical grooves of the pylon’s ribs. 

They press their palms and the soles of their feet into the living xylem and hold still. The cambium of the tower, always hungry for purchase, recognizes the starch in their blood. Within days, tender white tendrils no thicker than horsehairs prickle through their skin, weaving into their muscles, through their tendons, and down along the shafts of their bones. The process is not violent; it is a gradual, fluid incorporation. The pylon drinks them down, drawn by the same suction that pulls the clouds up from the white abyss below. 

Months later, you can look up into the ribs of the pylon and see their outlines—faint, human-shaped buttresses of clean, pale grain, perfectly flush with the timber, their long fingers transformed into structural splines that strengthen the cradle against the gale. 

Between these monuments, the great empty intervals remain. Sometimes, in the middle of a noon so bright that the horizon disappears into a haze of gold wood and white sky, a shadow will pass over the causeway. It is not a cloud. It is one of the siphon-skiffs—great, concave hulls grown from the skin of an aerial root, propelled by nothing more than the spray of an aqueduct striking its paddle-shaped stern. In it sits a solitary figure, motionless, holding a long steering-oar made of cured pith, sliding without a sound down a river that hangs unsupported in the sky, bound for an intake valve fifty miles away, where the floor has never yet been seen by an eye, but where the wood is calling for weight.
