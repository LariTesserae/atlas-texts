To speak of life here in terms of species or lineages is already to use tools shaped for a harsher world. In an ocean where nothing rots, where the water is thick with dissolved lime and starch, and where no winter ever arrives to kill the excess, living things do not crowd one another out. They simply persist, broadening their margins until their edges overlap, so that it becomes difficult to say where a creature ends and the sediment begins.

In the mid-depths, within the felted carpets of the maiden-moss, there are beings that are almost entirely intestine. 

They have the consistency of boiled marrow or cool lard, grayish-white and without skin, taking the shape of whatever fold of moss they happen to occupy. A traveler—if one could exist here without lungs crumpling under the alkaline warmth—might pass a hand through what appeared to be a cushion of pale weed, only to feel a thick, slow suction against the knuckles, like the mouth of a horse taking grain from a palm. These are the *velvers*. They have no eyes, nor organs of balance, for up and down have very little meaning within a mat that hangs between two miles of water. They possess only a systemic hunger that moves through them in ripples, digesting the dead vegetable snow as it settles through the weave. 

When a velver grows too heavy for the methane-buoyed moss to support, it does not drop like a stone. It slumps through the weave, inch by year, parting the fibers with a soft, oily slime that smells of scalded milk. As it descends into the dark under-meadows, its tissues tighten against the greater pressure; its tallow-colored mass clears, becoming amber, then tea-dark, until it reaches the granite floor. There, flattened against the schist whalebacks, it ceases to be an animal and becomes a skin. 

These skins cover thousands of square leagues of the stone floor. They are vast, living vellums, three fingers thick, that seal the rock from the water like sheets of lead. Through them, the planet’s warmth is filtered, sweetened, and discharged in millions of tiny, transparent blisters that rise like pearls. 

Between the vellum and the stone run the true scavengers of the deep: things that have never known the light of the bronze mist. They are long, segmented creatures, flattened like carpenter’s rules, constructed of pale horn and horn-like jointing. They have hundreds of paddle-shaped feet that do not swim, but rather stroke the slime beneath the vellum, moving them forward in a continuous, gliding click that can be felt through the soles of the feet miles away as a dry hum. They are the *scribers*. They do not eat the moss; they eat the lime that leaches from the granite, and as they graze, their mandibles leave tracks across the smooth stone—fine, interlocking zigzags and concentric spirals that never cross each other’s paths. Over millions of years, they have engraved the bedrock of the entire plateau into a shallow, microscopic relief, a library of hunger written in stone that no eye will ever read.

Higher up, where the ribbon-bark trunks rise toward the light, the life changes from grease and horn to something like wax and glass.

Here drift the beings that the scroll-like creatures merely prefigure. They are the *plies*: organisms whose bodies are composed of dozens of wide, leaf-thin sheets of flexible cartilage, bound together at a central spine like the pages of an uncut folio. A ply may be seventy cubits long, its leaves rippling in alternating rhythms that produce no wake, only a gentle displacement that sets the maiden-moss to swaying for an hour after it has passed. 

The plies are neither plants nor animals in any sense recognized below the meniscus. They have no mouths. Along the inner faces of their cartilaginous leaves are millions of microscopic, cilia-rimmed cups that host colonies of glowing green dinoflagellates. When the plies drift into the great columns of iron-black current that rise from the bottom, they open their pages like fans, allowing the heavy mineral water to wash between the sheets. When they drift back out into the amber sunlight, they clamp their pages shut with a sound like a wet leather book snapping closed, trapping the iron within their interiors where the light can cook it. 

They are the timers of this place. Because they rise and fall along the thermal columns on cycles that take forty to fifty days to complete, the other inhabitants adjust their own quiet metabolisms by the shadows the plies cast. When a school of them drifts beneath the bronze mist, the light below turns from gold-green to a bruised, grape-skin purple, and in that dimness, the maiden-moss relaxes its weave, dropping tons of trapped silt into the deeps like a sigh.

There are minds here, too, though they do not think in sequences. 

Near the great circular pavements—the ones laid down by hands that left no other bone or tool—the water is different. It is colder, sweeter, and tastes faintly of copper. In these depressions dwell the *weir-folk*, though to call them folk is to assume an outline they do not maintain. 

They are colonies of siphonophores that have occupied the stone drains. They do not drift; they have anchored their root-bells into the thumbs-wide channels cut into the ancient conglomerate, using the suction of the downward-breathing holes to hold themselves taut against the currents. Above each drain-hole, a weir-body rises like an inverted pine tree, sixty feet of braided, glass-clear polyps that end in small, violet-colored bells. 

These bells are sensitive to the micro-vibrations of the tableland. When a granite whaleback shifts an inch beneath its cushion of sediment, or when the great meniscus at the mountain’s lip trembles under the impact of a high desert wind miles below, the weir-bells ring. They do not ring with sound—the dense, alkaline water will not permit a chime—but with luminescence. A flash of turquoise passes up the trunk of the colony, through its branches, and out into the surrounding moss.

Other weirs, miles across the amber gloom, answer with pulses of cadmium or faint lemon-yellow. In this way, thoughts move across the sea floor: slow, distributed calculations regarding the weight of the water, the thickness of the silt, and the slow, inexorable tilting of the planet’s crust. It is not language; it is an ongoing adjustment of equilibrium, an arithmetic of pressure that has been conducted without pause since the mountains first gathered the water to their chests.

The weir-colonies remember the builders. They remember them not as gods or ancestors, but as a flavor that once washed through the drain-holes—a specific, bitter albumin that has not entered the water for twelve hundred centuries, but which the lower polyps still secrete in miniature, molecular rehearsals every time the sea’s meniscus bulges outward under the pull of the outer moons.

And at the very edge, where the sea meets the abyss—where the meniscus curves like the breast of a vast bottle, holding back thousands of feet of water from the empty sky—lives the simplest and most terrible thing in this place.

It is a single creature, or perhaps a single family of creatures that has never learned to divide. It lives inside the skin of the water itself.

To the naked eye, it looks like nothing more than an area where the meniscus is clearer than usual, where the light of the lower desert shines through without distortion. But within that clear band, only two feet beneath the surface of the curved water-wall, there is a net. It is composed of fine, glassy sinews, each as long as an ash-tree, woven into a geometric lace that mirrors the curvature of the world. It holds the water. It drinks the wind that beats against the exterior of the meniscus from the dead lands below, and it spits back a fine, cold vapor that smells of alkali and old stone.

It has no center. If one were to drift along the edge for a week, one would see the same diamond-patterned membrane running without a seam, trembling against the dryness of the void outside. It does not eat the moss, nor the plies, nor the snow of the lint. It feeds upon the gradient—the impossible difference between the wet, warm, milk-like heaven of the plateau and the dry, freezing terror of the air that surrounds it.

It knows nothing of the creatures that browse the whalebacks five leagues behind it. It knows only that it is stretched tight; that the mountain under it is heavy; and that if it were ever to relax its fibers for the space of a single pulse, the ocean would slip from the shoulder of the world like an unpinned mantle, and the silence would at last have room to break.
