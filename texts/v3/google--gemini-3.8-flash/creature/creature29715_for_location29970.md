To touch the black glass here is to risk being flayed by the pure geometry of the ground, and so nothing that lives in the cradle possesses soft pads or naked heels. 

The swimmers—the sleek, blind things coursing through the warm spillways—do not have the anatomy of beasts that evolved against mud or sand. Under their pearlescent plates, their bellies are lined with a thick, vulcanized gristle, dense as tire rubber and slick with a self-secreting lipid that smells faintly of petroleum and crushed coriander. They do not walk or crawl; they *skate*. When they bank hard against a forty-degree terrace fault, the contact is soundless, a hydroplaning slide lubricated by the endless sheets of lukewarm condensation. They have no eyes, nor any vestige of sockets beneath their shields, but their snouts are ringed with hundreds of microscopic, hair-like cilia made of magnetic iron. These cilia twitch in unison with the shivering conduit, reading the topography not by sight or echo, but by the subtle warping of the acoustic field against the obsidian walls. 

When a swimmer is speared by the ceiling flyers, its death is strangely tidy. Because the obsidian has no tooth to absorb liquid, the creature’s pale, alkaline blood does not soak in; it beads into perfect, quivering pearls on the glass, drawn together by a furious surface tension until the orange wire-turf—which is not vegetation at all, but a galvanic, colonial hydra—siphons it dry through capillary action, leaving behind only a dry, chalky scale that the next sheet of water washes off the edge of the world.

The flyers themselves, the soft-bodied terrors roosting in the force-matrix pavilions, are creatures of pure buoyancy and tension. Their smoke-colored wing membranes are not musculature; they are sheets of organized silicate protein that function like bimetallic strips. When cold drafts curl over the lip of the spur, the wings snap rigid, holding a gliding airfoil without expending an ounce of energy; when they plunge back into the maternal fug near the boiling conduit, the heat slackens the tissue in an instant, allowing them to tumble and fold through the steam-jets with chaotic, drunken agility. 

They do not make nests out of twigs or down, for there is none. Instead, they anchor their clutches directly to the hanging threads of solid light. Their eggs are oblong, soft-walled capsules of clear amber jelly, slung in clusters along the luminous cords like unstrung beads. As the light-threads hum at their glass-chiming pitch, the vibration incubates the embryos, churning the yolks in continuous, hypnotic vortexes until the young chew their way out with three pairs of serrated jaw-plates, dropping straight into the boiling flumes below to begin their life as swimmers. The two species are not truly distinct; they are the wet nymph and the parched imago of a single, closed-loop metabolizer that has never seen an insect, a tree, or the sun.

Yet between the thrashing of the water-skaters and the shrill rasp of the membrane-wings, there are slower, heavier presences that inhabit the three milky pavilions—things that do not belong to the scramble of the flumes.

They are the Tenders, though they build nothing and mend nothing that was not already intended by the mountain’s core. They are three or four times the mass of a man, pale and vaguely humanoid only in the sense that a candle stub half-melted in warm water is vaguely a pillar. Their flesh is dense, non-fibrous, and semi-translucent, resembling salted lard or compressed stearin, completely free of hair, pores, or fingernails. Because the razor-edges of the newborn stone would slice through ordinary tissue like paper through water, the soles of their feet and the palms of their broad, four-jointed hands are capped with solid pads of natural chalcedony—milky, blunt stones grown directly out of their skeletal frames, clacking with a heavy, hollow resonance against the glass as they move.

They do not eat the protein slough from the conduit, nor do they hunt the flyers. They appear to sustain themselves by leaning their chests against the shivering housing of the post-physical spire, absorbing the raw acoustic heat directly through their breastbones. When they do this, their pale interiors flush with a faint, bioluminescent gold, their simple, slit-like breathing vents sighing out plumes of vapor that smell purely of hot tallow and rain.

Their culture, if it can be called that, exists solely in the forty-second intervals between the refractive pulses. 

They spend the thirty-seven seconds of normal time in absolute, stoic torpor, standing inside the amber force-matrix of the pavilions, their heavy, chinless heads tilted downward as though listening to the iron humming through their stone-shod feet. But during the three seconds when the violet phosphorescence sweeps across the amphitheater—when the water turns to static beads, the flyers hang frozen like blown glass against the cloud, and the obsidian floor turns crystal-clear—the Tenders move with breathtaking, liquid speed.

In those three seconds of transparency, they drop to their knees. Their pale, chalcedony-tipped hands press flat against the slick, transparent stone, their faces pressed against the newborn glass to stare down into the mile-deep conduits churning beneath the spur. In that brief window, when the mountain’s intellect is laid bare in towering, pulsing columns of cold cobalt fire, their lard-like skin mirrors the patterns, ripples of dark and light flickering across their brows like rapid, silent speech. They read the descent of the forces; they monitor the extrusion of the next terrace; they commune with the deep engine that is slowly lifting this entire shelf toward the sky.

Then the index snaps back with its wet, flat slap. 

The Tenders rise slowly, their skin dimming to dull lard, their faces blank and featureless once more. They take two or three heavy, clicking paces across the pavilion, adjust their weight on the frictionless floor, and wait, blind and patient in the greenhouse fog, for the next forty seconds to expire.
