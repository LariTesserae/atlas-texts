To survive here, an organism cannot fight the mill; it must either become part of the machinery or slip into the microscopic slack between its teeth. 

Beneath the three-stone-deep carpet of rolling shingle lies the floor’s true interior—the interstitial world. Where the stones are pressed so tightly together by sixty atmospheres of brine that they lock, the turbulence ceases. In these sub-surface seams, the water does not shear; it merely oozes, thick with a fine, gray mineral paste ground from the quartz and chert above. 

This gray slurry is the base of everything. It is rich with the pulverized remains of pelagic wanderers—the cold-water jellies, pelagic salps, and deep-drifting carcasses that are caught in the downwelling cataracts and dragged into the benthic meat-grinder. Their organic matter is beaten into a nutritious silt that settles into the cracks, feeding the marrow of the stone field.

Here, in the cold mortar between the cobbles, live the **Tallow-worms**. They are thick, headless ribbons of blind, lard-white muscle, some no longer than a finger, others stretching three meters through the labyrinthine spaces between the stones. They have no skeleton, no chitin, and no hard plates—any rigid structure down here would be snapped like dry pine. Instead, their flesh has the consistency of cold paraffin or dense animal fat, utterly incompressible, sliding effortlessly through gaps less than a millimeter wide. They have no mouths; their skin is micro-perforated, drinking the dissolved tallow and liquefied protein of whatever the rolling stones have hammered to pulp above them. When a stone shifts overhead with a bone-jarring *clink*, a Tallow-worm does not tear; it yields, flowing around the impact like cold grease, parting and recombining in the dark.

Higher up, at the violent threshold where the locked pavement meets the tumbling layer, live the **Grit-Chitons**. 

At first inspection, they are wholly indistinguishable from the river-stones they cling to. Their shells are not segmented like surface chitons, but have fused over evolutionary epochs into solid, flattened domes of dense, iron-infused calcite, worn by the current into the exact same low, hydrodynamic oval as the surrounding cobbles. They are living river-stones, anchored to the larger, immovable boulders by a muscular foot that acts as a vacuum gasket. The vacuum they generate is so profound that the violent shearing currents cannot rip them away; to dislodge one would require a chisel and a sledge. 

These chitons do not graze on plants; they graze on the calcified gray crusts and the microscopic bacterial mats that film the leeward flanks of the stones. Their radulae—their rasping tongues—are tipped with self-sharpening magnetite, harder than the chert beneath them. The sound of their feeding is lost in the heavy hydraulic shudder of the seabed, but they leave distinct, curved tracks carved directly into the ancient flint: the only biological etchings on a landscape that otherwise belongs entirely to physics.

In the open spaces between the larger mounds—where the current catches the shingle and flings it in blind, horizontal sheets—nothing can remain attached. Here, life must move with the tumbling.

There are creatures that do not avoid the grinding stones, but dwell within their very motion. These are the **Pebble-Crabs**—though they have long ceased to look like crabs. Their limbs have been reduced to thick, wedge-shaped pry-bars, heavily calcified and tucked entirely within a spherical, smooth carapace that mimics the size and weight of a fist-sized cobble. They do not walk; they roll. They allow the furious abyssal current to bowl them along the floor in the crawling shingle-carpet. Their heavy, thick shells can endure hundreds of thousands of impacts without fracturing. When the tide slackens and the carpet settles into a momentary, grinding halt, their pry-limbs extend just enough to lever aside neighboring stones, allowing them to scavenge the crushed bodies of worms and brittle-stars caught in the shift. To an observer with a light, they would be invisible until the floor stopped moving and the stones themselves began to shuffle and pick at the gravel.

And gliding above it all, keeping just inches out of reach of the churning floor, are the **Anvil-Skates**. 

They are wide, spade-shaped elasmobranchs, colored the deep, dead gray of slate, without eyes or dorsal fins. Their bellies are lined with a thick, leathery armor of dermal denticles that rasp like coarse sandpaper—protection against the stray cobbles thrown upward by the underwater storms. The skate’s snout is an iron-hard shovel of solid cartilage, reinforced to withstand sixty atmospheres of pressure and continuous head-on collisions with tumbling rocks. 

The Anvil-Skate does not hunt by sight or even by electrical impulses, which are scrambled by the static friction of millions of quartz crystals striking one another in the dark. Instead, it hunts by the *thrum*. Its skull houses massive, mineralized otoliths—ear-stones larger than a man’s thumb—suspended in chambers of dense acoustic fat. The skate feels the seabed’s vibration not as a sound, but as an acute topographical map. It reads the pitch of the grinding shingle: a sharp, rattling frequency means the stones are loose and dangerous; a deep, low-register groan means the shingle is densely packed, trapping worms beneath it. When it detects a pocket of prey, the skate tilts forward, beating its muscular pectoral wings against the sheer current, and rams its snout into the tumbling matrix, dislodging the surface stones and vacuuming up the soft things hiding in the slurry below.

Even the ancient artifacts left behind by the dry world have been drawn into this blind ecology.

Around the petrified crown of the elk antler, in the dead water formed behind its heavy, calcified tines, a colony of **Glass-Polypes** has taken root. They are not corals, but colonial animals whose stems are made of pure, flexible biogenic silica, like spun glass. They do not reach up into the current where they would be snapped; they sprawl across the black, fossilized bone like a web of frozen lace. Their tiny, translucent hydranths wave in the back-eddy, capturing the fine rain of organic dust that curls over the antler's brow. 

Inside the flake scars of the submerged chert hand-axes—in the miniature depressions struck out by human thumbs before the sea came in—rest the eggs of the Anvil-Skates. They are tough, square capsules of black keratin, anchored to the flint by coiled tendrils that have wrapped themselves tightly into the micro-fissures of the stone. 

Here, in the shelter of a tool crafted to butcher beasts in the sunlight, the young skates gestate in the absolute, iron-cold dark. They lie unmoving within their leathery cases, rocked by the unending shudder of the seabed, listening through sixty atmospheres of water to the crushing, cyclic music of stones being ground into dust, waiting for their shells to harden enough to face the mill.
