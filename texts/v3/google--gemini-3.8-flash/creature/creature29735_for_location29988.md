To survive where noon is permanent and there is no breeze to carry away heat, a living thing must solve the problem of its own fire. 

In this basin, any creature that ran, or panted, or struck a blow would cook its own brain within minutes. The air offers no evaporative respite; it is already thick with the lung-warm sweat of the leaves, and the wood beneath is as hot as a fevered forehead. Movement, therefore, has not been eliminated, but it has been drawn out, smoothed into a hydraulic creep so slow that the heat of contraction can bleed directly into the plant-mat before it raises the core by a single degree.

The platter-arthropods—those broad, bone-white shields clinging beneath the table-brackets—are not the only things that tap the wood, but they are the most visible. If one touches the rim of such a disc, it does not twitch. The shell is not chitin as an insect knows it, but a composite of dense calcite and fibrous cellulose, cold and slightly waxy, like a slab of beef suet that has hardened under salt. Beneath that shell, there are no lungs, no spiracles to whistle with breath. A network of thin, transparent vessels brings the plant’s high-pressure sap directly into a wide, shallow sinus beneath the creature's back. It does not eat; it is plumbed in. Its heart beats twice in an hour, a heavy, single-chambered contraction that sends a ripple across the chalk-colored disc like a pebble dropped in cream. When it grows, it does not molt. The margins of the plate simply advance over the bark, secreting a pale bead of lacquer that seals the edge to the leaf-bracket, making the beast and the wood into a single hydraulic system.

Lower down, where the succulent trunks knot together into the false floor, the light filters through fifty feet of translucent green flesh, dimming from blinding selenite glare to a dense, chartreuse twilight. Here, in the damp chambers between the pale ribs, live the thread-dips. 

They are ribbons of clear gel, no thicker than a fingernail’s edge and six cubits long, that hang suspended from the bottom of the wood mattress over the dark, razor-sharp floor of split basalt. They do not drop. They are pinned between the roof of wood and the floor of stone by the immense surface tension of the water that seeps downward through the rift. They are nearly invisible, identifiable only by the double refraction of light through their edges, like wet glass rods hanging in steam. When a bead of latex or a mineralized flake sheds from the under-bark, it slides down the surface of the ribbon, absorbed by osmotic ingestion along its entire length. They have no organs, only polarities: an end that drinks from the warm pulp above, and an end that anchors to the cold, split basalt below, grounding the plant’s electrical charge against the raw slate.

And then there are the makers of the dry-stone platforms.

They are still here. They do not inhabit the enclosures as dwellings, for there is no weather to keep out, no darkness to huddle against, and no beast to fear. They occupy the platforms because basalt does not grow. In a world where every stalk, leaf, and knee of wood is expanding by an inch each day, threatening to envelope and swallow anything that pauses, the stone is the only dead anchor—the only ground that will not knit into your flesh if you lie against it.

They are bipeds only in the sense that they possess two weight-bearing columns below, but they rarely stand. To stand is to raise the head into the fiercest wash of the quartz lens, where the skull must bear the direct perpendicular weight of the light. Mostly they sit, their backs curved into smooth, white domes that match the curve of the succulent trunks.

Their skin is thick, bald, and the pale, waterlogged gray of peeled elder-wood. It has no pores for sweat; instead, along the lateral lines of their ribs and down the insides of their heavy calves, there are rows of soft, pinkish clefts—spiracles lined with fine, velvety cilia that do not breathe air, but suckle the water beads from the leaves. When they are thirsty, they do not drink with their mouths; they simply lean their flanks against a guttating branch, and the swollen spheres of water along the leaf-teeth collapse into these clefts with a faint, wet click.

Their faces are flat and featureless as an elbow, save for a broad, downward-pointing spade of horn where the nose and mouth have fused into a single feeding-edge. They have no eyes. In an unvarying noon, an eye is merely a weakness, an open gate through which the furnace-white ceiling would blister the brain. Instead, the entire front of their domed skulls is shot through with thin plates of translucent spar—the same selenite that armors the ceiling miles above. They sense the noon not as a picture, but as a directional pressure, a massive, perpendicular weight bearing down upon the crown. They navigate by the geometry of the light-fall; to step out of true verticality is to feel a slant in the skull, an imbalance they correct with a slow, heavy sway of the hips.

Their hands are terrible, beautiful instruments. The fingers are four, short and square-tipped, lacking nails, but shod in thick, calloused pads that feel like cured ox-hide. The joints do not creak. The blood inside them is not red; it is a pale, viscous lymph, rich in calcium and vegetable latex, moving under such high hydrostatic pressure that a puncture does not bleed outward, but immediately plugs itself with an instantaneous curd of white gum.

It was these hands that lifted the basalt.

The work was not done with levers or fire, for fire cannot live in this saturated, windless blanket. It was done with the slow, implacable lift of their whole bodies. Because the rift floor was shattered only yesterday, the blocks lay everywhere in giant, crystalline prisms, cleanly cleaved. A platform maker would lower its trunk between two slabs of razor-edged slate, wedge its thick-skinned thighs and calloused palms into the fracture, and wait. As its lymph vessels engorged under the fever-heat, its body would swell with hydraulic force—a slow, muscular expansion of hundreds of pounds per square inch, prying the stone from its bed without a sound, until the slab was freed and could be cradled against the belly and carried, step by single, measured step, to the circle.

They built the enclosures to have a place to cease.

Without the dry-stone floors, a sleeper would become a tree. If a platform maker remains on the succulent wood for more than three days, the calcified bark begins to recognize the calcium in its hide. The velvety felt-pores of the leaves reach out across the skin; the fine, clear latex of the stems bridges the gulf of surface tension and enters the spiracles; the wood begins to flow around the knees like warm tallow around an iron nail. It is not an attack—there is no malice or predation in the green—it is merely the law of the trough, which cannot tolerate an unjoined surface. 

Those who failed to reach the basalt are still scattered through the thicket: pale, human-shaped buttresses incorporated into the horizontal columns, their selenite-shot skulls tilted back toward the ceiling, their ribs now serving as the arches that support a dozen square yards of leather fronds. Their hearts have stopped, but their vessels are still open, carrying the boiling sap of the chasm up to the quartz-blind sky.

Those who remain on the stone platforms do not speak. The acoustic baffle of the leaves would choke a voice at ten cubits, and there is nothing to announce. They sit in pairs or solitary clusters, their four-fingered hands resting flat on the sharp basalt blocks, their backs turned to the center of the ring. Now and then, with a movement so deliberate it seems like the tilting of a shadowless sundial, one will extend an arm, reach out to the green wall that surrounds the platform, and snap a succulent shoot that has strayed over the boundary.

The snap is loud—a sharp, wet *thock* that sounds like a green bone breaking under a hammer. From the wound in the plant, a cupful of warm, clear latex wells up, hanging in a single quivering dome of surface tension. 

The maker does not lick it. It simply places the thick pad of its thumb into the center of the drop. The latex does not spill; it bridges into the skin, drawn up through the calloused horn by the thirst of the body’s interior pressure. When the drop is flat, the hand returns to the stone. 

The white light pours down, vertical and heavy as lead, caught and held in the selenite vault of their skulls. Beneath them, miles of living pulp hum with the silent work of noon. They sit upon the fresh-split stone, neither old nor young, waiting for nothing to change, because down here, the noon was finished before it began, and there is nowhere for the day to go.
