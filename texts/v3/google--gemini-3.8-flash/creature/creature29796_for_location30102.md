To live here, a creature cannot be made of wetness. 

A drop of water in this flue would be an obscenity, an impossible vapor instantly torn into the parched draft or swallowed by the insatiable moss. The things that inhabit the fissure are therefore built like the place itself: assembled from sheets of dry horn, spun glass, cold-pressed cartilage, and self-lubricating graphite. They do not circulate blood; they circulate friction and charge.

The most common among them are the *tine-striders*, though to call them insects would be to misunderstand their pedigree. They are jointed like calipers, no larger than an open palm, their carapaces made of laminated amber and thin, translucent flakes of selenite. They have no lungs, nor spiracles that draw air. Instead, their bellies are hollow resonators, ribbed like miniature concertinas. Because there is no muscle fluid to flex their long, needle-tipped legs, they do not crawl in any ordinary sense; they move by *slip-stick friction*, tuning their limb-joints to the 80-hertz mechanical drone of the mountain. By relaxing and clamping their dry talons against the basalt or iron in micro-intervals, they ride the vibration, skittering across vertical surfaces with a faint, dry rattling sound, like playing cards shuffled at tremendous speed. 

They graze upon the edges of the silica fronds, shearing off the brittle crystalline margins with jaws that work like sheet-metal shears—hardened plates of iron-rich chiton that strike tiny, silent sparks in the dimness. Inside their gullets, there is no stomach acid. They possess gizzards packed with sharp basalt grit; the constant, trembling roar of the mountain keeps these internal mills vibrating continuously, pulverizing the glass-fiber leaves into an ultra-fine, nourishing flour from which their dry tissues extract trace zinc, phosphorus, and bound static charge. Their droppings are not dung, but smooth, cold pellets of graphite and pressed talc that tumble down through the tangle to lodge silently in the crevices below.

Higher up, where the petrified hemp seals the flanged joints of the great vertical pipes, lives something broader and slower. They are flat, sprawling things—*pelt-slugs* or *bellows-wefts*—broad as carriage cushions and barely thicker than a piece of sole-leather. They are a deep, oxidized plum-black, their dorsal sides coated in a velvet nap of dry, fibrous cilia that sweep the air for mineral dross. 

These creatures do not have legs; they do not travel. They have anchored themselves across the boundary lines where the cold iron touches the basalt. The iron carries one electrostatic potential, the mountain stone another; the pelt-slug bridges them. It is a biological thermocouple. Across its underside runs a lattice of fibrous silver threads, drawing a faint, ceaseless current from the difference between metal and bedrock. 

If you peel one back from the pipe—which would require a cold chisel, snapping its root-bristles with the sound of breaking violin strings—you would find no guts, no moist meat. Its interior is a honeycomb of dry, vulcanized bladder-tissue, filled with powdered zinc and dry flake-sulfur. It lives entirely on the current and the drone. It breathes by expanding and contracting with the expansion cycles of the iron, a breath that takes six or eight months to complete a single stroke, measured only by the micro-shifts in the mountain's internal torque.

Between these two extremes are the weavers: long, ribbon-like vermin, pale as butcher’s twine, that thread themselves through the dead spaces inside the centrifugal pump housing. They have no eyes; what light reaches this depth carries no information they could use. Instead, their heads taper to cold iron needles—literal needles, mineralized styluses grown from their rostrums—which they keep pressed against the vibrating casing of the turbine. 

Through these needles, they "hear" with an acuity that border on architecture. They do not perceive sound as noise, but as a rigid geometric landscape. A hairline crack forming inside an iron truss three hundred feet down the shaft is, to them, a glaring geometric distortion in the hum; a loose rivet two stories above is an erratic stutter that draws them like a beacon. When they find such a flaw, they do not repair it, but settle into it. They pack the gap with their own bodies, extruding a brittle, lead-dense silk that sets as hard as cold solder, dampening the wobble, tuning the mountain back to its singular, pure note. They are the maintenance crew of an engine they have never seen, serving not a master, but the fundamental need of their own nervous systems, which cannot tolerate an uneven harmonic.

In the center of the pocket, wedged directly into the eye of the centrifugal exhaust casing, sits the oldest thing in the fissure. 

It does not move at all. At first glance, it appears to be nothing more than a swelling of the grease-covered pitch that has leaked from the bearings over four hundred years—a mound of blackened, congealed asphaltum the size of a bullock, bearded with wire-moss and silica flakes. But it has symmetry. Along its flanks run rows of pale, calcified spiracles that do not breathe, but act as acoustic pipes, shaped to catch the deep, teeth-shattering sub-bass of the buried floor.

It has become the flywheel’s parasite. Within its dark, tarry bulk, it maintains a slow, continuous piezoelectric crystallization. It grows like a geode, inwards, turning its own dead layers into faceted lattices of quartz and zincite. It has no predators, because there is nothing in it to eat; it is ninety percent stone and heavy grease, ten percent desiccated nerve-mat. It has sat against the drive-housing so long that its lower carpace has grooved itself precisely into the iron casting, matching every bolt and rivet head with an inverted socket.

It is barely conscious, but its consciousness, such as it is, is coextensive with the drone. It knows the mass of the spire above it; it knows the torque of the drive-shaft beneath it; it knows the tension of every ropy vine wedged into the rock face. It has been dying of wear for two centuries—not decay, for nothing rots, but *mechanical wear*. Its joints, where plates of silica rub against the bone-dry basalt, are slowly being ground down into powder by the invariant shake of the mountain.

When one of these creatures reaches its end, it does not collapse or bloat. Its dry sinews simply reach their limit of fatigue and part with a dull *snap*, like an over-wound clock spring. 

The tine-striders find the carcass within hours, drawn not by scent, but by the cessation of its vibration. They climb upon it, wedge their iron shear-jaws into its dried joints, and systematically reduce it to slivers of horn, flakes of mica, and gray dust. Within a week, the body has been dismantled and integrated: the mica absorbed into new carapaces, the fibers spun into repair-silk for the ironwork, the zinc swallowed to line the grinding gizzards. 

Nothing is left but an empty outline on the rock, a clean patch of basalt where the static charge was blocked for a century, ready to be recolonized by the parchment-lichen before the dust has even settled.
