To understand what lives in the cradle, one must understand that to them, the world has no bottom, no horizon, and no silence. It has only diameter, tension, and heat.

At the smallest remove, the air itself is an edible broth. The tallow that drips from the high cistern does not merely lubricate the iron; it atomizes under the violent, shivering resonance of the planetary gears, filling the warm mist with microscopic beads of sheep fat and mineral lard. This airborne emulsion sustains the *marrow-midges*—clouds of soft-bodied, blind flies whose wings are ribbed with chitinous combs that catch the vibration, using the acoustic roar of the pylon to vibrate their flight muscles without burning metabolic energy. They do not feed on nectar; they drink the atomized grease directly from the air, clustering so thickly around the exhaust seams that the rivets appear to be wrapped in grey, trembling velvet. Where they settle, they die in the millions, their bodies dissolving into a nutritious, alkaline paste that feeds the black lichens and the stiff, rubber-leaved aroids.

Living within the three-foot-deep pelts of moss are the true engineers of the under-struts: the *flange-ticks*. These are not arachnids, though they have eight jointed limbs, but heavily armored crustacean-analogues the size of a cobbler’s last. Their underbellies are flat, concave, and slick with an amber secretion that creates an airtight suction against the rolled steel plates. They spend decades scraping the sulfurous crusts and boiler scale from the iron, clearing the metal for the moss-mats to root deeper. A flange-tick may spend forty years circumambulating a single ninety-foot coupling, moving three inches with every change of season, its mouthparts grinding down the oxidized iron into a fine, black meal which it excretes as a cement that seals weeping rivets. The iron does not rust away because these creatures mortar the pylon with their own dung.

Above them, in the cavernous webs between the diagonal tie-rods, live the *girdermen*. 

They are hominids, though centuries in the acoustic pressure have remade them into something as specialized as the lichen. They are small—rarely standing four feet tall—with barrel-shaped rib cages so heavily thickened and fused with gristle that they resemble the boilers they tend. Their chests are designed not merely to breathe the thick, tallowy atmosphere, but to buffer their internal organs against the relentless, bone-conducted din. Their ears have lost their external pinnae entirely; where an ear should be, there is only a tight, hairless depression sealed with plugs of hardened wax and keratin. They are utterly deaf to sound as a human knows it, but their lower jaws are enormous, jutting out like iron anvils, hinged with dense, fibrous muscle. When a girderman wishes to "hear" the cradle, he presses his chin firmly against the wet metal of a catwalk. Through the bone of his jaw, the pylon translates itself: he can feel the slippage of an eccentric sleeve half a mile below, the thinning of a brass bushing in the western stanchion, or the shudder that announces the counterweight has dropped its hourly inch.

Their skin is the dead, pale grey of lard, stained indelibly along the creases with graphite, bitumen, and red lead. They have no body hair; the constant, hot condensation would rot it out, so their skin has taken on the texture of boiled leather, impervious to the bite of the acid-secreting figs and the hot spitting of the steam vents.

They do not walk as creatures of the flat earth walk. Their feet have no arches; the toes are enormously long and opposable, ending in thick, curved nails that have the hardness and contour of steel cotter pins. A girderman can sleep hanging upside down from the underside of an I-beam, both feet locked over the lip of a gusset plate, while the thermal winds buffet his slack body over three miles of empty air.

Their relationship with the machinery is neither reverence nor labor; it is the instinctive stewardship of a barnacle on a whale’s belly. They carry small pig-iron hammers and scrapers forged from the broken teeth of old gears, tapping along the cross-braces, clearing away the thick mats of rubbery ferns wherever the foliage threatens to bind a rocker-arm or choke a drainage scupper. When the central lubrication cistern drips its warm yellow tallow, small family groups gather on the catwalks beneath the crown wheel. They catch the splatter in shallow dishes woven from split fig-roots, mixing the fat with dried marrow-midges and the sweet, starch-rich pith of the vulcanized fern-cores to make a dense, greasy loaf that smells of boiled suet and engine oil.

They have no language of the throat. The sound of the drive-shafts would tear words to shreds before they left the tongue. Instead, they communicate across the void through the iron itself. A girderman will take his hammer and strike three measured blows into the web of a horizontal strut—not hard, but with the full, dead weight of his body behind the swing. Through the miles of interconnected lattice, the strike travels as a sharp, ringing harmonic. Across five hundred paces of sheer emptiness, another figure, clinging like a lichen-blotch to the sweating flank of a conduit, feels the vibration in his soles, pauses, presses his jaw to the flange, and taps back his assent. In this way, they pass news of broken valves, shifting wind-shears, and the slow, inevitable creep of the bevel gears.

There are larger things, too, that never touch the iron at all.

Drifting through the high, open ribs of the dome, well above the reach of the steam vents, are the *veil-rays*. Translucent, bladder-bodied things sixty feet from wing-tip to wing-tip, they enter the cradle through the open roof from the freezing, stratospheric desert above, drawn downward by the great rising chimney of tallow-warm air. Their bodies are paper-thin, undulating with sickening slowness as they trawl the empty spaces between the sixteen pillars, their long, trailing tendrils sweeping the air for the featherless swifts that nest in the bolt-holes. When a veil-ray drifts through a shaft of sunlight, it is nearly invisible—only a rippling distortion in the steam, a faint oil-slick iridescence hanging over the abyss. Sometimes, one will brush too close to the central axle; the immense suction of the planetary gears will catch a trailing skirt, and in an instant, without a change in the machinery's pitch, the creature is drawn into the teeth. For days afterward, the lower catwalks run with a clear, jelly-like grease that the girdermen harvest with quiet, frantic haste, for it burns in their moss-wick lamps with a clean, white flame that leaves no soot upon the metal.

Nothing here has ever known an end to motion. The girdermen do not conceive of a world where things stop. They believe that if the great drive-shafts were to cease their rotation, the air would instantly freeze into blue glass, their chests would collapse under the sudden silence, and the cradle would break from its spindling pylon and drop like an anvil into the white sea beneath. 

When an old one among them feels his knee-joints seizing with the thick, chalky gout that comes from a life lived on grease and cold iron, he does not wait to become a burden. He climbs out along the longest, thinnest tie-rod—the one that reaches toward the central lubrication cistern, where the drop is cleanest and the view of the void is uninterrupted by struts. He strips the tallow-scrapers from his belt and leaves them tucked neatly into a bolt-hole for the young. Then he steps into the clear air, where the roar is loudest, his pale, scarred body falling like a plumb-bob through the mist, swallowed by the cloud deck before the sound of his departure can even be imagined. The crown wheel turns its fraction of an inch; the tallow drop falls; the iron drinks the steam; and the cradle continues its slow, unmeasured work against the sky.
