To survive here, an organism must surrender its hollows. 

Anything with an air-sac, a tympanum, a dry lung, or a brittle shell would be unstitched within an hour by the twelve-second stroke. The vibration is not a sound; it is a shear-force that travels through marrow and water at identical speed. Therefore, whatever lives here is continuous with the water. It has no internal voids where pressure can shear one membrane against another. It is dense, hydropic, and solid as a cured ham.

The first hint of them is not movement, but texture where the machine oil meets the lime. 

On the blind warehouse walls, just above the reach of the high-water scum lines, the liverwort does not grow directly on the brick. It sits upon a living pelt—a tissue of colonial flatworms, each the width of an unrolled tobacco leaf, laid one over the other like wet slate shingles. They are greyish-translucent, the color of dirty mutton fat, and they do not have separate hearts. They rely on the beam engine’s downstroke to pump their fluids for them. When the mud-sills shudder and the stone groans under the counterweight, the sudden shock-wave compresses the entire wall; the flatworms are squeezed against the masonry, their pale, unvalved lymph forced through capillary channels in their flesh, circulating their dull blood by the city’s steam.

They feed on the tufa-skin, grazing with soft ventral scrapers upon the mineral bloom and the petroleum-eating yeasts that flourish on the grease-splatter. Where they have passed, the brick is left completely smooth, polished to the dark, naked luster of a river stone.

Lower down, in the drowned trenches where the black water runs warm toward the suction wells, there are larger shapes. 

They are ribless. A rib is an arch, and under this acoustic hammer, an arch is merely a tuning fork waiting to shatter. Instead, they are built like drowned sausages—pale, hydrostatic slugs the length of a stoker’s forearm, dense and heavy as lead sinkers. They have no eyes; in this fog, where light is only a grey ceiling forty feet above, eyes are an unnecessary tenderness. In place of a face, each has a puckered rosette of fleshy whiskers, greasy and thick, which they hold pressed flat against the submerged iron plates.

They are blind scavengers of tallow. A beam engine of this scale leaks lard by the gallon; its journals are packed with sheep-fat and mineral wax, which constantly weeps down the connecting rods and washes into the trenches in curdled, yellowish rafts. The creatures—the *tallow-eaters*—anchor themselves with broad, suction-cup bellies to the diamond tread of the drowned floor and graze the fat off the surface of the iron. 

Their skin is thick, like the boiled cowhide used for sole leather, soaked through until it has lost all stiffness. If lifted from the water, one would not flop or thrash; it has no skeletal leverage. It would merely slump into a shapeless, heavy pancake of muscle, weeping greasy water from its pores. But in the trench, cushioned by four feet of boiler runoff and buffered by the grease slicks, it is almost graceful. It undulates with the sluggish, thick-witted timing of the water-end piston, its whole body rippling once for every turnaround of the balance beam.

In the deepest well—the dark, rectangular cistern directly beneath the great rocker bearings—lives something that has grown large on a century of unattended runoff.

It is impossible to see it whole; the water is too full of particulate magnetite and suspended graphite, black as printer’s ink. But occasionally, when the great beam completes its descent and pauses—that single, breathless instant before the counterweight sighs downward—a vast, pale knuckle of blubber breaks the surface film between the pump-rods.

It has the bulk of a bullock, but none of its shape. It is a single, continuous mass of unsegmented flesh that has anchored itself around the submerged cast-iron intake strainer. Over decades, it has grown through the grate, its tissue swallowing the four-inch bars of the filter cage, flowing around them like hot pitch until the metal is inside it, serving as its skeleton. The iron does not kill it; the slick coating of black magnetite protects the meat from rust, and the creature’s own mucus, alkaline and thick as wallpaper paste, seals the metal from corrosion.

This thing does not possess lungs or gills in any common sense. Instead, its back is corrugated into thousands of deep, leaf-like ruffles that float up into the warm current like the leaves of a drowned cabbage. As the steam-engine drags three thousand gallons of water through the pit with every stroke, the suction draws the broth of decaying ferns, moss-slough, and suspended grease directly through these fleshy pleats. It does not swallow; it is an animal filter, a living strainer married to the mechanical one.

Its life is so completely synchronized with the beam that its resting state is twelve seconds long. It tenses when the brass steam-valves shriek, taking the impact of the mechanical shock directly through its bulk, absorbing it like a great rubber fender; it relaxes when the water gushes over its back, sighing with the exhaust vapor. 

If the boiler fires four miles away were ever drawn, if the coal cars stopped coming and the pressure in the mains dropped to zero, the basin would fall silent. The mist would cool into flat, cold rain. The three-stroke pulse would thin, shudder, and halt.

And within forty-eight hours, the dwellers in the sump would die—not from starvation or cold, but because their blood would cease to move. They would lie trapped within their leathery sacks, waiting for the floor to kick them, suffocating in the terrible, motionless silence of a world that had forgotten how to stroke.
