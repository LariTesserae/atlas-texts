Beneath the crust of the vegetation, in the dark where the root-hawsers press directly against the cleaved basalt, life does not walk; it threads.

To survive beneath four feet of dense, cold-hardened turf, an organism must be narrow, muscular, and indifferent to weight. The most common are the *spindle-dogs*—though they are not dogs at all, but some ancient, neotenic lineage of burrowing mustelid or blind carnivoran, their pelts greasy and bare of hair except for stiff, quill-like whiskers running the entire length of their flanks. They are long, ribless along their midsections like eels, moving through the root-tangle by anchoring their blunt, spade-shaped skulls into the peat and pulling their pale, accordion-ribbed bodies forward. They have no external ears; their auditory canals are sealed with plugs of solid, yellow fat to keep the marrow-shaking roar from pulverizing their brains. Instead, they navigate by the tooth-rattle: pressing their lower jaws against the wet basalt to read the shudder of the bedrock, finding the slight deviations in current that signal an open fissure or the proximity of a neighbor. 

The spindle-dogs eat the gray grubs that gestate in the root-knots and graze on the thick, starch-heavy weeping nodes of the moss, but their true quarry is the *mat-slider*. 

The mat-sliders live on the threshold between the vegetable carpet and the stone. Broad, flattened like skate, and roughly the size of a shovel head, they are land-bound cephalopods or shell-less mollusks that have calcified their dorsal skin into a flexible, slate-gray plate that mimics the cleavage planes of the rock. They move with an agonizing, glacier-like patience on a bed of cold, viscous slime that smells powerfully of ammonia and wet copper. As they creep, their rasped radulae sheer away the microscopic holdfasts of the moss, grazing on the film of iron-fixing bacteria that coats the weeping basalt. When a heavy boot or a falling bolt drops from the gantries above, the mat-slider does not flee; it flattens itself into the turf, venting a hiss of pressurized, freezing water from its lateral siphons, locking its perimeter to the rock with a hydraulic grip so profound that dynamite would be required to peel it away intact.

Above the carpet, in the bitter stream of the gale, the air is too violent for wings that flap. The solitary pipistrelles that occasionally flicker through the high sodium beams do not fly so much as tumble and tack. They are broad-winged and leathery, their membranes thickened with a waxy tallow that resists frostbite. They do not hunt insects in the traditional sense, for the wind shreds any swarm; rather, they cling with hook-like thumbs to the windward faces of the iron pilings, their flattened muzzles open to the blast, using their bristled lips like sieves to trawl the air for frozen spores, skin flakes shed by the carpet, and drifting iron-dust. Their hearts beat at an impossibly sluggish tempo—ten or twelve beats a minute—their metabolism chilled down to match the freezing threshold of the intake air. When they drop from the beams to shift feeding grounds, they let the fans do the work, falling backward into the monotone bellow and riding the pressure-wave like stones skimmed across ice until they can slam their claws into another pylon miles down the line.

And then there are the shacks, and what remains in them.

Humanity has not abandoned the rift, though it has grown thin, deaf, and mineralized. The people who inhabit the corrugated shelters on their iron stilts are known, to the rare supply-haulers who drop down the intake shafts, simply as the *Tenders* or the *Gripers*. There are perhaps three dozen of them scattered along twelve miles of the trench, and they have ceased to resemble surface folk.

A Tender does not speak. In an environment where a shouted syllable is swallowed before it leaves the teeth, the larynx becomes an idle organ; after a decade in the cut, their vocal cords wither into stiff, dry bands. They communicate through *striking*. They carry heavy, copper-tipped ash staves, and when they meet upon the swaying catwalks or in the shadow of a dead tractor, they rap out complex, syncopated rhythms on the black iron of the cables or the steel legs of the pilings. The steel carries the signal clean and true through the roar, a bright, metallic telegraphy that can travel three miles against the draft.

Their bodies are shaped entirely by the floor and the sound. They wear boots carved from laminated slabs of conveyor belting, thick and flat, bound to their shins with greased rawhide to distribute their weight across the springy mattress of roots. A Tender walks with a rolling, low-slung trudge, knees perpetually bent, leaning forward at an angle that would cause them to topple were it not for the constant, counter-balancing push of the fan-draft. 

Their gear is salvage and lichen: long parkas pieced together from industrial canvas, lined not with wool, but with the thick, dried felt of the peat-lichen, which traps body heat even when saturated with condensation. Their ears are caulked with balls of beeswax kneaded with powdered lead, over which they strap wide headbands of oil-cured leather. Yet they are not numb to the fans. If a brass bearing should seize four miles away, or if an intake blade should lose an edge to ice-shearing, a Tender will stop mid-stride, their eyes widening behind their round, soot-blackened goggles. They do not hear the change; they feel the drop in pressure in their intestinal walls, the sudden, nauseating shift in the floor’s micro-resonance that tells them the trench’s great lung has missed a beat.

They survive on what the carpet gives and what the machines leak. Inside their shacks—anchored against the draft by turnbuckles cinched tight to the bedrock—they boil the bitter white taproots in iron kettles, skimming off a greasy, nutrient-dense tallow that hardens into an edible suet. They dry the pale liverwort into bricks that smolder without flame in cast-iron stoves, throwing a heavy, starch-rich heat that smells like roasting acorns and charred machine oil. 

They do not try to tame the trench. They have lived alongside the roar long enough to understand that they are not miners anymore, nor mechanics. They are simply part of the silt. When a Tender dies, their companions do not dig into the basalt; they slit the dense weave of the root-turf with an iron billhook, lever up a thick, rubbery flap of pale moss, and slip the body into the wet, cold space between the plants and the floor. Within three shifts, the microscopic holdfasts have reached down; within a season, the hawsers have braided through the cloth and the marrow, and the deceased becomes part of the deck, another firm, springy rise in the floor over which the others lean their weight against the wind, trudging down the yellow line of sodium lamps toward bearings that will never cease to turn.
