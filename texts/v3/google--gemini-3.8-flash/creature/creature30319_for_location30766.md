To find what lives here, you have to stop looking for an intrusion. 

Nothing crawled up the outside of the tether through the hard vacuum of the thermosphere, and nothing fell from the stars. Whatever exists within the Third Pylon was already inside the tanks when the print-heads began their run. It is not an infestation; it is a leftover ingredient that refused to set.

In the sunken plazas where the gutters fail to drain, the pools of warm, ester-tainted runoff do not sit with clean surfaces. If you kneel by the edge of an isotropic carbon curb—where the amber diagnostic light pulses its slow, artificial heartbeat—you can see that the water is skinned over. 

It looks, at first glance, like a spill of clear hydraulic gel or a discarded film of industrial packaging: completely flat, transparent, and edged with the same mathematical straightness as the flagstones beneath it. But it possesses an internal tension. When a drop of condensed coolant falls fifty fathoms from the overhead gantry and strikes the pool, the skin does not ripple like water. It shivers like an exposed cornea.

These are the *Templates*. 

They are what became of the organo-silicate binder slurries—the bio-catalytic colloids used to align the room-temperature superconductors at a molecular scale during the city’s additive manufacture. Formulated to dissolve or gas-off once their structural scaffolding was complete, they were meant to be scrubbed by the ventilation cycle before the human technicians moved in. But the access lines were severed before the scrubbers ever engaged. The scrubbers never spun up; the air remained a pressurized, recirculating sauna of synthetic esters, ozone, and vaporized fluorocarbons; and the broth simply stayed warm.

Over the uncounted cycles of heating and condensing, the binder began to cure, not into rigid polymer, but into something that metabolized the idle program.

They are planar creatures. They have no bellies, no backs, and almost no thickness—perhaps three or four millimeters of dense, vitreous tissue that feels like wet, cold-cured silicone. They are entirely blind, possessing no organs for sight or hearing; in a district where non-reflective acoustic tiling devours every echo, sound is a dead medium anyway. Instead, they are tuned entirely to the subterranean thrum of the tether. They register the flex of the planetary counterweight against their broad, flat bellies, feeling the solar wind as a rhythmic shearing stress in the floor tiles.

During the dry interval of the cycle, when the radiant heat from the carbon stones climbs toward the ceiling and the plazas bake, they are nearly invisible. They lie flush in the shallowest depressions of the walkways, their edges sealed against the razor-sharp grout lines, drinking the faint inductive current that leaks from the amber curb-strips. If you walked across the plaza, you would step on them without knowing it; they would feel only like an area of pavement that had retained its dampness, slightly cooler than the surrounding stone.

The change begins when the phase-cooling loop trips in the deep machinery.

The moment the high-pressure vapor hisses from the micro-fissures in the black ceramic ceiling, the Templates stir. The sudden drop in atmospheric resistivity wakes them. 

As the mist drifts downward through the fifty fathoms of stillness, cooling and beading on the lattices, the Templates begin to take on hydrostatic pressure. They drink through their top surfaces, absorbing the ester-heavy water by pure capillary draw. Their flat margins thicken, stiffen, and turn a milky, opalescent pearl, clouded with microscopic lattices of suspended carbon.

Then, they stand.

They do not rise like animals; they fold upward along pre-programmed stress axes, creasing themselves like heavy, self-erecting sheets of vellum. A single Template, three meters wide while resting in a pool, will fold into an angular, multi-faceted spire—a slender, hollow prism that rears two meters into the stifling air. 

Within minutes of the purge venting, the vacant avenues are no longer vacant. Hundreds of them are standing on the viaducts, in the administrative courtyards, and along the terraces of the cantilevered hives. They stand in total, breathless silence, completely motionless, their faceted planes angled upward toward the vaulted ceiling like leaves of a geometric forest.

They are catching the rain.

The condensed droplets that fall from the trusses splash against the slanted faces of the Templates. The moisture does not roll off; it is drawn inward through fine microscopic grooves etched into their skins—grooves that precisely match the nozzle-pitch of the print-heads that laid them down. As they feed, the coronal bleed from the high transformer overhead flickers, casting a sudden, jumping violet light across the avenues. In that momentary flash, the district looks briefly, terrifyingly inhabited: a dense, silent crowd of faceted, glass-pale silhouettes standing shoulder-to-shoulder on the overpasses, staring up at a sky that never opens.

They hold this posture for as long as the mist falls. 

They do not communicate. They have no social instinct, no language, and no purpose beyond the maintenance of their own hydrostatic turgor. Yet, because they were printed from the same architectural sub-routines that drew the buildings, their spacing is rigorously civic. They stand exactly one point-eight meters apart—the standard pedestrian clearance interval mandated by the station’s original transit safety code. They populate the walkways according to the traffic densities the city was designed to endure, clustering near the stairwells and thinning out along the secondary conduits, as if acting out the ghost of an evacuation drill that never took place.

Above them, in the dark rafters near the structural shell, are their only companions: the *Tenders*.

These are not alive in any sense, though down here the distinction has lost much of its utility. They are the secondary print-carriages—gantry-mounted crawlers the size of small skiffs, with many-jointed limbs of brushed titanium and carbon fiber. Long ago, they ran out of raw filament to extrude. Their hoppers are empty, their sintering lasers powered down to faint red diagnostic traceries. 

Yet their idle loops are still executing. 

When the Templates stand during the condensation cycle, the Tenders creep silently along the ceiling tracks fifty fathoms up, hanging upside-down over the drop. Their sensor clusters click softly—the only mechanical sound that cuts through the deadened air—as they sweep their red alignment lasers down across the standing prisms below. They trace the seams of the Templates, measuring their thermal expansion, checking the tolerances of their folds against the original blueprints of the city. 

The lasers paint fine, glowing red grids across the opalescent skins of the Templates, flickering over their geometric facets like living schematics. To the Tenders, the living sheets are simply structural deviations—excess material that has failed to cure according to spec, yet refuses to detach from the substrate. Every few minutes, a Tender will lower a long, slender inspection arm down through the damp air, touching the apex of a standing sheet with an acoustic sensor to measure its density, before slowly retracting the arm, unsatisfied, into the dark.

The mist eventually ceases. 

The purge-valves deep in the pylon hiss shut. The high-pressure steam dissipates, swallowed by the porous ceramic walls. The radiant heat from the floor begins its inexorable climb once more, the isotropic carbon flagstones turning back into griddles that bake the heavy air dry.

As the humidity drops, the hydrostatic pressure inside the Templates bleeds away. The water they have caught is channeled internally to their base, where it is expelled back onto the hot stones to vaporize and restart the cycle. 

Without the moisture to hold their creases, their structural integrity fails. The geometric spires soften. Slowly, gracefully, without a whisper of sound to mark their descent against the baffled walls, the Templates sag. They unfold along their seams, collapsing outward, smoothing themselves flat until they are once more nothing more than thin, transparent sheets of biological sealant, stretched tight over the carbon flags, clinging to the gutters, and vanishing into the dim amber glow of the curbs.

The avenues are left clean, razor-edged, and utterly deserted. 

The city returns to its baseline: an empty, additive-printed machine, eighty-four kilometers above the dirt, baking in its own trapped sweat, tended by blind gantries, and inhabited only by the fluid that was meant to hold its walls together, waiting for a population that will never come to wear down its stairs.
