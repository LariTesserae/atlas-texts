The crawlers do not have names, because there is no one here to catalog them, but their logic is entirely the logic of the basin. 

They are longer than they first appear—some the length of a man’s forearm, though broad and flat enough to slide into the narrow clearance between an idle counterweight and its granite bed. Their carapaces are not chitin in the terrestrial sense, but an overlapping series of dense, mineralized slates, built up from layers of barium sulfate and ferric oxide cemented with a tough, fibrous keratin. They are dull slate-gray when dry, but across these flats they are never dry; they wear a permanent sheen of lubricating oil that gathers in the deep grooves between their segments, giving them the purple-black iridescence of wet coal.

Underneath, they have no legs to speak of. Instead, their bellies are lined with hundreds of paired, blunt-tipped hydraulic pads—muscular lobes filled with an internal fluid that does not freeze or boil, which pump rhythmically against the stone. They do not walk; they adhere, shear, and advance, moving with a heavy, sucking traction that keeps them grounded even when the ground directly beneath them thumps with the impact of an overhead trip-hammer. To lift one would require a pry-bar, yet they slide across the hematite-glazed rock as smoothly as a drop of grease down a warm shovel.

They are blind, lacking even the vestigial pits of former eyes, and completely deaf to airborne sound. Their entire world is proprioception and harmonic shear. Through their ventral pads, they read the structural health of the basin: the distant, microscopic chatter of a worn journal-box, the rising heat of a dry bronze slipper, the microscopic shifts in the magnetic black sands that tell them a sump is venting its condensate. 

They feed not by chewing, but by abrasion. Their mouthparts are heavy, rasping rings of gray corundum—synthetic sapphire formed naturally in their gizzards from ingested sediment—that grind slowly against the mineral scales clinging to the iron jackets. They eat the salt-crusts, the hematite scales, and the rubbery, tallow-fat biofilms that thrive where metal meets metal. They digest the organic tallow, strip the sulfur from the copper sulfate, and excrete small, dense pellets of compressed rust that immediately roll into the nearest fissure, indistinguishable from the rest of the gravel.

In the clear, lukewarm sumps, life is thinner and stranger. The water is free of silt, filtered through hundreds of feet of fractured basalt, but it is super-saturated with dissolved metal salts. Suspended in these pools are the *weavers*—slender, gelatinous threads that can reach thirty or forty feet in length, tethered by one end to the submerged copper ties. They are barely thicker than horsehair, translucent and pale amber, buoyed by tiny internal vesicles of methane. 

These threads do not move under their own power. They trail in the gentle convection currents stirred up by the submerged steam-pipes, swaying in exact resonance with the dynamos buried beneath the rock. As they sway, their mineral-hungry membranes pull copper, zinc, and tin out of the water, coating themselves in microscopic, flexible sheaths of metallic scale. When a thread dies, its anchor-point corrodes, and it drifts to the bottom to join the black sand, slowly dissolving back into the mineral soup or becoming caught in the rasping plates of the crawlers. 

There is a larger, rarer organism that inhabits the dry clearances beneath the walking beams: a solitary, low-profile creature that resembles a folded sheet of lead or vulcanized rubber. It has no joints and no distinct front or back. It wedges itself into the shadowed pockets where the stone meets the cast-iron footings—places where the hydraulic pressure reaches hundreds of atmospheres, yet remains completely stationary. These things are essentially living gaskets. 

Their bodies are dense, leathery bladders filled with an oily, viscous lymph that resists enormous crushing loads. They do not graze; they live entirely on the thermal differential between the boiling-hot iron of the machine’s foundations and the cooler bedrock beneath. Their metabolism is so slow that a single contraction of their mantle can take three days. To an observer, they would appear to be old pieces of abandoned packing material, pressed flat into the corners and blackened by grease. Yet if a beam-bearing shifts or a bolt shears loose, allowing the joint to cool, the thing will slowly, invisibly loosen its grip, peel its leathery mass from the stone over the course of several weeks, and drag itself across the rust flats to find another warm, pressurized seam.

Reproduction across the basin is divorced from seasons. There are no springs, no matings, no courtships. The equilibrium does not allow for sudden abundance. 

Instead, propagation occurs along the acoustic nodes. At certain points on the gravel flats, the vibrations of three or four distant engines cancel one another out, creating small, perfectly still patches of ground no larger than a dinner plate. These dead spots are the only places where the black sand lies completely flat, without ripples or spikes. 

Here, the crawlers converge. They are drawn to the silence the way moths are drawn to a flame, sliding into the calm pockets until they are stacked three or four deep. In this stillness—which must feel to them like an absolute void—they shed their outer plates and release clouds of microscopic, oily cysts directly into the grit. The cysts do not hatch into larvae; they are tiny, tough spheres of desiccated cellular matter that blow across the plains like dust until they find their way into a warm sump or the grease-groove of a rotating shaft. There, nourished by tallow and heat, they begin to accrete their mineral shells, growing in the dark clearances until they are large enough to withstand the acoustic pressure of the open basin.

Nothing rots here. Death is simply a change in friction. When a crawler’s internal pads finally fail and it can no longer hold itself against the vibrating stone, it does not decay; the relentless thrum of the floor shakes it apart. Its segments separate; its corundum rasp is ground down; its leathery tissues are dried by the engine heat and pulverized into the rust-red grit by the passage of other crawlers. Within a few cycles of the great hydraulic beams, the creature has been integrated into the mechanical bed, its fat absorbed by the tallow-mats, its minerals returned to the stone, its presence erased by the invariant, unblinking rhythm of the work.
