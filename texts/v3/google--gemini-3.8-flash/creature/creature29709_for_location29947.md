In the expansion joints and behind the shear-plates, where the tar has squeezed out under the weight of the slabs, the cold does not kill; it simply slows everything to the tempo of settling silt. 

The things that thrive here have no lineage of deep soil. They are the offspring of transport crates, pipe-dunnage, and whatever subterranean bilge was pumped through the intake lines while the forms were being poured. 

Close to the deck, along the bevels where the concrete meets the vertical web of the I-beams, live the grease-slugs. They are not gastropods, though they move with the same wet, muscular peristalsis. Pale as boiled lard and nearly as translucent, their flesh is dense enough to withstand the four-second turbine slap without tearing from the steel. They have no mouthparts to speak of; instead, their undersides are carpeted with fine, chitinous rasps that peel the thin, yellow tallow-crust from the machine bases. They follow the runnels of sulfurous lubricant like cattle tracing a creek bed. When the amber sodium light catches them, their interiors show black with swallowed filings and powdered slag. They leave behind tracks that look like clear solder, sealing the hairline fractures in the concrete with an oily mucus that never cures in the damp.

Lower down, in the dead-water behind the baffle walls where the thumb-sized amphipods graze, there are hunters that never leave the sumps. 

They are fish, of a kind—likely derived from the blind sculpins of the drainage aquifers three tiers above, but altered by the velocity and the dark. They are flat-skulled, shaped like iron wedges, their heads broad and hard as horn to wedge themselves into the seams of the timber-grain concrete when the spillways open. They have no scales; their skin is a thick, rubbery rind, gray-white and marbled with liver-colored veins that show close to the surface to drink what little oxygen remains in the froth. They have lost the lateral line typical of cave fish; the acoustic pressure of the turbines—that deep, skull-ringing *whuff* every four seconds—would shatter a delicate sensory organ. Instead, their chins and pectoral fins are tipped with stiff, barbed rays that tap the iron plate, feeling for the distinct click of an amphipod’s legs against a rivet or the soft, dragging mass of an isopod shifting between waves.

They do not swim with their tails. The current is too fast, the space too tight. They haul themselves across the aggregate using these calloused pectoral spines, moving with the clumsy, deliberate lurch of crabs, their bellies scraping the zinc decks until the metal is burnished bright.

Above the waterline, the fauna changes character. It becomes light-footed, skittish, and entirely obsessed with the twenty-minute cycle of the hydraulic gates.

In the high, dry gussets where the ceiling decking meets the purlins, fifty feet above the sheeting water, there are crows—or what the construction crews left behind of them. They are not black anymore; the sulfur and the lime dust from the aggregate have bleached their feathers to the color of wet newsprint. They do not fly through the open spaces of the bay; the air displacement from the four-second turbine stroke drops them out of the air like thrown wet rags. Instead, they climb. They have learned to use their beaks and their feet to scramble along the perforated cable-trays, hopping from bracket to bracket with their wings pinned tightly to their sides. 

They watch the water below with eyes filmed over by milky nictitating membranes, shielded against the sting of the cold ozone. They know the klaxon. When the three low, vibrating blasts sound from upstream, the pale birds begin an erratic, chattering clatter, beating their bills against the galvanized deck plates. The sound is tiny, lost entirely beneath the gathering roar of the spillway, but they do it compulsively—an involuntary resonance with the ironwork as the hydraulic rams take the load. 

The moment the gates fall and the deluge shears away, the crows drop down the hanging messenger cables like falling fruit, arresting their descent with clumsy, wing-assisted flings just inches above the wet floor plates. They have less than ten minutes before the floor dries and the sumps drain. In that window, the plates are strewn with the debris of the lower floors: pulped weed-mats, split amphipods battered against the drain grates, and sometimes the fat, white sculpins that failed to wedge their horns into the concrete corners and were torn down the chutes. The birds gorge in frantic silence, their talons skittering on the cold steel, before laboriously clambering back up the iron columns before the air begins to tighten again for the next cycle.

And there are the men, though to call them that is to assume a life outside the twenty-minute pulse.

They are culvert-tenders, left over from the contractor shifts that poured the sixth tier, or perhaps second-generation drifters who came down the intake shafts when the ventilation towers were still open to the intermediate levels. There cannot be many—perhaps six or seven across a quadrant the size of a county—because they never congregate. To meet another body in this space is to compete for the dry-gussets and the grease-traps.

They wear heavy, vulcanized suits assembled from vulcanized rubber sheeting and duct-taped neoprene pipe-lagging, sealed at the wrists with broad bands of greasy copper wire. Their boots are weighted with lead offcuts bolted through the soles—not to sink, but to keep their footing on the slime-filmed deck plates when the air-pulses hit. They are completely deaf; no human ear can survive three months of the turbine’s four-second concussion against bare steel without the drum splitting and scarring into dead gristle. They communicate, if they must, by striking the structural beams with twelve-inch cold chisels, sending sharp, metallic pings through the frame that travel farther than any shout.

They live in the "dead pockets"—the triangular voids formed where the heavy intake ducts pierce the concrete bulkheads high above the reach of the high-water mist. There, the air is nearly hot, thick with the oily steam off the turbine bearings, and the noise is a constant, flat roar rather than a percussive blow. 

Their fingers are thick, yellow-calloused, and permanently curled from gripping the rungs of wet iron ladders. They live on what they can lift from the drainage screens: they net the thumb-sized amphipods with wire mesh pulled from air filters, boiling them in sulfurous water scooped from the conduit condensations over small, sputtering grease-burners fueled by tallow scraped from the slug-runs. They smell of raw zinc, machine oil, and the dry, sour sweat of men who have not seen wool dry in three years.

When the klaxon sounds, they do not look toward the gates. They have their own markers: a certain water-drop from a conduit three meters thick that stops falling two minutes before the lift; a particular grease-line on an I-beam that bubbles as the pressure changes. 

When the three blasts vibrate through the soles of their leaded boots, they simply back into the nearest expansion recess, wedging their shoulders between the pre-cast concrete teeth. They brace their knees against the cold, timber-grained cement, pull their rubber hoods low over their scarred brows, and wait for the cold white wave to tear the dark apart, their bodies trembling in exact, rhythmic sympathy with the four-second gasp of the lungs that keep the sky from falling in.
