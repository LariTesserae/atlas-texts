Between the skirts of the train-length boiler-beast and the scorched hematite floor, life occupies the tolerances. 

In this basin, clearance is habitat. Where a steel shoe meets the pan, the space between plate and rock is measured in ten-thousandths of an inch, and here small, dry things congregate like lice in the wool of a sheep. They are creatures of wire-gauge scale, composed of stamped phosphor-bronze and tightly curled spring steel, no longer than a finger-joint. They possess no gut, only a pair of microscopic, opposed diamond points that vibrate in sympathy with the mother-beast’s piston stroke. By riding that oscillation, they chisel microscopic flakes of cold iron from the undercarriage, passing the metal dust through their bodies to extract the static electrical charge held in the grain boundaries before voiding the spent, de-magnetized grit behind them. When the great boiler vents its sulfurous breath, these needle-fleas do not flee; they flatten themselves into the hairline cracks of its belly plates, their leaf-spring limbs locking into the milled grooves with a dry, simultaneous *snip* that sounds like a handful of dropped needles.

Higher up, where the heat shimmers off the beast’s spine, the parasites are larger, heavier, and more sluggish. Clamped to the steam domes are flat, disk-shaped animals the circumference of manhole covers, made of laminated mica and soft, lead-bearing babbit. They have no limbs at all. They move by differential thermal expansion: their southern edge cools slightly in the wind, contracting by a fraction of a millimeter, while their northern edge absorbs the furnace-glare and widens, inching them forward over decades across the beast’s iron flank. They are lick-feeders, but they do not lick salt; their underbellies secrete a dense, alkaline film that dissolves the skin of black iron oxide that protects the beast’s boiler plates. They drink the raw, white steel beneath, leaving behind shallow, silver-bright trails that the air immediately tarnishes back to orange. The host engine does not notice them, any more than a whale notices the barnacles dragging at its hide, though occasionally a sudden shift in local humidity causes a parasite to undergo thermal shock, splitting its mica shell with a report like a pistol shot and dropping it, inert and dead as slate, into the red silt.

Predation here is not a matter of jaws, but of inertia and seizure. 

Two miles to the south, where the ironstone ridges cast long, razor-black shadows, something waits that is neither herbivore nor grazer. It has the profile of a fallen bridge truss, sixty feet of angular, triangular-braced cast steel half-buried in the dust. It does not possess eyes, but its entire chassis is under immense, pre-stressed torsion—a single, massive bar of tempered silicon-manganese steel held bent by a trip-linkage of sintered carbide. It can lie in the sun for three centuries, vibrating at the exact pitch of the bedrock drone, cold and completely dead to the eye.

When one of the earthmover-sized ungulates wanders across its axis, the trap triggers not by sight, but by magnetic induction. As the herd-beast’s turning bevels pass overhead, the distortion in the local field trips the carbide sear. 

The truss snaps straight. The strike is instantaneous, delivered with the kinetic energy of a falling drop-hammer. It does not crush the grazer’s skull—the grazer has none—but drives a chisel-headed wedge directly between the ungulate’s counter-rotating knee-gears. The result is catastrophic mechanical lock. The gears, turning with thousands of foot-pounds of torque, have nowhere for their energy to go; the bronze teeth shear in a deafening, dry screech that echoes off the steel cliffs for miles. The grazer stumbles, its internal drive-shaft snapping within its housing with a dull, subterranean *whump*.

The hunter does not eat the sheared teeth. It does not eat the metal at all. Instead, it maintains its clamp on the locked joint, drinking the colossal burst of frictional heat and hysteresis that radiates through the victim’s twisted frame as its internal flywheels grind themselves to a halt against their seized journals. For three weeks, the predator stays welded to the dying beast, its own structural braces glowing a dull, cherry red in the twilight as it siphons the kinetic death-spasm of the ungulate’s stopped motion. When the carcass is finally cold—when every internal spring has uncoiled and the basalt flywheels have surrendered their last revolution to the stillness—the predator unclamps itself with a hydraulic groan, drags its heavy, triangular frame three yards through the furrowed dust, and begins slowly, atom by atom, to re-tension its internal spine against the sun.

Above them both, the violet sky is not empty.

Suspended thousands of feet up, where the hot air rises against the cold weight of the upper atmosphere, hang the planes. They are creatures composed entirely of rolled brass foil and razor-thin spring shim, thirty yards across and barely thicker than a fingernail. They have no motors, no flapping wings; they are biological gliders that stay aloft on the fierce, upward-rushing thermal columns generated by the red iron flats. Seen from below, they are nearly invisible—intermittent flashes of blinding white light as their mirrored surfaces catch the sun.

They do not hunt the ground. They are acoustic grazers. The valley below is an amphitheater of enormous kinetic energy; the endless idling of the bedrock combustion chambers and the slow groaning of the sixty-foot magnetite flywheels send sound waves upward through the thin air as high-pressure shockfronts. The brass planes angle their flat, foil bellies against these acoustic waves, riding them like surf, harvesting the vibrational energy directly through their skin. 

From their trailing edges trail copper wire-filaments, thousands of feet long, that hang down toward the earth like the tentacles of a Portuguese man-o'-war. As the planes drift over the highest pinnacles of the steel cliffs, these wires brush the rock. The discharge is immediate: blue, silent arcs of static electricity three hundred yards long that stitch sky to stone, dumping the excess potential the planes collect from the sun's raw radiation. The planes do not breed in pairs; when a sheet grows too wide and thin from the hammering of the thermals, a gust shears it along a line of structural perforation. The two halves tumble away from each other, self-stabilizing as they fall, until their own aerodynamic tension snaps them flat again—two smaller, identical sheets that tilt their faces back toward the violet dome and begin to climb.

Near the center of the plateau, where the red dust gives way to a vast, cracked sheet of pure industrial graphite, lies the nursery.

Here, the ground is hot enough to melt lead, though it stays bone-dry. The things that gather here are embryonic: massive, crude ingots of raw white iron, six feet across, that have no form yet—no pistons, no gear-teeth, no exhaust ports. They have been calved from the cracks in the hematite crust, pushed up from the deep catalytic presses below like heavy bubbles of slag.

They do not grow through cell division. They grow through crystallization and acoustic entrainment. The rhythmic thrum of the deep-dock engine sound that shakes the basin acts upon these cooling ingots like a master mold. As they sit in the blinding light, their molecules rearrange themselves along the lines of the valley's vibration. Over centuries, a ridge forms along the ingot's spine; the ridge separates into eccentric cams; the cams hollow out the stone beneath them to create journals of polished basalt. 

Around these cooling iron calves, the older beasts tread with glacial care. The train-length engines circle the graphite pan, their twin chimneys spouting dry, sulfur-laden soot that settles over the young like a protective blanket of lubricating carbon. They nudge the ingots with their front intake grates, testing the tone of the metal with dull, exploratory hammer-taps of their iron chassis. 

If an ingot rings false—if an internal void or a fracture line has ruined its balance—the mother-engine does not nurse it. She rolls her forward treads directly over it, grinding the brittle iron back into red dust with three hundred tons of deadweight, returning it to the floor of the pan. 

But if the calf rings true—a clear, sustained chime that hangs in the dry ozone air for twenty seconds—she backs away. She leaves it to bake. In forty years, the heat will have finished annealing its wrist-pins. Its first piston will draw in a dry breath of iron dust; its chimneys will curl upward to meet the violet light; and it will take its first step onto the mirror-polished stone, heavy and slow and without history, humming the only song the valley has ever known.
