The things that arrived first were not dropped from above; they were already inside the mountain when it was sliced. 

In the deep matrix of the volcanic tuff, sealed in tiny, gas-blown cavities since the mountain’s last eruption, were the desiccated cysts of stone-mites—pale, globular creatures no larger than a grain of crushed salt, blind and without pigment. When the adzes split the blocks and the court was sunk into the ground, the constant, greasy vapor woke them. They came out of the freshly planed walls by the thousands, not in a swarm, but as a slow, flour-like drift that settled into the joint-lines to feed on the damp mineral salts sweating from the rock. They have no mouths to speak of; they simply press their translucent bodies against the cut faces and soften the rock’s skin, leaving behind microscopic tracks of clear, insoluble grease that makes the stone shed water like duck down.

Where there are mites, there are the herders. In the vertical seams between the great ashlar blocks—crevices so tight a copper needle could scarcely pass between them—live the long-bodied blind geckos. They are entirely white, like peeled radishes, and their ribs show through their skin like the wickerwork of a basket. Because the tuff has no natural fissures here, they do not burrow; they compress themselves, living in two dimensions against the stone faces, their bodies flattened until their heartbeats can be seen pulsing against the raw chisel-marks. When they move across the floor at night, they do not run; they slide on bellies coated in the same slick film as the puddles, leaving faint, vanishing tracks that the next condensation-cycle washes away. They have never known a shadow, because the light from the slot above has no edges, and so they have no concept of hiding—only of stillness.

Near the base of the northeastern wall, where three stones meet in an offset T-joint, the drainage slot is slightly wider than the others. Here, the floor has been modified, though so subtly that an eye looking down from the sky-slot thirty feet up would never notice. A single slab has been undercut. 

Beneath it lives a woman whose hair has taken on the slate-gray tone of the tuff. 

She is not a prisoner, nor is she an exile; she is a relict of the measuring-out. When the foundation was squared by the city’s surveyors, someone had to stay below with the line-levels and the lead bobs to watch the plumb against the settling of the mud. Then the city above grew outward instead of upward, spanning across the shaft with arches and lintels, and the stairs were never cut into the sheer stone faces. The ropes were hauled up; the scaffolding was struck and carried to the northern sector; the project simply ceased to require looking down.

She has been there long enough that the soles of her feet have lost their arches, flattening into broad, leathery pads that distribute her weight so evenly she can cross a puddle without breaking the meniscus. Her skin smells of the turnip greens—which are actually the thick, peppery taproots of a dwarf dock that grows along the wetter joint-lines. She harvests these with a scraper beaten out of a copper wedge left behind by the masons, cutting the leaves cleanly so the crowns can sprout again under the greasepaper light.

She does not speak, because there is no reverberation in the court; the raw, porous stone drinks sound like water. Over decades, her voice has withered down to a dry, clicking palate-noise she uses to call the small mud-turtles that live in the sump beneath the center flag.

These turtles are peculiar to the court, descended from three or four that washed down an open storm-gutter during a deluge in the city’s early years. They are small—no broader than the palm of a girl’s hand—with flat, smooth carapaces the exact color of wet volcanic dust. Their shells lack the high dome of river turtles; they are streamlined for shallow films of water, their legs splayed out like paddles so they can scoot across the flags with a sound like wet leather being slapped against an apron. The woman keeps them fed with rolled pellets of green moss and crushed moths. In turn, they keep the shallow saucers of condensation clean of beetle larvae, nudging through the glass-clear water with their long, snouted heads, their tiny yellow eyes blinking against the milky glare from the clouds.

In the dead corner where the ferns grow thickest, there is another presence, less defined. A low, square mound of unworked tuff blocks, laid dry without mortar, serves as her bed and her pantry. In the hollows between the blocks, she stores the paper-thin skins of the broad moths, which she peels from the walls after they have died their natural, soundless deaths. She presses them flat between two river-slates she found in the bottom silt, hundreds of them, until they form a pale, velvet-like felt that she uses to cover her chest when the cold draft drops down the shaft in the middle hours of the night.

She knows the city above only as a weight. She knows when the grain-carts pass three streets away because the water in the saucers shivers, forming concentric, circular ripples that touch the edges of the flagstones and return to the center without a splash. She knows when the seasonal rains begin above because the drainage slots begin to sigh—a long, cool respiration of damp air pushed downward through the stone pipes by the weight of the water falling on the roof-slabs far overhead.

Sometimes, a feather drifts down the oblong shaft. A sparrow’s quill, or the gray down of a city dove. It takes many minutes to fall through thirty feet of dead, unmoving air, drifting from one wall to the other like a pendulum. 

When it lands, it does not move again. The woman does not pick it up immediately. She sits on her heels by the aroids, her hands folded between her knees, watching it for hours until the condensation gathers on its barbs, weighting it down, turning it the same uniform, saturated gray as the floor, until the stone claims it, and it becomes part of the masonry’s slow, unpeopled beginning.
