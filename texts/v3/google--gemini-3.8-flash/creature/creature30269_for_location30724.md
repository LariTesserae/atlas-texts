To survive here, flesh must have the properties of wet leather, bone must be as pliable as fresh ash-wood, and life must anchor itself or be torn away into the white drop.

Nothing here flies. In air so thin and sheared by violent thermals, a feathered wing is useless; it would be stripped to the quill or snapped like dry straw. The creatures that inhabit the high framework do not drift; they *clinch*. They are creatures of tension, hydraulic suction, and terrifying grip.

### The Turnbuckles

They are the largest moving things on the pylons: communal organisms that are less like animals and more like living rigging. 

A Turnbuckle can span eighty feet. Its body is a flat, segmented ribbon of yellow-white muscle, thick as a ship’s fender and smelling faintly of turned vinegar and wet grain. It possesses no face, only clusters of black, oil-filmed pressure blisters at either extremity that read the strain running through the timber. At each end of its length, its tissues fray out into dozens of calcified, barb-tipped talons—dense as pig iron—which it drives deep into the porous grain of the green wood.

When a fresh truss sprouts from a pier and begins its blind, spear-tipped lunge across the chasm, the Turnbuckles creep after it. They move with an agonized slowness, anchoring their hind-clasps to the mother-tower and hitching their mass forward, locking their forward talons into the unhardened, lime-colored wood of the new shoot. Then they contract. 

Their internal hydraulics are terrifying; they dump fluids between their segments with wet, coughing chuffs, hauling the runaway timber out of the prevailing gale and holding it steady against the torque. They are biological stays. Hundreds of them will hang suspended over the mile-wide chasms, their pale flesh quivering under thousands of pounds of static tension, parboiled by the steam clouds that billow off the flumes. 

Sometimes the wind wins. When a cantilever shears off, the Turnbuckles anchored to it do not let go; they do not have the neural speed to unclamp. Their bodies are ripped in half with the sound of tearing sailcloth, their severed ends whipping wildly in the wind, spraying clear, starch-thick blood into the squalls before retracting into the safety of the fluted pillars.

### The Scald-Crabs

In the roaring, open-topped flumes, where water hot enough to cook an egg rushes downhill at the speed of a falling stone, life takes a mineral form. 

The Scald-Crabs are not true crustaceans, but nothing else describes their flat, heavy geometry. They are the size of wagon wheels, wedge-shaped and hydrodynamically smooth, clad in thick, semi-translucent carapaces made of the very silicates dissolved in the water. They look like poured bottle-glass, frosted by heat and scouring sand.

They do not swim; they walk the floors of the flumes against the current, their heavy, pick-axe legs biting into the woven floorboards. Their entire biology is predicated on the violent through-put of boiling broth. Their bellies are open filters that sift out the strings of vegetable gum and embryonic seed-pods that the pylon cores churn upward from below. 

To remain in the flume is life; to be washed over the lip is death. If a crab loses its footing, it skims along the smooth surface like a thrown stone, shoots out into the open sky where an aqueduct terminates, and falls for three minutes through the sulfur fog before shattering on the unseen world below. 

Because of this, their mating is a desperate, violent act of locking: males and females drive their silicate spurs directly through each other's outer shells, bolting themselves together into a heavy, four-ton raft of living crystal that sits anchored against the boiling current while their gametes wash downstream together.

### The Joiners

In the broad, springy courtyards and along the spiraling stairways that ring the hexagonal vents, there is a bipedal silhouette. 

They are thin—skeletally, impossibly thin—standing ten to twelve feet tall, their limbs long and double-jointed, covered in a hairless, parchment-colored skin that does not sweat. Sweat would be a fatal waste of moisture in this boiling, desiccating draft; instead, their skin secretes a clear, bitter wax that hardens into a glaze, sealing their vital fluids against the thin air. 

Their heads are narrow, wedge-like, and dominated by four sunken nostrils fitted with internal, fibrous baffles to cool and filter the scald of the clouds. They have no eyes; the high, flat, shadowless light of the white sky offers little contrast, and the steam would blister an exposed cornea in hours. Instead, their faces are covered in fine, hair-like acoustic antennae that tremble in the gale, reading the micro-fractures in the wood beneath them. They "see" the framework as a vast, singing web of structural stress.

They carry no tools of stone or metal, for there is none to be had. Their tools are their own secretions and the bodies of other things. 

They are the system’s frantic, ritualistic maintenance crew. It is not clear whether they designed the towers, or if the towers grew them as an immune response, but their entire existence is spent in a state of emergency triage. They move across the lurching, howling cantilevers with a terrifying, rhythmic gait, their long, four-toed feet prehensile and tipped with recurved horn that clicks into the braided hawsers. 

When a beam begins to whistle—the acoustic prelude to cavitation and structural fracture—a team of Joiners will swarm the site. They carry bladder-sacks of live, masticated wood-pulp, chewed from the succulent growth-tips and mixed with a fast-curing, caustic saliva they generate in their secondary stomachs. Working directly in the teeth of the thermal gales, suspended over the blue void by single ropes of plaited reed lashed around their waists, they caulk the whistling cracks. They drive wooden wedges into split seams with the heels of their hardened, calcified palms, striking with the force of wooden mauls. 

They do not sleep on the open decks. When the wind rises to a hurricane pitch, the Joiners retreat into the hollow cores of the buttresses. There, in the dark, hot, deafening chimneys where the water-pipes shudder and the raw sap rises, they hang themselves from the internal ribbing by their foot-claws, suspended like bats in the updrafts of warm steam. They hook their bodies together, intertwining their long limbs to form living shock-absorbers that dampen the violent vibrations of the tower’s wooden skeleton.

They are completely mute. The ambient noise of the architecture—the bellow of the water, the artillery-crack of green timber, the moan of lignum under torque—would swallow any vocal chord. But when they touch the wood, they communicate through their palms, drumming on the tense fibers of the hawsers in rapid, staccato codes. A Joiner standing on a raked balcony at the edge of the void can feel the tap-drumming of another five miles away, transmitted through three arching trusses and a pylon core: a silent, urgent chronicle of where the wood is holding, where it is green and wet, and where, in the next second, the world is about to tear itself apart.
