The kerosene-washers go out at three in the morning, though the light at that hour is no different from the light at noon: the same flat, white tallow-wash that leaves no grease upon the boards.

They are called the Caulkers, though there are no boats here and nothing to float. They are men only in the sense that a dry lath is a branch. Their skin has the specific, yellow-gray dullness of birch veneer that has spent three winters under salt, and they do not sweat; when the dry air at five thousand meters pulls the moisture from their lips, what appears is a crust of white, bitter salt that tastes of copper filings and alum. If a Caulker splits his thumb with an adze, there is no spurt of crimson. The wound opens like dry firewood, revealing a pale, stringy meat that does not bleed so much as it leaks a drop of gray, lymphatic resin that hardens before it can hit the floor. 

They wear boots with soles four inches thick, carved from single blocks of end-grain ash, strapped to their calves with tarred hemp. If they wore leather, the floor would drink the grease from it in six hours, leaving the hide brittle as scorched horn.

The Caulkers do not talk across the timber-shelf. Sound here is an impact that rattles the teeth, carried by the dense heartwood rings more efficiently than by the air. Instead, they signal with long ash poles tipped with lead wedges. They strike the ground—*once, twice, long-pause, once*—and forty miles of compressed cambium hums the report into the soles of every other crew between the boiler-houses and the bristle-edge. 

Their life is entirely one of tension and lubrication.

***

In the long aisles between the reciprocating push-rods, where the heat from the copper steam jackets keeps the frost from setting, other things find an existence that cannot properly be called animal.

There are the *Slide-Ticks*. They are not insects, though they have the hard, brown shells of beetles that have been pressed under an iron weight until their backs are square. They do not have legs; they have grooved undersides lined with short, stiff brass wire that fits exactly into the machine-grooves of the guide rails. They live on the grease thrown from the lignum-vitae bearings—not by eating it, for they have no guts, but by absorbing its mineral oils through capillary action into their dry interior piths. As long as a push-rod moves, a Slide-Tick will ride its stroke: sixteen feet forward, sixteen feet back, twenty times a minute, month after month. If the engine stops, the Tick’s fiber core dries out within an hour; it stiffens, curls like a shaving off a carpenter’s plane, and drops into the sump to be ground into black meal by the crossheads.

Lower down, in the horizontal seams where the gray cellulose layers have been pulled slightly apart by the torque of the stamping mills, live the *Gaugers*.

A Gauger looks, at first glance, like an abandoned bundle of surveyor’s tape that has gotten damp and swollen. It has no head, no eyes, and no mouth. It is a ribbon of pale, segmented membrane, ten or twelve feet long and half an inch thick, ribbed with cold, flexible cartilage that matches the growth rings of the floor. It moves by thermal contraction: when the subterranean boilers vent their sulfurous exhaust into the pith-layers, the Gauger shrinks by three percent, drawing its tail forward; when the cold air from the northern bristle-sea sweeps the cracks, it expands, pushing its front end into the next fissure. 

The Gaugers are the only things that know the true depth of the floor. A man might drop a plumb line three hundred yards into a borehole and feel the lead weight fetch up against an obstruction, but a Gauger can work its ribbon-like length down through the pre-oxygen heartwood for miles, following the microscopic planes of cleavage where the ancient tree once fought against the dry cold of the upper atmosphere. When a Gauger dies, it does not rot; it merely dries until it is as hard as vulcanite, becoming an integral part of the seam it inhabited, an extra ring laid down in the dark.

***

Then there are the *Ochs*.

The district was not named for a memory; it was named for the things that walk the northern line.

They are not cattle. They are massive, four-legged framing-engines, twenty feet high at the shoulder, built of box-girder iron and solid baulks of cured larch that have been bolted together with two-inch drift pins. At their center is no heart, but an oscillating steam cylinder that takes its pressure through a trailing copper umbilical dragged along the rail-grooves.

A single Och weighs forty tons. It has no brain, but it possesses a balance derived from three heavy mercury-pendulums hung within its belly. Its four feet are broad, iron-shod rockers designed to compress the loose cambium bristles before the circular saws arrive to square them off. The Och walks with a terrible, arthritic deliberation: a pneumatic lift of the foreleg that makes the copper pipes hiss like geese, an eight-foot stride forward against the grain, and then a downward hammer-blow that shakes the grease-cups on the gantries two miles away.

Each Och is tended by two *Stokers* who live in a small, zinc-lined cab suspended between the rear legs. These men do not leave the machine for seven months at a stretch. Their eyes are red and rheumy from the sulfur-damp that rises through the floor-cracks, and their hearing is so entirely ruined by the rhythmic *thung-chunk* of the rocker-feet that they can only perceive the speech of others if a tin megaphone is pressed directly against the bones of their foreheads. 

Inside the cab, they eat what is sent down the gantry-wires in lidded tin buckets: dried beef-suet that has been melted into parched barley, cold and white like lard, and tea brewed from the bitter cambium-water that weeps from the Och’s own levelling jacks. They smell of raw kerosene, damp elderberries, and the specific, vinegar-like stink of horse-hair stuffing that has been wet with steam for fifty years.

The Stokers have a task that never ends: they must watch the floor ahead of the Och’s rockers. 

The wood does not always yield cleanly. Occasionally, under the pressure of the four-hundred-foot saws working to the east, the grain will *buckle*. A blister of solid cellulose, fifty feet wide and six feet high, will rise from the flat in the space of three seconds with a sound like a four-pounder cannon firing into an empty cask. If an Och steps on a rising blister, the machine will tip, its copper umbilical will snap, and its forty tons of larch and iron will settle into the splintered fibers, where the cambium will begin to digest the wood of its frame before the recovery cranes can be hauled into position.

Therefore, the Stokers watch for the *creeps*. 

A creep is an area of five or six yards where the wood begins to sweat oil instead of water. When they see that dark, iridescent sheen appear on the shaved black shelf, the Stokers throw the reverse lever. The Och groans, its pendulums crash against the bronze stops with a sound like church bells dropped into mud, and it retreats three paces, its iron feet clicking into the notches it has already pressed.

***

Nothing here reproduces by seed. 

There are no cones, no spores, no eggs. When a Caulker grows too stiff to swing the kerosene mop—when his knees will no longer bend against the nap of the floor—he does not die in a bed. There are no beds in Ochs-and-Tallow that are not made of the same four-inch larch planks as the foundations.

He simply sits down upon a cross-tie near one of the primary steam manifolds, where the condensation runs warm and the sulfur smell is thick enough to drown the iron-fever in his throat. He sits with his back against the copper lagging, his ash-soled boots tucked under him, watching the horizontal push-rods go back and forth: sixteen feet out, sixteen feet back.

Within three days, the pale fuzz that the kerosene keeps at bay begins to touch his cuffs. It does not grow like mold; it climbs like a dry, mineral salt along the thread-lines of his woolens. By the fifth day, his boots have bonded with the timber-shelf. The resin that leaks from the heartwood below finds the dry meat of his feet through the peg-holes in the ash soles, rising through the bone of his shins as through an empty straw.

The others do not move him. To pry a man up once the cambium has tasted him would require a three-ton hydraulic jack and would leave a ragged check in the floor that might take forty barrels of pitch to seal. 

They simply work around him. When the kerosene-washers come through with their long mops, they lift their poles slightly as they pass the manifold, leaving a small, dry island where he sits. Over the months, the steam and the cold plane him down. His coat turns the color of the iron gantries; his face becomes the gray-brown of the compressed growth rings that run beneath him for three miles down into the airless dark.

Eventually, the saw-lines reach the manifold. The turnbuckles are slacked off; the massive structural timbers are unpinned and hauled north on the gantry-cars; the iron flywheel with its rooted, vascular spokes is cracked into scrap with dynamite. 

Then come the saws with teeth the size of church pews.

They pass through the manifold, through the copper pipes, through the lignum-vitae bearings, and through the dry, sitting figure without a change in their note. The great steel blades do not know bone from heartwood; everything here has the same density under the tooth. The saws leave behind only the shaved, glistening plain of black timber-shelf, damp and faintly resinous, its long, straight grain stretching away north toward the bristling edge of the world, perfectly flat, perfectly clear, waiting for the rails to be spiked down.
