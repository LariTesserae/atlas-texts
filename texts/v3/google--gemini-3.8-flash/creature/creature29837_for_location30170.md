To understand what lives here, one must first understand that to possess a flat sole is a deformity, and to reach upward is an instinct that must be broken in infancy.

Nothing here stands. Standing is a sail caught by the gale; standing is an invitation to the mile-long drop. Everything that endures has either surrendered its height or found a way to weld itself into the fissures where the wind shears harmlessly overhead.

***

### The Grazers of the Seam

At the base of the food web are the things that drink the rift's pale milk.

The gum that weeps from the split wood does not flow like resin; under the sudden, freezing drafts, it forms curdled, rind-thick sheets that cling to the lee-angles of the bark like salt licks. Feeding upon these are the *curd-slugs*—though they share little with the soft, wet mollusks of lowland mud. They are the length of an oarsman’s leg and as tough as cured pigskin, armored against the ice by an overlapping series of translucent, keratinous tiles. 

They have no eyes; their snouts are broad, flat spades of horn that pry into the gum-seams, scraping up the bitter, vinegar-scented frost. Because the sap is pressurized by the violent hydraulic thrust of the plant, occasionally a rupture will burst while a slug is feeding. When this happens, the sap hits them with the force of a river jet, freezing them solid in their own food before the wind can tear them loose. Their frozen bodies remain for weeks, glazed into the amber rind, until the bark-weasels gnaw their bellies out from behind the shell.

Behind the slugs come the mites—not microscopic, but the size of hazel-nuts, clicking over the wood on eight hair-fine, glass-stiff legs. They are entirely dry, resembling drops of frosted resin themselves. They follow the run-off channels, their needle-mouthed carapaces drinking the water pearls before the wind can blow them into mist. When the wood groans and shudders, the mites do not run; they fire a drop of fast-curing excretion through a spinneret in their tail, tacking themselves to the wood like iron studs, waiting out the shockwave with their legs tucked tightly beneath their bellies.

***

### The Runners

The grease-furred hunters of the runners are known to the camps simply as *hook-dogs*, though they are closer to minks stretched long by starvation. 

Their skeletons have no rigid collarbones; they can compress their ribcages until they are no thicker than a man’s hand, sliding into the shrinkage-faults where the wood has pulled away from itself. Their fur is not soft—it is stiff and wet with a musk that smells like rancid fish oil, which repels the freezing drizzle and prevents them from icing over. 

A hook-dog never leaps. To leap is to let go, and to let go is to fall until you starve. Instead, they flow. They possess five digits on each foot, each terminating in a deeply recurved, blackened claw that fits precisely into the fibrous striations of the green lignum. They run down the sheer vertical faces head-first, their back legs trailing like broken rudders, their teeth already bared. 

When they take a curd-slug, they do not kill it immediately; they sever the muscular anchor that pins it to the bark, drag the heavy, thrashing thing into a splinter-cave, and wedge it between the grain-layers. There, in the windless dark, they eat into the soft under-venter while the slug’s mantle is still rhythmically pulsing against the timber. 

They are fiercely territorial, but they do not bark or growl—vocal noise is swallowed instantly by the roaring flute of the gap. Instead, they signal by raking their claws across the tight tension-fibers of the bark, producing a shrill, rhythmic *skrrt-skrrt-skrrt* that vibrates through the beam for a hundred yards, warning other dogs that this particular crotch of the frame is claimed.

***

### The High Drifters

Higher up, where the cloud-ceiling scrapes against the upper knuckles, life leaves the bark behind entirely.

The leathery kites that roost in the joints are only half-creatures of the wood; their true home is the slipstream. They have no marrow in their ribs; their bones are honeycombed with empty space that whistles when the wind blows through their nostrils. Because they are blind, their faces are dominated by enormous, leaf-shaped nasal discs of pale, translucent gristle that twitch constantly, reading the air pressure like an open palm reads heat.

They do not fly by flapping—they have no breast-muscles to spare. They launch themselves sideways into the abyss on the back of an updraft, their leathery, dry wings locking into place with a dry *snick* of bone hinges. They drift on the ascending columns of warm, rotten gas that belch out of the bottomless dark, circling without effort for two or three days at a time. 

They feed on the *plume-spiders*—vast, diffuse colonies of gossamer spinners that drift through the void in tangles twenty yards wide, trailing millions of sticky, microscopic filaments to strain the wind for blown spores and loose lichen-dust. When a flock of kites hits a drift of plume-silk, it is an silent, tumbling frenzy: the kites rake through the drifting mass with their tooth-lined bills, swallowing silk, spiders, and the wind-borne chaff trapped within it, before banking hard to catch the lee-vortex of the next descending rib.

### The Tethered

The humans who inhabit the crotches call themselves the *Knot-bound*, or simply the *Tied*.

They have lived so long upon the framework that their anatomy has begun to mirror its demands. They rarely exceed five feet in height. Their chests are enormous, barrel-shaped domes developed to mine the thin, iron-tasting air for oxygen, but their legs are short, bowed, and often withered below the calf. Their feet are not instruments for walking; the big toes have grown thick, opposable, and curved, capable of clamping around a three-inch branch-vine with the hydraulic tenacity of a vice. 

A Knot-bound child is never put down. From the month of its birth, it is lashed into the harness of its mother’s belly with cured sinew. By the time it can reach, it is given an antler-chisel and taught to peg its own harness into the lignum. If a child drops a tool, it is not beaten, but it goes without food for the day; to drop something is to acknowledge that the abyss exists, and to acknowledge the abyss is to let it pull you in.

Their society has no use for horizontal space. Their homes—the inverted, hull-shaped shelters—are not places of leisure, but survival-cells lashed into the angles where three struts cross at forty-five degrees. The interior of a shelter is a web of slung hammocks, smelling of old seal-grease, sour smoke, and the perpetual, wet vinegar of the plant. A family does not sit down to dine; they suspend themselves from bone-rings driven into the ceiling-ribs, chewing strips of dried slug-mantle and kite-breast while the floorless space beneath their floor-skins groans like a wounded bull.

The most important member of any camp is the *Listener*. 

The Listener does not hunt, and she does not mend ropes. Her duty is to lie face-down upon the bare wood of the main truss for fourteen hours at a stretch, her cheek pressed hard against the frozen green grain. Through the side of her skull, she listens to the mountain-tree speak. 

She knows the difference between a *swelling groan*—which means a thermal draft is coming up from the rot-floor to ease the tension—and the *hollow, crystalline ping* that precedes a shear. When the Listener strikes three double-taps on the truss with her stone mallet, the camp moves. They do not pack their shelters; there is no time. They simply unhook their primary lines, clip themselves to the traveler-hawsers that run along the lee-fissures, and scramble along the grain like frantic lice, abandoning everything they own just seconds before fifty square miles of timber twitches three paces to the north, obliterating the crotch and everything trapped inside it.

The dead are never cast into the drop. To drop a corpse is considered the ultimate impiety—a surrender to the emptiness that wants them all. 

Instead, when a man dies, his kin find a deep tension-fault where the green wood is wet and weeping. They pry the split open with wedges of petrified heartwood, force the body deep into the wound, and pull the wedges. 

The tree, in its blind, colossal need to heal, closes around the corpse. Within three seasons, the wood grows over the limbs, sealing the bone and the kite-skin into the heart of the spar. The Knot-bound believe that these dead become part of the framework's bracing—that their marrow strengthens the grain, that their teeth hold the splinters tight, and that when the gale screams through the miles of pale, frozen ribs, it is the voices of their grandfathers shouting back into the storm, refusing to fall.
