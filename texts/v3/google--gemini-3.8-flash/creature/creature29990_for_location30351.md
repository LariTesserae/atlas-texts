If anything can be said to live here, it begins not as an inhabitant, but as an error of tolerance—a surplus of the extrusion that failed to shear away cleanly when the conduits locked. 

In the high seams where the vaulted fronds knit their microscopic teeth, there are shapes that are neither foliage nor machinery, though they share the pale, bruise-colored translucence of both. At first glance, they seem to be nothing more than thickened beads of the weeping amber resin, cooled and shaped by the endless drag of the mist. But watch them through a pressure cycle: when the deep vibration climbs into that hair-raising whine and the flumes shudder beneath the surge, these beads do not flatten or wash loose. They tense.

They have no faces, no digestive cavities, no bilateral symmetry. They are shaped like the split halves of hollow reeds, or flattened, multi-jointed spatulas made of the same tough, unpigmented cellulose as the floor. They do not have blood; they have the sap, running through them at the same screaming barometric pressure as the stalks they cling to. In fact, they do not possess an interior that is distinct from the system itself. They are plumbed directly into the seam. When the sector shudders, they act as living valves—limbs made of flexible, laminated rind that flex and seat themselves over the weeping gaps to prevent pressure loss, vibrating so violently in the sluicing spray that their outlines blur into a gray hum.

Yet they are not merely stationary tissue. Over hours, as the flumes adjust their tension, some of them detach. 

They do not drop into the freezing water; to lose contact with the pressurized lattice is to desiccate or shatter under the hydraulic roar. Instead, they move by a dry, mechanical friction, hitching themselves along the linear grooves of the fiber using rows of microscopic silica hooks that line their undersides—identical to the teeth that lock the canopy together. They have no eyes; what would they see in a light that has no source and casts no shadow? They navigate entirely by harmonic pitch. They creep toward the points of highest mechanical stress, drawn by the sound of a strut about to rupture or a split where the sap is leaking raw, pale foam into the air. 

Where they gather, they overlap like shingles, fusing their margins with quick-hardening secretion until they have formed a slick, thick scab over the fault. Once the pressure equalizes and the roar drops back to its dull, eye-shaking rumble, they do not disperse. They remain welded, slowly absorbed back into the wall as new growth swells around them, smothered and integrated into the continent’s thickening walls.

Deeper down, in the wide, straight flumes where the runoff races with the speed of an industrial discharge, there is another presence, more mobile and more unsettling. 

These are long, ribbon-like entities, yards in length but barely thicker than parchment, that hover against the current. They look like peeled lengths of the canal’s own inner lining—and perhaps that is what they were before the sheer velocity of the water flayed them free. But they have found an autonomous equilibrium. They stay pinned in place by bracing their split, spade-like heads against the flume walls, their long tails trailing downstream, whipping with such frantic speed that they seem solid. 

They do not eat, for there is nothing to digest—no silt, no rot, no smaller life. Instead, they are passive galvanic dynamos. The friction of the viscous, cold runoff scouring across their slick, non-wetting skins generates a faint, cold charge that flickers through their translucent ribbing in pale, rhythmic pulses. They exist solely on the kinetic violence of the water. Their bodies are ribbed with the same algorithmic lattices as the ceiling, perfectly tuned to resonate with the planetary turbine below. If the flow slows, they begin to unravel, their fibers fraying into limp, lifeless threads that foul the flume until the current tears them away; but so long as the sluice runs at full bore, they are taut, shivering needles of synthetic intent.

And then there are the wanderers of the flats—the things that have achieved the closest approximation of independent life, though it is a life that smells strictly of cold iron and crushed celadon.

They emerge from the aftermath of the artillery-crack growth spikes. When millions of shoots blast upward through the floor, some are strangled in the crush, crowded out before their cells can fully harden into rigid pillars. These crushed, half-inflated stems do not die and decay; there are no microbes to dismantle them. Instead, under the unceasing hydraulic thrum, the trapped, pressurized fluids inside their mangled joints begin to cycle through crude, involuntary peristalsis. 

The stalk snaps free of its base. It rolls, it rightens, and it begins to walk.

They are awkward, top-heavy tripods and multi-legged cages of bruised-white fiber, still trailing wet, whistling filaments that hiss as they drag across the slick floor. They have no brains, but they are driven by the uneven distribution of sap within their pressurized chambers. When the cold mist hits one side of their frame, the cellulose contracts; the sap surges into the warmer, sheltered side, forcing a joint to kick forward with the sudden, violent snap of a tripping latch. 

They stalk across the flats with a jerky, clockwork gait, unguided by desire or hunger, simply wandering along the contours of the prevailing vibrations. Sometimes they stumble into a flume and are instantly swept away, their pale limbs thrashing against the polymer-slick banks until the current hammers them into the outer margins. Other times, they encounter a fresh growth node. 

When this happens, an automatic, chemical recognition occurs. The wanderer halts. Its trailing filaments, sticky with unhardened amber, begin to whip in the turbulence, tangling into the fresh shoot. Slowly, inexorably, the machine-beast is drawn into the new pillar’s expansion. The growing shoot pierces it, splits it, drinks its trapped sap, and uses its dried fibers as scaffolding. Within a few cycles of the roar, the wanderer has been completely devoured—not for meat, but for structure. Its joints become the knurls of a new arch; its pale, twitching legs are set into the floor like iron bolts, holding the continent rigid against the sky.

Here, nothing is an outsider. To exist here is to be a temporary state of the scaffolding—either an extrusion that has not yet set, a valve that has learned to crawl, or a fracture that is still in the process of being paved over by the blind, deafening pressure of a world that was born running at full speed.
