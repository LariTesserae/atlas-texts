The organisms that inhabit the middle air—those grape-skinned cruisers with the pale, rib-caged contours—do not drift by chance. Their buoyancy is an electrical appetite. Within their bloated dorsal bladders is an oily, non-conductive lipid that expands when exposed to the high-frequency leakage of the pylons. They are drawn to the spars not merely for the moss, but for the warmth of the fields; when a cruiser drifts too far out into the dead center of a gulf, where the induction fades, its bladder cools, contracts, and it begins a long, spiraling fall toward the slate. Thus they stay tethered to the metal by invisible margins, circling the shafts like fat, slow moths around an unlit flame.

Their mouths are not set with teeth, but with overlapping plates of dark, flexible horn that shear the aerophytic mats down to the bare alloy with a sound like someone slicing wet leather. They have no eyes; the bruised twilight offers nothing worth looking at. Instead, their snouts are covered in arrays of fine, gelatinous pits that read the shifting electrostatic gradients of the air. When the induction current surges before a cooling cycle, these pits tingle, and the herd reacts: thousands of them turn together, their translucent oars beating out of rhythm to throw their bulk under the broad, overhanging gutters of the spars, tucking their fringes flat against their flanks to wait out the falling deluge.

The water, when it comes, brings down the *kettles*.

These are the tiny, hard-shelled entities of the horizontal lines. Seen closely, they are not insects, though they have the three-part symmetry of wasps. Their shells are neither chitin nor bone, but a dense, cold composite of scavenged copper and zinc, which they file from the pylon coatings with hardened ventral rasps and incorporate into their carapaces. They are parasites of the city’s unworked surfaces. By the millions, they line the underside of the transit rails, locking their hooked legs into the microscopic grain of the steel. Their endless, nerve-shearing stridulation is an act of drying: they vibrate their flight-cases at thousands of cycles per minute to shed the atmospheric damp that would otherwise bridge their joints with verdigris and freeze them in place.

When the six-hour condensation breaks, the kettles do not drop. They flatten. Their curved shells are hydro-dynamically neutral, shaped like river pebbles. The thousands of tons of warm water that tear down the vertical tracks merely wash over them, clearing out their dead and carrying away their gray, egg-laden frass, which rains down into the void as a nutrient-rich mist.

It is this mist that feeds the gulf below.

Down on the floor, the black slate is not as dead as it appears from the struts. It is kept clean by the *stilts*. 

These are creatures whose bodies are no larger than a clenched fist, but whose legs are three meters long, thin as piano wire and made of a springy, fibrous cartilage that rings like iron when it strikes the stone. They are solitary, blind, and nearly weightless. A single stilt can stand motionless for days in the pitch-dark center of a five-mile void, its thread-like limbs resting on four points of the slate, reading the vibrations of the architecture.

When a swimmer dies of age or is struck loose by the wash, its heavy, waterlogged carcass does not bounce; it hits the newly split stone with a wet, echoing report that carries for leagues through the floor. Within minutes, the stilts are moving. They do not run; they bound across the black desert in low, parabolic leaps, their needle-feet tapping the slate in a dry, rhythmic code. Dozens will converge upon a single fallen body before its grape-colored flesh has ceased quivering. 

They do not eat the meat directly. Instead, they pierce the skin with hollow, hypodermic mouthparts and pump into the carcass a sour, pale enzyme that converts the muscle into a clear, rapidly setting gel. They pack this gel into spheres, roll them across the stone using their middle limbs, and bury them in the narrow expansion joints between the slate slabs—the only cracks that exist on the floor. There, safe from the boiling heat of the next induction cycle, their grubs hatch in the dark, feeding on the preserved jelly until their legs grow long enough to lever them out of the seams.

Higher, where the architecture leaves the open air and disappears into the tectonic cap, there is another kind of life, one that belongs to the original design, or has at least learned its grammar. 

In the hollow guts of the pylons—vast vertical shafts meant for elevator trunks that were never hung—live the *weavers of the core*. They are few, perhaps only a few hundred per tower, and they are long-lived. Their bodies are pale, human-proportioned, but stretched and slack, without the muscular density required by high gravity; they move through the interior service-ladders with an effortless, boneless reach, their skin the color of lard and their fingers tipped with broad, spade-like nails that can peel the transit cables down to the copper lead.

These beings do not speak; the acoustic roar of the basin would make voice impossible, and their ears have long since closed, scarred over into thick, cartilaginous knobs that deaden the machinery's shrieking induction. They communicate through the cable itself, pressing their bare foreheads against the vibrating steel to sense the frequency shifts. They know when a cooling cycle is coming by the change in the tower’s pitch, thirty minutes before the first droplet beads upon the outer flange.

They live on the swimmer-meat, which they harvest by hanging long, weighted lines of plaited fern-bark from the exterior maintenance scuttles. When a drifting swimmer brushes against a line, the barbed hooks set into its grape-pale rind, and the weavers haul it slowly, hand over hand, up through the bruised twilight into the grease-scented safety of the shaft. They dry the meat upon the warm induction coils, turning it into black, saltless strips that smell of river mud and metal shavings.

They do not consider the city unfinished. They do not have a word for *builder*, nor do they imagine that the sky was ever anything other than a soot-colored ceiling leaking heat. For them, the towers are not objects placed in a landscape, but the landscape itself: an endless system of vertical plains, greasy with life, continually rinsed by an mechanical sky that breathes every six hours with the cold regularity of a lung. When they look down from the maintenance lips into the mile-deep drop, they see nothing missing. The empty stone below is simply where the water goes to die, and the screaming air between the spars is full.
