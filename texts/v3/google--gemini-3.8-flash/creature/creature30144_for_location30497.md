The silt-carapaced builders do not experience themselves as individuals so much as localized thickenings of a single, continuous pressure. 

To look closely at one of them is to see an anatomy that has abandoned the fluid, slipshod shortcuts of softer worlds. Their bodies are long, low-slung, and three-segmented, held off the razor-edges of the basalt by six legs jointed like surveyor’s calipers. Where their limbs meet the rock, they do not click; the underside of each tarsal segment is furnished with a dense turf of micro-setae—branched, hollow hairs of modified chitin that fit themselves into the microscopic fissures of the glass-sheared stone. They grip not by clamping, but by intermolecular cling. A builder walking along a vertical slab moves with the steady, adhesive calm of a drop of cold grease sliding down slate.

They have no eyes in the vertebrate sense, nor the great, bulging optical globes of common insects, which would dry out and crack within an hour of exposure to this thin, ultraviolet-soaked air. Instead, their head-plates are fitted with narrow horizontal slits lined with thin, dark plates of natural mica. Behind these shutters lie clusters of non-moist photoreceptors that read the terrace in gradients of infrared heat and the dull luminescence of mineral salts. 

They perceive their city as a relief map of temperatures: the blistering white-hot teeth of the basalt crests, the cool, bronze-warm shelters of the resin galleries, and the faint, sweet warmth of their siblings’ blood-surrogates. For blood, in their bodies, is not water. It is a dense, yellow tallow, rich in long-chain waxes and saturated hydrocarbons that remain liquid at kiln temperatures and do not boil off or evaporate when a limb is severed. When a builder is damaged, it does not bleed; it crusts immediately, the wound sealing with the dull opacity of cooling lard.

Their life begins deep below the terrace, in the cold, lightless fissures where the rift bites into the mountain’s roots. Here, where the living rock still groans with tectonic memory, the brood-chambers are kept. 

The larvae do not look like grubs; they are dry, segmented ingots of chalky white, entirely wrapped in tightly wound, calcified silk. They do not wriggle. They are tended by the *Millers*—a sub-caste of the builders whose mandibles have grown into massive, scoop-like spades of iron-hardened chitin. The Millers spend their entire existences along the subterranean gypsum veins, shaving the soft, pearlescent crystal into a flour as fine as smoke. This gypsum flour is fed into the larval cells, where the young ingest it dry. Within their guts, through a slow, anaerobic metabolism that produces no heat, the bound water molecules are stripped chemically from the calcium sulfate lattice. The gypsum becomes anhydrous plaster; the freed moisture is bound instantly to synthesized fatty acids to make tallow; and the child grows not by swelling with juice, but by laying down dense, concentric laminations of shell.

When a builder finally emerges from its chrysalis, it is already old. It possesses the complete chemical memory of its lineage, laid down in the resinous varnish that lines the brood-comb. 

There are no names among them, but there are distinct *tastes*—a complex vocabulary of stearic acids and aromatic plant-terpenes smeared upon the forehead plate. When two citizens pause on a spun-pitch bridge, their meeting has none of the hurried, jittery twitching of ants. They step close, their leg-pads adhering to the causeway, and lower their heads until their frontal carapaces make contact. The sound is a soft, leathery *tuck*. They hold the pose for three seconds, five seconds, sometimes a minute. In that contact, lipids pass from pore to pore. One informs the other of a tensile failure in Tower Four’s ninth floor; the other transfers the exact molecular signature of a seam of pure sulfur discovered three hundred meters down the cliff face. When they pull apart, their plates are faintly glossy with grease. The information has been absorbed, integrated, and filed.

Living among the towers, in a kind of languid, structural truce with the builders, are the great bellows-lizards—the *Chalk-Bellys*. 

These are heavy, blunt-snouted reptiles, their bodies flattened like flagstones so they can slip into the horizontal crevices where the basalt has sheeted off. They are the only vertebrates that have survived the drying of the terrace, and they have done so by turning their entire respiration into a mechanical bellows. Because the air is thin and holds almost no water, their nostrils are baffled with spiral cartilage lined with bone-dry, oil-secreting velvet that condenses and traps the body’s moisture before it can escape on an exhale. Their breathing is rhythmic, deep, and dry; if you put an ear to a mud wall behind which a Chalk-Belly is resting, you do not hear a breath—you hear the slow, dry slide of a card being drawn from an envelope, over and over, every twenty seconds.

The lizards do not hunt the builders. They are too slow, and their jaws are not built for crushing chitin. Instead, they are the terrace’s gardeners and thermal sinks. They graze upon the thick, white wax that coats the paddle-leaves of the tallow-fronds, scraping it away with chisel-teeth that leave parallel grooves like the marks of a wood-carver’s gouge. This scraping does not harm the plant; it stimulates the frond to secrete fresh, aromatic grease, which the builders collect from the margins of the scars to compound into their building-mortar. 

In return, the builders use the lizards as living hearths. During the brief, freezing hours of mountain twilight, when the basalt ridge gives up its kiln-heat to the empty sky, the Chalk-Bellys crawl into the communal sleeping chambers. The builders cluster over them in layers three deep, pressing their velvet feet against the lizard’s wide, heat-retaining back, absorbing the calories the reptile stored during twelve hours of baking on the upper basalt slabs.

The lizards shed skin continuously, not in long tubes, but in stiff, rectangular sheets that come away like mica flakes. These sheets are gathered by the builders before they can hit the terrace floor. They are translucent, flexible, and utterly impervious to wind. The builders use them to glaze the ventilation ports of the deep chimneys, setting the lizard-scales into wet bronze resin, creating tiny, skylit clerestories that illuminate the grain-vaults with a dull, rose-gold glare.

Higher up, stationed on the lintels and the sharpest edges of the basalt drop, are the silent ones: the mantises. 

They are immense, some nearly a meter in length, but they weigh no more than a handful of shavings. Their bodies are not green like the wet leaves of a swamp, but the color of jade that has lain in dry graves for dynasties—mottled with gray-white lichens that are not lichens at all, but crystallized urea extruded through their armor to form a sun-shield. They do not have wings; wings are sails that catch the terrible updrafts that scour the cliff face, and to be blown off the terrace into the five-thousand-meter void below is death in the thin air. 

These mantises are nearly inanimate. A mantis will select a lintel above a nursery door and remain there, without moving a single joint of its raptorial forelegs, for three changes of the season. They do not blink; their compound eyes are covered by thick, protective horn that gives them the look of blind statues carved from soapstone. They eat only once or twice in a solar cycle. They wait for the *strangers*—the occasional wind-borne wanderers carried up from the wet, rot-choked jungles of the distant low plains by the violent thermal chimneys: migrating beetles, winged ants, or desperate birds that arrive at the ridge exhausted, dehydrated, and half-mad from the pressure change.

When such a creature tumbles onto the terrace, gasping and soggy with alien moisture, the mantis does not rush. It waits until the intruder blunders near, its moist breath betraying it like a flare in the dry air. The strike is instantaneous—a single, dry whip-crack that breaks the terrace’s silence for a fraction of a second. The mantis does not eat the meat; meat rots before it can be digested here. It inserts thin, needle-like stylets into the soft joints of the prey and pumps in a concentrated lipid solution that precipitates the alien creature's water, separating it from the tissue and drawing it into its own sealed reservoir-glands. What is left—the dry, desiccated husk—is dropped into the avenue below, where the builders immediately break it into powder to temper their mortar.

Nothing in this place dies into mud. There is no decay, because there are no bacteria that can survive without a film of free water. Death here is simply a transition into architecture. 

When an elder builder feels its tallow thickening into solid paraffin, when its joints begin to grate with dry sulfate dust and its forehead-plate will no longer absorb the chemical scripts of its fellows, it does not hide. It walks to the perimeter of the active city, to the raw edge where the young basalt rift is still creeping outward into the sky. 

There, it chooses a gap—a fissure between two foundation towers, or a weakness in the arch of a new bridge. It wedges its caliper-legs deep into the basalt glass, locks them mechanically through an involuntary drying of its flexor tendons, and bows its head. It secretes from its abdominal glands a final, massive bolus of amber resin, bathing its own thorax and anchoring its head-plate to the stone. 

Within two days, the mountain wind and the kiln-sun have cured the resin into bronze. The other builders pass over it, stepping on its back with their velvet pads, their setae finding purchase on the familiar ridges of its carapace. They lay their fresh, masticated shale against its flanks, smoothing the seams with their mouthparts, working through the night, until the old builder is no longer an inhabitant of the tower, but the lintel itself—dry, silent, unyielding, holding up the street for the generation that has not yet learned to grind the stone.
