Because there is nothing to flee from and nothing to chase, whatever moves here has lost the habit of limbs.

The water that skims the dark basalt is too shallow for swimming and too warm for hurried blood. If you watch a single slab of slate-colored stone through an entire turn of the light, the surface—which at first seems as unpeopled as a mirror—begins to reveal slow, hydraulic displacements. 

Flat against the rock, beneath the glass of the runoff, dwell things the shape and thickness of soaked birch leaves. They are not leaves; they are animals of a very old, unjointed kind, soft as boiled tripe, with margins so thin they are translucent. They have no eyes, for there is nothing here that requires vigilance, only rows of pig-iron-colored pigment spots along their rims that feel the warmth of the sun and the warmer pulses rising from the cracks below. They do not crawl. They do not swim. They drink the stone. By pressing their broad, pale undersides against the wet basalt, they secrete an acid so weak it takes a decade to etch a thumbprint, absorbing the iron, the dissolved lime, and the microscopic flakes of silica shed by the eroding amphitheater. When they move, it is by an imperceptible peristalsis, a traveling pucker of flesh that carries them forward two inches between morning and dusk. When two of them meet, they do not fight or mate in any convulsive way; their wet margins touch, overlap like damp vellum, and remain together for months, sharing fluids through their skins before parting along the drift of the water.

Under the emerald pelt of the liverworts, where the heat from the deep fissures is trapped and smells faintly of bruised copper, lives another presence. 

It is a colony-beast, or perhaps a single branching mind made of white, tubular gristle. It occupies the hollow spaces where the stone has shrunk away from the moss—galleries no taller than a finger-joint, floored with hot basalt and ceilinged with root-hairs. It has no head. It pushes itself through the moss-felt in blunt, glistening lobes, white as blanched asparagus, moving toward the places where dead fern-fronds have drowned and settled into the silt. It does not devour the leaves; it coats them. It wraps the drowned black foliage in a tough, rubbery curd that excludes all air, sucking the remaining tannins out of the plant until the leaf is transformed into a brittle, translucent skeleton of pure flint. In doing this, the creature feeds on the sap-ghosts of the vegetation, but it also armors the floor. Much of what appears to be solid stone beneath the water is actually the calcified work of this burrowing white gristle, which has paved the saucer over millennia with a crust of petrified litter.

And higher up, in the open corridors between the great ferns, there are things that might be taken for pieces of the architecture until they breathe.

They rest upon the broad, rounded saddles of rock—the places shaped like sleeping shoulders. They are solitary, four or five in the whole basin, shaped like river boulders that have grown a skin of wet, gray leather. They have the bulk of hogs, but none of a hog’s grease or hunger; their skin is rough, pimpled with tiny, lime-encrusted tubercles that gather silt until moss begins to take hold on their flanks. They are nearly motionless. Their metabolisms are tuned to the sluggish drift of the warm mist. A single respiration takes forty minutes: the gray flank rises a fraction of an inch, drawing the warm, saturated air through slit-like spiracles along its belly, holds it while the blood extracts the moisture and the faint traces of volcanic gases, and then falls with a long, soft *chink* of wet stone settling against wet stone.

They have no teeth. Their mouths are wide, underslung slits like the seams in a leather boot, fringed with stiff, horn-like combs. At twilight, when the water cools just enough to sink toward the fissures, these great gray lumps ease their bellies into the flow. They do not browse the ferns—the ferns are toxic, saturated with heavy metals from the subterranean steam—but instead scoop up the oily seed-sacs that drift across the pavement. They digest them slowly, over seasons, their stomachs functioning like fermentation vats kept at blood-heat by the floor beneath them. Their dung is not filth, but a pale, chalky paste of concentrated lime that the seeping water washes out across the rock, feeding the very liverworts that carpet their resting places. 

Sometimes, one will crawl with monumental slowness toward the center of the depression. It takes days to cross thirty yards of open slate, leaving a dark, polished trail through the algae that stays visible for a human lifetime. 

When it reaches the scattered tools—the black dolerite wedge, the split shale trough—it does not disturb them. It settles against them. The animals seem drawn to the dense, foreign weight of the worked stones, perhaps because the dolerite and the river-pebbles hold the geothermal heat differently than the bedrock, remaining three degrees warmer through the chill of the high-altitude dawns. A creature will press its leather flank against the haft of the soot-black adze, wedging itself into the cleft alongside the tool, its slow, heavy heart beating once every two minutes against the plaited bark cordage. The mineral lime that weeps from the animal’s skin mingles with the mineral lime of the water, slowly thickening the translucent collar that binds the handle to the living mountain.

There are no voices here. Even when these creatures die, they do not rot in any way that makes a stench. They simply cease to lift their flanks. The warm broth washes over them; the liverwort pelt creeps up their flanks like water rising on a wharf; the white gristle beneath the moss sends exploratory fingers into their joints; and within fifty years, they are another dark, rounded hump of basalt, indistinguishable from the rest of the floor, waiting beneath the mist for rain that never turns cold.
