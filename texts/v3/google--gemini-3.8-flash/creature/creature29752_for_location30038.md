Where there is no wetness to circulate, blood cannot exist. In its place, the things that move through the cleft rely on dry clearances and the transfer of shear.

At the base of the galena reed, deep within the shadow cast by its flywheel, the stone floor is not entirely unoccupied. At first glance, what rests there appears to be a scattering of discarded off-cuts: small, rectangular ingots of bismuth-white and lead, each no longer than a finger-joint, lying parallel to the stepped right angles of the basalt. They do not twitch. They have no antennae, no eyes, no recognizable front or rear. 

Yet when the flywheel’s shadow creeps three millimeters to the east, exposing one of these ingots to the kiln-glare of the white sun, the change is instantaneous. The metal does not soften; it bows. It is a bimetallic laminate, an organism constructed of two dissimilar crystalline plates grown fused along their flat axes. As the upper face expands under the radiant heat while the lower face remains shaded and cool, the strip snaps into an arch with a sharp, dry *clack*. 

The energy of that thermal expansion does not dissipate. It triggers a minute, internal escapement—a tooth of cold-formed quartz slips one notch along a serrated ratchet of horn-like keratin. The creature hops. It bounds a clean six inches into the cooler air, flips twice in the rarefied void, and lands with a heavy, deadened *thud* precisely within the next band of shade. 

These are the *detents*. They are grazers, though they consume neither leaf nor pulp. They feed entirely on the electrostatic potential that clings to the base of the resin-weeping camshafts. When three or four of them gather around a venting succulent, they arrange themselves like small, dead weights along the seam of the rock. As the pod exhales its dry sulfur hiss, throwing off needle-thin sparks, the detents absorb the charge through ceramic-sheathed footings, their internal laminated springs coiling tighter, storing the volt-amperes against the coming of the night—if night ever touches this place—or simply packing tension into their frames until they are as stiff and perilous as drawn crossbows.

Higher up, where the copper-braided trunks press flat against the mirror-sheened fracture planes, movement takes a different form. 

There are long, multi-jointed climbers—creatures whose lineage must have once been arthropodic, but whose carapaces have long since abandoned chitin for layered sheets of pyrophyllite and graphite. They move with an excruciating, stuttering precision, like the hands of an astronomical clock. Each of their eight legs ends not in a claw, but in an optically flat pad of polished diamond-hard crystal. They do not grip the stone; they adhere to it through pure molecular contact, utilizing the Casimir effect against the clean, unweathered cleavage faces of the basalt. 

To take a step, the climber must flex a tiny piezoceramic tendon within its knee, applying a localized current that distorts the pad just enough to break the vacuum seal with a microscopic, percussive *pop*. 

Its body is long, narrow, and segmented like a machinist’s height gauge. Down its dorsal line runs a series of counter-rotating discs—thin as paper, spun from cold-drawn bronze—that whir incessantly, driven by the thermal differential between the baking direct sunlight above and the deep shadow cast by its own belly. This rotation is not waste motion; it is the animal’s nervous system. The discs act as gyroscopes, stabilizing it against the violent thermal updrafts that occasionally rip through the cleft from the chasms below, and their centrifugal drag regulates the flow of dry, powdery lubricants through its ball-socketed joints.

It feeds upon the plants, but it does not chew. Instead, it positions its mouthparts—two opposed, razor-ground wedges of tungsten-gray ore—around the tensioned copper fibers of a leaf-stem. With slow, hydraulic pressure, it does not bite through the braid; it crimps it. It deforms the metal, interrupting the flow of heat-torque toward the leaf. As the stranded leaf above begins to shudder, deprived of its cooling inversion and rapidly swelling with trapped, blistering heat, the climber drinks the thermal flux directly through its jaws, siphoning the runaway calories into its own internal flywheels until they whine at a pitch beyond the range of the thin air to carry.

Predation here is not a matter of pursuit and dismemberment; it is a problem of seizing stored kinetic energy before it can ground out.

Suspended across the narrowest gap of the split, where the rock walls face each other like two sheared dies, hangs a structure that looks like an unfinished loom. It is strung with hundreds of dry, monofilament cords drawn from some vitreous, spun-slag secretion. The spider-analogue that tends it—a heavy, disc-shaped thing of sintered iron and dull vulcanite, two feet across—hangs dead-center, perfectly motionless.

It has no sensory hairs, no web-signal lines to feel for tremors. Instead, it measures the tension in its web using the same principle as an acoustic strain gauge. The web is constantly humming, tuned to a specific resonant frequency by the draft of sulfur gas rising from the pod-thickets. 

When a climber, bloated with heat-torque, slips from a fracture plane, or when a bimetallic detent miscalculates an expansion-hop and arcs blindly across the void, it strikes the web. There is no adhesive on the cords. The web does not stick; it clutches. The impact snaps the trip-wires of heavy, spring-loaded slip-knots. The entire web collapses inward around the intruder with the instantaneous, mechanical snap of a bear trap, driven by the pre-stressed tension of the slag-glass fibers.

The predator does not inject enzymes. It descends along a central lead-screw, its descent governed by a governor-clutch that clicks rhythmically—*tik-tik-tik-tik-tak*. It clamps its wide, vice-like ventral plates over the struggling victim, pressing it flat against its own heat-sink belly. 

The struggle is brief and dry: a sound like gravel turning in an iron drum. The predator’s cold mass drinks the frantic, spinning flywheels of the climber down to a dead, frozen stop. It drains the tension from the detent's springs, relaxing the curled metal back into flat, inert leaves. When the prey has been rendered thermodynamically neutral—brought to the exact, ambient room-temperature of the stone around it—it simply falls apart, a loose collection of dry washers, needles, and mineral plates that tumble into the chasm below, joining nothing, leaving no stain on the clean rock floor.

And behind the thickest copse of the mica-fronds, half-buried in a natural groove where two tectonic facets parted without a single crumb of debris, something larger is turning.

It is not an animal in any sense that implies an individual with a face or an appetite. It is a long, continuous chain of segmented segments, linked pin-to-hole, winding endlessly through the roots of the copper-trees and back into the dark recess of the mountain. It has no head. Its links are each the size of a train car’s knuckle-coupler, cast from raw, unrefined pig iron that has never seen an open hearth, but has crystallized directly out of the stone under immense lithic torque.

It moves at the speed of the gnarled camshaft—one inch every twenty minutes. As it crawls, it scores the basalt, cutting fresh, immaculate right-angle grooves with hardened cutters that form its underside. It is not building anything; it is simply the physical expression of the ridge’s internal pressure, an extruded, living gear-train driven by the deep mantle below, surfacing into this dry, attic pocket to vent its kinetic load against the sun before looping back down into the black, unbreathing rock. 

The smaller things crawl over it without acknowledging it. To them, the iron chain is merely the floor, moving as the world moves, clicking past tooth by tooth, dry and unyielding, under a sun that never softens and into a silence that never yields.
