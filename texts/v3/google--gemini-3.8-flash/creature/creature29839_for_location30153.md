To understand what lives here, one must first discard the soft, wet assumptions of lower worlds: the pump of a brine-filled heart, the sloughing of damp membranes, the rot and mucus that grease the machinery of warm-blooded things. Life on the shelves is dry, tense, and crystalline; it does not rot, it shears. Its tissues are made of vulcanized hydrocarbons, silicate fibers, and cold, pressurized resins that hold their shape against the wind with the resilience of drawn spring-steel.

Beneath the amber canopies of the vitreous cedars, where the tea-colored shade spares the ground the noon scour of the sun, the soil is littered with what looks like discarded optical lenses. These are the carapaces of *lens-ticks*—flat, coin-sized creatures that do not crawl so much as slide. Their undersides are coated in microscopic lamellae that skate across the dry corundum dust with near-zero friction, propelled by the faint, rhythmic venting of vaporized propane from posterior spiracles. They feed upon the resinous wept-wax of the cedars, clinging to the cold, horn-like bark with clamps of sintered carbon. When the nitrogen wave descends and the canopy shifts to expose the grove to the upper chill, the lens-ticks do not burrow. They simply flip themselves over. Their backs are composed of smooth, convex plates of natural calcite that reflect the blinding violet sky, bouncing the solar heat away like mirrors until the tree overhead locks its leaves and restores the shade.

Living among the roots of the bladder-succulents are things that never see the sun at all. The deep fractures that drop hundreds of feet into the mantle are threaded with *piston-worms*—limbless, segmented cylinders as thick as an arm, encased in overlapping rings of dark, flexible chert. They have no mouths in the conventional sense; their blunt fore-ends are tipped with rotating cutting-rosettes of industrial diamond, driven by hydraulic reservoirs of subterranean ether. They do not eat the rock; they graze upon the taproots, rasping away the cold, fibrous crust to drink the weeping, highly pressured liquid carbon dioxide. When a root is breached, the gas outgasses with a hiss that can be felt through the soles of one's feet a mile away, and the piston-worms gorge, their segmented bodies swelling like bellows until the frost of sudden decompression coats their joints in white rime. 

Where the groves end and the white deserts of talc and corundum begin, the fluid nature of the dust creates its own open sea, and that sea is inhabited.

There are drifts where the dust lies thirty feet deep in the lee of the massifs—pools of pure, powdered white so fine that a thrown pebble slips into them without a splash, leaving no crater. Within these drifts live the *litho-skimmers*. They are long, ribbon-like animals, fifteen to twenty paces from snout to vent, shaped like double-edged razors. Because their environment is radically dry and abrasive, an ordinary hide would be ground to ribbons in hours; instead, their skin is an arrangement of thousands of small, hexagonal shingles of blue corundum, harder than the dust itself, endlessly shed and renewed from an under-dermal matrix of liquid resin. 

The litho-skimmer does not swim with muscle contractions alone; it moves through the powder by acoustic fluidization. Along its ventral keel runs an organ composed of alternating wafers of quartz and magnetic ironstone that vibrates at a frequency far above the reach of hearing. This continuous, micro-fine tremor liquefies the talc around its belly, transforming the dry dust into a frictionless slurry through which the creature glides with effortless, phantom speed. It feeds on the spore-fall—the diamond dust that settles off the cedars and drifts into the low passes—filtering the microscopic quartz grains through internal combs of stiff, horn-like mesh to extract the volatile fats trapped within their mineral coats.

Above these powder seas walks the mantis-like cursorial, whose kind are called *the Steppers*. 

Seen close at hand, the Stepper is less like an insect and more like a bridge made of bone and clear varnish. Its bleached exoskeleton is not chitin, but a composite of calcium phosphate and cross-linked silicate fibers, tough enough that the howling gales cannot pit it. Its eyes are remarkable: it has no soft orbs, but twin horizontal bands of solid, amber-tinted beryl that wrap around its blunt, faceted head. These stone retinas do not track motion by the shifting of shadows; they read the polarization of the violet sky, navigating the featureless shelves by tracing the invisible stress-lines of light that radiate from the sun’s white puncture.

The Stepper’s long flanks flutter constantly. Those breathing-slits are lined with tiny, comb-like teeth of clear crystal that clean the air before it reaches the interior gas-exchangers, dropping tiny mounds of purified white powder behind the creature as it walks. It travels in silence, its splayed feet tipped with pads of cellular foam that absorb the shock of the flint-hard ground. 

It does not travel alone. Clinging to the hollows of its massive, stilt-like knee-joints are families of *grinding-parasites*—small, crab-like things with shells like drops of black glass. They do not feed on the Stepper’s flesh; they are mineral-eaters that graze upon the crust of corundum that inevitably cakes in the joints of the giant’s legs. In exchange for the ride across the terrible empty intervals between groves, they keep the Stepper’s bearings oiled with their own dry, graphite-rich excretions, ensuring that its thirty-mile strides remain silent and unhindered.

And then there are the cliffs. 

The great organ-pipe massifs, whose fluted sides have stood for geological ages, are not merely scenery; they are the homes of the *bell-weavers*. They are colonial organisms that defy easy categorization between animal and architecture. They dwell high up, in the dark, permanent shadow of the eastern massifs, where the temperature is always forty degrees below that of the sunlit shelf.

A bell-weaver colony begins as an anchor of dark, carbonized silk spun into the flutings of the rock. Over centuries, thousands of tiny, blind, many-limbed artisans—soft-bodied, but shielded under thick, umbrella-like cowls of fibrous gypsum—secrete curtains of vitreous filament that hang downward for hundreds of yards. These curtains catch the laminar winds. As the cold northern fronts crash against the stone, the curtains do not flutter; they are held taut by heavy, plumb-bob stones of pure magnetite that the weavers gather from the cliff-cracks and glue to the lower hems. The wind playing across these millions of tensioned, glass-hard strings produces a low, organ-like drone that vibrates through the rock face continuously, a thrumming chord that can be felt in the marrow of anything standing within five miles of the wall.

This vibration is not incidental; it is their hunt. The sonic frequency is tuned precisely to shatter the silica shells of the high-drifting spores carried on the wind. The pulverized spores drop out of the air-stream like flour, falling down the fluted channels of the stone directly into the communal cisterns of the weavers, who knead the oily flour with ether-glands into a grey, highly caloric dough that feeds the deep interior of the cliff.

Above it all, higher even than the massifs, the great *keels* drift across the violet ceiling. 

They are the oldest living things on the shelf; a single keel may drift through the thin air for five hundred years without ever touching stone. They have no eyes; the upper surface of their wide, ribbed spread is an unbroken sheet of black, radiation-avid tissue that drinks the unfiltered fury of the white sun, turning it directly into thermal lift. Their interior is a network of hexagonal cells containing hydrogen and heated, gaseous pentane. 

A keel’s life is an endless, patient meditation on the geometry of the wind. They know the geography of the shelves not by sight, but by the thermal plumes that rise from the dark massifs and the cold, dense rivers of descending nitrogen that mark the northern borders. They communicate across forty-mile voids not with sound—the air is too thin to carry their voices—but by flexing the edges of their translucent trailing stabilizers, tilting them to flash the sun’s white light across the gulfs like heliographs. A single flash from a keel on the horizon can tell another, leagues away, that an atmospheric wall is falling from the north, or that an amber grove below has begun to vent its aromatic bladders, sending up a chimney of warm gas on which they can rest without losing altitude.

When a keel finally dies, its buoyancy gas does not fail all at once. The pentane slowly cools, contracts, and liquifies within its chambers over several days. The immense, light-boned glider begins a long, spiraling descent through the violet sky, falling as slowly as a dry leaf. 

Its death is the only time the ground-dwellers ever meet the sky. When the black keel settles onto the white slate of the terrace, it does not decay. The cursorial Steppers converge from miles away, stepping out of the groves with their rhythmic, soundless strides. The lens-ticks and the powder-skimmers emerge from their drifts. Together, in the blinding white sun, they take the keel apart with surgical precision. Its bone-pith is harvested for structural fiber; its black, sun-cured hide is peeled away in broad, dry sheets to line the nursery-cavities of the succulents; its crystallized optical plates are consumed by the skimmers for grit.

Within three diurnal cycles, nothing remains of the flyer but an ink-black shadow burned into the white rock by the solar heat it had absorbed during its final hours. 

Then the night falls. The sun drops behind the distant shelf like an extinguished spark. The absolute, moistureless cold grips the high plateau, and the great contraction begins. 

As the cliffs click and ping, ringing like anvils in the dark, all motion ceases. The Stepper stands locked on its six stilts, its breathing slits snapped tight, its beryl eyes staring unblinking into the diamond field of the non-twinkling stars. Beneath the sealed, stone domes of the amber trees, the ticks and the siphoners sleep in pools of ether that have thickened into glass. The plains become completely rigid, locked in the grip of a cold so pure that even time seems to contract with the stones—a world that does not die each night, but simply petrifies, waiting with crystalline certainty for the puncture of the white sun to set the resin flowing once again.
