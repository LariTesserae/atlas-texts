To find what lives here, you have to look for the things that move against the direction of the warp.

At first, you assume the movement is entirely structural—the twitch of a cooling beam, the slow, hydraulic expansion of a vine swelling with tallow. But if you watch the seams where two floor-plates pinch together under the thermal heave, you see that the chaff does not settle randomly. It is raked.

There are things here that are not animals and yet possess the terrible, narrow intentionality of starvation. They do not have meat on them; meat would fry, turn to jerky, and carbonize within an afternoon. Instead, they are woven. They are the offspring of the fast-growing silicate fiber and the runaway machine housings, born from root-cuttings that curled into rotating gearing until they were ground, spit out, and somehow kept a fragment of that repetitive, cyclic turn in their grain.

Call them *spindles*. They are the size of an uncoiled arm, gray as cinder, jointed with dry graphite knuckles. They do not possess muscles; their motion is entirely bimetallic. Their outer hulls are layered plates of dry carbon and thin sheets of drawn iron peel, salvaged from the boiler flues. When the under-floor cherry glow brightens beneath a grate, the plates expand at unequal speeds, and the spindle *clicks*—snapping forward an inch with the sharp, mechanical violence of a spring trap.

They cluster where the pipes leak pressurized animal grease. They do not drink through mouths; they have no digestive tracts to hold a fluid. Instead, their bellies are bundles of parched, ultra-fine capillary wicks—silicate wool so thirsty that it draws heavy petroleum and rendered fat straight out of the fittings by surface tension alone. A spindle will jam its chest against a weeping rivet and stay there for three days, vibrating faintly as its fibers saturate. When it is full, its body does not soften; it simply grows heavier, dense as an ingot of lead, and its clicks become low, muffled, bone-cracking thuds.

They are blind, deaf, and indifferent to everything except heat gradients. If you were to drop something cold into the chaff—a block of stone brought from some damp, forgotten surface world—the spindles within twenty yards would twitch in revulsion, thrown into frantic, clattering convulsions as the sudden absence of heat buckled their limbs backward, snapping them like dry tinder.

Above them, through the dense, acoustically dead hedge that fills the space between the gantries, moves something much larger, though it is difficult to say where the plant ends and the walker begins.

The *riggers* do not have a fixed anatomy. They are loose colonies of the black vine that have wrapped themselves around discarded structural scrap—crane hooks, lengths of roller chain, bent push-rods—until the iron acts as an internal armature. They do not walk so much as counter-balance. A rigger moves by selectively shedding its own water-deprived burls. To step forward, it snaps the parched lignin tendons holding a thirty-pound iron counterweight aloft, allowing the metal to swing through an arc, dragging the tangled, fibrous body along with it through the brush.

The sound of a rigger traveling overhead is like someone dropping bundles of dry firewood down a carpeted staircase: a sequence of soft, hollow, dull impacts, followed by the dry *hiss-shack* of mica plates sliding across one another.

They are the pruners of this place. They do not eat the vines; they clear the flues. A rigger will wedge its iron-reinforced limbs into a clogged thermal stack, bracing its gray-wood bulk against the brickwork, and slowly, over twelve hours of shuddering, ratcheting exertion, rip free an entire plug of compacted carbon-brush that was choking the heat. It does this because it needs the convective chimney-draft to survive. When the chimney clears, a blast of three-hundred-degree furnace air roars up through the stack, and the rigger stands directly in the throat of it, trembling, its mica leaves rattling in the gale, siphoning the thermal kineticism to recock its wooden springs.

If two riggers meet in the narrow spaces between the black trusses, there is no fight in the animal sense. There are no teeth, no claws. There is only a slow, silent, crushing contest of thermal displacement. One will deliberately crowd the other away from an upwelling draft, starving its rival’s expansion joints until the other’s limbs seize in the cold, stiffening into parched immobility. The frozen one will hang there for a cycle or a century—it makes no difference—until a fresh structural shift brings a wave of heat back to its dead iron knees.

Beneath everything, down in the narrow, lightless cavities beneath the steel decking where the cherry glow is brightest and the floor is a maze of hot flues and red-hot pipe brackets, lives the closest thing this world has to an eye.

They are thin as laths, no thicker than a slate shingle, creeping through the perforated deck like pale tongues of slate-colored shale. They are sheets of living, highly structured graphite that feed on the electrical eddy currents leaking from the dying dynamos. They have no brains, but they possess an immense, slow-wave continuity. They lie flat against the glowing steel, absorbing the subtle changes in magnetic flux that ripple through the structural iron when a girder bends miles away.

They feel the upland breathing. To them, the entire basin is a single, vast, iron lung whose ribs are war-girders and whose phlegm is boiling tar. When a structural arch begins to buckle—hours before the decking shudders, before the sound of the chicory-scented shearing tears through the dark—these flat things begin to migrate. They slide across the violet rust in a silent, dry tide, thousands of brittle laminates slipping over one another with the dry, papery whisper of dead leaves pushed by a rake, seeking the exact point where the compression will give way.

They do not flee the collapse; they seek it. When the decking explodes upward and the vines shatter with the friction-heat of their own breaking, these sheets gather at the fracture lines. They drink the momentary, flash-point spark of sheared iron hitting sheared iron. They press their wafer bodies into the white-hot lips of the freshly torn steel, basking in the brief, fierce life of the machine’s self-inflicted wounds, before the shoots spear up to stitch the dark back together, burying them again in the hot, unrotting dust.
