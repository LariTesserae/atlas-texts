The people who hold the basalt ledge do not think of themselves as parched. To them, wetness is the condition of rot: a weakness of the deep basins where bodies go slack, bloat with foul juices, and turn to black slime in the mud. Up here, where nothing ever spoils, life is cured down to its absolute iron core.

They are small-framed, narrow-hipped, and move with a stiff, scuttling efficiency that mimics the dust-striders. Their skin has long since ceased to behave like mammalian hide; it is an ash-gray rind, fissured into fine, regular scales like the baked mud of a dry salt pan, continuously rubbed with tallow and the amber insect-resin to keep the hyper-oxygenated gale from scouring it off the bone. They do not sweat—an inhabitant of the ledge who lost a gill of liquid would simply clinker like slag and cease to twitch—and their eyes are narrow, horizontal slits shielded by thick, translucent secondary lids, like amber lenses, which stay closed against the pumice-drift while they work.

Speech is impossible in the scream of the flue. Even if their throats could bear the scouring air, the Marrow-groves drown all vowels in their pipe-organ roar. Instead, the bivouac-dwellers speak with their bones. They wear rings of carved strider-horn upon their knuckles and carry short batons of dry flint; with these they strike the basalt beneath their feet or rap upon the resonant stems of the nearest cane. Two sharp taps and a sliding drag means an downdraft is curling over the rim; a rolling, syncopated clatter against a quern-stone calls the clan to butcher. Within the bivouacs, where the hide-lashed roofs dull the gale to a thrumming vibration, they communicate by clicking their teeth and twitching the dry tendons of their wrists, an intricate finger-sign that rustles like mice in parchment.

Their relationship with water is not a thirst, but a slow, interior chemistry. They do not drink; they parse the cane. 

When a stalk of marrow-cane reaches the thickness of an elk’s ribs, its interior ceases to be fibrous and fills with a dense, sulphur-yellow paste—the *medulla*. It is an oily, alkaline lipid, cold to the touch and tasting violently of burnt matchheads and lithium. In the stone querns beside the wind-baffle, the women grind this marrow together with the chalk-pods, crushing the seeds until the friction of the granite stones heats the meal into a pale, greasy bread. This is their meat and their water both. Their bellies burn it with an internal heat that would sear a lowland man from throat to vent, turning mineral fats directly into metabolic energy without ever passing a drop of waste fluid. Their leavings are only dry cakes of gray lime, tossed over the ledge to dissolve in the updraft.

They share the terrace with the things that feed them, locked in a fierce, bloodless domesticity. 

In the deep cracks where the volcanic vents still puff their dry, sulfurous heat live the resin-wasps—insects the length of an adze-blade, armored in rings of copper-colored slag. They do not construct hives of wax; they harvest the molten pitch that sweats from the lips of the flue and pump it out through their mandibles to build great, hexagonal chimneys that rise among the basalt slabs like black glass organ pipes. The ledge-dwellers do not kill them. In the lulls between the downdrafts, hunters wedge split-cane tongs into the wasp-chimneys, tearing out plugs of cooling resin to seal their roofs and cement their stone walls. The wasps, sightless and vibrating with the heat of the stone, attack not with venom, but by spraying a stream of boiling, fast-setting varnish that hardens into amber within three heartbeats. A hunter’s arm is often banded with layers of this golden lacquer—accidental armor from years of raiding the flue-nests.

Beneath the wind-baffles, crouching in the lee of the bivouacs, are the *cask-hounds*. They are not dogs, but four-legged, lungless mantids whose abdomens are large, translucent bladders of hard horn. The people keep them tethered by cords of braided wire-grass. During the high-gale cycle, when the sky turns bronze and the static storms set the cane-tips glowing with violet brush-fire, the cask-hounds tilt their heads back into the screaming wind, opening their fan-like throat-valves to ingest the drifting clouds of wind-blown mites. For hours they pump, filtering the living dust through internal sieves until their bellies swell taut, amber and humming. At dusk, the children of the bivouac unhook the hounds, prise open their flank-spiracles with smooth bone picks, and spoon out the compacted mite-butter—a salty, sulfurous tallow that keeps their joints from seizing in the dry air.

Death on the shelf is clean and musical. 

When an elder’s joints finally weld together with mineral crust, or when a hunter’s foot slips on the mica-shards and leaves him battered on the rock, there is no weeping. Tears are an unimaginable squandering of substance. The dead are stripped of their skin—which is boiled with lime into tough, clear parchment for basket-liners—and their tendons are drawn out for bowstrings and quern-straps. The bones are the greatest treasure: long, straight, completely hollowed of marrow, and seasoned by years of breathing the alkali gale. 

The clan carries the skeleton to the western lip, where the wind-shear screams the loudest. There, wedged firmly into the natural bore-holes of the edge, they plant the bones upright, drilled with small, deliberate stops along their length. 

As the gale roars out of the basin, the skeleton begins to whistle, each bone sounding a clear, piercing note that cuts through the shriek of the cane. A family’s history is marked by these bone-whistles along the rim—a chorus of grandmothers and scouts, dry as glass, screaming back into the storm that failed to dissolve them.
