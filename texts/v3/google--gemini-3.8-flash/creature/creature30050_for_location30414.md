To see nothing at first is the natural consequence of looking at the basin from its walkways. At this scale, life does not present itself as an inhabitant of the machine; it is smeared into its tolerances, living in the slack between moving parts and the dead air trapped behind bolted iron.

The scarcity of life on the open decks is an illusion of the wind and the sheer drop. Beneath the moss mats—where three inches of saturated sphagnum have had fifty years to rot against galvanized plate—an entire interstitial fauna operates in the dark. It is an ecology dictated by the slow dissolution of metal and the drift of coal dust. Pale, thread-thin nematodes, blind and nearly transparent, knot themselves by the thousands in the silt that lines the bottom of every conduit raceway, feeding on the bacterial slicks that digest machine tallow. Wherever a zinc coating has begun to fail, leaving a powdery white crust along an electrolytic seam, minute collembola—springtails no larger than salt grains—graze on the minerals, their bodies a milky, chalk-white that never sees the slate sky above.

Deeper down, where the water leaves the light entirely and enters the labyrinth of concrete cisterns and siphon-pipes, the silence changes character. 

These flooded lower tiers, completely submerged beneath the lowest corrugated decks, are not empty. High mountain char, descendants of whatever fingerlings were sucked out of glacial tarns when the upper pass was first dammed, inhabit the cisterns. In two centuries of absolute darkness and cold, they have drifted away from their ancestors. They are stunted, rarely longer than a man’s finger, their scales having lost their pink spots in favor of a uniform, bruised violet-gray. They have no use for eyes; their lateral lines have thickened into swollen, cartilaginous ridges that run the length of their flanks, registering the micro-shudders of the valley’s sluice gates miles upstream. They feed on the endless drift of organic slurry that filters through the deck drains: dead liverwort, drowned spiders, and the gelatinous molts of stonefly nymphs that cling to the manganese gratings. When a deck above tilts to vent its ballast, these fish do not flee the turbulence; they orient themselves against the sudden roar of water, their pale, translucent fins working furiously to catch the fresh wash of oxygen and cold drift before the valves hiss shut again.

Above the water, inside the structural members themselves, lives the heat-seeking fauna. 

The valley is cold, but its dynamos, steam jackets, and high-pressure steam-feed lines are not. Every structural stanchion of hollow-box construction acts as a thermal chimney, drawing up warm, moist air from the lower machinery rooms toward the frost-slicked upper gantries. In these iron verticalities, hidden from the shearing fog, live colonies of soot-mice. They are not true mice, but an offshoot of high-altitude voles whose pelts have grown dense, oily, and completely waterproof, smelling strongly of paraffin and boiled linseed. They do not burrow in soil; they weave football-sized hanging nests from the shredded fibers of wet canvas insulation, hair plucked from their own bellies, and the dried fronds of maidenhair ferns pulled through the weep-holes. They are sure-footed to a degree that seems mechanical, using their clawed, splayed toes to run headfirst down vertical, grease-caked channel iron, raiding the automated gantries for the vegetable-tallow blocks used to lubricate the wheel-bearings.

And then there are those who tend the system—though to call them tenders is perhaps to assign them too much agency. They are more like symbionts, or a specialized vermin of the balance mechanisms.

They live in the dry voids formed beneath the great ball-and-socket swivels—cavernous hollows protected from the wind by the overhang of the tilting decks, warmed by the massive friction of the joints and the radiant heat of the return pipes. They are human, or retain the heavy, barrel-chested architecture of mountain people whose blood has long adapted to the thin air of the pass, but they move with an unhurried, low-slung economy that belongs entirely to the iron.

Their clothing is constructed of thick, multi-layered wool, felted until it is as stiff as gasket board, soaked in lanolin and mineral oil to turn the perpetual drip. On their feet they wear no leather or nails; their soles are carved slabs of old conveyor belting or blocks of dense, river-cured ash that will not slip on moss-slick iron or strike sparks near the vented gases. 

They do not speak with words across any distance. The acoustically dead air—choked by ferns and the cold mist—swallows human speech within a dozen paces. Instead, they communicate along the iron itself. A tender crouched at the rim of an upper ballast tank signals to another three levels down through a series of rhythmic taps with a bronze-headed drift-pin against a non-structural flange. The ring does not carry through the damp air, but it travels through the black iron framing for half a league, humming into the soles of the boots of anyone touching the steel.

Their lives are metered entirely by the valley’s hydraulic equilibrium. They do not keep clocks; they know the time by the shifting of the counterweights. When the northern well thumps—that dull, mattress-muffled drop of ten tons of iron into its pit—they know that three hours of drainage have accumulated in the mid-tier collectors. 

They work in the interface between the organic and the mechanical, performing the only tasks the automated system cannot achieve: the clearing of the small throats. Armed with long, curved scrapers made of saw-steel, they patrol the underside of the decks, hanging from webbed harness-lines over the bottomless gray mist. They scrape the liverworts from the drainage scuppers; they reach deep into the weep-holes with iron wires to pull out plugs of fibrous fern roots that would otherwise back up water and cause a deck to list past its design angle. They are fanatical about balance. If an accumulation of saturated peat on Deck Nine threatens to jam a trunnion, they shovel it off with broad wooden blades, watching the heavy, green-black slabs vanish silently into the clouds below, falling thousands of feet toward the lost floor of the pass.

In return, the system feeds them. They set baskets of woven copper wire into the bypass flumes to catch the char and the heavy caddis-worms. They harvest the pale, thick shoots of the water-creepers where they run near the steam jackets; boiled in iron pots over the blue, nearly invisible flame of vented methane taps, the shoots yield a bitter, starchy paste that tastes of tannin and sulfur, but keeps the teeth firm in their gums. They skim the surface of the oil-separator pits for thick, dark fats, refining it in small brass retorts to burn in their lamps, though mostly they live in the slate-colored daylight and the faint, wet amber halo of the filament bulbs.

They have no history of the valley from before the iron. If you were to tap a question along the stanchions about who built the pass or where the stone cliffs end, the answer would come back along the lattice as a slow, single vibration—a refusal of the premise. To them, the iron is not a structure placed upon a mountain; the pass is simply an organ of the world that breathes water, weight, and grease, and they are the small, deliberate creatures that keep its valves from sticking.
