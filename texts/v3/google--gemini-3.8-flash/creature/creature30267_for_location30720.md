If vacancy lasts long enough in an atmosphere so dense with food and warmth, it ceases to be empty. A space that is ninety-five degrees, wet, and permanently dark is not an absence; it is an incubator that has forgotten what it was meant to hatch, churning through the chemistry of tallow and sediment until the broth curdles into intent.

What lives here was not invited by the architecture. It was precipitated by it. 

The first sign of tenancy is not an encounter, but an obstruction in the current. In the knee-deep water of the grand boulevards, where the flags run flat and true for miles, the flow is occasionally interrupted by what feels, beneath the foot, like a curb where no curb should be. It is smooth, slightly yielding under heavy pressure, like a block of cold salt pork that has sat for weeks in brine. If you were to reach down through the dark into the tepid soup, your fingers would find no skin, no scales, and no shell, but a firm, rubbery meniscus—the boundary between the water of the street and a volume of water that has learned how to hold itself together.

These are the *drifts*, though they do not truly drift; they slump. They are massive, oblong lenses of colloidal gel, sometimes forty feet long and broad enough to span three carriage lanes. They have no skeleton to hold them upright in air, but in this shallow, endless slough, the water supports their bulk. They contain no blood, but their interiors are cloudy with suspended silicates, grey river mud, and pale threads of the same lipid fleece that hangs from the cornices. 

They do not swim. Their motion is a sluggish, hydraulic peristalsis that takes days to carry them past a single administrative block. A drift moves by allowing the front of its body to dilute slightly, letting the ambient water carry a sheet of its substance forward along the stone, while its trailing mass thickens, dehydrates by a fraction of a percent, and draws itself after. Where they pass, the basalt is scraped clean of silt, polished to an oily sheen by the passage of millions of microscopic, mineralized granules embedded in their bellies. They are the street-sweepers of a road system that never had a purpose, grazing on the warmth, the sulfur, and the falling sweat that washes down from the ceiling.

Higher up, where the architecture grows claustrophobic and the monumental monoliths press close against one another, life takes a more structural form. 

The recessed entryways—those doorless, windowless mouths that open into the seamless stone—are rarely vacant. Nearly every portal is occupied by a *plug*. 

When an archway or a vestibule sits empty for two or three hundred years, the pale filamentous fat that drapes the lintels begins to accumulate in the dead-air pocket of the entrance. It does not grow like a plant; it silts out of the humidity, layer upon microscopic layer, until it fills the doorway completely. Over time, the heat and the steady drip compress it into a solid, fibrous organ that fits the entrance so precisely it appears to have been poured into a mold. 

A plug does not think, but it reacts to pressure. If you press a hand against one, it yields with the dense, springy resistance of a waterlogged mattress, then slowly, over the course of several minutes, pushes back, restoring its perfect, flush alignment with the stone facade. It breathes without lungs: every three or four hours, as the air pockets deep within the basin’s mud floor thump and shift, the atmospheric pressure in the alley rises slightly. In response, every plug in the row swells by an inch, their pale, tallowy surfaces weeping streams of clear, sour liquid that run down the jambs. When the pressure drops, they shrink back, sighing a wet, suctioning gasp as they pull the scalding mist deep into their porous, spongy cores.

They are the tenants of the facades. Behind them, the interior rooms are not dry because they were engineered to be; they are dry because the plugs have sealed them away from the soup, drinking every atom of vapor that attempts to cross the threshold. Inside those black, sealed volumes, there is only absolute stillness, a vacuum of sound, and the faint, sweetish smell of stearic acid radiating from the inner face of the living door.

And then there are the things that belong to the air itself. 

In the intersections where gravity falters, where the water creeps up the vertical faces of the buildings to form trembling, swollen pools on the underside of balconies, the liquid does not always fall. Some of these hanging reservoirs have been colonized by an ancient, floating grease. Over decades, the spheres that detach from the ceilings do not burst when they strike a surface; they bounce. 

They are buoyant, hollow globes of translucent, leathery membrane, ranging from the size of a human skull to that of a carriage wheel. They drift through the humid dark at the height of the third stories, held aloft by the rising thermal drafts that belch from the hot mud. Because there is no light, they have no color, appearing only as greyer silhouettes against the grey-black fog. They have no sense organs, no mouths, and no members. They are balance-organs without an animal attached—simple spheres filled with hot, trapped gas and a single, loose pebble of siltstone that rolls along their lower interior curve. 

As they float through the avenues, riding the gentle convection currents between the monoliths, the pebble shifts. The globe reacts to the pebble’s roll by sweating a droplet of moisture from its opposite side, a tiny, hydraulic jet that steers it away from walls, out of the path of descending water-ribbons, and down the precise centers of the boulevards. They navigate the grid with an eerie, mechanical obedience to the municipal layout, tracing the avenues for miles, turning at right angles at every major crossing, caught in the geometric wind tunnels created by the basalt canyons. 

If two of them collide in the dark, they do not burst; they adhere with a wet, sticky smack, drifting together for hours as their skins slowly fuse, their interior pebbles clicking against each other in the muffled silence like dice in a leather cup.

Nothing here dies in a way that leaves a carcass. When a drift grows too large for its boulevard, its mass simply catches on the corners of an intersection. The warm rain beats upon it; the water works its way into the seams of its gel; and over six months, it dissolves back into the broth from which it thickened, leaving behind only a faint, greasy tide-mark along the lower three feet of the siltstone walls—a high-water mark of fat that the younger drifts will come to lick away.

It is a world without hunger, because everything is already immersed in sustenance; without fear, because there are no teeth to tear or limbs to pursue; and without solitude, because the city, the water, and the things that curdle within them are made of the exact same matter, merely resting at different stages of solidity. The city extrudes a room; the moisture fills it; the moisture stiffens into a tenant; the tenant weeps water that wears away the room. It is a slow, closed circuit of warm fat and wet rock, ticking away in the darkness, profoundly indifferent to whether it is ever witnessed, understood, or named.
