### The Sole and the Shard

To survive upon the floor of the trough, an organism must solve the problem of the blade. The obsidian does not wear down; it cleaves into finer, thinner lancets under pressure. Therefore, whatever walks cannot rely on soft pads or hooves that wedge and splinter.

The hound-sized arthropods—the *pelt-crabs*—solve this through a sacrificial biology of the joint. They do not possess singular feet; their eight limbs terminate in clusters of curved, hair-fine stilettos of solid chitin that are shed and replaced with the frequency of a shark’s teeth. As they scuttle, their gait is a continuous, dry tinkle—the sound of needles falling into a pewter basin. When a stiletto catches in a cleavage plane of the glass, the limb does not snap; the terminal spike uncouples cleanly from a ball-and-socket hinge, leaving behind a calcified plug that seals the wound before hemolymph can weep out. By the time the crab has climbed thirty feet up a basalt scarp, three new spikes have already slid from their dermal sheaths, soft as wet gristle, hardening to vitreous density within three breaths of the greenhouse air.

Beneath the crabs move the *ribbed hides*, the amphibians whose bellies resemble wet, salt-cured sailcloth. They do not walk so much as compress and heave. Their bellies are not skin in any conventional sense, but thick, stratified laminates of keratinized fiber interspersed with pockets of dense, yellow fat that congeals the moment it meets the air. When an edge of glass scores their undercarriage, the cut does not bleed red; it bleeds a clear, fast-setting tallow that plugs the trough, trapping the shard inside the animal’s body. Old hides are lardaceous, studded internally with thousands of polished obsidian flakes like mosaics set in tallow. They are heavy, broad-chested things, blind as turnips, their nostrils shielded beneath overhanging shelves of snout-bone that plow through the shards. They feed by vacuum: their mouths are enormous, ringed with muscular, circular folds that seal against the stone and inhale whatever sits upon it—pelt-crab shed, broken fronds, deadfall meat, and their own young.

The young are born small, wet, and instantly ambulatory, tumbling from the cloaca by the hundreds into the dark. They are transparent, their tiny hearts visible through ribcages no thicker than pine needles, and they immediately begin eating the tallow sloughed off their mother’s flanks. If they lag behind her heave, the carpet-scavengers take them.

These scavengers are the *scale-mats*. They are neither worms nor roaches, but flat, coin-thin discs of liver-spotted muscle that overlap one another across the stone like roof shingles. They move in waves, passing along the obsidian by peristalsis. Where the scale-mats flow, nothing organic remains; they secrete an acid so hot and alkaline it dissolves even the oily films left by the vines, leaving the glass beneath momentarily dry, bright, and nakedly dangerous before the condensation falls again.

### The Heat-Drifts

Higher up, where the vines sling their thick, greasy bulk across the clefts, there are things that never touch the glass. 

The *whip-eels* live entirely in the tension of the cords. They are twenty feet of pale, ringed gristle, thicker than a horse’s neck at the midsection, tapering to muscular whips at either terminal. They have no skeleton; their rigidity is hydraulic, maintained by the ferocious blood-pressure typical of the rift’s fauna. Along their flanks run double rows of pit-organs—not the narrow slits of vipers, but cavernous, wet funnels lined with crimson velvet that register thermal differentials of a hundredth of a degree.

To the whip-eel, the rift is not dark. It is an architecture of heat: the cold, dead walls of basalt; the lukewarm downpour of the dripping vault; the boiling, yellow-white stars of the thermal vents; and the scuttling, five-legged flares of animal warmth moving through the green cables.

They hunt by contraction. An eel loops its body twice around a hawser-thick creeper, anchors its tail-ring into the bark, and lets its forward third hang free in the dark, swaying like an empty noose. When a ribbed hide passes below, radiant and steam-plumed, or when an engineering party of bipeds moves along a lower hunting line, the eel drops. It does not bite to consume immediately; it constricts with a hydraulic violence that bursts the abdominal walls of its prey, showering the lower vines with hot entrails. The eel feeds only on the steam and the fountain of warm fluid, licking the meat with a tongue as broad and rough as a stone-mason’s rasp, before letting the crushed carcass fall to the floor to be erased by the mats.

### The Brow-Folk

The bipeds do not have a name that uses sound, for the roar of the trench is a solid, crushing volume that erases the larynx. They communicate by the *whistle of the sinus* and by percussive thrumming against the basalt.

They are the *Brow-Folk*, named for the massive, bony hoods that project over their mid-faces. Beneath these heavy cantilevers of bone, their eyes have withered to twin scars—small, yellowish depressions no larger than lentil seeds, completely skin-covered and useless. But the bone itself is hollowed out like a dry hive, riddled with air-cells that connect directly to their enormous nasal chambers. 

Their sensory world is tactile and olfactory, magnified to an hallucinatory clarity. Their whiskers are not feline; they are thick, flexible quills of cartilage, banded with nerve rings, that sprout from the margin of the upper jaw and brush the stone before every step. Through these quills, they read the rift: they feel the micro-fractures running through a basalt shelf before it shears; they taste the sulfur content of the steam three miles upwind; they can distinguish between the heavy, rhythmic tread of a pregnant ribbed hide and the dry, nervous ticking of a crab-swarm four hundred yards below in the dark.

Their skin is thick, bald, and greasy. It smells strongly of smoked fat and wet bark. Because their bodies are constantly scraped by the razor-dust that rides the wind, they produce a grey, lard-like sebum from pores between their shoulders, which they spread by hand over their bellies, their knuckles, and the thick pads of their prehensile feet. This grease repels the lukewarm drizzle and causes the edges of the obsidian to slip harmlessly along their sides rather than biting bone.

They move on four points when traversing the basalt scarps, their knuckles callused into great, round knobs of horn that resemble hoof-matter. When they reach their hunting platforms—the great shelves where their deadfalls are rigged—they rise onto their hind legs. They are short-waisted, broad-shouldered, with long, ape-like arms whose tendons are as thick as hemp lines. Their chests are enormous, barrel-shaped bellows that churn the thick, oxygen-poor air through their sinuses with a rhythmic, wet snort that can be felt by another biped six feet away through the vibration of the rock.

### The Knappers of the Shelf

A Brow-folk family does not build shelters. A shelter would be crushed in forty-eight hours by the shifting scree or pulled apart by the creeping vines, which grow with such hydraulic force that their tendrils can pry two-ton basalt blocks from the cliff face within a single generation.

Instead, they build *anchors*.

Into the sheer scarp they drive the heavy bones of the ribbed hides, using flat river-stones as mallets. These bone-spikes do not rot; the chemical makeup of the rift’s water, saturated with iron and silica, petrifies them in place within weeks, turning them into pale, stone pegs. To these pegs, the Brow-folk lash their cordage.

The vines they choose are the *wire-runners*—slender, iron-hard creepers that grow along the margins of the thermal vents. These vines do not have bark; their skin is a smooth, green leather that resists cutting. The bipeds strip these vines using their teeth, pulling them through their split-front incisors to scrape away the caustic sap, then braid them three-fold while they are still wet. When the braid dries in the hot steam, it shrinks, tightening around the bone pegs with the force of iron bands.

Their tools are terrifyingly direct. They do not shape obsidian by chipping flakes from a core until a symmetrical knife appears; they have no time for symmetry, and the light is insufficient to see the bevel. They understand the *grain of the drop*. 

A hunter will carry an eighty-pound river-boulder to the lip of a basalt shelf, raise it above his bony brow, and hurl it downward into a natural depression where raw obsidian has pooled and cooled. The sound of the impact is lost in the ambient avalanche-roar, but the result is a spray of fresh blades. The hunter drops down into the pit, sweeping his whisker-frills over the wreckage. He does not look for beauty; he feels for the *bite*. When his quills brush an edge that shears the hair instantly, he lifts the shard by its blunt heel, wraps the butt in four layers of wet rib-hide, binds it with gristle-string, and drives a bone wedge through the wrapping to bite into his hand-heel. The tool becomes an extension of the wrist-bone—a single, massive, curved wedge of black glass, sixteen inches long, that can part the hide of a mature amphibian in a single pulling heave.

### The Chute and the Feast

Their society is an engineering of panic. 

They do not follow the animals; the animals move in frantic, mindless circuits, driven by the heat-gradient and the sudden explosions of fungal beds that lift from the glass floor like giant, liver-colored pancakes overnight. The Brow-folk wait on their shelves, high above the scree, until the vibrations through their foot-pads tell them the migration has turned.

When the tide of ribbed hides begins to funnel through a cleft, the bipeds go to their trip-cords. These are not delicate triggers. A Brow-folk deadfall is a five-ton block of basalt, undercut over months with bone chisels until it rests on three pins of petrified thighbone. 

At a percussive signal—a hunter striking the scarp with the flat of his obsidian cleaver, sending a sharp *tock* vibrating through the floor—three hunters throw their weight against the release levers. The pins splinter. 

The basalt slab does not fall silently; it hammers down like a piston into the narrow trough. The acoustic shockwave is so dense it knocks the pelt-crabs from the adjacent walls. Down in the gap, twenty or thirty ribbed hides are instantly pancaked against the glass, their tallow and purple blood splashing thirty feet up the scarp.

Before the slab has even settled, before the dust of shattered obsidian can clear, the Brow-folk are sliding down the guide-vines. 

Their descent is frantic, manic, bordering on terror. They know the clock. They have perhaps four minutes before the scale-mats arrive, smelling the tallow, and six minutes before the whip-eels drop from the upper lattices.

Working in pairs, their whiskers vibrating with a high, buzzing frenzy, they hack into the carcasses. Their obsidian wedges rise and fall with mechanical, unswerving speed. They do not skin the kill; they slice long, twenty-pound baulks of the white, fat-streaked muscular flanks, hook them onto bone gaffs tied to their belts, and immediately begin the ascent. 

They climb with their feet and their free hand, their knuckles finding the tiny, razor-bright crevices with a hideous certainty, their quills trailing over the stone to avoid the fresh fractures. Behind them, down in the chute, the red-violet light begins to pulse: the dying hides are weeping their luminous, bruised secretions as the scale-mats reach them. 

The floor of the trough becomes a boiling, purple lake of moving plates. The scale-mats climb over one another five deep, an acid-wet sludge of appetite that strips the crushed carcasses to the spine while the Brow-folk are still forty feet above them, hauling their dripping meat into the fog.

### The Whisker-Lash

In the upper forks, where the heat is dampest and the smell of fermenting sap is so sweet it makes the tongue numb, the Brow-folk huddle together on suspended platforms made of interlaced creepers. 

They do not sleep in shifts; they sleep in clumps, eight or ten bodies pressed tight, their greasy hides interlocked to form a single, steaming mound of tallow-coated muscle. Their bony brows are turned outward toward the dark, their whiskers twitching in their sleep like long grass in a light wind, constantly tasting the rising updrafts.

Here, in the damp heat, they groom one another. Because they cannot see the vermin that cling to their hides—pale, tick-like things that hide in the skin-folds behind their brow-ridges—they groom entirely with their split front teeth and the tips of their sensitive nasal quills. A female will spend three hours tracing the rib-scars of an older male, her whiskers fluttering over each line of healed gristle, feeling for the tiny, hard peas of embedded glass and levering them out with an obsidian splinter. 

The shards they remove are not thrown away. The Brow-folk swallow them, or push them into the flesh of their own ear-cavities to seal the auditory canals against the deafening churn of the floor.

They have no monuments. Their stone is too new, their world too fast. A dead Brow-folk is not buried; to leave a body on a shelf is to invite the whip-eels into the roost. When a hunter dies—his knuckles shattered by a stone-slip, or his lungs filled with the caustic white dust of a fresh basalt slide—his fellows do not wait for the body to cool. 

They strip his tools. They untie the gristle from his wrists, reclaim the bone pegs from his kit, and then, with three synchronized blows of their cleavers, they part his limbs at the large joints. They do not eat the meat—the tallow of their own kind is bitter with acid and produces violent spasms of the liver—but they do not waste it either.

They climb out onto the longest, thinnest vine that hangs over the central trench, where the darkness is five miles deep, and they drop the pieces one by one into the roaring throat below.

They listen with their quills against the vine. They feel the heavy *thwack* as the meat strikes the glass three thousand feet down; they feel the momentary, violent surge through the stone as the scale-mats converge; and then they turn their blind, hooded faces back toward the cliff, snorting the warm steam through their honeycombed bone, their hands already reaching into the green tangle for the next runner to braid.
