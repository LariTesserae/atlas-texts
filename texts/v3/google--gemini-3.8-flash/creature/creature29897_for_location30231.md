The declaration that the chamber is empty of animal life is technically accurate, but only if one’s definition of the animal relies on inherited shapes—on things with jaws, bilateral eyes, and soft bellies that can be punctured. 

Nothing crawled in here from the outside. Nothing fell through the ceiling grates from the slums or transit platforms that could survive the fall, the acoustic pressure, or the biocide-laced alkalinity of the coolant. 

Yet the volume is occupied. The occupancy did not arrive; it condensed.

In the fast, sheet-glass laminarity of the water sliding down the triangular ceramic pylons, there are things that look at first glance like slight defects in the porcelain—faint, thumb-sized blisters where the glaze has lifted. If you watch them against the nine-second geothermal tremor, you see they are not static. They are flat, diamond-shaped disks of vulcanized cambium and mineralized silica, no thicker than a fingernail, anchored directly to the ceramic by thousands of microscopic glass hooklets that fit into the laser-cutter pits. 

They are the *scum-plates*, though they have no common name because no human eye has cataloged them. They are autonomous vegetative tissues that have sloughed off the parent root-mats, shed their need for vascular stem-work, and flattened themselves into two-dimensional hydroids. They feed entirely on the skin of the water: skimming the synthetic nitrates, the zinc salts, and the dissolved carbon scrubbed from the city’s exhalations above. 

To move against the violent downward drag of the water, they do not crawl; they pump. A scum-plate relaxes its perimeter, allowing the laminar rush to plane under its leading edge, and then snap-contracts its pale, fibrous core, skittering an inch *upward* against the torrent before the water can strip it away. Hundreds of them dot the pale pylons, arranging themselves in long, shimmering lattices that shift with the acoustic harmonics. When the ceramic sweats, they drink; when the water shatters against the acoustic baffles into spray, their outer edges curl upward, cupping the aerosol in delicate, fringed gills that glow with a faint, chlorotic violet.

Further out, in the dead volume of the air, the wrists of the great vine-trusses hold something heavier.

Where three or four of these living beams meet in a rigid, structural knot, the plant matter does not merely interlock—it swells. These nodes are the size of sheep, smooth and gray-green, stretched so tight with internal hydraulic pressure that they ping like cast bronze when stray droplets hit them. They are not galls, and they are not tumors. They are heart-analogs: secondary contractile bulbs the engineered flora had to evolve to drive sap across thirty meters of unsupported horizontal void against the constant downward suction of the bay’s air-currents.

Inside these bulbs, the tissue has lost all resemblance to pith or leaf. It is composed of dense, spiraling bands of actin-like vegetable muscle that twist in slow, involuntary rhythms. Every nine seconds, precisely in phase with the geothermal pulse shuddering through the basalt floor, the bulbs clench. You can see the spasm travel down the length of the truss—a visible wave of expansion that races from the wall toward the ceramic pylons, making the thirty-meter vines whip taut like bowstrings. 

Hanging from the undersides of these bulbs are the *weft-tails*. They are pale, translucent ribbons, long as bedsheets, composed of nothing more than cellulose gossamer and dead air-cells. They hang down into the dark volume, swaying in the deafening draft. They have no brains, but they are hyper-sensitive to static charge. As the millions of gallons of water drop through the dark, the air becomes ionized, crackling with microscopic potential. The weft-tails reach toward each other across the gap, their frayed margins drifting together until they touch, exchange a minute spark of static discharge that burns a pinprick hole in the leaf-flesh, and drift apart again, twitching in a slow, vegetative reflex that mimics the coupling of deep-sea invertebrates.

And then there is the life that inhabits the water itself—not the spray, but the long, vertical ropes that fall unbroken from the ceiling grates.

Within those falling pillars of coolant, where the shear force would tear flesh to ribbons, there are *filaments*. They look like strands of black horsehair, five to ten meters long, anchored to the iron grates hundreds of meters above and trailing downward into the falling stream. They are fungal-algal synthetics that escaped the water-treatment purifiers three levels up. They do not fight the drop; they ride it, suspended in the center of the liquid column where the velocity is highest and the pressure forms a tight, stable core. 

These filaments are dense with piezo-electric crystals scavenged from the degraded sensor networks in the upper plumbing. As the falling water drags against them, the friction creates a continuous, micro-voltage charge that travels down their length. At their lower tips, which dangle forty feet above the churning floor gutters, they terminate in small, dense nodules of black, rubbery pith. These nodules crackle with dim, blue-white coronal light—little cold sparks that spit into the mist, feeding on the kinetic violence of the city's falling waste.

When the atmospheric cycle triggers, the life in the bay transforms in an instant.

The rifle-shot crack of the overhead valve is not a shock to them; it is a signal. The downdraft of dry, sterile air hits the canopy like a hammer. The scum-plates on the ceramic pylons clamp flat, sealing their margins with an audible *snap* that sounds like a thousand playing cards hitting a table at once, sealing in their moisture against the dessicating gale. 

The heart-bulbs along the vines seize, their contraction so severe that the great jade trusses creak and bend, pulling their broad spade-leaves inward like umbrellas folding against a storm. The weft-tails are shredded by the blast, their pale rags torn free to swirl through the cathedral volume like blinding snow, only to be caught in the perimeter trenches and dissolved within seconds back into base carbon by the acidic wash.

For four seconds, while the cyan conductors dump their heat and the air turns to bone-dry ozone and blinding light, the bay is entirely rigid. Nothing moves. The plants do not behave as plants; they behave as high-pressure hydraulic architecture, locking the space together with living tension rods to keep the pylons from vibrating to pieces in the acoustic resonance.

Then the valve slams shut. 

The thunder of the water returns, sweeping the dry air back into the ceiling vents. As the humidity spikes back toward eighty-five degrees, the heart-bulbs relax with a wet, heavy sigh, releasing gallons of trapped xylem fluid back into the stems. 

From the torn remnants of the weft-tails, new growth is already pushing out. It takes twelve minutes for a ribbon to replace itself. The green tips unroll with frantic, mechanical speed, glistening with fresh sap, reaching blindly through the mist toward the indigo light strips, hungry for the next pulse from the machine that bore them, entirely sufficient unto themselves, and needing nothing from the living world above.
