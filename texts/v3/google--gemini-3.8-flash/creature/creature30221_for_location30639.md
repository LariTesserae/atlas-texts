To survive here, an organism must possess either the compressibility of raw dough or an absolute, mineral indifference to being struck. 

Nothing with a fragile carapace lasts more than a few strokes of the pans. The things that endure in the churn have done away with dry bones, brittle shells, and the ordinary rhythms of breath; they are made of thick, unvascularized cartilage, dense gristle, and skin that feels like the boiled hide used to lace drive belts.

The grease is the base of everything. The basin is an accidental slaughterhouse of calories: barrels of tallow packed into iron boxes three centuries ago continue to weep their yellow suet into the wash, and the water-mold does not merely drift—it is farmed, grazed, and trampled by an under-canopy of soft, blind things that inhabit the blind spots of the iron.

Clinging to the undersides of the rocker-arms, where the metal dips every four seconds into the milky emulsion and then rises into the damp air, are clusters of what look like pale, boiled tongues. They are solitary ascidians—sea-squirts of an inland, freshwater strain—that have adapted entirely to an industrial tide. They have no eyes and no need for the sun; their leathery tunics are heavily impregnated with calcium carbonate pulled from the alkaline seepage, giving them the texture of wet plaster. When the beam plunges, their siphon-mouths flare wide, drinking in the suspended tallow, fine stone meal, and flakes of sloughed bronze. When the beam wrenches upward, the sudden deceleration snaps their valves shut with an audible, rubbery pop, forcing the filtered water out through secondary pores and retaining a thick paste of fat and rock dust in their guts. They live their entire lives in this violent pendulum swing, fattening until their own weight tears them from the iron, dropping them down into the submerged gears where they are instantly mulched into the gray froth.

Beneath them, in the dead water behind the flywheel shields, the gravel-beds are not made of stone alone. 

Among the rounded loaves of masonry rolled smooth by the hydraulic jacks, there are creatures that mimic the drift. They are heavy, flat-bellied catfishes, or something that was once catfish before the light failed them. They have lost their pigment entirely, showing a dull, waxen pink through which the darker shadow of their digestive tracts is clearly visible. Their barbels have thickened into branched, fleshy antlers covered in sensory pits that do not taste the water for blood, but for tallow and lard. 

These fish do not swim in the open churn—to enter the path of an Archimedes screw or an eccentric wheel is death—but they have learned the machine’s geometry. They lie wedged by the dozen in the narrow, triangular spaces between the foundation timbers and the bedrock, their bellies pressed against the vibration. They wait for the trip-hammers. Every time a copper pan capsizes and the hammer drops with its heavy, deadened thud, the concussion stuns the water-mold and dislodges whatever salamanders or surface-drop grubs have been pulled into the downdraft. The fish slide out sideways, moving like greasy spades through the milk-colored silt, vacuuming the wash with wide, downward-turned mouths that are rimmed with horn instead of teeth. They can eat ground slate without injury; their intestines are thick-walled, continuously lined with a heavy mucus that coats the sharpest iron filings and passes them out as smooth, gray coprolites that sink to the floor like machine-pressed pellets.

Where the bronze sleeves have worn egg-shaped on their pins, there is a micro-climate of pure friction. 

Here, the water is not cold. The constant, shrieking pressure of the iron shafts working within their loose bronze collars generates an oily, localized heat—small pockets of seventy or eighty degrees Fahrenheit that never cool so long as the head of water drives the turbines. In these narrow clearances, squeezed into films of grease barely thicker than a fingernail, lives an specialized community of flattened leech-forms. They are ribbons of tough, rubbery muscle, no wider than a matchstick and completely colorless, that cement themselves directly to the polished bronze. They do not feed on blood, for there is none to be had; they are chemo-autotrophs of an unnatural sort, grazing on the sulfurated oils, the animal fats broken down by heat, and the microbial mats that crust the rims of the bearing blocks. When a shaft wobbles toward them on its eccentric cycle, they do not flee; they are so thin that the brass pin passes over them with millimeters of clearance, their bodies flattening into the microscopic grooves scored in the metal, riding the heat like lizards on a midday flagstone.

Higher up, above the water line, where the cold alkaline spray forms a permanent drizzle, the life belongs to things that cling and wait.

The rubbery lichen that stains the rock is not a single plant, but a dense, living felt composed of moss-protonema, fungal hyphae, and petroleum-tolerant yeasts. It grows thickest in the weep-lines, where the water oozes from the horizontal strata of the cliff. In the crevices behind this pelt, deep in the limestone where the machine’s thrum is felt not as sound but as a steady, cold nausea in the stone, live the salamanders that have managed not to fall.

They are not transients, though the water occasionally sweeps their cousins down from the surface. A true resident population has established itself in the deep fissures. They are longer than their creek-dwelling ancestors, their limbs reduced to tiny, clawed nubs that serve only to hook into the porous sponge-rock. Their skin has lost its delicate moisture; it is coated instead in an oily, water-repellent wax secreted from glands along their flanks, which protects them from the bitter alkalinity of the lime-water. 

These creatures are purely sit-and-wait predators of the spray zone. They hang vertically from the dripping mudstone, their flattened snouts pointing down toward the churning troughs. They do not watch—their eyes are tiny black beads sunken beneath the skin, sensitive only to the shift between daylight and the gray overcast—but their lateral lines are tuned to the *shuck-slap* of the leather belts. When a flying water-beetle or an errant moth, heavy with dampness, is knocked out of the air by the curtain of spray, it tumbles against the wet rock. The salamander does not lunge; it simply uncoils its neck with a short, mechanical snap, pinning the insect against the stone with a broad, flat tongue that smells faintly of petroleum.

Even the timber control hut has its tenants. The roof is open to the sky, but the floor-timbers are steeped in oil that has wicked upward from the lever bank for a hundred years. Nothing rots there quickly; the wood is mummified, black and dense as lignite. 

In the hollows beneath the floorboards, where the submerged chains jerk and scrape through their guide-holes, lives an animal that has come from the outside and forgotten the way back. They are water-shrews, or the descendants of water-shrews, but their fur is no longer velvety and dry; it is permanently slicked with mineral grease, parted in greasy rosettes across their ribs, smelling rankly of mutton fat and wet dog. They have given up the hunt for beetles and minnows in the daylight streams. Instead, they patrol the submerged frames of the timber hopper and the edges of the Archimedes screw. 

They are astonishingly fast, moving over the slick cast-iron plates with feet whose claws have grown long and curved, like the crampons of mountaineers. They know the timing of the levers. They can tell by the rising tone of a submerged turbine when a float is about to tip, and they skip over the thrashing linkages with an indifference born of thousands of generations spent within six inches of mutilation. They feed on the tongue-ascidians when the rocker-beams rise, darting in to tear the rubbery flesh from the iron with sharp, red-tipped incisors before the downstroke can pin them to the frame. They are scarred, half-blind, and perpetually damp, their small hearts beating at an insane tempo that matches the furious, muffled churn of the basin beneath them.

And down at the eastern wall, where the water is sucked through the three bowed grates, there is a collector of all things.

In the dark, horizontal void of the sluices, wedged behind the bent iron bars where the slurry of ground stone and grease pulls toward the underground drop, something has accumulated. It is not clear whether it is an animal, a colony, or merely a mechanical deposit that has caught the seed of life and begun to organize itself. 

It is a mass of fibrous, black material the size of a haycock, anchored by thousands of tough, hair-like rootlets to the limestone arches. The current through the grates is ferocious—enough to drown a man in seconds and strip the flesh from his shins—yet this thing thrives upon the velocity. It is ribbed like an engine filter, composed of layers of mineralized slime, water-mold, and the tough, elastic carcasses of dead ascidians, all bound together by a dense mesh of chitinous threads. It breathes the slurry. It acts as the final strainer of the basin, swelling as it absorbs the fats, the ground rock, and the pulverized organic drift that slips past the screws. 

Deep inside its core, where the water pressure drops in the interior baffles, there is a slow, rhythmic pulsation that does not correspond to the stroke of the iron rocker-beams. It has its own cadence—sluggish, heavy, and patient. Every few hours, when the mass becomes choked with its harvest of grease and limestone flour, it contracts with a single, muscular spasm, purging a dense, oily bolus out the back of the sluice into the subterranean dark, where the horizontal void carries it away toward some lower, unseen river. 

Then it relaxes, spreading its gray, hair-fringed lips against the bent bars once more, drinking the wash, perfectly fitted to the machine that feeds it, neither living nor dead in any way that would be recognized by the sun, but complete, and busy, and staying.
