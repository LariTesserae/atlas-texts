The light never relents, so nothing here has developed the dark-adapted, saucer-eyed fragility of natural abyssal fauna. The creatures that thrive in the basin are thick-skinned, rigid, and profoundly deaf.

In an environment where every cubic meter carries the concussive, bone-shearing hum of multi-megawatt axial pumps, an ear is a liability. The pelagic fish—lineal descendants of Atlantic herring and chub mackerel released during the wet-commissioning phase—have undergone a brutal, generational culling. Those with delicate otoliths or broad, resonant swim bladders died early of acoustic trauma, their internal balance organs pulped by the pressure-relief cycles. The shoals that remain are dense, muscular things with thickened craniums and reduced, oil-filled swim bladders that do not rupture when the valves fire. 

They do not sleep, because the sun-lamps never set. Instead, their lives are metered by the hydraulic rhythm of the basin. When a relief valve cracks miles to the south, sending its concussive wave through the turquoise clarity, the silver spheres do not scatter; they shudder simultaneously, collapsing into tight, hard nuggets of flesh that vibrate in place, then elongate into slipstreams that use the acoustic shockwave like a following sea. 

Their scales are perpetually scarred. In an open ocean, a fish turns against water; here, the turns are executed against sixty-meter fluted concrete columns and cross-braced steel lattices. Entire generations have lived without ever touching silt, kelp, or stone. They know only the slick, unyielding aggregate and the zinc sacrificial plates. Their skin has taken on an unnatural, mirror-bright sheen, free of the mottling that camouflages fish against natural bottoms. Under the quartz lamps, a fleeing school does not resemble living tissue so much as a spray of industrial ball bearings ricocheting off the sheer faces.

Below them, hunting the perimeter of the light-cones, are the squid. 

They are an ommastrephid strain, heavy-mantled and blunt, but stripped of the wild chromatophoric poetry of wild cephalopods. In this geometric world, they have abandoned the dappled reds, browns, and iridescences of the reef. Their camouflage is strictly architectural. When they rest against the underwater pylons, their skin locks into the exact tone of wet aggregate—a flat, unvarying 18-percent gray—bisected by hard, diagonal bars of soot-black that mimic the bituminous seams of the caissons. Some have even been observed displaying pale, vertical hash marks that align with the millimeter increments of submerged weld-gauges.

These squid do not hunt from ambush in the dark, for there is no real dark. They hunt from the blind spots of the floodlight arrays—the narrow, knife-edged wedges of shadow cast behind the horizontal I-beams. They hang there in clusters, balanced on the reverse-drafts of the cooling vents, their large, amber eyes permanently narrowed against the halogen glare. When a shoal of mackerel is funneled between two structural risers, the squid launch downward like hydraulic pistons, seizing their prey and dragging them down into the structural angles to strip them against the rough concrete.

Life on the bottom caissons is different; it is bound to the grid.

Three hundred fathoms down, across the thousands of square miles of vibrated slabs, there is no loose sand to burrow into. The only topography is the seam: the four-inch-wide expansion joints filled with tar-black bituminous mastic. These seams form an unbroken, rectilinear labyrinth that extends to the horizon in every direction, an orthonormal subterranean highway system.

Here live the mastic-eels. They are not true eels, but an opportunist blenny or wolf-fish variant that has elongated and flattened until its body matches the exact gauge of the joints. They wedge themselves belly-down into the soft, petrochemical-scented tar, their dorsal fins level with the concrete floor. Millions of them lie parallel and perpendicular to one another, tracing the square caisson edges in endless, right-angled geometries. 

They feed on the "fall"—not the soft marine snow of dying plankton, but the mechanical fallout of the basin: paint chips sloughed from the girder bays, flakes of white zinc oxide shed by the sacrificial anodes, and the pressure-crushed offal of pelagics that were drawn into the flumes and pulverized by the turbine runners. The eels do not crawl; they slide along the mastic tracks like cables through conduit, hunting tiny, bleached amphipods that have learned to browse the thin green diatom biofilms on the lamp-lit faces.

Occasionally, something larger moves across the grid. The spiny dogfish here have grown pale, almost albino, from the lack of natural spectra. Their eyes are milky, clouded by cataracts from the unshielded ultraviolet output of the quartz lamps, but their rostra are broadened and heavy with ampullae of Lorenzini. They navigate not by sight or smell, but by the colossal electromagnetic fields generated by the submerged power-transmission conduits that run beneath the floor. 

A dogfish will cruise six inches above the concrete, perfectly tracing the route of a 500-kilovolt oil-filled cable across twenty miles of dead volume, its tail beating with a rhythmic, mechanical economy that matches the sixty-hertz vibration of the grid. When they die, their carcasses do not sink into soft mud to be buried; they tumble across the caissons, rolled along by the deep, artificial Coriolis currents, until they are fetched up against the forty-foot trash-racks of the lower flumes.

At the trash-racks, life is dense, violent, and frantic. 

The intake suction is constant, pulling millions of gallons per minute through steel bars spaced four inches apart. Anything that weakens in the vast water-column—a squid exhausted by the current, a dogfish blinded and deafened beyond survival, a school of mackerel caught in a sudden diversion downdraft—is pinned flat against the steel grates by hundreds of tons of hydrostatic pressure.

Here congregate the cleaners: massive, rubbery-limbed spider crabs that have migrated from the construction shafts. Their legs are long, squared-off, and tipped with recurved dactyls that hook directly into the bar-grating like rigging iron. They do not swim; they cannot be pulled through the bars because their carapaces are too broad. They live permanently pinned to the intake racks by the suction, facing outward into the torrential inflow with their claws raised. 

They feed continuously on the incoming drift, clipping off the fins and heads of pinned mackerel as the current wedges them against the steel. They are armored, industrial scavengers living in the mouth of a mechanical leviathan, oblivious to the thunder of the generation bays behind them, grown fat and thick-shelled on a diet of pure, pressure-fed casualty.

Above it all, in the vast, bright, vacant miles where no steel or concrete intervenes, there is only the water: pale turquoise, colder than bone, sterile as surgical wash, humming with the power of an absent world that built this place to run forever, and then left it running.
