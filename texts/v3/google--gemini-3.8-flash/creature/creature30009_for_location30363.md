The mud here is not mud; it is iron settling out of solution, and whatever walks or burrows through it must live on what is essentially machine-dross and the corpses of anaerobic bacteria. 

Beneath the four-inch blanket of red-gray flour that covers the steel floor plates, the silt is threaded with white, blind tubificid worms. They are thinner than sewing needles and utterly translucent, save for a central blood-line that burns a bright, unnatural scarlet from the sheer volume of hemoglobin needed to wring oxygen from this cold, drowned pocket. They do not feed on leaf-mold or pond detritus; they ingest the fine bacterial film that binds the flakes of rust together, turning the industrial fallout through their guts until the red floor is worked into tiny, uniform pellets, like gun-metal shot. Where the current slackens behind a valve manifold, these worms stand upright by the thousands, waving their tails in unison to catch the slight convective drift of the nine-second surge, resembling an expanse of pale, shivering mold that vanishes into the silt the instant a pressure-wave jars the plates.

Casting across this worm-meadow are the amphipods—flat-bodied, colorless scuds the size of pumpkin seeds, whose carapaces are so thin that the dark grey nodules of their hepatopancreas are visible through their backs. They have lost their eyes entirely; in their place, their second antennae have lengthened into whip-like filaments twice the length of their bodies, constantly sweeping the rust ahead of them with a nervous, shivering stroke. They graze on the "curtains"—the greasy, dangling sheets of iron-oxidizing bacteria that hang from the underside of the catwalks like sodden felt. When the nine-second shudder comes through the water, the amphipods do not flee; they simply tuck their legs, drop to the steel deck, and let the backwash slide over their curved backs, weighted down by the tiny granules of hematite that adhere to the bristles of their joints.

The bream that drift in the mid-water are not the deep-bodied, olive-bronze fish of the upper river reservoirs. Two centuries in the intake cavern have pulled them into something narrower, flatter, and blanched of all pigment except for an uneven, coppery stain around their opercular flaps and the bases of their dorsal spines—an accumulation of heavy metals their kidneys cannot shed. Their eyes are still large, but they are milky globes, cataract-struck and useless in the murk, functioning only as crude light-meters tuned to the amber smudge of the accumulator lanterns overhead. 

They navigate instead by a lateral line system so hypertrophied that the scales along their flanks are notched and pitted like antique brass ruler-stock. They feel the cavern: they know the precise distance to the southern blast door by the dampening of the high-pressure hiss from the ceiling leak; they know when they are drifting too near the intake screens because the cold pulls harder at their pectoral fins. Their bellies are permanently distended from a diet of manganese crust and worm-castings, their lips developed into thick, callous scraping pads that leave bright, scratched tracks of raw steel through the bacterial fur on the crane columns.

Closer to the metal itself, never rising more than three inches from the rivets, are the sculpins. 

They are heavy, miserable-looking things, four or five inches long, with broad, depressed heads that look as though they have been tamped down by the hydrostatic weight of the ninety feet of water above them. Their skin is scaleless, thick, and studded with hard, wart-like prickles that pick up the rust-flour until the fish are indistinguishable from the iron castings they rest on. They have abandoned swimming almost entirely. Their pectoral fins have developed thick, fleshy rays with tips like blunt fingers, which they use to crawl across the slick, vertical webs of the I-beams and hook themselves into the crevices where angle-irons meet. 

They live by vibration. A sculpin will sit motionless on a crane-rail for forty hours, its belly flattened to the steel, feeling through its pelvic girdles the slow, mechanical dying of the facility: the distant groan of the shaft bearings, the slap of a cable against a conduit, the tiny, sharp clicks of amphipods tumbling across a valve-flange. When an amphipod drifts within reach, the sculpin does not lunge; it expands its huge, bucket-like mouth with such sudden hydraulic violence that the water, the prey, and a mouthful of rust are sucked in together. It spits the rust back out through its gills in two red clouds, like a small, underwater puff of engine smoke, and settles back onto the bolt-head.

In the deepest recesses—the machine pits where the water turns oily and the current fails entirely—live the burbot. 

They are the oldest things in the battery, some of them five feet long, with bodies like waterlogged cedar balks. Their skin is a dull, liverish yellow-brown, blotched with irregular slate-colored clouds, covered in a slime so heavy and alkaline that it sloughs off in stringy ribbons that drift through the dead water. They have taken up residence in the cavities of the machinery itself: one lives inside the hollow casing of an open sluice-actuator, its tail coiled where the helical drive-screw once turned; another occupies the rectangular crawlway beneath the main breaker bank, its blunt snout protruding just far enough from the concrete lip to sample the intake draft.

These burbot are slow to the point of being mineral. Their metabolic rates have dropped to match the nine-degree water and the endless, unvarying dark. They feed rarely, their long, pale chins bearing single, thick barbels that drag through the silt like leadlines. When they strike, it is not with the darting speed of river fish, but with a blind, heavy heave of their muscular tails that moves fifty gallons of water at once, rolling up a cloud of iron dust that takes six hours to settle back onto the catwalks. They take the stunted bream whole, or crush the larger sculpins between broad pads of cardiform teeth that feel like coarse rasp-files. 

Around their gills and between the soft rays of their fins cling pale, flattened leeches, three inches long, their bodies stained the same sickly ochre as the steel. These leeches never detach; they are born on the burbot, they feed on the iron-thick lymph of the burbot, and when the fish finally wedges its head into a drainage notch to die, they crawl into its mouth and the hollow cavities behind its eyes, continuing to draw moisture from the decaying meat until the cavern’s small, transparent amphipods arrive to clean the skull down to its white, spongy cartilage.

Above them all, near the high barrel vault where the soda-straws hang, there is a different sort of inhabitant: thousands of tiny, calcareous tube-building hydrozoans that have encrusted the lower inches of the dripping stalactites. They are river-creatures that have wandered down from the lake above, stunted by the absence of sun and the cold pressure, forming dense, brittle collars around the lime-tubes. They extend minute, thread-like tentacles into the rising current, catching the bacterial flakes that peel off the high-pressure leak. 

The light from the sour accumulator lanterns touches them first, turning their translucent bodies the color of weak cider. When the lanterns occasionally stutter—a brief drop in the two-hundred-year-old chemical reserve that makes the filaments flutter like dying moths—the whole colony contracts in a single, dry spasm, a faint, papery ripple that travels sixty yards through the dark, before the water-drum takes over again, and the nine-second shudder shakes the iron, and the cavern returns to its blind, rhythmic sift.
