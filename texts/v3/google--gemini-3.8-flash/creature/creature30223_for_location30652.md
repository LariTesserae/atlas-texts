They have the low, heavy-hipped walk of people who live on wet timbers. 

Their toes are splayed wide, the joints thick and knobbed from gripping slick birch poles and the sharp, stepping edges of slate. Almost no one wears hard shoes. To wear leather with a stiff sole here is to slip and break a collarbone against the basalt. Instead, they wrap their feet in strips of unshorn sheepskin, wool-side in against the skin to keep the blood moving, the outer hide soaked in tallow until it turns translucent and gummy, like dried tendon. These wraps are scored across the soles with a flint blade—criss-cross patterns of fine cuts that bite into the wet lichen and the film of stone-sweat. When they walk across the roof-alleys, they make no more sound than a wet sack dropped from a hand’s height.

Their bodies are broad, shortened by constant ducking under low lintels, with shoulders rounded from levering stones in tight crawlways where a man cannot stand upright. Because the water is cold enough to ache in the marrow, they grease themselves. Every morning, before the gray light has even cleared the eastern lip of the fracture, they rub their arms, throats, and thighs with rendered suet mixed with powdered charcoal from the hearths. The fat keeps the seeping run-off from soaking into the skin; the charcoal turns them the color of the wet ironstone walls they lean against. In the dim, tallow-lit tiers, a person looks less like an animal and more like a pillar of wet shale that has decided to shift its weight.

Their hair is never worn loose. Wet hair in this cold is a sickness; it trails in the troughs, gets caught in the rawhide lashings, and robs the skull of heat. Both men and women braid it tight against the scalp in six or eight parallel rows, then plaster the braids down with sheep tallow until the head looks as smooth and dark as a river pebble. 

They speak in the chest, never in the teeth. High sounds—whistles, shouting, sharp laughter—are deadened immediately by the sodden awnings and the curtain of falling water, but a low hum carries through the stone itself. If a weir-stone slips three levels down, the woman sitting over her grease-lamp forty paces above does not hear a crash; she feels a brief, thudding stutter in the seat of her pelvis through the basalt bench, and she reaches for her mallet without looking up. Their language has shed its sibilants and its long vowels. It is mostly clicks of the tongue against the palate and short, guttural thumps that do not disturb the cold air enough to turn into mist. When they wish to be heard across an alley, they do not call out; they rap twice on a structural post with a bone ring worn on the middle knuckle. Two taps: *clear the gutter*. Three taps: *the weir is holding*. One long scrape: *the lower stack is flooding*.

There are no old people among them whose hands are not hooked. The constant wet, combined with the draft that pulls perpetually down the cut from the plateau, bends the knuckles into hard, unstraightening talons by the fortieth year. Yet these hands are remarkably dexterous with wet materials. A man whose fingers cannot lay flat on a board can still tie a wet half-hitch in rawhide in the dark, tightening the knot with his teeth while his hands hold the tension against an eight-hundred-pound lintel.

They are not native to the dark, but their eyes have settled into it. The irises are pale—watery hazel, milk-gray, or the color of washed flint—and the pupils remain wide, even when the midday silver strikes down through the cut. They squint upward, hating the sky. To look up is to take the drip full in the eyes and risk the small, glass-like flakes of shale that the wind occasionally kicks off the northern rim. When they look at one another, their gaze is level, sliding sideways along the horizontal seams of the masonry.

Beside them live the sheep, though they are hardly recognizable as such. 

They are small, stunted beasts, kept in the deep, lightless vaults carved into the dry seams behind the huts, where the rock does not weep. There are only a few score of them in the whole cut, brought down as lambs in wicker baskets from the steppe above and never returned to the sun. They do not have wool in the common sense; the constant damp has caused their fleeces to mat into dense, felted sheets three fingers thick, greasy with natural lanolin and stained iron-black by the floor. They are fed on bundles of dry dwarf-willow and steppe sage lowered on long hemp cables from the rim, which the sheep chew in the dark with a dry, rhythmic grinding that sounds like stones turning in a mill. Their hooves do not wear down against the soft, wet shale of their stalls; every month, the boys must pare them back with slate knives, carving away the soft, curling horn so the beasts can stand level. They do not bleat. They have learned the dead quiet of the cut, and only make a wet, coughing snort when a lantern is brought near their stalls.

Between the roofs and the stone faces live the blind spiders. They are pale, almost transparent, the size of a thumbnail, with legs as thin as horsehair. They spin no webs to catch flying things, for there are no flies; instead, they stretch single, sticky trip-lines across the chinks where the liverwort grows, waiting for the water-swollen beetles that crawl out of the soaked birch bark. When a beetle touches a strand, the spider does not bite to kill; it coats the beetle’s joints in a milky, water-hardening glue, cementing it to the stone until it starves, then feeds on the cold liquid within the shell.

In the lower tiers, where the gutters converge into the deep sumps, life becomes slower and softer. 

Here live the tallowers and the salt-scrapers. Their skin is different from that of the people who work the high bridges; it is white, puffy, and perpetually water-logged, the skin of the palms peeling away in thin, pale ribbons like birch bark. They spend hours neck-deep in the cold run-off, their toes wedged into the drainage slots to feel for blockages of silt or drowned beetles before the pressure backs up and drowns the hearths above. They are the ones who harvest the blind loaches, reaching into the deep rock bins with willow wicker baskets, feeling for the cold, muscular thrash of the fish against their calves. The loaches are not cooked—wood is too precious to waste on small meat—they are split with a fingernail, salted with the subterranean crust scraped from the dry fracture-walls, and eaten raw, their bones soft as wet thread.

When someone dies, they do not carry the corpse up to the wind on the plateau. To lift dead weight four hundred paces up the vertical ropes is an enormous labor, and the steppe has no wood to burn them. Instead, they wrap the body in the dead person’s own sodden reed mats, binding it with the gray, swollen rawhide stays taken from an old roof. They do not bury it in the rock—there is no earth to dig—they wedge the bundle into one of the deep, pressurized crevices where the water flows fastest from the native ironstone.

In that cold, sterile wash, the flesh does not putrefy. It cures. Over the course of three or four seasons, the salt and the minerals in the seeping water replace the soft fats of the body, turning the limbs into something heavy, hard, and gray, indistinguishable from the petrified birch timbers of the foundations. 

Eventually, when the weir needs shoring or a lintel cracks under the weight of the upper tier, they reach into the crevice with wooden crowbars, lever the cold, stony shape out of the current, and stack it into the masonry of the wall. The town is built of shale, basalt, and bone; and when a man leans his greased back against the door-post to rest between the surges, he is often resting against the iron-hard thigh of his grandfather, both of them holding up the city, both of them wet to the core, both of them keeping the cold water from the flame.
