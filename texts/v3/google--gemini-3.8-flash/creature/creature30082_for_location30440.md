They are called the Silt-Footed, or simply the *Drift-Holders*, though they have no name for themselves that relies on sound. 

To look at them is to see the slab repeated in meat. They have lost the waist; their torsos are thick, barrel-shaped columns of lard and sinew that taper directly into thighs as broad as cedar stumps. Under their skin lies an unbroken layer of dense, cold-cured fat, yellow as suet, which keeps their internal organs from chilling against the sheet-water that constantly spatters their knees. They do not have toes in any ordinary sense; generations of binding their feet in boiled stomach-hide have fused their digits into solid, spade-like pads, calloused half an inch thick and curved slightly inward, the better to plane over the olive felt without stubbing against the lips of the runnels.

They rarely straighten their spines. To stand erect here is to invite the purple sky to scrape your crown, and so they live in a permanent forward crouch, their long arms dangling until their knuckles graze the current. Their knuckles are tipped with broad, flat nails like horn spatulas, stained dark by iron-rot. When they skate across the stone, they use these knuckles as trailing outriggers, four-pointing their way through the spray with a rhythmic, sweeping lope that leaves twin wakes of white froth behind them.

Because their ears are corked with black plugs of lake-tar and sheep-tallow, they have moved their language into their skeletons. 

When two of them meet along a weir, they do not gesture with their hands—hands are for holding the chert-headed scrapers or hauling the wet-nets. Instead, they drop to their knees in the sheet-flow, lean forward until their foreheads touch, and vocalize through their skulls. It is a dry, interior grating, a grinding of the molar beds and a vibration of the frontal sinus that buzzes directly through the bone of the brow. A man can feel his neighbor’s hunger as an electric itch behind his eyebrows; he can feel an approaching brine-spout as an aching rattle in his teeth three miles before the stone uncouples. 

Inside their shelters, beneath the great bowed arches of the filter-feeder ribs, it is not dry—nothing here is ever dry—but the water is still. The rib-huts are anchored by three-ton stones lashed with braided sinew, and inside, the sheet-flow is baffled by low dikes of river-cobble, creating a dead-water pool only an ankle deep. 

Here, they burn the skimmers.

They do not have wood for fire, but they have grease. The plate-skimmers are mostly shell and oar, but beneath the lead-grey dorsal dome lies a soft, greyish-yellow liver that liquefies at the touch of a warm finger. The people press this paste into shallow troughs pecked out of the gneiss floor and drop in wicks made of twisted felt-lichen dried over a slow salt-smear. The lamps burn with a flat, blue-green flame that gives off no smoke, only a sharp, reeking steam of vaporized phosphorus and old marrow. In this dim, drowned light, their faces are revealed: hairless, lashless, their eyes milky with the permanent cataracts that come from staring down into the water’s bacterial sheen for sixty seasons.

They eat the skimmers raw, popping the small, thumb-sized juveniles into their mouths whole, like sea-grapes, their flat molars cracking the uncalcified horn with the sound of snapping twigs. The larger ones, the dinner-plate elders whose backs are encrusted with barnacles of iron-slag, must be broken with the chert adzes. The meat is stringy, grey, and tastes of cold salt and copper, but it is rich in oil. A hunter will smear his lips and eyelids with it before going out into the spray, until he looks as though he were carved from wet lard.

Alongside them, sharing the low clearance of the sky, are the *Thong-Hounds*—though they are neither dogs nor eels, but something stranded between the two. 

These are ribbons of pale, scaleless muscle, eight feet long and no thicker than a man’s wrist, that live entirely in the thumb-deep runnels. They have no limbs, but their bellies are studded with four rows of hooked, cartilaginous claws that fit into the microscopic scratches in the stone. They move with an awful, fluid speed, sliding through the anoxic film like dropped ribbons of grease. They are blind; their small, blunted heads terminate in four fleshy, star-shaped nostrils that taste the wake of the plate-skimmers. 

When a thong-hound finds a skimmer, it does not bite the shell; it flows beneath it. Its body is so flat it can squeeze between the moving oar-limbs and the stone, sliding into the soft gill-vaults on the underside. Once inside, it inflates its middle section with brine, popping the skimmer’s dorsal plate from its belly-shield with a wet *clack*, then devours the animal from the center out while the oars are still feebly paddling the current.

The people hunt the hounds with weighted gaffs, not for their meat—which is sour with urea—but for their hides. The skin of a thong-hound, stripped whole and turned inside out, makes a seamless sleeve that can be drawn over an arm or a leg to keep the felt-rot from taking the skin. 

For the felt is hungry, too.

The black-olive carpet that covers the gneiss is not vegetable; it is a single, continental animal, dormant where the current is fast, but ravenous in the shallows where the water warms by half a degree. If a man sleeps with his ankle trailing out of his skin-bed into the dead-water, he will wake to find the felt has crept over his heel—not stinging, but sealing itself to his pores with millions of microscopic calcareous suckers. By mid-day, the skin beneath will be soft, white, and liquefied, digested by the colonial amoebae into a smear of grey mush that the felt drinks down to the rock. 

Because of this, the old do not die in their beds. When a person’s wrists grow too stiff to hold the chisel, or when the bone-talk in their brow becomes jumbled with the hum of the bull-worms, they walk south-southwest. 

They do not take their boots. They go out bare-soled onto the greased stone, sliding with the current, letting the inch of melt wash over their arches. They walk until their numbed legs betray them, and when they fall, they do not fight the sheet. They lie face down in the thumb-deep rill, their arms spread wide, their mouths open to the cold iron-wash. 

Within an hour, the plate-skimmers have found them. The disc-shells crowd around the body like drifting coins, their jointed oars clicking and scraping as they climb over the shoulders and the hips, rasping away the grease and the fat with their chert-hard tongues. Behind them come the thong-hounds, whipping through the shallows to tear at the soft belly, and beneath it all, three feet down in the boreholes, the bull-worms feel the thud of the carcass settling against the rock and hammer their great horn plugs against the granite, drumming the dead down into the basin of the world.

By dusk, there is only the slab again, smooth and dark, and the water sliding across it, south-southwest, carrying a few strands of grey gut toward the lip that no one has ever seen.
