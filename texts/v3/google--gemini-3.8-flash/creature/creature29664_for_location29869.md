They have no word for light, but they have forty-seven distinct designations for the texture of dampness against skin. 

To call them people is to lean on a shape that only roughly applies: they are erect, two-legged, and possess the long-fingered forelimbs of an artisan, but they are no longer than a hemlock needle. Their skin is not meat-colored; it is the curdled, bluish-white of lard kept too long in a dark croft, semi-translucent so that against the damp cambium of the root wall, the gray branchings of their mesenteric vessels pulse with the cold sluggishness of winter oil. They have no eyes. Where eyes might have belonged in some long-forgotten surface ancestor, there are only twin, thumb-like depressions filled with fatty cushions, topped by clusters of stiff, pale vibrissae—the temple-bristles—which stir whenever the air rolls down from the fissure. 

They call themselves nothing that translates to a name, but the sound they make when marking territory along the xylem ledges is a double-click of the tongue against the palette, like a thorn snapping under a fingernail: *Tcha-k*.

### The Citizens of the Grain

A citizen does not walk; they adhere. The tips of their toes and fingers terminate in blunt spatulas of self-sharpening horn, perfectly contoured to catch the micro-grooves left by the oak’s tracheid vessels. When an artisan traverses the vertical pitch between the Third Shingle District and the Minnow Quays, they move sideways, their flat bellies pressed against the weeping wood, reading the moisture with their rib-margins. 

They live in dense, familial clans within the "hollow-grain"—the narrow, vertical chambers opened where the winter freeze pulled heartwood fibers apart like celery strings. A single dwelling might house four generations in a vertical shaft no thicker than a quill. At the core of each dwelling sits the eldest female, the *Stump-Wife*, whose skin has grown yellow and leathery from absorbing the concentrated oak tannins of the seepage. Her fingers have long since ceased their knapping; she spends her decades anchored by her toenails to a deep anchor-bore, her hands constantly sifting thistle-fluff to grade it for caulking, while her mouth regurgitates a sticky, sweetish paste of masticated springtail fat and digested cambium pulp, which feeds the naked, grub-like young.

The young are born blind even to touch, curled in transparent caul-sacks that look indistinguishable from the heavy water beads clinging to the wood. They are hung in clusters from the dry underbellies of beetle-wing roofs until their horn-tips harden enough to grip the bark fibers.

The men and non-breeding females are the *Heifers* and *Chisel-men*. Their entire existence is defined by maintenance against two encroaching deaths: drowning in the rising tannin pool, and being crushed by the tree itself.

The tree is alive, and it is trying to heal. The smooth, white callus-tissue—the wound-flesh the burr oak produces to close the tear—creeps forward at the imperceptible speed of cold syrup. To the citizens, this is not growth; it is an invading, fleshy stone. Day and night, squads of four chisel-men, bound together by ropes of twisted hog-peanut root, attack the creeping domes of callus with tiny picks fashioned from the serrated jawbones of water shrews (dragged down, piece by piece, from ancient drift-wrack caught in the upper crack). They quarry the wound-flesh before it can solidify into lignified wood. They pare it into translucent shavings, which are dried, salted with mineral dust scraped from the fen-water, and bundled into the city’s primary building material: *skin-slate*.

### The Fauna of the Crevice

Between the scaffolded avenues of skin-slate, the cavity swarms with non-citizens, creatures who belong to the dampness rather than the craft.

The most numerous are the **Tallow-Mites**, flat, round arachnids the size of sand-flea eggs, entirely without pigment, that drift in slow constellations over the wet surfaces. They are neither hunted nor eradicated; they are the cleaners. They follow behind the chisel-gangs, their mouthparts sweeping the slick trail of sweat and epithelial slough left by the citizens’ bare feet. A citizen resting on a terrace will allow hundreds of tallow-mites to crawl over their torso, cleaning the fungus from their skin folds with minute, tickling shears.

Lower down, where the wood gives way to the black, stagnant depths of the bottom trough, rule the **Dwarf Mud-Minnows**. These are ancient, degenerate stock, stranded in the root-pockets during the high floods of autumn and adapted to a life without horizon. They are two inches long, scaleless, their skins mottled like wet slate. Because food is rare, their jaws are hinged like those of deep-sea vipers, able to swallow a creature half their own bulk. They hang motionless in the tannin water, suspended between submerged root-spurs, their milky, cataract-covered eyes sensing only the fall of debris from the ledges above. 

When a hunting party leaves the docks, they do not cast nets; the water is too dense with sunken oak-fibers. Instead, three hunters lower a "chime"—a hollowed beetle tibia strung with tiny shards of quartz. They tap the submerged wood with a bone rod. The vibration mimics the drowning struggle of a heavy fen-beetle. When the minnow strikes, a barbed toggle-iron, filed from the rib of a vole, is driven through the soft flesh behind its gill-raker. 

The butchery of a minnow is a civic holiday. The fish is hauled up the vertical grain by windlasses made of spider-silk cord and pine-splinter cogs, its tail beating violently against the tenements, spraying the lower districts with icy, tea-colored brine. Once landed on the shingled docks, the crowds descend with flint scrapers. Nothing is wasted. The silver-white skin is cured with wood-ash to make water-repellent cloaks for the deep-ditch patrols; the fatty liver is pressed in tiny stone mortises to yield a clear, grease-scented tallow that smells of ditch-bottoms; and the long, needle-fine ribs are immediately claimed by the Knappers’ Guild to be ground into new chisels.

Above the city, in the "Crown"—the high, narrow vault where the frost-crack pinches together toward the surface of the fen—hangs the **Spiracle Colony**. These are thousands of pupae of an unknown crane-fly, suspended by single, sticky threads. They are the city’s barometers. They do not drop down, nor do they feed; they merely breathe. In the absolute cold, their metabolism is slowed to a whisper, but their great, branched respiratory horns are hypersensitive to the atmospheric weight of the swamp above. 

When a heavy snow settles on the fen, pressing the moss down into the peat, the spiracles of the pupae contract with an audible, collective click—a sound the citizens liken to dry peas dropped upon a drum. It is the warning. It means the peat is sealing, that the seepage of air will soon thin, and that the "Long Breath" is coming.

### The Pulse and the Passing

Every twenty minutes, the lung of the marsh heaves. 

It begins not with a sound, but with an oily sheen appearing on the black surface of the water trough. The tannin pool rises half a fingernail’s width as the fen-ice settles. Then comes the draft: a freezing, leaden displacement of air that smells of sulfur, ancient reed-beds, and the wet hair of drowned things.

The citizens do not speak during the pulse. 

In the workshops, the chisels stop. In the nurseries, the Stump-Wives press their broad, cold palms over the caul-clusters to keep them from shivering loose. The springtails, which ordinarily drift in continuous, white-sheet migrations across the shoals, fall flat, anchoring their jumping forks into the wood grain until their millions of bodies form an immobile, frosted crust over the uncarved timber.

In that twenty-minute lull, when the city holds its breath, the taproot speaks. 

It is the only god they know: the deep, subterranean groaning of the burr oak as the frost works through its crown forty feet above in the gray winter air. The wood creaks with a sound like iron chains being dragged across gravel. The fibers stretch. A single droplet of xylem-sap, clear as tears and bitter with iron, detaches from the cambium ceiling, falls through three inches of black void, and strikes the fish-scale pavement of the Great Pier.

*Plink.*

A vibration travels through the wood. Every citizen feels it through their horn-toes, their rib-margins, the hairs of their temples. They read in the impact whether the crack has widened by a hair's breadth, or whether the frost has loosened its grip, or whether the white root-tips are beginning to swell with the terrible, crushing promise of the thaw.

Then the draft releases. The swamp above inhales.

The chisel-men lean their weight against the shrews' teeth once more. The springtail carpet breaks into a frantic, salt-shaker rattle of movement. Down on the quays, the toggle-ropes hum as another harpoon is launched into the black water, and the city continues its furious, minuscule carving into the flesh of the thing that holds it.
