The people who live in the scoop have ribs that do not taper toward the hip as the ribs of valley-dwellers do, but drop straight like the staves of an unhooped cask, giving them chests as deep from breastbone to spine as they are across. When a man takes off his hide vest to work a wedge into a juniper log, his back shows no distinct shoulder blades; the muscle lies in flat, overlapping slabs from the base of the skull down to the kidneys, cross-hatched with pale stretch-scars from the carrying-yokes. They do not pant. Their breath is long, low, and shallow, drawn through nostrils narrowed to slits by thick, fleshy wings that keep the grit from drying the throat. When they speak, they use the teeth and the palate more than the throat, producing a dry, clicking mutter that sounds remarkably like the beetles in the moss-mats—a language stripped of breathy vowels, engineered not to waste water on the air.

They call themselves simply the *Tallow-keepers*, though there is little tallow to be had; the name comes from the yellow fat of the rock-badger, which is their currency, their medicine, and their god.

A badger is never killed casually. The creatures live in the foundation courses of the terraces, in runs lined with smooth river-shingle that have been polished by forty generations of bellies. They are the size of fat wethers, flat-headed and gray-bristled, with three-toed feet that are soft and moist beneath, like the pad of a dog’s foot dipped in pitch. They do not bite their minders; they have forgotten how to flee. At noon, when the rock is at its hottest and the heat-tremble makes the lip of the drop look like water running over a weir, the badgers crawl out onto the flat thresholds of the lower tier, their legs splayed behind them like spatchcocked fowl, groaning in their throats with a sound like oil bubbling in a pan. Children go among them with flat horn scrapers, lifting the greasy yellow crust that exudes from the scent-glands between their hindquarters. This grease is saved in bladders of badger-gut; it never spoils. It smells of wild sage, old mutton, and the bitter copper of the rock. With it, the people grease their eyes against the bronze glare, oil the joints of their stone-hammers, and fry the bitter leaves of the *palo-seco*.

The children themselves are slow-footed and quiet. They are taught from the second year to keep three points of contact on any stone that slopes steeper than a saddle-pommel. A boy of seven does not run after a kicked pebble; he watches it bounce four times and vanish over the rim into the white sea below without moving his head. His eyes are small, ringed with amber grease, and the whites have a yellowish, liver-spotted cast from the dust that never leaves the air.

Around the settlement’s edge, where the grey porphyry rounds off toward the great drop, dwell the dew-scrapers. These are the youngest men, whose lungs are still wide enough to bear the dead chill of the northern faces before the sun reaches them. They wake when the swiftlets are still silent—an hour when the mountain feels heavier than at noon, cold as iron, and the stone creaks as it shrinks. They climb by the greasy grooves worn into the vertical ribs of the rock, their bodies naked save for a loin-apron of braided broom and a sheep-horn trough slung across the breast.

The scraping is an art of the wrist. The dew does not lie in drops; it is an ash-grey skin, no thicker than the film on cooling milk, that clings to the north-facing bulges where the stone has been licked smooth by the clouds. If the horn scraper is held too flat, it gathers stone-meal; if held too steep, the water shatters into fog and is lost to the rising thermal before it can trickle down into the horn’s hollow. A scraper who fills two goatskins before the bronze light turns yellow is a man of weight in the scoop. His knees are like walnuts, calloused to the grey bone from kneeling on porphyry, and he carries his chin thrust out from the constant crane of looking upward for the wet veins in the dark.

The old people do not die of rots or fluxes. They dry. At sixty, their skin has the consistency of salted calf-skin; their ears shrink against their temples until they resemble the withered ears of corn left on an autumn stalk. They lose their teeth not to decay, for there is no sugar here, but to the flour ground from the dried broom-seeds, which is one-tenth granite silt. A tooth wears down until the root is exposed and the pink nerve calcifies into a brown plug, and then the tooth is no more painful than an old thumb-callus.

When an old woman can no longer lift the heavy stone spindle or scrape the moss, she does not take to her bed, for beds are unknown; people sleep on flat slabs of slate set three inches off the floor to keep out the centipedes, covered with mats of split *palo-seco*. Instead, she sits at the sun-line where the heat stays longest, against the eastern buttress of the bread-ovens. She holds a smooth stone between her palms, turning it over and over to polish it for a hearth-block, and drinks the *leche-negra*—the boiled-down juice of the black succulent roots that grow in the crevices where the vultures roost.

It takes three months for a woman to die of the juice. Her urine turns the color of iron-rust, then stops altogether. Her flesh sinks into the hollows of her bones until her shins look like dry split juniper, and her eyes sink so deep into the sockets that from two paces away she appears to have four nostrils and no eyes at all. When the heart finally stops—always in the dry, still hour after sundown—she does not smell. She smells only of the *palo-seco* and the dung-smoke that has soaked into her hair for seventy years.

They do not dig graves. To break the stone would be an insanity that would dull six score of their thin iron picks, and the caliche clay is too precious to waste on dead clay. They carry the body up to the high ledges where the bearded nettle hangs down like the grey hair of an old man drowned in a cistern.

There, in the thick of the hanging weed, live the *tijeretas*—long-bodied, six-legged earwigs the size of a man’s middle finger, with shells like burned horn and great, blunt pincer-tails that can snip a dried broom-stem like straw. They live by the millions in the dark mats of the nettle, feeding on the swiftlet-chicks that fall from the high holes and the winged seeds that float up on the thermals from the wet green valleys down below.

The dew-scrapers lash the corpse to the roots of the nettle with thongs of badger-hide. They do not strip the dead; they leave them in their grease-blackened tunics, with their horn spoons still tucked into their belts. Within forty-eight hours, the *tijeretas* have found the soft parts under the wool. In three weeks, there is only the frame, white and clean as salt, swinging in the beard of the weed whenever the wind gets high enough to rock the scoop. The bones do not fall; the nettle grows through the empty eye-holes and around the ribs, knotting them into its own living curtain, so that the rim of the hollow is hung with three or four hundred years of skeletons, all woven together by the grey fibers, their lower jaws hanging open toward the great fall of air as if they were shouting down into the clouds.

The vultures know this rim. They roost among the lower rib-cages, where the stone provides a three-inch lip out of the wind. They are small for peak-vultures, no larger than fat geese, with naked necks of a sickly lilac shade that turns scarlet when they quarrel over the swiftlet-eggs. They have lived with the Tallow-keepers so long that they will walk into the house-lanes to snatch the rind of a succulent from a child’s fingers, hissing like wet wood on a fire, their broad, blunt-clawed feet clapping on the caliche blocks with the sound of flat slippers. The dogs ignore them; a dog knows that to snap at a lilac-neck is to invite a strike from a beak that can shear the grease-wart off a badger’s flank.

These hairless dogs are the true clocks of the scoop. They have no names, only lines of breeding that run back into the dark stone before the terraces were three tiers high. They are pale, liver-colored, their hide covered in a fine, greasy scurf that comes from sleeping in the ashes of the bread-ovens. They never bark; the altitude has narrowed their windpipes until they can only produce a dry, coughing whistle, not unlike the badgers, though more rhythmic.

At midday, when the sun is directly above the bronze ceiling of the sky and the light has no shadows—only a flat, tallow-colored weight that makes the knees ache—the dogs do not sleep. They gather on the highest threshing-floor, twenty or thirty of them, and turn their muzzles toward the southern wall of the scoop.

They watch the *seam*.

The seam is a crack in the ironstone three hundred yards above the highest house. It is no wider than a spade-blade, but it runs deep into the cold bone of the mountain, down where the old water used to dwell. Once or twice in a moon, when the bronze haze gathers thickest and the lightning down in the valleys sends up a smell like burning sulphur, the seam breathes out.

It is not wind; it is a single, slow exhalation of air so cold that the grey dew on the upper rocks turns instantly to white fur. With the cold comes the dust of the deep rock—a flour so fine it hangs in the nostrils like flour from an old bin, smelling of things that were dead before the mountain was rounded.

When the seam breathes, the dogs rise on their toes, their hairless ears pricked until the tips touch, and let out their coughing whistle in unison. Down in the narrow ways, the women hear it. Without looking up from their spindles, they reach out with their bare feet and draw the hide sledges of dung-cakes under the overhangs of the roofs. The children leave their pebbles and sit in the doorways behind the grease-cured curtains, their knees pulled to their chins, listening.

The breath lasts no longer than it takes to turn a spindle thrice. Then the bronze haze settles back into the scoop; the air thickens again to its stagnant, body-warm weight; the swiftlets resume their pea-rattle in the sandstone pores; and the dogs drop back into the warm grey dust of the threshing-floor, one by one, like sacks of salt thrown down from a shoulder.

Nothing has changed. A cubit of caliche has cracked beneath the weight of the third tier; an old man has turned his stone between his palms four thousand times; a swiftlet has dropped from the rim, fallen four hundred feet through the amber light, opened its wings with a sound like a whip-crack, and climbed back up toward the sun that no one in the scoop can see.
