To understand what lives here, one must understand that they do not possess a history of the wild. There is no ancestral memory of mud, drought, or hawk-shadow coded into their nerves. They are native only to the tolerances: to thirty-eight degrees, to four inches of clearance beneath a cross-beam, to the alkaline slick that coats the galvanized stanchions. They have no claws meant for digging, because there is nothing to dig; no shells meant for crushing armor, because there is nothing that strikes; and no eyes worthy of the name, because in a world lit everywhere by the same amber-white glare, the gaze is entirely useless.

The largest among them—the broad, wallowing things that crowd the flumes—are known to the logic of the place simply as the *rafts*. 

They are nominally vertebrate, though their skeletons have lost the rigid lime of terrestrial bone, softening into a flexible, gristly cartilage that yields under pressure without snapping. They are shaped like truncated bolster pillows, six to eight feet in length, their hide an unblemished, greasy chamois, somewhere between the color of skimmed milk and the underside of a scalded tongue. They possess four paddle-like appendages that have ceased to be limbs and become broad, pneumatic cushions. With these, they do not crawl; they shift. They paddle against the lead plating with a slow, hydraulic ripple of their flanks, levering their great mass through the shallows with no more effort than a tongue moving inside a cheek.

Their faces are flat discs of muscle, dominated by an immense, ring-shaped mouth lined not with teeth, but with overlapping plates of dark, wet horn. These plates do not chew; they plane. A raft spends fourteen hours of the day anchored by suction to the base of a transformer bank or the submerged lip of a basalt conduit, shaving away the yellow paraffin-rich caulking that oozes from the gaskets, or drinking the dense, starchy scum that the misters beat down onto the surface of the water. 

When they sleep, they sleep in rafts of twenty or forty, wedged so tightly into the troughs that the water is forced to run *over* their backs rather than around them. The water does not chill them, and they do not chill the water; their internal temperature has been measured to the tenth of a degree against the resistance coils below the floor. Their hearts, three-chambered and the size of oil drums, beat only five or six times a minute. To rest a hand upon the flank of a sleeping raft is to feel the vibration of the distant sumps transmitted directly through three hundredweight of warm, blubbery meat.

Beneath the rafts, occupying the two inches between their white bellies and the diamond-tread iron, live the *shingles*.

These are the crustaceans that form the false floor. Seen individually, a shingle is a pale, rectangular disc of flexible keratin, perhaps fifteen inches across and thin as a horn spoon. Its carapace is completely smooth, lacking the spines, hairs, or calcified knobs of marine crabs; it is engineered for stacking. Millions of them lie imbricated across the submerged pavement, overlapping like wet slate on a low-pitched roof. 

Their life is an exercise in pure capillary action. Through microscopic grooves on their ventral plates, they draw the thin film of water that runs between the lead floor and their shells, stripping it of the synthetic amino acids metered out by the brass diffusers. They do not have eyes, nor antennae; in their place, they extend fringes of silken, colorless filaments that knit into the filaments of their neighbors. Through this network, a single touch—a boot heel, or the heavy drag of a passing raft—is conducted across half an acre in a fraction of a second, not as an alarm, but as a minor adjustment of hydrodynamic trim. The entire floor breathes downward by three millimeters, locks its plates to let the mass slide across, and then floats upward again on a cushion of warm discharge.

They do not reproduce by dispersal. There are no planktonic larvae to be lost down the drainage grates. Instead, the female shingle retains her brood beneath her abdominal apron until they are already broad, flat, and weighted with tiny, ingested beads of lead shot. When they slip from her, they do not swim; they drop immediately to the metal, find an empty seam between two plates, and wedge themselves in.

Higher up, out of the water, the vertical architecture has its own inhabitants. 

The A-frames, the steam-risers, and the overhead gantry rails are the territory of the *weft*. They appear, at first glance, like thick, trailing cables of pinkish-white wax that have dripped from the zinc rafters. In truth, they are colonial chordates—creatures related to the sea-squirt, but stretched into ribbons eighty feet long, clinging to the rivet-lines by thousands of microscopic cup-grips. They live where the mist is thickest. Their skin is porous, drinking the saturated atmosphere, absorbing the atomized mineral fats that rise from the open weir-lips. 

The weft do not fall. If a length is severed by the passage of an automated gantry crane, the severed portion does not die; it hangs from the lower flanges, its lower extremity already swelling into a blunt, blind bulb that begins to produce new adhesive disks before the crane has traveled another mile down its rail.

Nothing ever rots here. This is not because death does not occur, but because the margin between the living and the mechanical is too narrow for decay to find purchase. 

When a raft reaches the end of its life—when its gristly heart finally desynchronizes from the sixty-cycle hum of the floor—it does not bloat. It does not float. The density of its tissues is balanced so precisely against the specific gravity of the alkaline water that it remains seated on the lead floor, its mouth still pressed to the nutrient vent. Within minutes, the shingles beneath it begin to feed, their horn plates working with the calm, methodical speed of planing machines. From the drainage trenches come the *siphon-worms*: translucent tubes of pure muscle, thick as a man’s forearm, that emerge from the perforated brass covers. They enter the carcass through the spiracles and the mouth. 

There is no smell of carrion. There is only the faint, sour musk of warm lard and the clicking of millions of tiny chitinous blades against zinc. In twelve hours, a four-ton animal is reduced to a clean, grey heap of cartilage nodules, which are themselves slowly dissolved by the mildly caustic wash until nothing remains to interrupt the three-inch depth of the water. The lead plate beneath is scrubbed brighter than it was before.

The empty cradles pass overhead, their steel wheels humming on the greased monorails. Below, in the endless amber noon, a sow raft shifts her flank to make room for three smaller ones that have drifted down from the weir-lip. She does not open her nostrils wider; she does not flinch as the hot water cascades over her brow. 

She has no voice. The rafters have no echo. There is only the sigh of the misters, the dull metallic clank of an automatic weir tipping five miles to the south, and the warm, deep, unmolested slumber of a billion tons of meat that knows it is exactly where it was made to be.
