At first, the only evidence of an occupant is negative: an absence where condensation ought to be. 

On the enamel flank of the central switchgear cabinet, among the thousands of trembling, identical beads of water that form and swell in the cold, there is a clean, horizontal smear. Something broad and slightly greasy has pressed against the steel, wiping a swath through the dew six inches above the deck. The track is not fresh—the droplet-skin has begun to re-knit across its margins—but near the lower edge of the cabinet door, where the metal meets the channel-iron base, the water has been interrupted more deliberately. A tongue, or something working with the flat, rhythmic stroke of a tongue, has licked the condensation clean from the zinc plating, leaving faint, parallel striations where coarse papillae scraped through the film of machine oil.

Higher up, where the horsehair felt is bound to the refrigeration lines with spiraled wire, the insulation has been interfered with. It has not been ripped away with violence, but picked at, loosened strand by strand, as though tested for nutritional value or warmth. Small tufts of the coarse, black-and-grey hair have been plucked out and pressed into the damp corners where the floor-angles meet the bulkheads, forming small, sodden wads that have begun to host their own faint crowns of turnip-smelling mold. 

Whatever makes these marks does not move across the open spaces. It cannot. The clearance between the valve wheels and the unbolted tread-plates is too narrow for an upright spine, and the cold rising from the earth is an active deterrent. To stay low is to touch the freezing cement; to stay high is to risk the bitter frost of the refrigeration manifolds. 

Life here exists exclusively in the thermal seam. 

Behind the step-down transformer, in an eighteen-inch gap between its warm, vibrating sheet-metal housing and the bitumen-lagged brine pipe, the air is neither freezing nor burning. It is twenty degrees warmer than the rest of the hollow, sheltered from the pneumatic exhaust, and thick with the smell of hot mineral oil and wet wool. 

There, wedged into the upright crevice, something breathes.

The respiration is nearly impossible to distinguish from the mechanical cadence of the room. It does not pant; panting would lose moisture, and here moisture is an economy calculated in individual drops. Instead, there is a low, shallow expansion and collapse of tissue that aligns itself, almost by acoustic suction, with the sixty-cycle hum of the transformer coils. When the iron bell of the pressure regulator clinks on its lever, the breathing hitches, pauses for the duration of five valve-ticks, and then resumes its flat, heavy draw.

In the gloom beneath the terminal racks, where the green and red pilot lights cast their tiny, surgical glare, you can see where it rests its extremities. 

They are limbs, but limbs that have taken on the texture and palette of the floor framing. What look at first like two discarded lengths of dirty, hemp-braided hydraulic hose resting parallel on the steel plate are actually legs—folded tight, the hocks tucked under the belly, the skin gray and hairless at the joints where the hide has rubbed bald against raw iron. 

It is a dog, or it began as one. 

It is large, deep-chested, and long-muzzled—perhaps an English bull-terrier cross or a lurcher, one of the rough-coated curs kept by night-watchmen to clear rats from the timber-cribbing during the early excavation phases, before the four foundations met and the bulkheads were dogged shut. It must have crawled into an open conduit run or slipped through an unsealed drain sleeve during the final pours, seeking the only warm thing in a mile of freezing trench work: the exothermic heat of the setting Portland cement.

Now, it has been inside long enough for the site to have forgotten it, and long enough for its own body to have entered an altered, mineral existence.

Its coat has lost whatever color it once had. It is the uniform, chalky slate-gray of dry concrete, saturated with hydraulic grease that has worked into the fur along its shoulders and flanks until the hair lies in stiff, overlapping shingles, like the mill-scale on the I-beams. Where it has squeezed repeatedly between the flange joints to reach the leaking valve gland, its sides are marked with red oxide primer, rubbed deep into the skin like tribal paint. Its whiskers are broken short, gummed with white silicone sealant and powdered zinc.

Its eyes, when they open to the half-voltage amber light of the marine lamp, have no shine of moisture on them. They are small, sunken, and filmed with a dull, milky opacity from the constant, sulfurous off-gassing of the curing materials, but they follow the room with a terrifying, mechanical competence. 

It does not bark. It has not made a sound since the hatch above was bolted down. In an enclosed steel chamber eight paces across, a single bark would be an ear-splitting agony of reverberation against the drum-tight ductwork. It has learned that noise belongs only to the hardware.

Instead, it lives by the clockwork.

When the twenty-second interval nears its end, the creature shifts. It does not rise—there is no room to stand—but slides forward on its sternum, its claws making a faint, dry *tink-tink* against the mill-scale of the floor plate. It extends its long, narrow head between two bundles of lead-sheathed cable, precisely below the crossing of the cold manifold and the transformer casing.

It waits, its black, cracked nose lifted toward the sheet metal. 

Three drops fall. 

The creature does not wait for them to strike the duct with their sharp *ping*. It catches the first on the gray leather of its upper lip; the second on the rough flat of its tongue; the third it allows to strike its forehead, where the drop breaks and trickles down between its eyes, channeled by the wrinkles of its brow directly toward the corner of its mouth. 

Then it pulls its head back into the warmth.

To eat, it browses the edges like an herbivore. It has cleared the floor-angles of their white cushions of turnip-smelling fungus, rasping them away with its front teeth down to the bare hemp fibers of the expansion joint. In the crevice where the valve gland weeps, the pale, rubbery stalks never grow more than an inch tall before its lips find them in the dark, pinching them off cleanly at the base. The rubbery, sulfur-laden tissue provides a slow, foul nourishment, supplemented by the sizing in the pipe-lagging and the starch in the hemp rope that binds the cables. Its digestion has slowed to an absolute crawl; its belly is tucked up hard under its spine, a hollow cavern of ribs that shivers only when the pneumatic relay vents its dry *chuff*.

It has become symmetrical with the chamber. Its claws are worn square and flat from the tread-plates, resembling small iron wedges. Its breath smells of engine sump and damp earth. Even its temperature has struck a balance: its left flank, pressed against the transformer casing, is hot to the touch, baking in sixty-cycle vibration; its right flank, facing the room, is damp and cold as a turnip in a root cellar.

It lies there, chin resting on a cold galvanized flange, watching the tiny red pilot indicators with a slow, mechanical blinking. It does not dream of daylight, because daylight has no weight, no hum, and no water. It waits for the regulator counterweight to clink. It waits for the twenty seconds to pass. It is the only soft thing in four thousand tons of iron and stone, and every day it becomes a little less soft, hardening into the space that was never meant to hold it, until it is hard to tell whether the machine has trapped an animal, or the room has finally grown an organ that can feel the cold.
