To understand what lives here, one has to follow the fat.

A flame burning marrow-tallow in an air-pocket devoid of dry wood requires an immense and continuous yield of grease. It does not fall from the roof, and it does not grow from the stone. It comes from the drowned flume at the bottom, dragged up hand over hand against the draft.

The primary engine of flesh in the rift is an eel—though calling it an eel implies a suppleness it does not possess. It is a blind, blunt-headed rail of pure cartilage and gristle, thick as an oak bole and six to nine cubits long, that spends its life fighting the catastrophic current in the subterranean siphon below. It has no eyes, no scales, and no swim bladder; it exists solely as a dense muscle anchored by an enormous, disk-shaped sucker-mouth to the basalt lip of the drowned drop. It feeds on the biological drift of a world miles upstream: the carcasses of drowned deer, the rotting bark-hash of surface floods, and the endless, pale snowfall of dead insect larvae washed through the mountain’s karst plumbing.

Because these creatures live in water that never varies from thirty-four degrees, their livers are immense, pale lobes of liquid oil, and their marrow is not red sponge but a hard, yellow stearin that can be scooped with a spoon of shell.

The people catch them with bone hooks tied to three-ply river-gut, but they do not haul them straight up. The weight would snap the lines or pull a man from his saddle on the stone bridge. Instead, they drive wedges of soft slate into the beast’s gills from narrow corbels set inches above the froth, choking it with silt until it drowns in its own element. 

The people who do this are not many. Perhaps eighty souls inhabit the hive, though counting them is difficult; they spend long periods pressed so deeply into their niches that they look like the dry-stacked slate itself.

They are small-bodied, but extraordinarily heavy. Generations without salt-depleting sweat and a diet consisting entirely of cold liver, raw crab, and boiled mussel-silt have given them skeletons of abnormal thickness. Their ribs do not flare; they form a rigid, barrel-like cage that protects the lungs against the concussive thump of the hydraulic vibration. Their skin is not white—which would suggest a lack of pigment—but the dead, yellow-gray of lard, mottled across the shoulders and forearms with iron-rust stains from leaning against the wet manganese rock. 

They do not wear clothes, which would rot into slime within three days in this super-saturated air. Instead, they wear their tallow. Every waking cycle begins with an anointing: two handfuls of cold grease scraped from a hollow cobble and worked into the armpits, the groin, the scalp, and the soles. The grease is mixed with the fine black river-grit that settles everywhere. When dried by the draft, this forms a flexible, water-repellent rind that keeps the body’s core heat from being stolen by the freezing aerosol. A man standing under a weeping lintel sheds the moisture in round, clean beads that run off his ribs like rain off a grease-dipped roofing tile.

The hair of their heads is cropped close with flakes of sharp flint, not for beauty, but because long hair in the pocket is a death trap. It snags on corbels, collects water until it weighs pounds, and fosters a leathery, white mold that eats into the scalp. What hair they cut is saved, washed in lye made of mussel-lime, and twisted into the wicks that keep the darkness back three paces from their doors.

Their ears are strange. The canal is small, often completely sealed by a rounded, hard-tissue plug that grows inward from the tragus during childhood—an involuntary defense against an environment that would otherwise split the tympanic membrane within an hour of birth. They are deaf to the air. If you screamed into the face of one of their old women, she would see your throat tighten, but her eyes would not flicker. 

Instead, they hear with their teeth and the soles of their feet. 

The acoustic violence of the lower siphon is so immense that it turns the slate walls into a sounding board. A child sitting on a flagstone can tell, by the quality of the vibration in his sitz-bones, whether an eel has fouled a snare three hundred feet below, or if a wedge of calcite has sheared off in an uninhabited chamber across the gulf. When they converse, they do not sign—the tallow-lamps are too dim and oil is too dear to waste on watching fingers. They press. 

A mother speaks to her infant by laying her forehead flat against the child’s sternum and humming a low, chest-deep fundamental that bypasses the dead ears and enters directly through the ribs. Two hunters, meeting on a bridge eight inches wide over an abyss of thunder, will lean together until their jawbones touch, clicking their back molars in rapid, rhythmic patterns that resonate through the bone directly into the inner cochlea. It is an intimate, hard-edged language; they smell of stale river-fat and iron-dust, their teeth cold as pebbles against each other’s skin.

Beside them, the other life of the pocket lives in parallel indifference.

The broad-bodied amphibian that sits in the grout of the high empty cells is a *mud-dog*, though it lacks legs. It is an unsegmented, rubbery wedge of meat, three spans across, that does not leave its stone for decades. It has no teeth; its mouth is a slit like a knife-cut in tallow. It lives by capillary action and patience. The condensation that weeps down the slate walls carries with it microscopic films of chemotrophic bacteria and iron-eating diatoms; the amphibian tilts its head downward, and the slick fluid runs across the folds of its throat, where thousands of fine, mucous-covered cilia filter out the grit and organic slime before the water drips away from the tip of its snout. 

The people do not eat the mud-dogs. The flesh is dense with arsenic and iron-oxides that would turn a man’s bowels to water. Sometimes, during the coldest turns of the water-year, a child will wedge himself into an empty cell beside one of the creatures, resting his greased back against its broad flank. It is not warm, but it does not shiver; it holds the dead temperature of the mountain with a stubborn, rubbery inertia that keeps off the wind.

Lower down, where the spray is coarse enough to sting, are the *crawlers*. These are not only the blind crabs, but a species of giant stone-louse, broad as an open palm and as flat as a coin, that lives between the loose slates. They are the scavengers of the grit. When a man drops a bone, or when an old person dies and is placed according to custom into the lower sumps to clear the living-space, the lice find the meat within twenty pulses of the water. Their carapaces are brittle, made of calcified river-lime, and the people harvest them with baskets of twisted gut, crushing them into the gray paste used to mortar their hearth-niches.

Nothing here is young. Because there are no seasons—no spring thaw that does not sound identical to autumn flood, no sunlight to measure a day—the inhabitants have no words for *tomorrow* or *then*. There is only *the run* (the current beneath) and *the stay* (the rock they occupy). 

They sleep in bins hollowed out of the inner walls, six or eight heaped together like salt-pork in a barrel, to keep the blood moving. An outsider would find them indistinguishable from the dead: their respiration is so slow, so shallow, that a feather placed before their grease-smeared nostrils would hardly flutter in the heavy air. They do not dream of open sky, for they have no visual memory of distance; their minds operate in three-pace radiuses—the length of a tallow-halo—beyond which exists only the wet, singing stone.

When one of them becomes too stiff to span the footbridges, when the calcium that stiffens their mortar begins to stiffen their hip-joints and the bridge of their feet, they do not fall. They move inward, back into the dry recesses where the air is still and the calcite draperies grow from the roof like frozen lard. 

There, in the dead niches where the roar of the flume is reduced to a hollow, chest-shaking drone, they sit down on a bed of dry grit. They pull their knees up to their ribs, rest their chins on their shins to catch the last vibrations of the hive, and let the aerosol do its slower work. Within five years, the lime-water dripping from the ceiling has laid a skin over their shoulders. Within fifty, their greasy skin has become the nucleus of a flowstone pillar, smoothed and thickened by the mineral-run, until the face is merely a soft swelling on the corner of a doorframe, holding up the cell of someone whose tongue has never made a sound.
