To live here is to be an interchangeable part in an unyielding tolerance. 

Nothing in this sea has the soft, undulating frailty of open-ocean life; there are no trailing jellyfish bells, no frantic schools changing direction in silver flashes, no scavengers tearing at carrion. The coldness and the unshifting light do not permit frenzy, and the sheer hydraulic violence of the engines would pulverize anything that relied on muscle alone to hold its shape. The fauna are built like the fittings of a powerhouse—heavy, geometrically precise, and governed by the cycle of the stroke.

Clinging directly to the four-hundred-yard flanks of the engine-trunks are the *gasket-beetles*—though they are beetles only in the sense that they are segmented and armored. Their carapaces are flat, disc-shaped plates of dull, burnished horn, thick as bulkheads and impregnated with metallic silicates that give them the color and grease-sheen of turned phosphor-bronze. They have no eyes; eyes would be useless against the constant wash of resin-rich mucilage that coats the wood-iron bores. Instead, their edges are milled with micro-serrations that lock into the fluted grain of the heartwood. 

These creatures do not crawl so much as step in index-clicks. They move only during the split-second pause at the apex of the piston’s upward stroke, when the internal pressure briefly eases. In that half-second of dead-center stillness, tens of thousands of them shift forward an exact fraction of an inch with a dry, simultaneous *clack* that can be felt through the wood. They feed on the scorched, vulcanized film that forms where the sliding piston-cylinders meet their sleeves. Their mouthparts are hardened, self-sharpening chisel-blades that shear away the spent resin before it can foul the stroke. As they digest, they excrete a white, paraffin-dense paste that cures instantly in the cold brine, perfectly filling the microscopic scoring left behind on the sleeve walls. They are not parasites; they are living packing-rings, and without their steady grazing, the great cylinders would seize within a century.

Further down, where the water discharges from the venturis at speeds that would strip flesh from bone, exist the *spindle-blades*. 

They are twelve to fifteen feet long, perfectly straight, and shaped like double-edged bayonets carved from bottle-green bottle-glass. Their carapaces are smooth, glass-hard chitin that contains no calcium—calcium would dissolve or fracture under the shear stresses—relying instead on cross-linked lignin drawn directly from the sea-floor sap. They do not swim. Their tails terminate in a flared, cruciform wedge that slots into deep, naturally formed anchor-notches along the rim of the discharge tubes. 

Pinned there by the thousands, pointing directly into the roaring laminar jet, they remain entirely rigid. The force of the expelled water is so great that their fins cannot afford to flex; instead, their bodies act as static diversion vanes, straightening the turbulence of the jet into a frictionless, cohesive stream that shoots out into the cold basin for miles without dispersing. Their mouths, set in the exact center of their blade-noses, are fixed circular orifices rimmed with combs of fine, stiff reed-teeth. The high-pressure brine forces sap-lipids and suspended vegetable wax deep into their throats, where the nourishment is stripped out mechanically, purely by the inertia of the flow. They have no hearts; the immense hydrostatic pressure of the jet forces their internal fluids through a network of non-return valves, their circulation driven entirely by the engine they are anchored to. They live in a permanent, white-knuckle state of hydraulic paralysis, their life measured not in years, but in billions of liters channeled.

Between the towers, in the vast, architectural voids where the light falls through miles of silent lime-tinted sea, the scale shifts. Here, the current dies away, and the water is so still that suspended particles do not drift—they hang like motes of dust in an empty cathedral. 

Here hang the *manometers*. 

These are solitary, vertical organisms, seventy or eighty feet long, as slender as telephone poles. They are utterly translucent, ghostly cylinders of amber jelly held rigid by an internal, fluted spine of hollow wood-bone. They have neither fins nor limbs; they are living dipsticks. Inside their transparent outer jackets, two parallel chambers run their entire length, one filled with pale, buoyant hydrogen gathered from the bubble-lines rising off the copper catenaries, the other filled with heavy, dense brine. 

By using microscopic galvanic pores along their hulls to expand or compress these internal gases against the water’s pressure, a manometer holds itself at a mathematically fixed depth. They do not drift horizontally because they possess long, hair-thin tendrils of copper-rich fiber that hang down for hundreds of fathoms, trailing through the catenary fields like mooring lines, grounding them in the electric potential of the system. 

They appear entirely dead, suspended like plumb-lines in the aquamarine abyss, until one looks closely at their centers. There, a small, dark red chamber—an internal pendulum—beats in exact synchrony with the engine-trunks miles away. When the low-frequency stroke rolls through the water, the manometer’s pendulum swings, compressing a tiny cambial crystal that emits a slow, rhythmic flash of pale blue piezoelectric light. Miles apart, across the vast intervals, these solitary needles blink at one another in the vaulted clear: flash, pause, flash, pause—a slow, optical circuit measuring the stability of the basin's pressure across leagues of empty water.

Closer to the copper catenaries, where the current leaks into the sea in steady streams of hydrogen and oxygen beads, live the *shunts*. 

These are heavy, jointed organisms resembling massive calipers of dark, varnished wood, ten feet across. They straddle the suspended vines with grooved, insulated legs lined with dry, cork-like bark. They are blind, sluggish, and cold-blooded to the point of near-petrifaction. Their entire existence is defined by the flow of amperes through the vine beneath them. 

The shunts do not have digestive tracts; they have galvanic guts. Within their broad, flattened torsos lie plates of alternating zinc- and copper-rich vegetable tissues, separated by membranes soaked in acid sap. They draw natural current directly through their leg-clamps, storing it in biological capacitors that hum with a faint, dry buzz like high-voltage transformers. 

When the current along a catenary fluctuates—when a piston miles away misfires or a discharge valve sticks—the shunt senses the drop in potential. Instantly, involuntarily, its limbs contract, digging its mineral-tipped spurs deep into the vine's vascular cambium, discharging its stored charge back into the cable to smooth the wave. They are living surge protectors. When a catastrophic short occurs, a shunt will often fuse to the vine, its shell carbonizing into an inert, hard lump of anthracite that remains clamped in place for decades, overgrown by new fiber until it becomes just another knot in the wood-cable.

Even the surface of this world, that flat, plate-glass skin where the sea meets the dead, windless air, has its caretakers. 

Underneath the ceiling of the water, hanging upside-down by surface tension alone, drift the *skimmers*. They are paper-thin, hexagonal sheets of pale green chitin, six feet wide, completely flat. Their upper surface is dry, repelling the water with an oily, microscopic fuzz, while their submerged undersurface is a labyrinth of capillary channels. 

They glide across the underside of the surface like oil slicks, pushed along by the gentle radial currents that spread outward from the domed heads of the engine-pillars where the mist vents. They harvest the film of volatile terpenes and light organic spirits that boil out of the engines' exhaust plumes and float on the sea. The skimmers draw these chemicals through their margins, polymerizing them within their wafer-thin bodies into a clear, flexible glaze. 

When a skimmer dies, it does not sink. It simply dries from above, its upper face hardening into a pane of pure, transparent plant-glass that remains suspended on the meniscus, seamlessly joining with thousands of others to form a rigid, floating tessellation. In some areas, miles from the engine-heads, the sea is covered not by water open to the air, but by a continuous, crystal-clear ice-sheet of dead skimmers, through which the noon daylight passes without refraction, illuminating the deep machinery below like an engine beneath a glass museum case.

There are no bones here to rot, no blood to spill, no offal to drift. When an organism fails—when a blade-fish snaps its anchoring tail under a sudden pressure spike, or a manometer loses its buoyancy and begins the long, silent descent—it falls down through miles of clear, pale turquoise water toward the floor. 

It does not decay on the way down. The water is too cold, too pure, too thoroughly sterilized by electrolytic currents to allow bacterial putrefaction. It falls cleanly, turning slowly in the quiet like a dropped tool. 

Down on the floor, in the pressurized darkness beneath the engine-bases, the ancient, rubbery heartwood waits. The fallen creature lands without a sound on the glossy, vulcanized plain. Within hours, the petrified-green cambium detects the contact. Thick, glassy tendrils of cold resin slowly ooze from the floor, creeping over the carapace, filling every hinge, every groove, every valve-seat. Within days, the body is completely engulfed, drawn inward by the relentless, slow-motion suction of the floor-mass, dissolved into its constituent lignins and copper salts, and pumped back up through the roots of the engine-pillars to be cast again into the next piston-wall, the next valve-leaf, the next catenary vine.

Nothing is wasted, because nothing is separate. The life here does not live *in* the place; it is the lubricating fluid, the sacrificial zinc, the packing material, and the monitoring instrumentation of a world that was assembled once, set into motion, and found to be entirely sufficient unto itself.
