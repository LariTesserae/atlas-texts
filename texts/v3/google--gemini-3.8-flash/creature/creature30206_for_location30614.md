The people who inhabit the basin do not use the word *settlement*; in their dialect, which is clipped, low-pitched, and devoid of open vowels, the term they use translates roughly to *the drift* or *the foothold*.

They are broad-palmed, thick-torsoed folk whose bodies have adapted to an architecture that never stops sliding. Their balance is extraordinary, kept not in the inner ear—which is perpetually confused by the dull, sub-audible vibration of the bedrock—but in the tendons of their ankles and the thick, pad-like calluses that rim their heels. They walk with a rolling, slightly crouched gate, their toes curled inside flexible boots of oil-cured bladder-leather that grip slick basalt and greasy iron plate with equal tenacity. 

Because the daylight is so cold and unsoftened, their skin has taken on the color of wet river-slate or blanched turnip, heavily marbled around the knuckles and jawline with the faint purplish trace of burst capillaries—the permanent signature of the basin’s knife-edge chill. They do not wear wool, which soddens and rots too quickly in the cooling-tower spray; instead, they dress in layers of dense, felted moss cloth pressed with animal grease, topped with heavy aprons of split scavenger-hide that repel both the scalding trickles from steam joints and the pale, acidic sap that spurts from severed vines.

To live in the basin is to maintain a state of suspended domesticity. In the textile tenements, a family does not purchase an apartment; they occupy a *pocket*. Nothing is nailed to a wall, because walls are ephemeral. Furniture has no legs; chests, bedding, and larders are suspended by iron chains and turnbuckles from the primary trusses overhead. If the stone below shears upward during the night, the floorboards may shatter and tilt into a vertical ramp of splintered pine, but the hanging bunk remains level, swaying slightly in the draft.

When a hydraulic main punches through a parlor, the residents do not flee the district. They step aside for an hour, boiling bitter tea over the newly exposed steam flue, and then loop their cook-pots and drying lines around the new iron casing before its paint has even cooled. They have no concept of ancestral homes. Memory among them is tied to tools—a brass plumb-bob, an offset spanner, a bone-handled skinning cleaver—rather than places. A man might point to a three-story piston cycling rhythmically through a sunken dining room and remark, without rancor, that his sister was born where the counterweight now falls.

Their social organization is dictated entirely by the maintenance of the valves. There are no mayors or parish priests, only the *Watchers of the Lead*—men and women who carry long, polished iron rods tipped with boxwood. They place the wood between their back teeth and press the iron tip against the basalt, listening through their jawbones to the internal calving of the mantle and the pulse of the subterranean boilers. They can tell, three hours before an upthrust, precisely which row of tenements will be split, and they walk through the corridors with chalk, marking white diagonals across the doors of those who must unhook their bunks and move thirty paces to the west.

They are not alone in the wet, iron-ribbed twilight between the buildings. The ecology that has sprung up around their metabolism is neither wild nor domestic, but an opportunistic machinery of its own.

The silver-furred mammals—the ones that hunt the tallow-crickets—are called *laths*. They have skeletons of flexible cartilage rather than rigid bone; a full-grown lath can flatten its ribcage until it is no thicker than a man’s thumb, sliding effortlessly through the expansion seams between boiler plates. The human children catch them with hair-snare traps laid along the cold-water pipes, not for their lean, stringy meat, but for their fat. A handful of boiled lath-grease rubbed over the wrists and collar will keep the condensation-rot from eating into human skin for a week.

In the lower sumps, where the grease-traps overflow into vast, lukewarm sludge-canals, the heavy scavengers hold sway. The basin-dwellers call them *drudges*. They are massive, barrel-shaped beasts, blind or nearly so, with naked, wrinkled skin that looks like wet harness leather soaked in brine. They possess no true teeth, but flat, crushing plates of ridged horn that can pulverize waterlogged timber, clumps of fibrous moss, and the sodden rags discarded from the loom-halls. 

The drudges are so slow, and their hides so thick, that colonies of the wax-white succulents frequently take root in the folds of their shoulders, fed by the slurry that sloshes over their backs as they churn through the sumps. The people treat them with a strange, offhand reverence. They are the only things in the basin that never haste. A crew of pipe-fitters will work around a slumbering drudge for two days, using its warm, steaming flank as a windbreak while they bolt together a high-pressure manifold, occasionally feeding it handfuls of spent tea leaves or tallow-cake to keep it from shifting into their scaffolding.

Higher up, where the clear light hits the iron trusses and the cold air bites deepest, the horn-beaked swallows have formed a violent symbiosis with the copper-works. They do not build nests of mud; there is no mud to be had. Instead, they snip the tough, hemp-like lichens with their serrated beaks and combine them with the sticky, non-drying grease that weeps from the pneumatic bearings, shaping rock-hard cups directly against the rivet-lines. They are fiercely territorial, diving at the heads of the maintenance crews with dry, clattering snaps of their beaks. Their dung, rich in copper salts and sulfur, runs down the corrugated zinc roofs in bright, turquoise streaks, etching the metal into intricate, frosted patterns before the rains wash it into the moss-beds below.

Even death here is metabolized without delay. In a land without soil, burial is an engineering problem. The dead are not laid to rest; they are *cleared*. When a worker is crushed by a shifting bedplate or taken by the lung-chill, their body is stripped of boots and metal, wrapped tightly in three layers of felt-moss, and lowered into the aeration troughs beneath the main cooling towers. 

There, in the torrential, mineral-heavy spray and the furious, enzyme-rich froth of the algae beds, the flesh disappears in a matter of weeks, leaving only clean, white calcium that is ground down to powder to dress the drive belts and keep the leather from slipping on the flywheels. It is not considered an indignity. To a people whose sitting rooms are routinely bisected by iron piles, the idea of demanding six feet of unyielding ground for eternity seems not only arrogant, but geometrically absurd.

At mid-afternoon, when the hard daylight reaches its narrow peak, the basin enters its briefest, tensest lull. The cold does not relent, but the condensation clears for an hour, the fine drift of droplets blown away by a sharp, clean draft from the open mantle-cracks to the south. 

During this hour, the people emerge onto the narrow catwalks strung between the foundries. They do not speak much—their voices are thin, raspy whispers, worn raw by the dampness—but they stand along the railings in their dark, moss-felt coats, their pale faces turned toward the sun like the colorless succulents that cling to the boilers. They watch the shadows of the gantries stretch out across the newly solidified glass of the basin floor, waiting, with an engineer’s cold patience, for the next seam to split.
