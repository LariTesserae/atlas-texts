At first, it seems that to ask what lives here is to misunderstand the room. Living things have bellies; they carry water in wet envelopes; they swell and contract. In this place, an open eye would turn to a salt crust before the lid could sweep it, and an exposed lung would freeze dry like beef left in a winter gale. 

Yet there are eyes here, though they do not sweep.

They are set in long, wafer-thin slats that cling to the leeward sides of the ceramic fins—creatures so compressed by the geometry of the space that they possess neither breadth nor belly. They have the profile of bone splinters or carpenter’s shims, four inches long, half an inch broad, but scarcely thicker than a playing card. Their backs are composed of overlapping shingles of clear, horny plate, like the dried gill-covers of cod, through which one can see the faint, yellow tracks of their viscera, entirely desiccated, operating without blood.

They do not crawl across the surfaces; they are pinched between them. They live by friction. Their sides are fringed with rows of microscopic hooked cilia, turned backward like the barbs of an ear of wheat, which lock directly into the stamped, repetitive indentations of the unglazed earthenware. 

To look directly at one is almost impossible, not only because of the flat, blinding white glare that eliminates all contour, but because their shells have been scoured by the airborne chalk dust until they have taken on the dull, matte texture of the slate behind them. Only their organs of sight betray them: along each lateral margin runs a string of twelve or fourteen hemispherical lenses, not of soft gelatin, but of clear crystalline calcite. These do not see shapes. They drink the roaring, focused noon. In this vault, where the light arrives cold and unshifting down the five shafts, the creatures use the radiance as an engine: the light heats their interior shells by a fraction of a degree against the well-cold slate, creating a tiny, internal thermosiphon of dry dust-salts that keeps their nerve-cords alive. 

They are deaf, or rather, they are entirely made of hearing. 

Because the chord of the room never shifts its pitch, their long carapaces have grown to match the wavelength of its fundamental harmonics. The creature is an acoustic reed. When the sky above drives the flutes into their heaviest fury, the plates of its body hum in sympathy, fluttering against the ceramic fins at thousands of cycles a second. This is how they move. They do not walk; they *slip*. By altering the tension of their skin-plates against the terracotta, they uncouple themselves from the standing wave, and the vast acoustic pressure of the room shoots them an inch upward or an inch down, along the groove of a fin, until they wedge themselves once more into a node of stillness.

They feed upon the only thing the sky sends down: the flour-fine mineral flour worn from the inside of the chalk shafts by the passage of the air, and the microscopic flakes shed by the brittle lichens. They do not have mouths that chew. Their fore-ends are split into paired scrapers of hard, translucent amber—stiff spatulas that rasp continuously against the ceramic fins, driven not by muscle, but by the sympathetic vibration of the fins themselves. The stone sings, and the creature’s jaws chatter against it like telegraph keys, reducing the lichen-scales and chalk dust to an impalpable powder that sifts directly into their hollow thoracic trunks.

They have no sexes, or none that require contact in the roaring glare. At certain heights of the noon, when the light down the five shafts is so upright that it strikes the limestone dice of the floor and turns the bottom inches of the air into an incandescent fog, the creatures cast off their outer shingles. 

These sheds do not fall; the wind is too dense in the baffled slots. They stand wedged between the slates, dry and transparent as mica, until the scouring chalk turns them milky. Inside these hollow sheds, sheltered from the shearing draft, tiny, dry seeds of their kind—nodules of concentrated wax and horn—mature over years, utterly motionless, until they too are broad enough to span the slot from fin to fin. Once their margins touch both sides of the terracotta, the room takes them: the vibration begins to hum through their new shells, their crystalline eyes align with the noon shafts, and they begin to rasp.

There are hundreds of them—perhaps thousands—in the deep, inaccessible interstices of the baffles. One could look into the vault for an hour through an upper slit and see only an engine of silent, sunlit slate and reed, thinking the space empty of everything but stone and fury. 

Yet if the wind were ever to fail—if a winter should come so absolute that the air ceased to drop down the chalk pipes, or if some blockage choked the flutes above and allowed the deafening chord to fall silent—the entire room would drop its life at once. Without the standing wave to pin them, the shims would lose their grip; they would rattle down the ceramic slots like falling pennies, piling by the hundreds in the cracks between the floor dice, leaving the vault to become, for the first time, truly dead.
