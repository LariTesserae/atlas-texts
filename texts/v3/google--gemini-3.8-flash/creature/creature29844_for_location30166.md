The creatures that cling to the iron are only the visible margin of an ecology that behaves more like a circulatory system than an animal kingdom. 

Those dog-sized plates—the pale arthropods—are called *gliders* only by the virtue of how little they resist the current when they fall. Their underbellies are not soft; they are lined with thick, fibrous pads like compressed horsehair, stained the color of curdled milk by the biofilm they crop. They have no eyes, nor do they possess the nervous agitation common to insects. Their internal pressure matches the hydraulic dead-weight of the flues below, which is why a drop of forty feet onto wet basalt does not crack them; they land with the heavy, dead thud of a side of salt pork, right themselves with the slow levering of three-jointed legs, and crawl directly toward the nearest sluice.

They do not drown in the boiling gutters. The gutters are where they mate. 

Down in the subterranean runnels, where the water is dark and runs under pressure at temperatures that would slough human flesh from the bone, these creatures gather in dense, clinking rafts. The boiling foam carries them miles into the deep galleries beneath the avenues. There, in the black heat where the steam pipes cross, they shed their outer plates, leaving behind translucent, brittle husks that wedge into the grates and slowly dissolve into a gray paste that feeds the slime. The newly molted are blind, soft, and worm-like, pink as scalded fingers, drifting in the subterranean rapids until they find a vertical flue with enough draft to draw them upward. They crawl back into the twilight along the inside of weeping drainage conduits, hardening as the mineral spray coats them, emerging months later onto the iron columns to begin the slow scraping of the sulfur-mats once more.

***

Higher up, where the cathedral-sized transformer banks meet the lower lip of the steam ceiling, the life changes character. It becomes thin, dry, and starved of moisture.

In the dead angles beneath the gantries, where the copper blooms like turquoise mold, there are things that cannot touch water at all. If a drop of the hot, greasy condensate falls upon them, it burns through their tissue like vitriol. They resemble mantises pressed out of dried parchment, no thicker than a slate shingle, with limbs that terminate in sharp, needle-like hooks of semi-metallic horn. They move with an arid, ticking cadence, stepping exclusively upon the brittle crusts of sulfate. 

These things do not breathe the sodden wash of the streets. Their bodies are closed vessels that harvest moisture chemically from the mineral blooms they chew, grinding the dry copper salts with mandibles that sound like pestles working in stone mortars. They have long, thread-like antennae—four or five times the length of their bodies—that they cast out into the humid air currents beyond the overhang. They are fishing for the grease that rides the mist. The vapor carries an aerosol of fats, dead bacteria from the flues, and microscopic flakes of shed carapace; the dry things draw their antennae back across their mouthparts hour after hour, licking them clean with a dry, rasping efficiency, like old paper rubbing against old paper.

***

Between the floor and the iron rafters, drifting through the eight-track expanses of open avenues, are the *veils*.

They are difficult to distinguish from the curtains of sulfurous vapor until they pass before one of the soot-crusted pilot flames. They are colonial organisms—sheets of translucent, rubbery tissue, five or six meters across, suspended by internal bladders of heated sulfur gas that they generate through fermentation in their upper margins. They do not fly so much as they displace; they are buoyancy-neutral in the dense, hot air of the shelf, rising when an avenue vents its hourly thermal wash, and sinking slowly toward the wet basalt as the air cools toward iron-gray stillness.

As they drift, their lower skirts drag along the wet stones like wet lace. These skirts are fringed with millions of microscopic, sticky filaments that sweep the runoff. They drink the mineral grit—the tin, the salt, the copper wash—incorporating the heavy elements into their tissue until their trailing edges become stiff, metallic, and leaden. When a veil becomes too heavy with metal, it cannot ride the thermal pulse. It settles over a basalt block, draping it like an oily tarpaulin.

For days it will lie there, slowly digested from below by the yellow biofilm that recognizes it as meat, while the sheets of boiling runoff slide over its back. By the end of a week, nothing remains of it but a silvery, rectangular stain on the dark rock, perfectly square, like the shadow of an absent crate, which the running water will take fifty years to wash away.

***

And then there are the walkers. 

They are rare, but they are not hidden. They move along the center line of the vast avenues, where the basalt is worn deepest by the centuries of flow. 

It is impossible to tell if they are what remains of the builders, or if they are merely an animal that wandered up from the lowlands before the plateau was fully sealed and spent ten thousand generations becoming like the iron. They stand roughly three meters high, though their posture is so hunched and folded that their true height is hard to measure. They walk on four limbs, but their front appendages are longer than their hind ones, terminating in broad, calloused pads that look like blocks of vulcanized rubber—pads that have grown thick enough to stroll through two inches of boiling water without the heat penetrating to the bone.

They have no faces in the primate sense. Where features should be, there is only a smooth, sloping hood of leathery hide, black and wet as an aubergine, cross-hatched with deep, diagonal folds that act as drainage channels. The boiling rain strikes their heads and sheets off down their flanks in organized gutters, directed away from the single, low-set breathing slit beneath their breastbone. This slit opens only once every two or three minutes, taking in the hot, acidic air with a sound like a wet bellows, then clamping shut behind a thick, fibrous filter that catches the mineral grit before it can ruin the wet lungs within.

They are solitary. Two will pass each other on an avenue eighty paces wide and make no sign of acknowledgment. They do not fight; there is nothing here worth disputing, since the food is an inexhaustible paste of sulfur and the warmth is everywhere. 

Their sole occupation appears to be tactile. They move from pylon to pylon, bastion to bastion, with an unhurried, rhythmic persistence. When a walker reaches an iron column, it does not graze. It rises up on its shorter hind legs, leaning its broad, damp chest against the metal, and presses its fore-pads into the rivet seams.

It stays there for hours, perfectly motionless, while the water braids down its spine. 

It is listening. It feels the subsonic vibration of the deep pumps that run beneath the mountains; it reads the groaning expansion of the girders as the thermal surge builds; it tracks the chime of the cisterns through the soles of its feet. If it finds a drainage flue choked with the shed carapaces of the arthropods, it will work at the blockage with its heavy, spade-like thumb until the obstruction gives way with a wet sucking cough, and the hot water roars through once more. 

It does not do this out of duty. It does not seem to know that the city is a machine, or that the machine has a purpose. It clears the flues the way an ox rubs against a gatepost or a beaver straightens a branch—because the flow is the only law the plateau has ever had, and to stand in the way of the water is to feel an itch in the bone that cannot be scratched until the channel is clear and the stone begins to hum again.

When the hourly surge finally arrives, the walkers do not seek shelter. As the flagstones begin to tremble and the rivets pop like pistol shots through the murk, they simply turn their backs to the prevailing draft, lower their heads until their hoods touch the basalt, and let the scalding foam break against their knuckles. They wait, blind and heavy as the transformer cathedrals themselves, while the steam climbs toward the sky that has never seen them, and the water does what it has always done.
