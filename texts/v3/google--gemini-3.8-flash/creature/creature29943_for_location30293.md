The creatures that hold to the Scarp do not have names that travel far, because the people who gave them names rarely come down, and those who live below have no use for animals that cannot be eaten without boiling them in vinegar to draw the antimony out of their fat.

At the base of everything are the slate-backs, which the few pipe-fitters who remain call simply *the tiles*. 

They are not true reptiles, though they have the dry, tallowy smell of an unwashed tortoise. A full-grown tile is three feet across and scarcely four inches deep from spine to breastplate, hexagonal in its primary scutes, so that when five or six of them settle into a hollow, their margins jam together like dry-laid flagstones. Underneath, where the flesh meets the basalt, they have no scales at all; the belly is a single, broad pad of naked, gray-veined gristle that functions as an acoustic horn and an anchor. It sweats a clear, tallow-like grease that prevents the boiling mineral water from sloughing their hide away. 

To look closely at a tile’s face is to look at an unthreaded nut: they have no eyes to speak of—merely two horn-lidded pits sensitive to the long-wave red of the fissures—and their mouths are circular, transverse files of black, self-sharpening magnetite. They do not graze so much as they mill. When thousands of them are working a runnel at dawn, the sound is like ten thousand dull chisels being dragged through wet sand, though twenty paces off, the thin air eats the noise entirely. Their blood is thick, dark as elderberry syrup, and laden with copper compounds that turn green when a shear-vulture’s claw punctures the flank. They can remain motionless for sixteen days while an iron-intake valve cycles overhead, their flat skulls wedged into the angle between the steel plate and the rock, waiting for the rust-bloom that forms wherever the wet sulfur air strikes bare metal.

Between the tiles, threading the gaps where the scutes don't quite marry, live the ribbon-dogs. 

They are long-bodied, sinuous things—the "river otters" a man might imagine if he saw them only from an aerial skip through a heat-shimmer. In truth, they are degenerate monitor lizards whose ribs have multiplied until they can compress their torsos to the thickness of an axe-handle. Their hide has the dry, granular texture of wet sandpaper, colored the gray-pink of an open gum. They do not drink; they do not possess bladders. Instead, they press their long throats against the condensation runs, drawing moisture through porous neck-gills that filter out the arsenic and leave white, calcified rings around their throats like bead collars. 

The ribbon-dogs are the true currency of the Scarp. They eat the tick-wasps that tumble from the birds above; they eat the soft, tallowy margins of the tiles’ bellies while the tiles sleep; they worm their way into the unlagged elbows of the six-inch steam pipes to lay clutches of leather-skinned eggs in the insulation wool. When an egg hatches, the young do not emerge with claws; they have sharp, prehensile chin-hooks with which they drag themselves along the overhead cables, dangling like dirty rags above the drop, snapping at the sulfur-flies that dance in the hot column of the exhaust vents.

Then there are the shear-vultures, though they have almost forgotten the use of a leg. 

Their tarsi are short, fused knobs of bone, capped with wide, flat pads that have grown into the stone through decades of calcified droppings. A shear-vulture does not perch; it settles like a blister on the granite cornices and stays there until the wind shifts. Their plumage is not soft. It is soaked in the oily, hydrocarbon steam that drifts off the deep boreholes, then baked by the high sun into a hard, bituminous lacquer. If you strike an old vulture’s wing with a spanner, it rings like a curved stove-tile. 

They are deaf. The industrial vents, pulsing for three generations at ninety decibels of wet pressure, have blown their tympani inward, leaving only gristle-plugged holes behind their pale, lidless eyes. They hunt by pressure alone. When the noon migration begins and the rock-faces slide down into the shadows, a vulture does not drop from the cornice; it merely unhooks its wing-hinges—which lock with a dry, wooden snap—and allows the rising draft of the caldera to lift it four thousand feet in six seconds without a single stroke of its black, rectangular wings. They do not carry carrion away; they cannot grip it. They drop like dropped anvils onto the caracal-lizards that linger too long in the sun, driving their heavy, adze-shaped bills through the lizard’s shoulder-hump to break the nervous knot, then sit upon the carcass on the ledge until the sun and the sulfur have stewed the meat into a gray pulp they can scoop out with their lower bills.

The tick-wasps that cover them are not larger than a grain of cracked wheat, but they are so dense upon the birds' flanks that a roosting vulture appears to have a second, trembling skin. They have lost their wings; they have no stings. They possess only three pairs of pale, curved needles for legs and a throat that secretes a weak sulfuric acid. They do not bite the bird; they dissolve the salt that crusts the bird’s quill-sockets, drinking the brine and the feather-wax, and in return they scour the vulture’s skin so clean that no fungus can take hold in the wet heat. When a vulture dies of dry-rot or age, the tick-wasps do not leave the body. They burrow into the marrow of the hollow wing-bones, sealing the pores with their own dried cast-skins, and wait there in the dark for five, ten, fifty years, until another bird nests upon the same spot and its body-heat wakes the clutch.

Yet the Scarp is not entirely wild; it is *inhabited*, though the inhabitants have taken on the flat, gray economy of the stone.

In the shelter of the No. 4 Compressor Shed—where the corrugated roof has collapsed under thirty seasons of salt-drifts—live the Gasket-Crawl. 

They were men once, or their grandfathers were, brought up on the heavy haulage cables from the valley during the consolidation of the salt-conduits. There are perhaps forty of them along the thirty-mile lip. They do not stand upright; to stand at eleven thousand feet on the Scarp is to offer your chest to a dry, ninety-mile gale that will blow the salt out of your spit and tip you into the boiling sink of the inner caldera. They move on four points, their knees and palms protected by pads cut from discarded vulcanized conveyor belts, lashed to their limbs with copper winding-wire.

Their skin is the color of an old inner-tube, cured by the sulfur fumes until it does not bleed when scratched by basalt, but merely weeps a thin, yellowish lymph that smells of burnt horn. They have lost the bridge of the nose; the thin air and the acid steam rot the cartilage by the twentieth year, leaving their faces flat, two-slitted, and broad-mouthed, like the tiles they live amongst. 

The Gasket-Crawl do not maintain the machines—the machines were built by an empire that used ten-inch cast steel and bronze sleeves that do not care about human time—but they *attend* them. 

They know that when the valve on Borehole 9 begins to "chitter"—a rapid, dry clicking like a fingernail against a tin cup—the sub-crustal pressure is rising and will blow the seals within an hour. They do not run. They crawl into the low drainage conduits beneath the pump-housings, dragging three or four tiles in front of them to block the portal. They wedge their heads between their knees, stick their fingers into their ears, and wait while the steam screams through the cracks with a violence that shakes their teeth loose in the bone.

After the purge, when the mist clears and the runnels are running five inches deep with scalding, gray broth, they come out with long iron hooks made from old cable-strands. They hook the dead things that were caught in the vent-blast: the caracal-lizards that were cooked where they clung; the shear-vultures whose wings were stripped of their black lacquer in an instant; the thousands of river-dogs that have floated up from the deep cracks, bloated and pink like parboiled sausages.

They do not speak. They cannot. Between the thinness of the air, which offers no purchase for a vocal cord, and the perpetual, mountain-shaking hum of the deep turbines, their tongues have grown thick and dry in their mouths, like salt-fish. When a Crawl wishes to signal to another across the width of a fluted column, he takes a flat piece of basalt and strikes the six-inch brine pipe twice: *tink-tink*. 

The iron carries the blow through five miles of rock, through the grease-jackets and the packings, through the nesting-places of the tick-wasps and the anchor-pins of the skips. Half an hour later, three miles away, another figure—scarcely distinguishable from a resting tile save for the grease-rag wrapped about his forehead—will lay his naked chest against the pipe, feel the double twitch in the steel, and spit a yellow drop into the runnel to show he has heard.
