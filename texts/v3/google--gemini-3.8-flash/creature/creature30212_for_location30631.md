To live here is to live inside a grid that has been digested. Because there is no loose soil except what has rotted in the gutters, every creature’s feet, pads, and bellies are shaped by flat planes, ninety-degree corners, and the gritty, lime-slick tooth of fired clay. 

The drainage channels—the miles of square-cut brick flumes that thread through the terrace flanks—are the true thoroughfares. They are never dry. In them run the civets: long, low-slung beasts with pelts the gray of wet mortar, their spines unusually supple from generations spent negotiating ninety-degree turns in four-inch channels. They have lost nearly all their pigment; their tails are short, blunt rudders, and their paws are broad, rubbery, and bare-skinned, resembling the feet of marsh-otters. They do not hunt by sight—their eyes are milky beads, milky-blue and clouded by corneal cataracts common to animals that spend their lives in perpetual mineral wash—but by the vibration of the brick. When a gecko drops three levels above, a civet two hundred yards away in the dark gut of a retaining wall halts, pressed flat to the bottom of its sluice, feeling the impact ring through the masonry like a hammer against an anvil.

Below them, down where the water drops through the scuppers into the sunken crypts, the cisterns have silted up with a thousand years of black nightshade mulch. In this warm, oxygen-starved sludge lives the blind softshell turtle. Some have reached the diameter of millstones. Their carapaces are not round, but have flattened into oblongs that let them wedge into the drainage intake arches. They feed on whatever falls down the chutes—dead flying foxes, sodden nests, the occasional bloated civet—lying in the tepid slurry with only the fleshy tubes of their snouts breaking the surface. Their skin is white, wrinkling into thick, gelatinous folds around their necks, smelling intensely of swamp gas and old lime. The frogs do not avoid them; they climb over their backs in thousands, using the turtles’ ridged carapaces as islands in the oily backwaters.

Higher up, where the terraces step back and the fig canopies lace across the artificial ravines, the middle world belongs to the serows. They are not like the mountain goats of open cliffs; these are thick-necked, black-maned animals with short, heavily muscled forelegs. Their hooves are unique: the outer horn is soft and rubbery, wearing away constantly against the slate flags, while the inner frog is deeply concave, creating an absolute vacuum against wet, vertical brick. They do not bound; they walk with a low, slinking, deliberate gait, their bellies almost dragging on the balustrades. They move along the narrow tops of three-story retaining walls with the heavy confidence of spiders, their broad snouts rasping away the liverwort and the saltpetre crusts that bloom out of the lime-mortar seams. The salt is their craving. Where an ancient join has wept a white tongue of lime down a vertical buttress, three or four serows will stand stacked on one another’s shoulders against the brick, licking the mineral fur until their tongues bleed black against the red clay.

Nothing here leaps far. The gloom does not allow for flight that depends on landing targets more than twelve feet away. Even the macaques—stout, short-tailed, with dense, greasy coats that shed the lukewarm drift—rarely jump. They have abandoned the trees for the ashlar towers. They move with three points of contact always anchored in the recessed mortar beds, their fingers long and spatulate, the nails worn down to square studs from digging out grub-galleries in the compost-packed joints. They are quiet animals; the jungle-roar of the insects and geckos drowns any small vocalization, so they communicate through masonry-knocking. A dominant male does not scream; he finds a loose capstone that rocks on its bedding-bed and drops his weight against it rhythmically: *clack-tonk, clack-tonk*. The sound travels three terraces down through the solid core of the wall, and across the artificial gorge another male answers on a hollow lintel.

And then there are the keepers of the high lofts.

They are men, or what has followed men here, though they would not recognize themselves in the bones of the builders. They live entirely above the vegetation line, in the dry crawlspaces between the upper vault ceilings and the external slate roofing of the viaducts—places where the bat guano has accumulated into dry, warm beds eight feet deep. 

They are small-bodied, rarely exceeding five feet, with rib cages that are extraordinarily deep and barrel-shaped to draw what little vigor can be had from the stagnant, low-pressure air. Their skin is yellow-gray, dusted perpetually with the fine, powdery efflorescence of dried bat droppings and lime, so that when they sit still against a brick pier, they disappear completely into the architecture. Their feet are broad and prehensile-toed; they can hang by the heels from the decorative corbels to harvest the clusters of sleeping bats that coat the vault ribs like clusters of greasy dates.

They have no metallurgy. They have forgotten how to make fire, or perhaps they let it go because nothing here ever dries enough to kindle without choking a vault in thick, unbreathable smudge. They eat cold: persimmons split by hand and fermented in slate troughs until they turn to wine-sour mash; the fatty, sweet breasts of flying foxes torn raw from the skin; and the large, translucent grubs that bore through the fig-roots. 

Their clothes are not woven on looms, but beaten. They take the fibrous inner bark of the wild figs that strangle the towers, soak it in the calcium-saturated water of the cisterns until the pulp rots away, and beat the remaining white cords against the flagstones with brickbats until it felts into a thick, pale fabric that is stiff as pasteboard when dry, but limp and cold as tripe when wet. They smear this cloth with the black grease from the glands behind the bats’ ears to keep out the lukewarm drift.

They speak, but their language has been crushed by the acoustic pressure of the place. It has no vowels to speak of—vowels blur and boom in the vaulted brick arches until they are unintelligible. Their speech is a sequence of sharp, palatal clicks, dental chirps, and tongue-slaps that mimic the geckos with uncanny perfection. A family group can sit along a six-inch cornice sixty feet above an abyss, invisible in the brown gloom, and trade precise information about which fig branches are bearing three terraces down by clicking through their teeth, the sounds cutting cleanly through the roaring baseline of crickets and the drone of the frogs.

They do not look at the sky. They do not know it exists. If you spoke to them of a blue vault or a ball of fire that circles the earth, they would understand it only as a myth about an impossible lantern in a ceiling that has no scuppers. For them, the universe is vertical and composed entirely of two materials: that which is struck from clay and laid straight, and that which rots and climbs back over it.

Their dead are not buried; there is nowhere to dig. They are taken down to the silt-traps, to the long, narrow channels where the water from the upper terraces slows before it falls into the cisterns. The family lays the body flat on the stone floor of the sluice, wraps it tightly in fig-felt weighted down with halves of broken brick, and leaves it. Within three days, the blind crabs—pale, flat things with legs as long as reed-stems—have found the seams in the felt. Within a month, the fine black leaf-drift has silted over the bricks, and the ferns, finding the calcium of the bones beneath, shoot up their fiddleheads through the joints: broad, blackish-green fronds, thicker than anywhere else on the terrace, uncurling in the dark against the eternal, sweating wall.
