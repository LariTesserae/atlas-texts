To understand what lives on the plateau, one must understand that flesh here is not distinct from the chemistry of the shelves. It is merely another state of salt, temporarily mobilized.

In the shallows, the platter-flat things—sometimes called *pelts* by those above—are not born so much as they crystallize out of the thicker brine pockets. They have no organs for seeing or hearing; their entire dorsal surface is a single, continuous sheet of keratinous mica that mirrors the sun. Underneath, their bodies are composed of thousands of minute, pale hair-combs that work through the pink sulfate sludge, separating heavy lithium salts from the softer hydrates. They are entirely cold to the touch, their fluids matching the tepid, bubbling forty-degree wash they slide through. They do not die in a way that involves decay. When their internal filters foul with excess silica, their joints simply seize. The calcium in the water settles over their rims within an afternoon, cementing them to the travertine bed. Half the steps of the grand terraces are paved with the backs of their ancestors, mortared into the geological strata like pale, hexagonal tiles.

Above them, where the iron struts plunge into the scald, the ecology becomes vertical. 

The salt-wasps do not build nests of paper or mud; they have no saliva for it. Instead, they cluster in their millions along the shady underbellies of the bronze siphon tubes, their barbed silica legs hooked into the lime-scurf. They feed on nothing that moves. Their abdomens are translucent chambers filled with a photosensitive brine that ferments in the UV-rich glare of the thin mountain air. They are essentially flying batteries, spending hours motionless, drinking the light, until their abdomens turn an incandescent, bruised violet. Then they crawl down into the dark, oil-choked vents of the processing tenements, crowding around the massive brass turbine shafts to discharge their heat into the cold copper heatsinks. It is a symbiotic loop centuries old: the wasps shed their thermal charge where the machines run coolest, and in return, the condensed tallow fumes inside the turbine halls coat their brittle joints, keeping them supple enough to fly.

And then there are the walkers of the catwalks.

They are few—perhaps only three or four hundred scattered across an industrial grid that could house millions. They are the descendants of the original shift-engineers, though they have long ceased to think in terms of shifts, wages, or departures. 

They are impossibly tall, their frames elongated by generations in low atmospheric pressure and a world where one never crouches or burrows. Their limbs are like the dry tamarisk poles that grow nowhere near here: long, double-jointed at the ankles, and wrapped tightly from thigh to heel in thick, vulcanized hemp soaked in mutton tallow to shield them from the boiling splashes. They never set foot on the stone flats. To touch the travertine with bare flesh is to be eaten alive by alkaline caustic within an hour; they live entirely on the galvanized gratings, five meters above the scald.

Their skin is not pale, but a dull, cured-leather gray, darkened by centuries of the mountain sun reflecting off calcium mirrors. Because the air is too thin to carry human speech more than a few paces before the vapor swallows it, their vocal cords have withered into dry, whistling reeds. They make no vocal sound beyond a sharp, wet *clack* of the tongue used to signal proximity in the steam. 

Instead, they are creatures of vibration. 

Every walker carries a sounding-pole of solid, turned phosphor-bronze, tipped with an iron shoe. They move with a strange, high-stepping gait, planting the pole ahead of them on the rails. By leaning their foreheads against the smooth bronze knob of the staff, they can hear through their skull the entire state of the works: the rhythmic gulping of a siphon two miles south; the grinding friction of a turbine bearing in need of grease down in the submerged vaults; the sharp, pistol-shot crack of a travertine shelf failing under the weight of its own salts far out on the eastern rim.

Their eyes are concealed behind heavy, circular spectacles of smoked mountain-mica, wired shut with lead thread so that only a horizontal slit remains. Behind the mica, their pupils have widened into horizontal bands, identical to those of goats, allowing them to scan the endless glare of the horizon without burning their retinas away. When they enter the deep shade of the zinc tenements, they do not take the spectacles off; they have learned to navigate by the heat radiating from the iron walls and the persistent, low-frequency hum that rises through the floorboards.

Inside the tenements, their domestic life is sparse and frictionless. 

There are no beds, for there are no blankets or straw; they sleep suspended in wide hammocks woven from copper wire, which neither rot in the humidity nor harbor the sulfur-wasps. They eat almost nothing that resembles food from the lower world. From the undersides of the zinc roofs, they scrape a thick, rubbery black fungus that grows on the metal wherever lead-solder and grease meet the warm steam. It tastes of tin and battery acid, but it is rich in hydrocarbons. They supplement this with the distilled water that drips like rain from the eaves of the passive condensors, water so pure it tastes flat and dead, like boiled glass.

They have no names, only locations. A walker is the *Northern Siphon Tender*, or *She of the Fifth Weir*, or *He Who Cleans the Gland-Boxes*. They do not seem to reproduce often; a child is born perhaps once every three seasons, an event marked by no ceremony. The infant is rubbed from head to foot with heavy petroleum jelly to protect its delicate skin from the chemical air, then placed in a wicker cradle hung directly beneath a fresh-water exhaust, where the steam is sweet and free of sulfate dust.

Their work is not frantic. The machines were built so grandly, and with such vast, hydrodynamic tolerances, that they do not break; they only foul. A walker’s day consists of walking a half-league of iron grating, finding a spot where the lime has crested high enough to choke a drainage gate, and leaning over the rail with a heavy chisel on a pole to strike the mineral crust away. The travertine falls into the shallow scald with a heavy, wet *thunk*. The walker watches it sink, watches the pink water sheet over the newly cleared lip, and then continues.

Sometimes, at noon, when the sun is at its most piercing and the purple sky turns nearly black toward the zenith, one of them will stop on a high viaduct. 

They will lean their staff against their jawbone, close their mica-shielded eyes, and listen. They are not waiting for an order, nor are they waiting for an end. They are simply feeling the pressure differential—the huge, slow lung of the plateau drawing the boiling brine from the bowels of the peak, cooling it against the void of the upper air, and letting it fall, crystal by crystal, until the works and the mountain are completely, silently the same thing.
