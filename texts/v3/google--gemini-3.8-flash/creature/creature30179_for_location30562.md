At the smallest register, life here does not announce itself with movement; it announces itself as a consistency of surface. 

The biofilm is not an inert layer, but a carpet of slow, colonial hunger. Under the thumb, it has the slippery, yielding tension of raw liver. In the shallowest recesses where the lime-water spreads out flat across horizontal flags, it thickens into scalloped mats—mottled like curdled cream, tinted with faint, sulfurous yellows and the pale gray of river mud. It smells faintly of cellar turnip and iron shavings. It drinks the calcium directly from the water, knitting its microscopic chains into the travertine until the stone and the slime are the same substance at different stages of hardening.

Feeding on this are things that seem to have forgotten how to have an inside and an outside. In the runnels where the water is no deeper than an eyelid, small flatworms cling like soaked flakes of parchment. They have no color at all—they are not white so much as absent—and if the water ceases to slide over them for an hour, they disintegrate back into the grease of the rock without leaving a husk. 

Further down, in the basins where the water gathers its dead weight, the pale amphipods drift. They are articulated like fleas, but drawn out, their segments loose, their antennae as long as their bodies. They do not swim toward anything. They live by the logic of the drip: when a heavy drop strikes the water above them, the shockwave tilts their carapaces three degrees, their long, hair-thin legs flex against the vibration, and they glide into the dead pocket beneath the stone scalloping, where they hang face-down. They do not flee a finger. If touched, they simply adhere to the skin, mistaken in their blindness, treating a knuckle as another pillar of wet marl to be gleaned for scum.

Between the pillars, where the gaps are no wider than a man’s calf, live the spiders. They are not the web-spinners of the upper daylight; they have abandoned silk because the mist coats it and drags it down under its own weight. Instead, they are crab-like, broad-chested and flattened from belly to back, their legs splayed out in the horizontal plane so they can insert themselves into the hairline bedding-planes of the limestone. Their hides are buff, the precise color of the marl above, dusted with a dry-looking patina of dried lime-flour that flakes when they move. They move perhaps once in four days, and then only the width of a coin, shifting an eighth of an inch to clear their respiratory slits from the creeping mineral film that threatens to seal them to the stone. When they die, the water does not carry them off; it washes through them, fills their hollowed shells with calcite crystals, and turns them into tiny, eight-legged stones that remain glued to the rock face forever.

And then there is the body on the platform.

It is easy to look directly at it through the vertical ribs of the flowstone and see only another bulbous drapery of calc-tufa, another smooth shoulder of stone rounded by water-sheeting.

It sits sideways, because the gap does not permit sitting square. Its legs are drawn high, the knees pressed flat against the chest, heels hooked over the edge of the river-slabs where the handholds were pecked. It has been there long enough that the gray dusk of the fissure has settled into its pores like lead dust.

It is human, or the narrow, water-starved remainder of what was once human. Its skin is not hairless, but the hair is fine, bleached to the color of wet straw, and plastered down by the mineral broth of the air until it resembles the slick biofilm itself. The back is wide and ridged like the pillars it leans against—scoliotic, warped to fit the precise hollow between two tufa teeth where the draft from the upper sump does not reach. One shoulder is permanently raised, tucking the chin into the clavicle to keep the nose clear of the continuous drip that falls from a shelf an inch above its forehead.

The creature’s eyes are open. They have the dull, bluish cast of skimmed milk, the pupils shrunk down to pin-heads that do not dilate even when the leaden dusk above thickens toward night. It does not see the room; it feels the weight of the room through the heels and the palms. Its palms are flat against the rock, permanently spread, the calluses on the heels of the hands so thick, pale, and water-logged that they look like the white flesh of a turnip. 

Its breath is so slow that the water sheeting down the stone beside its ribs does not waver. When it draws the air in, there is a tiny, wet clicking in the sinus—a sound that belongs entirely to the dripping clockwork of the basins. It exhales not through the mouth, which is shut and sealed with a pale, crusty scum of saliva and lime, but through one nostril, an imperceptible warm sigh that leaves no vapor, because the room is already as warm and wet as the lung that produced it.

It does not hunt. The effort of chasing would break the balance that keeps the skin from tearing on the tufa teeth. 

Every few hours, or perhaps every few days—time here does not break into parts—its right hand moves. The motion is not sudden; it has the thick, dragging quality of wood swelling in a bucket. The fingers, long and jointed like ash twigs, slide down into the nearest travertine pot. The fingers do not grasp; they comb. They dredge the bottom silt, where the dead amphipods and the drowned beetles that have fallen through the upper chimney accumulate into a fine, gray tallow. 

The hand comes back up, glistening with gray mud and wet stone-meal. It brings the fingers to the lips, smears the grease against the lower teeth, and the jaw works—three dry, grinding bites that sound like two pebbles being rubbed together in an oily rag. Then the mouth closes again. A drop falls from the ceiling, strikes the back of the hand, washes a thin streak of pink skin through the gray silt, and the surplus water trickles back down the wrist to return to the basin.

The creature has forgotten the name of the cup wedged into the tufa beside its elbow. It does not touch the bone pins. They are too hard; they do not yield fat; they are simply ribs that belong to the rock now. Once, perhaps a generation ago, or three, it had language enough to dread the cold; now it knows only that the stone beneath its rump is warm, that the water tastes of rust and old chalk, and that if it remains very still, the lime in the water will eventually seal the small crack in its left ankle where the bone was chipped against the sump ledge.

Already, the calcite is at work on it. Along the outer edge of its right thigh, where the water from the upper drapery has splashed the same four inches of flesh for forty years, the skin is not soft. It is crusted with a delicate, yellowed lace of mineral deposit—crisp as dried salt, brittle as an eggshell. It does not hurt. It feels like a dry, heavy sleeve, holding the leg fast against the stone, sparing the muscle the work of holding itself upright. 

In another century, if the ceiling holds, the water will have finished the bridge between the hip and the pillar. The breathing will have stopped, or simply shrunk down into the microscopic pores of the stone itself, but the shape will remain: a smooth, buff-colored swelling in the wall, narrow enough that whoever next turns sideways to squeeze through the knit teeth of the west will brush their shoulder against it, thinking it only a rib of lime, greasy with water and older than the hills.
