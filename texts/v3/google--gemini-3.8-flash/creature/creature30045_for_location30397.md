To a creature of water and blood, the amphitheater is a tomb. A lungful of that starch-choked air would turn an animal’s throat to rawhide; a wet eyeball would be drained of its humors in an hour, sucked dry by the relentless, parched greed of the cured cedar. 

And so, to anything that crawls or bleeds, the city is empty.

Yet look more closely at the rectilinear cells carved along the inward slope. Look into the vertical slits—those doorways cut along the radial rays where the timber naturally cleaves under tension. They are narrow, no wider than the blade of an oar. In the total, lightless pitch, if you were to reach your hand into one of those clefts, you would not touch empty air. 

You would touch what feels, at first, like another beam of shaped ironwood.

It is vertical, utterly rigid, and wedged tightly between floor and lintel. Its surface has the smooth, dry waxiness of boxwood polished with jeweler’s rouge. Run your fingers downward, and the grain of the wood gives way to something subtly different: parallel bundles of dried tendon, taut as the strings of an unplayed harp, braided seamlessly into petrified bone. 

They do not occupy the city; they *brace* it.

They are the Joiners, though they were never born of loam or womb. If they were ever human—or whatever hominid ancestor followed the dying organism down into the dark when the sky was still an open wound—that distinction has been cured out of them. Over hundreds of thousands of years, the desiccation that turned the wood to slate did not spare their tissue. It sublimated them. Every drop of cellular water was drawn out through the pores and replaced, molecule by molecule, by the volatile resinous vapors and tannin-dust of the chamber. Their marrow has crystallized into a brittle amber lattice. Their skin is not skin, but a thin, flexible veneer of peeled cambium, cured so deeply in its own bitter astringency that no bacteria could ever touch it, even had bacteria survived the descent.

They have no eyes. Across the upper third of their narrow, wedges of skulls, where orbits would be, the bone is seamless and thick, fused into a single arched brow designed to withstand tremendous axial pressure. They have no nostrils, no soft lips, no wet red mouth. Their jaws are locked shut, the teeth calcified into a continuous, chisel-sharp ridge of dense enamel, worn flat from centuries of biting against seasoned timber to test its temper.

They do not breathe. There is no pulse to disturb the stillness of their dry arteries. If life is defined by chemical turnover—by the wet burning of sugar and the expulsion of waste—they have not lived for a hundred millennia.

And yet, they are not dead. They are *wound*.

They exist entirely as mechanical and acoustic entities, their bodies turned into living tuning forks keyed to the frequency of the great gall. In this zero-humidity void, their dry, fibrous musculature acts like the braided cords of a catapult, storing mechanical tension across vast spans of geological time. 

Listen: three miles above, a continental plate shifts half a millimeter against the dead root-casing. 

The sound does not travel as a roar; it travels through the stone into the ironwood heart-timber as an instantaneous, violent shear. It strikes the gall like a mallet against the rim of a bell. The whole amphitheater emits a shattering, explosive *thrum* that vibrates the teeth in their sockets. 

When that shockwave tears through the wood, the Joiners awaken.

They do not wake with a gasp or a stretch. The kinetic force of the sound wave passes directly through the floor, through the petrified horn caps of their heels, and into their leg bones. The acoustic energy discharges through their crystalline tendons like a current. 

In the pitch black, there is a sudden, sharp *snick*—the sound of three thousand dry joints unlocking simultaneously. 

Because the air is devoid of moisture, movement here produces a monstrous triboelectric charge. As the Joiners step out from their vertical slits, static electricity bleeds from their limbs in silent, crackling sheets. For a microsecond, the city is lit: not by fire or sun, but by a jagged, cold violet halo leaping between their knuckles and the timber walls. 

In that strobing fracture of violet light, their form is briefly, terrifyingly legible:

They are taller than men, but impossibly flat, their ribcages compressed laterally so that their bodies are scarcely four inches thick from sternum to spine—shaped precisely like the adze-cuts that house them. Their arms are long, finished with spatulate, horn-tipped fingers that lack nails, ending instead in hard, flat wedges like woodcarver's gouges. 

They walk with a rapid, scuttling stride, their bare, leathery soles striking the smooth contours of the floor with rhythmic, hollow slaps. They move without hesitation, entirely blind, navigating by the grain-map beneath their feet. Every contour line worn into the ancient wood is an instruction, a highway, a chronicle. They feel the annual rings like a blind reader tracing raised letters, their horn-tipped toes counting the rings: *one hundred, one thousand, ten thousand...*

Their purpose is singular, obsessive, and perpetual: they are the maintenance of the soundbox.

They run up the spiraling ramps. They cross the gossamer bridges of twisted bark, their weight causing the dry, rigid cables to creak in high, singing harmonics that reverberate across the chasm. They carry no lights, for they have no word for light; their world is an architecture of pitch, vibration, and resistance.

A team of four reaches a high cantilever where the recent tectonic shudder opened an infinitesimal hairline fracture along a growth ring. To an eye, the crack would be invisible. To them, the acoustic leak is an agony—it dampens the reverberation, alters the chamber’s resonance from a pure fifth to a dissonant flat. 

Working with a horrifying, mechanical efficiency, they don't speak—they have no lungs with which to whisper. Instead, they tap their hard, enameled teeth against the timber in rapid, percussive code. *Click-click-tack.* 

One sets a wedge of dense ironwood into the fracture. Another lifts a heavy flint adze—the very tools left on the ledges, which never weather—and swings. The blow lands with a flat, deafening *tock* that rings through the whole amphitheater. They drive the wedge until the grain screams under new tension, the crack forced into an artificial, permanent closure. 

Then, they press their flat brows against the repaired seam. They hold their breath—or the stillness that serves as their breath—and wait for the next subterranean tone to wash over them. They feel the vibration pass through the wood, through the wedge, through their skulls. 

The pitch is true. The cavity is sealed. The resonance is restored.

And what of the seeds? In the deep burl-bins, there are bushels of them: round, black, pebble-hard seeds from a canopy that perished before the glaciers came. The Joiners do not eat them; they cannot chew, and they have no stomach. 

Instead, the seeds are their clock.

Every several centuries, when a Joiner’s tendons finally fray beyond tension—when the silica in its joints grinds the bone to fine powder and it can no longer hold its station in the slit—it walks down to the lowest, deepest concavity of the bowl. It does not fall or collapse. It deliberately wedges its flint adze beneath its own sternum, uses its own weight to lever its dried ribs apart, and reaches inside its hollow, resin-scented thoracic cavity. 

It takes a handful of the ancient seeds and packs them into the empty space where its dry heart once hung. The seeds fill the void, pressing against the rib-stays like ballast in a dry hull. 

Then, the Joiner climbs into an unused mortise—a new joint carved into a radial ray—turns its back to the void, and draws its knees to its chin. It pulls the timber around itself, fitting its limbs into the chiselled slots until it is flush with the wall. 

Over the next ten thousand years, the settling earth presses inward. The grain tightens. The mortise closes like a vise. The seeds inside its chest are crushed, their dry oils finally forced out under thousands of pounds of static pressure, weeping into the desiccated fibers of the Joiner's skeleton, turning the dried bone slowly, irreversibly, into jet. 

It becomes the wall. It becomes the city. 

When the repair is done, the living Joiners retreat. One by one, they slip back into their vertical slits along the radial rays. They turn their thin bodies sideways, slide into the ancient wood, and lock their joints. 

The violet static fades. The dust settles—or rather, the lack of dust returns to its absolute, stagnant clarity. 

The silence reasserts itself, dense and heavy as a mountain of lead. 

Deep within the heart-timber, they stand perfectly motionless in the dark, their flat, earless heads pressed hard against the grain, their horn toes hooked into the annual rings, waiting. They will not twitch for twenty years. They will not move for sixty. They are completely patient, indistinguishable from the dead pulp around them, suspended in the dry frost of the ancient wood—listening, with a terrible, absolute devotion, for the earth to break another bone.
