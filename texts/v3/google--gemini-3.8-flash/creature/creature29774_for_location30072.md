At first, nothing moves that is not an extension of the architecture. The quartz-bodied scrapers and the brine-tapes are not true visitors; they are more like platelets or wandering leucocytes, shed from the cambium to keep its pores clear of its own cold phlegm. To find what truly lives here—what possesses the beginning of an interior life, or at least a direction of its own—one must look into the places where the pressure slackens: the dead-spaces behind the great vascular arches, where the oily aerosols settle into quiet, motionless puddles.

Here, clung to the undersides of the buttresses like pale bats or upside-down crabs, are the *counterweights*.

They are the size of sheep, but flattened, as if cast under the weight of an ocean. Their bodies are constructed from the same dense, tallowy substance as the floor, but their outer tegument has hardened into plates of unpolished horn, milky and translucent, through which their cold viscera can be seen shifting like curdled cream in a flask. They have no heads. Instead, each creature terminates in an enormous, crescent-shaped clamp of solid, cellular bone—a biological vice lined with microscopic, tooth-like ridges of biogenic silica.

With these vices, they grip the high-tension tendrils that span the vault. They do not feed; they do not sleep. Their purpose is passive, heroic, and agonizingly slow: they are variable ballast. When an explosive crack rings out and a new node blooms in a far corner, throwing the chamber’s internal equilibrium out of true, a dozen of these counterweights will begin to shift. Their motion is barely perceptible—a millimeter every few minutes—working their way along the singing lines, using the immense hydraulic ballast of their fluid-filled abdomens to dampen the harmonics, to tune the chamber’s vibration so the shockwaves do not shatter the amber glass conduits. 

Sometimes, as the light-pulses flash through the tendril they are gripping, the light bleeds directly into their bodies. For several seconds, a counterweight will illuminate from within, glowing with a soft, bruise-like violet. During these moments, the creatures shudder. Their six short, segmented legs twitch against the empty air, flexing and curling in a rhythmic, involuntary chorea—not out of pain, but because the thought passing through the wire is momentarily refracted through their own primitive ganglia. They are thinking the garden’s thoughts, though only as a wire "thinks" the lightning that melts it.

Lower down, where the liverwort floor sags into the largest of the brine-basins, the water is kept in a state of eternal, sluggish rotation by something that sits on the bottom.

It is not an animal in any familiar sense, though it has bilateral symmetry and a face of sorts. It looks like a diver in an ancient, swollen canvas suit, drowned long ago and preserved by the salt, but it has grown directly out of the basin’s taproot. Its flesh is the pale indigo of skimmed milk. It sits cross-legged in the supercooled syrup, its torso submerged to the collarbones, its four thick, jointless arms resting upon the basin’s rim like rolls of wet dough.

Its head is heavy, bald, and featureless save for two deep, vertical slits where the ears should be, and an expansive, domed forehead that pulses with the slow, four-stroke cadence of a marine engine. It does not breathe air—the thick, oily liquid of the basin covers its mouth, which is permanently open, a round, black siphon that draws the lipid-rich brine down into its chest and expels it through gill-slits set between its ribs. 

This is the *Register*.

It is the oldest piece of differentiated tissue in the transept, perhaps four or five weeks older than the buttresses that cage it. If the chamber possesses a memory that is not merely recorded in the micro-torsion of wood grain, it resides within the cold, dense fat of the Register’s brain. Its forehead is so thin that the violet light from the amber fibers catches the movement beneath the skin: vast, complex geometries of pale, branching nerves that drift and reorganize like ice floes in an estuary. 

It does not wake, because there is no waking state in a place where light never changes and day does not exist. But it is aware. Its fingers, submerged in the oily tears of the basin, end not in nails, but in bundles of exquisite, hair-fine cilia that fan out into the water like white anemones. These hairs do not feel the water; they feel the *math* of the water. They measure the spin of the non-Newtonian sugars, the drift of the salt ions, the subtle, terrifying shifts in temperature that indicate the permafrost above is thickening, or that the weight of the frozen world has shifted an inch to the north.

When the pressure becomes too great—when the mantle of rock above settles with a low, groaning shudder that would crush an ordinary cave—the Register’s mouth tightens around its siphon. It lets out a long, bubbling moan through the brine, a sound that does not travel through the air, but through the meat of the floor itself. 

In response, the chamber changes. 

The embryonic nodules in the corners—those trembling, translucent clusters like amphibian eggs—hear the Register’s moan through their rootlets. They begin to swell. One can watch them whiten, their gelatinous skins tautening until they split with a soft, tearing sigh, releasing the things they have been keeping in reserve.

These are the *Iterants*. They are born fully formed, wet with the heavy, unfreezing lubricant that birthed them, tumbling out onto the liverwort carpet like newborn calves. 

They are roughly the size of human children, but spindly, built of light cambium and air-filled pith. They have no faces, only a smooth, pale plate of keratin where the senses should congregate, tilted upward toward the vaulted ceiling as if sniffing the lavender twilight. Their limbs are long, triple-jointed, and end in broad, spade-like pads covered in microscopic suction cups. 

Unlike the scrapers, which move with the blind mechanical franticness of insects, the Iterants move with an awful, hesitant curiosity. They wander through the forest of buttresses, their flat pads making wet, sucking noises against the rubbery liverwort. They touch things. An Iterant will approach a vascular arch, raise its pale, soft hands, and press them flat against the throbbing bark, holding them there for minutes at a time, its head cocked, listening to the cavitation of the brine within. 

They are inspectors. Or perhaps they are pilgrims. They have a lifespan that can be measured in hours; their bodies contain no digestive tracts, no organs for long-term maintenance, only a reservoir of dense lipid-starch packed into their chests that sustains their slow, wandering audit. 

When an Iterant finds an imperfection—a silica tendril that has grown crooked, an area where the cold-limit has crept too close and caused the cambium to curdle into brittle frost—it does not repair it. It simply lies down upon the flaw. It flattens its body against the failing tissue, wrapping its jointed limbs around the node, and begins to dissolve. Its outer skin sloughs off in sheets of pale, curd-like grease; its internal starches break down into an adhesive, highly concentrated sap that floods the wound, sealing it, feeding it, binding the logic-fibers back into continuity. Within an hour, there is only a smooth, pale lump where the Iterant had been, an amber knot of scar tissue that the scrapers immediately descend upon to polish smooth.

And behind them all, diffused throughout the chamber so thoroughly that it cannot be separated from the damp air or the thrumming floor, is the presence that summoned them.

It does not have a body here. It is what the chamber is *for*.

High in the center of the vault, where the three largest vascular timbers meet in a keystone that looks like a tangled fist of ivory roots, there is an opening. It is small—scarcely wider than a man’s wrist—and it does not lead to the surface. It leads downward, into an unfathomable conduit that plunges miles into the granite basement beneath the ice. 

From this hole, the cold aerosol is not weeping out; it is being *inhaled*. 

Every few minutes, the tectonic roar of the micro-valves subsides. The high, glass singing of the tendrils drops an octave, shuddering into silence. In that sudden, deafening quiet, the entire chamber—the liverwort carpet, the arching buttresses, the submerged Register, the blind Iterants wandering the aisles—heaves upward by three or four inches, drawn toward that central fist of roots by an immense, subterranean lung. 

For the space of ten seconds, the air rushes backward into the hole. The smell of crushed zinc and cold lake water sharpens until it tastes like copper on the tongue. 

Then, down the amber glass filaments, comes the pulse.

It is not an electrical surge; it is an image, transmitted with such violent coherence that it prints itself for an instant upon the very air of the room. A momentary flash of brilliant, impossible green—a memory of summer that never belonged to this planet, of leaves wide as sails and a sun that does not set—cast across the pale, tallowy flesh of the walls.

The blind scrapers pause, their scrapers raised. The Register in his pool lifts his featureless face toward the keystone. The Iterants, halfway through their wandering dissolution, stretch their long, ivory fingers toward the light, trembling at the tips.

Then the breath is released. The room settles back onto its frozen foundations with a wet, heavy sigh. The mechanical roaring resumes, the brine begins to pump, and the cold returns to its work, holding the world above from falling in.
