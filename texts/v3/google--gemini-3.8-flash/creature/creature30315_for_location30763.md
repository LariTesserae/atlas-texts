Under the three millimeters of water, pressed so flat against the terracotta that they appear to be flaws in the firing, are the ribbon-leeches. 

They are not parasites; there is nothing here thin-skinned enough to yield blood. They are the width of a finger and the thickness of wet vellum, translucent and pale as lard, sliding over the square tiles on rows of microscopic ventral cilia. They spend days navigating a single pan, their margins fluttering with a faint, undulating rhythm that never breaks the surface film above them. To cross a mortar ridge, they must wait until their bodies are engorged with the lime-milk that leaches continuously from the seams. When full, they stiffen into curved splints, levering their snouts over the thumb-groove, then spill their bulk into the adjacent pan with a dry, rubbery slide. 

They feed on the paste. Where the white lime smells of river mud, they settle in rows, their mouth-parts—soft, toothless funnels like the ends of cut reeds—pressed into the joint. They draw out the grain of the paste, filter the crushed shell from it, and excrete a clear, heavy mucous that dries, even underwater, into a glaze harder than flint. It is their slow, mindless circuit across the millions of squares that keeps the joints from dissolving into the water. If one dies, it does not rot; its water simply leaves it, passing into the pan without a ripple, until only a wafer of salt-colored scale remains, bonded to the brick forever like a dropped flake of mica.

The low-slung grazers do not notice them, though their wooden pads come down upon them twenty times an hour. When a hoof strikes a leech, the creature simply takes the weight like wet dough, squashed momentarily into the adze-scallops of the timber or the coarse grit of the brick, before its tallow-colored margins ease outward again, intact and indifferent.

These grazers have no sex, or rather, they have only the one sex that needs no counterpart. Within the deep, unribbed barrels of their torsos, there are no guts as beasts elsewhere possess them. Open one—though nothing here has claws to do it, and their hide resists any casual fracture—and you would find neither bile nor blood, but an orderly mass of tightly rolled fern-fronds, preserved in clear water like herbs in an oil jar. 

Their skeletons are not lime-bone. Their ribs are curved hoops of the same pale heartwood that floors the world, pegged into a spine of dense, porous grit that resembles unglazed earthenware. They do not sleep, because the light never shifts enough to suggest an end to walking, but occasionally one will pause where the joinery is particularly tight. It will lower its long, jawless head until its snout touches the wood, and from its nostrils it will discharge a slow, lukewarm stream of water that smells faintly of boiled turnip. This is its only exchange with the air. It takes in water through its pads by capillary draw; it returns it through its face when the timber beneath it is parched. They are not tenants of the floor; they are its ballast, keeping the great, unanchored timbers weighted down against whatever vast buoyancy presses against them from below.

Between their legs, where the air is stagnant even for this place, hang the small ones. 

They do not give birth. Instead, from the soft rind of their groins, where the pebbled hide gathers into thick, grease-heavy folds, bud smaller forms—nodules of hairless leather no larger than plums. These nodules grow over centuries, slowly taking on the square-nosed silhouette of their parent, until their wooden hooves begin to click faintly against the adult’s inner flank. When their pads are hard enough to bear the wet mud, they drop. They do not stumble. Their flat, yellow eyes are already open, already dry, already adjusted to the white glare that casts no shadows beneath their bellies. They take three steps away, find a seam where the lime is wet, and begin to strip the bark from the nearest fern with broad, horn-plated lips that sound like leather shears clicking together in an empty room.

Higher up, where the saucer-sized beetles hover in the heat, the air belongs to things that have forgotten the purpose of motion.

The beetles are hollow. If one were plucked from the air—and it would offer no struggle, drifting into an outstretched palm with the dry balance of an autumn leaf—its underside would reveal neither legs nor abdomen, only an inverted basin of dark, burnt-umber glaze. Inside this dome, a solitary, violet muscle suspended from three white tendons twitches with the regularity of an unwinding clock. It is this pulse, beating once every twelve seconds, that keeps the creature buoyant. It displaces not weight, but the floor’s intention for it. Each pulse makes a minute, sucking sound—*chut*—like a boot lifting out of thick silt, but so small, so drowned by the density of the air, that one must press an ear against the lacquer shell to hear it.

They gather in loose geometric alignments above the roofless brick chambers. If there are four walls of raw-pork brick, there will be four beetles hanging precisely sixteen inches above the four corners, their polished backs reflecting four identical squares of blank, blinding sky. 

Inside these chambers, where the finger-marks in the gray mortar are still wet enough to leave a grease on the palm, live the smooth-shouldered things.

They are rarely seen in their entirety, for they do not leave the low brick walls. They have the color and consistency of lard that has been beaten with river sand—heavy, gray-white, and cool to the touch despite the fever of the air. They have no faces, only a broad, blunt swelling between their shoulders that turns toward any movement with the slow, hydraulic swing of a sundial. They sit in the shallow water of the enclosures with their legs folded beneath them, their gray flesh bulging over the low parapets like dough rising over the lip of a trough.

Their hands—if the broad, spatulate lobes at the ends of their thick arms can be called hands—have no joints. They are five-lobed pads of firm, gristly muscle that work continuously against the floor. They do not build; the walls are finished. But they press. 

With a rhythmic, heavy stroke that never quickens, a smooth-shouldered one will reach out, find a place where the gray mortar has begun to skin over, and draw its broad thumb along the line. It leaves a clean, deep furrow, identical to the ones beside it, and wipes the displaced gray paste onto its own thigh, where it is absorbed into the gray skin like tallow into leather. They are the ones who keep the impressions fresh. If they were to stop, the mortar might finally set; the world might cool by half a degree; the moisture hanging on the vertical face of the pork-colored brick might finally gather its weight and drop. 

So they do not stop. They lean their weight forward—a slow, dull thrust of sixty stone of tallow-colored brawn—and draw their thumb along the seam. 

The sound it makes is a wet, low *schlock*, repeated across miles of open brickwork at intervals so wide they sound like their own echoes. 

And below them, in the seams between the bottomless layers of tile, down where the light cannot reach but somehow still exists—a faint, amber phosphorescence that smells of river lime and shell-grit—something else moves. It has no shape at all, or the shape of a wedge driven between two oak planks. It is long, as long as a canal, and it moves without haste, sliding between the third and fourth tiers of tile with a dry, grinding friction that sounds, from above, like the distant settling of a stone wharf into mud. It has no eyes, for there is nothing to look at but the tight, oiled joints of the mason-work, and it has no mouth, for the paste itself feeds it by osmosis through its skin. 

It is merely an elbow of the world, turning over in its sleep. When it shifts, a thousand pans of water shiver—not into ripples, for the surface cannot break, but into a sudden, brief tension that makes the meniscus bulge upward like a blister of green glass.

Then the tension passes. The meniscus snaps back into flatness. A tapir-thing lifts its wooden pad, brings it down upon a seam of pale heartwood with that heavy, hollow *clop*, and tears another strip of rind from a plant that has never dropped a leaf since the adze first smoothed the sill beneath it.
