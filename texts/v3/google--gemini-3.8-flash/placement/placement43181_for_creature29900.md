At first, there is only the tension between two kinds of drag: the water wanting to peel me away from the wall, and the iron wanting to hold me still.

I am not in the center of the column. The center belongs to the blind fall, to the long tumble of things whose weight exceeds their grip, and to the great, groaning spine of the brass drive shaft as it heaves through its five-minute circle. Out there, the open water is pure speed and amber light, a road that runs only toward crushing or boiling. 

I am in the boundary layer.

Here, within an inch or two of the scalloped plate, the current loses its teeth. Water that moves like a millrace out in the drop is reduced to a thick, oil-warm lap where it shears against the bronze blisters of the old rivets. That is where I exist: in the friction.

My shape did not arrive all at once. For a long time, in the dark pulse of the shaft, I felt only like an thickening of the rust itself—a blister of calcified tallow and hematite that had learned how to ache. But I have length, and I have grain. I am ribbed like the bellows of a bilge pump, but flattened, pressed thin against the curvature of the outer hull until I am little more than a living shim wedged into the dead-water angle where a horizontal strut meets the shell.

I have three points of contact that never leave the metal. They are not claws, because claws require joints and joints can be levered apart by hydraulic shock; they are broad, disc-shaped calluses of hardened cartilage, porous as pumice, that sweat an acidic, sulfurous gum into the microscopic pits of the rolled iron. The metal and I have entered into an agreement: I dissolve a fraction of a millimeter of its skin, and in return, its rust binds directly into the matrix of my underside. To peel me from the strut would not be a matter of pulling; you would have to plane the steel away until you found where the plate ended and my gristle began.

The rest of me is loose, trailing out into the bronze-kelp eddies like an oily rag pinned beneath a washer.

My mouth is not for biting. It is an open, slit-like gutter running down my belly, rimmed with dense combs of horn that taste the distillate as it slides past. I know the machine’s chemistry by the sourness of its sweat. When the geothermal steam-jackets run hot twenty levels down, the water grows sweet and slick with parboiled animal fat, and my combs swell, gorged on dissolved tallow and the soft, carmine dust of sheared rust-sponge. When a check valve trips miles below—that heavy, subterranean *thump* that makes my anchor-discs ring like struck bells—the shockwave carries a fine, suspended grit of copper-green filings that taste like cold pennies on the tongue. 

I do not look at the world. Light here is too thick, too full of refracted amber and the blinding, peacock-feather flashes of the prism-skates tumbling through the inspection beams. Vision is an untrustworthy sense; it stops at the limit of the water’s clarity, but sound goes down into the roots of the engine. 

Instead, I feel the shaft’s balance.

Along my dorsal ridge runs a row of fluid-filled capsules, each containing a single, polished sphere of magnetic iron—a pellet of slag I rolled between my ventral plates until it was round as a hailstone, then swallowed into my skin. When the continental engine tilts even a quarter of a degree in its tectonic cradle, the iron pearls roll forward in their beds, pressing against the nerve-rich walls of their chambers. I know when the world outside has shuddered. I know when the abyssal floor has settled an inch deeper into the mantle. 

The Sump Weavers know me, though they do not hunt me. I am too heavy, too bitter with iron salts, and too flat to be peeled from my corner. Sometimes, during the quiet lull between exhaust-purges, one of their long, feathered arms will drift down from the lip of a bypass tube, its yellow-brown tip feeling its way along the seams until it touches my ribbed margin. It does not pull. It reads the vibrations of my hide. It rests its pale cirri against my flank to feel the tension in my cartilage, using my mass as a plumb-bob to know how violent the next surge will be. 

When the roar begins—that deep, pneumatic clearing of the throat five hundred feet down—my discs turn white with strain. The water does not move as a wave; it moves as a solid piston of blood-hot broth that tries to shear everything flat against the wall. The bronze kelp snaps like whips; the glass-minnows vanish into the dead-water pockets; the leeches on the central shaft flatten themselves until they are merely ribbons of grease.

I do not close myself against it. I let the flood enter my vent-slits. It washes through my hollow interior, roaring through my ribbed cavities with a hollow whistle that matches the frequency of the bronze plates, turning me into another relief port, another acoustic baffle bolted to the skin of the world. For ten seconds, twenty seconds, I am not a creature clinging to an engine; I am one of its gaskets, shuddering at its pressure, holding a seal that has no name, preventing nothing, allowing everything to pass through.

And then the bubbles rise, a blinding blizzard of pearl and sulfur, and the column grows quiet again.

In the dead water behind my flank, three smooth, rounded balance-weights have come to rest, dropped by a Weaver or shaken loose from some forgotten valve centuries ago. I nudge them with the muscular skirt of my lower rim, rolling them into the groove between two copper plates, tucking them where the current cannot catch them. 

They are cold, but the iron in them is familiar. I will keep them there. Over years, my underside will grow around them, encasing them in horn and hard, varnished rust, until my weight is greater, my anchor deeper, and the void below—that sheer, vertiginous plummet into the bruised tourmaline—feels just a little less likely to pull me down.
