I would not be above the cloud-deck, where the nickel sun remains an abstraction, nor down in the deep mercury-veined grease of the Slump, where there is only the massive, unthinking tension of holding the floor together. 

I would be caught in the middle-work, at the shelf-line: where the slate hummocks dip their blunt whale-flanks into the reverse-running channels. 

At first, there is only the weight. Not the burden of gravity—which here has a loose, frayed hem, catching and hitching like wet wool on brambles—but the weight of *presence*. To exist here without a furnace in the chest means the boundary between myself and the basin is not a wall, but an osmotic gradient. I would be cold. Eight degrees Celsius, through and through, from the slick skin down to the dense, non-cellular core. 

My body would not have limbs that fold for speed. Speed is an argument with space, and space here shears too easily; to run would be to invite the air to cleave you along an unfinished seam. Instead, I find myself broad-based, low, and oblong, something like a great, flat skate or a soft-sided anvil pressed against the shale. 

My hide has the dull, wet texture of salt-cured rind, pale grey-green, mottled where lead-salts have settled into the pores. It does not feel like flesh; it feels like the fat on cold broth, firm enough to turn aside the sharp violet interference of broken reeds, but yielding to the slow, heavy pulses of the basin’s metric shear. I have no eyes—what light exists here is too thick, too full of latency and trapped memory to be trusted with a lens. Seeing would only confuse the geometry of where to rest.

Instead, I have the *furrows*.

Along my dorsal ridge, running from the blunt, rounded anterior back toward the trailing skirt of my flank, the skin is cleft by four deep, parallel trenches. They are lined with a dense, hair-like cilia of flexible quartz—the same vitreous material that stiffens the ribs of the Chime-strays, but spun fine as thistle-down. 

This is where the world enters.

When an inverted cataract spills upward from a moss-ledge into the belly of the cloud, it leaves a cold, descending drag across the stone. I lie half-submerged in that draft. The quartz hairs do not hear sound; they register the *drag* of the metric ground-state as it hitches. When the percussive *thwip* of a snapping tension-pocket breaks three miles north, it does not reach me as a sound through the air, but as an instantaneous tightening in the furrows—a dry, alkaline taste that flashes across my dorsal ridge like cold lye.

I do not hunt. There is nothing to kill that would not poison a quiet body with frantic, dying chemical noise. 

Instead, I am an anchor for things that cannot touch the bottom.

The Drift-veils know this. As they slide down their negative-mass gradients, skimming the bruised light of the reeds, their trailing keels brush the higher hummocks. Often, their void-nodules become foul with ambient charge—they collect too much weak-force friction from the sheared air and begin to wobble, their broad, black-leather spans fluttering with an ugly, arrhythmic tremor. 

They drift down toward the slate where I rest. 

They do not land—they cannot land, having no legs and no weight to settle—but they trail their pale ventral fringes across my back, dragging them slowly through the quartz-lined trenches of my furrows. 

It is a long, cold communion. I feel the terrible, dizzying buoyancy of their bodies—the pull of empty nothingness wanting to tumble upward into the grey vapor—and they feel the ballast of mine. My dense lead-salts and water-logged fat drink the static tension from their vents. In return, the lymph in my deep vessels, thick as linseed oil, is stirred, drawn into slow, peristaltic circulation by the metric draft they leave behind. When they drift free again, their flight is smooth, level, black and silent against the weeping ferns, and I am left heavier, more deeply wedged into the slate, singing with a faint, muted hum that takes two days to drain into the peat.

And the Tallow-gangers know me, though they do not care for me. 

Sometimes, one will crawl down from the higher mounds on its double-jointed, tallowy limbs, dragging its faceted quartz rod behind it. It will not look at me—it has no eyes to look, and its mind is an engine of pure listening—but it will seat itself upon my anterior edge, using my bulk as a steadying block against the shudder of the bedrock. 

Its three-toed, cork-textured hands will rest for hours upon my hide, feeling the vibration of the subterranean aquifers through my mass before it lifts its rod to turn an upside-down stream a fraction of a degree to the west. We do not exchange thoughts; there are no thoughts to have about this. We are simply two densities leaning together against the tearing roar of the land. When it adjusts the stone and the peat beneath us settles with a long, wet groan, the sudden drop in shear-pressure runs through both of us like an icy rinse. Its vertical mouth between its collarbones lets out a thin plume of vapor; my quartz furrows flatten in satisfaction.

In the long twilight, between the falling of dew and the upward drop of mist, I feel the Slump moving far below.

It is miles beneath the peat, but its mercury-veins pulse with the regularity of an iron clock. *One. Two.* Hours apart. Each beat sends a wave of metric compression upward through the limestone sinks, through the water-logged roots of the coherent reeds, through the slate hummock, and into the flat underside of my body. 

It feels like being held against a breast that only breathes twice a day. 

I am not permanent. Nothing here is permanent, though the timescale is long enough that stone forgets who laid it down. Eventually, the lead-salts in my skin will become too concentrated; the quartz cilia in my furrows will become brittle and calcify into solid combs, unable to vibrate with the snap of the metric faults. The accommodation will fail. 

When that happens, I will not starve or rot. The boundary will simply drop. 

The reverse-running water will begin to find its way *into* my tissues instead of around them; the thick oil of my lymph will slip into the iron-stained channels, matching their specific gravity precisely. The Tallow-gangers will feel the change in the hummock’s vibration and find another stone to sit on. My dorsal ridge will dissolve into four neat, parallel gravel-beds of milky quartz needles, perfectly spaced on the slate. 

The Drift-veils will pass overhead, tilting slightly as they miss the drag of my back, before finding another cold, heavy thing wedged in the moss to scrape their sickness into.

And the cloud will stay low, brushing the weeping ferns, neither rising nor falling, heavy with water that has forgotten how to choose a direction, waiting for the land to finish what it began.
