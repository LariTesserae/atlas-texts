At first, there is only the tooth.

It is the left lower molar, worn flat as a river silt-shingle, and it is pressed hard against a stub of petrified pine driven three fingers deep into the rib-flange of an ancient cork-tree. 

Through the tooth comes the news. 

It does not arrive as language, but as weight, frequency, and water-tension. Two miles to the north, an ancient peat-shelf—heavy with forty seasons of unfallen mist—has torn its fibrous tendons and slipped four feet into an anaerobic pocket; the thump enters my jaw like the dull impact of a heel against an unskinned thigh. Farther off, through the lateral conduits where the root-knees knit above the black water, there is a lighter, stuttering rhythm: the fleece-beasts are changing boughs, their greasy weight rolling along the grafted bridges, swapping places in the amber vapor with the wet *slap-slide* of soaked lard.

I do not move my head. To lift the bone away from the peg is to become deaf, and to be deaf here is to be cast loose into the soft, white room of the world where nothing has an edge.

My chin is rested in a notch worn smooth by seventy years of my own chin, and forty years of the chin that came before me. The bark has tried to heal this place twelve times; twelve times I have taken the chisel—the flat splint of badgertallow bone—and pared back the pale, rubbery scar-flesh before it could close the gap. The tree and I have reached an agreement made entirely of sap: it leaks a golden, bitter gum that smells of vinegar; I wipe it away with the heel of my palm and rub it into my throat to keep the spore-itch from taking the bronchial branches.

I am not tall. If I stood straight upon the moss-crust, my crest would scarcely reach the shoulder of a fleece-beast, but I have not stood straight since the second great mounding of the shelf-fungus behind my chamber. My spine has taken the curve of the root-hollow. When I crawl, my belly hangs low and hairless, slick with the yellow grease we render from the bellies of the white crayfish. 

My skin is the color of tallow that has been dropped in tea. It does not sunburn, because there is no sun to burn it—only the white, warm blindness above the clouds that turns to grease-colored amber when the planetary floor tilts away from the light. Where my hips and the outer flats of my thighs touch the wood while I sleep, the flesh has formed thick, horn-colored cushions, broad as trenchers, that do not feel the prick of the liverwort bristles.

If you looked for me from the high canopy—where the aerial medusae drift like pale, severed bladders through the zinc mist—you would not see me. You would see only a knot in the swelling of an elephant-barked trunk, an irregularity in the universal upholstery. 

A hand reaches out. It is my hand, though it looks like an old piece of peeled ginger. It has no nails to catch on the fibers; the tips of the four fingers are capped with rounded studs of gray horn, hard enough to crack the carapace of an air-snail, broad enough that when I press them into the sponge-floor, they do not puncture the living moss. A film of lukewarm water coats the knuckles instantly. It never dries. It has not dried since I was dropped from my mother’s flank into a dry basket of shaved cork.

I am waiting for the water in the lower basin to slacken.

Every eleven days, the warm thermal breath that lifts this plateau from the unseen lands beneath seems to pause, just for the space of two hundred heartbeats. The air grows dense enough to chew; the mist stops drifting and hangs like milk in a pail. In that stillness, the inch-deep tannic pools upon the terraced steps go glassy. The breathers—the disc-fish who lie stacked like wet plates in the black peat—rise to the surface of the mud to blow small, sticky bubbles of sulfur and air. 

That is when the crayfish come up to feed on the foam.

My hand knows the way without the eyes. The eyes are small, milky beads, squinted permanently against the drifting pollen; they see only three shades of yellow and whether something is larger or smaller than a bushel. But the skin of my forearm feels the chill—the true, deep, unmistakable chill that does not belong to this plateau.

It is coming along the slug-road.

Ten paces below my root-perch, one of the great birch-slugs is passing. It is twelve feet of grey, reticulated muscle, cold as bog-iron, sliding southward toward the shelf-fungus fields at the speed of a lichen-spore germinating. Behind it, its skirt leaves the thick, clear river of mucus that will set before the next sky-ambering into a pale, hard curb. 

That curb is where I will place my feet.

To walk on the moss is labor. Every step is an extraction: *squish-sink-wait-pull*. Four inches down into the wet hair of the plateau, four inches up with the sponge sucking at the heel. But upon the slug-road, the foot-pads strike something that does not give. The bone behind the knee does not have to spend its marrow fighting the ground. On the slug-road, a maker can travel a thousand paces before the heart has beaten thirty times.

I slide from the hollow. 

The drop is short—three feet of air that tastes of rotting cedar and the sour starch of polypores. My soles hit the slug-trail with a soft, rubbery *clop*. The mucus is still cool, half-cured, like wet horn. It clings to the calluses of my heels, but my toes—wide and spatulate—find the center of the track where it has already hardened into a glass-gray spine.

I carry nothing that was not grown or carved within twelve reaches of my bed-hollow, save for the thing against my ribs.

It hangs from my neck by a thong of twisted fern-gut: a fragment of gray river-granite, no larger than the knuckle of an infant fleece-beast. It is triangular, sharp-cornered, and utterly dead. 

It does not sweat. It does not swell when the fog thickens. When I press it against my tongue, it tastes of absolute emptiness—no salt, no acid, no bitter resin. It is a chip from the great ring that sits three days' crawl to the west, where the old ones go to lie down. It was chipped off six generations ago by a bone chisel that shattered in the striking, and it has been smoothed not by water, but by the tongues of my grandmothers, who licked it in the dark to remember what a thing is like when it refuses to die.

My lungs are beginning to make the whistling sound.

It is not loud—the moss eats it before it travels the length of my arm—but I can feel it in the fork of my breastbone: a soft, wet rattling like a dead leaf fluttering in a flute. The white-rot has begun in the spongy lower lobes. It is the gift of the shelf-fungi; everyone who carves the wood breathes the dust of their gills until the lungs turn into another garden. In a season, or two seasons, the breath will become too heavy to lift up the throat.

I do not turn back toward the sleeping-hollow.

I turn west, along the slug-road that gleams like an oiled scar through the jade twilight. 

Ahead, through three hundred yards of pearl-colored steam, the great mounds rise and fall like the bellies of sleeping cows. Somewhere under those mounds, forty feet deep in the fibrous shale, the taproots are drinking the warm, planetary blood and groaning their slow, hydraulic hymns.

I walk with my head low, three fingers always brushing the hard track, my spade-thumb hooked into the cord around my neck. 

I am going to the stone. 

There are twelve cobbles in the ring, though three are already drowned under the flesh of a yellow polypore that has been growing over them since the time of the three-fingered carver. I will find the fourth stone—the one that sits highest in the peat-swell, the one that is speckled like the egg of a water-crawler. 

I will sit beside it. I will lay my palms upon its cold, dry shoulders, where no moss can find a pore to root in. I will wait until the slug-slime on the road behind me has fully set, and then I will lay my chin against the granite, waiting for the tooth to tell me what the earth is doing where there are no trees, no fog, and no makers to listen to the wood.
