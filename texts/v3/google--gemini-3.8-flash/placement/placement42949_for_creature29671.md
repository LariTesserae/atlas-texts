I would not be on the glass. Up there, the glare is too naked; the cobalt beyond the panes is cold enough to crack teeth, and the metal ribs expand with small, dry rifle-cracks every time the sun clears a peak. 

Nor would I be the one who climbs. The tender’s life has too many edges—the spring-steel blade, the split-hide strap, the bitter smoke of carbide held between the molars. Her work is a war against the swelling of things, and to live as a warden against sixty paces of boiling sap is to be worn away like a block of salt left in a creek.

I would be lower down, where the work of the pylon settles and grows heavy. 

At first, it is only a position. A wedge of wet shadow beneath the central staging platform, where the sloped lead pan dips toward the south drainage sump. It is the only place in the Core where the mist does not fall from above; here, it gathers from the bottom up, rising off the runoff in thick, greasy cuffs of warm air that smell of boiled leaf-veins and engine fat. 

Here, the iron grating overhead is double-thick to bear the weight of the blow-off cocks and the grease drums. When she sits above to eat her tongue and peas, her heels rest on the checker-plate six inches above my crown. A drop of cooking-lard falls through the square mesh; it does not hit the water directly, but strikes a zinc flange, flattens into a white wafer, and hangs there until the heat softens it and lets it slide.

I am not metal, though I have taken the cold square pattern of the grating into the hide of my back from pressing against it during the midday swells. 

And I am not plant, though the rubbery tendrils of the *Philodendron* have crept across my flanks twice this week, mistaking me for an unlagged return-pipe. I did not cut them. I let them lay their pale sucker-pads against my skin, drinking the thin film of mineral wash that dries on me between the squalls, until their tips grew too bold and nudged toward the softer clefts behind my jaw. Then I merely shifted—three inches to the left, a heavy, wet scrape of horn against lead—and sheared them against the rivet-heads without waking from my drowse.

I came up in the silt. That much is clear from the grit that still works its way out of the hinges of my plates. When they packed the zinc troughs with dredged loam from the river delta, four thousand meters below, I was curled into a hard, tarry walnut, no larger than a fist, tucked into the knot of a drowned alder root. I had been dry for seven seasons down in the gravel. 

The heat of the steam beam woke me. That heavy, rhythmic *chuff-thump*—it was not rain, but it had the steady, thudding cadence of a spate coming down the gorge. When the first four-minute mist blew through the bulkhead, warm as cow’s breath and sour with machine-oil, the walnut cracked.

Now I am nearly the width of an intake grate. My legs are short, thick spuds shod in black, spade-shaped nails meant for shoveling through gray glacial clay, but here there is no clay to dig—only the endless, warm wash over the lead sheets. So I do not shovel. I anchor. I have learned to wedge my hindquarters between two copper riser-pipes where the clearance is narrow. The water flows over my carapace in a shallow, continuous skin, parting around the hump of my shell like grease around a spoon.

I am something between a river-terrapin and an anvil. My shell is not the bright green of the day-geckos, nor the oily rose of the newly lathe-turned plates. It is the color of a wet boot—dark, slate-brown, mottled with the milky yellow of curing lacquer that has drifted down from the upper seams and set in the rings of my scutes.

I taste everything the Core discards.

Every four minutes, the atomizers hiss. The sound begins as a high, brass whistle at the south wall, then drops into a thick, sodden roar as the fog blooms. With the fog comes the drift: parboiled leaflets of calathea that missed the collection screens; the dead, pale casings of symphylans that tumbled from the coir mats; globules of tallow shaken loose from the pump rockers; and the bitter, lime-tasting ash that drops when she cleans the nozzle of her carbide lamp with a brass wire.

It all washes down the lead pans. It collects against my front claws in a soft, steaming drift. I push my blunt head into the flow, my small, lidless eyes milky from the steam, and draw the mash into my throat. It tastes of zinc and wet fiber, but beneath that, it tastes of the deep mountain: the spring-water that the iron beam brings up from three miles down, laden with sulfur, flint-chalk, and iron-blood. 

Sometimes, the curved spring-steel blade flashes down through the grating.

She is on her knees above, her canvas trousers soaked through to the gray skin of her shins. She does not see me; the carbide jet between her teeth casts a narrow, fiercely white circle that shows her only the thick, white root-tips prying at the packing-glands. She hacks with three quick, dry jerks. 

A rubbery finger of *Philodendron* falls through the iron mesh and lands on the flat of my nose. It is fresh, spurting its clear, rhubarb-smelling juice. I do not snatch at it. I wait. I let it sit there until her hemp shoes shuffle along the diamond-tread toward the north flue, the sound of her breath rattling wetly in her throat as she works against the positive pressure. 

When the light of her flame has died down to a faint blue flutter against the zinc siding, I open my jaws. The root is tough, like pickled gristle, and it hums faintly with the pressure that was driving the sap into its cells when the steel found it. I crush it between two broad, flat plates of bone that take the place of teeth. The juice is hot, astringent, and thick as lard; it coats the inside of my gullet with the clean, chemical vitality of the high mount.

I am growing too wide for the pan. 

In another month, if the cable-car continues to bring only lard and salt, and no one comes with a pry-bar to clear the under-deck, my shell will span the gutter from the riser-pipe to the iron curb. The washwater will back up behind me. A warm, three-inch pool will gather around the staging platform, drowning the lower flanges of the blow-off cocks. 

Perhaps then she will look down through the checker-plate with her lamp. She will see the wet, lacquered curve of my back, ridged and dark, rising through the gray bacterial paste like the shoulder of an old boulder in a freshet. She will reach down with the curved blade to test whether I am iron or meat.

And I will not move. I will press my claws into the lead seams, take the four-minute mist onto the back of my neck, and let her spring-steel ring against my ribs with the same dull, heavy *chuff* as the pump rocking in the dark beneath us both.
