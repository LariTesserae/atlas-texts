At first, the instinct is to imagine oneself low—to seek the thick, wet felts of liverwort, to be the heavy mutton-slug dragging its leathery mantle across the *Marchantia*, dumb and insulated, eating cold rot while the stone hums against the belly. There is a terrible safety in that kind of weight: to be so dense, so low, and so coated in grease that the world cannot knock you over because you are already the floor. 

Or further down still: to be one of the flatworms sealed in the half-inch drill-holes, suspended in the Faraday ripples, anchored inside a geometry someone else cut with sand and iron two hundred years ago. There, life would be a permanent lattice of tension, an existence lived entirely inside the surface tension of a single drop of mineral sweat, matching the rhythm of the cilia to the thirty-cycle heave of the river beneath.

But neither of those places is where I settle. The floor is too full of violence. The bedrock does not allow for interior life; it does not even allow for quiet. To lie against it is to have your organs shaken into uniform paste, to have every thought or sensation crowded out by the pelvic hammer of the water forcing its way through the limestone choke. 

No, to find oneself here, one must pull back from the strike of the stone without leaving the room. 

I would be on the southern shelf, just above the reach of the gravel floor, where the blackened oak baulks wedge into the rock seam. But I am not inside the wood. To be the cerambycid grub, chewing cold tallow-scented oak in a twenty-year night, is a sentence of slow consumption; it is an inheritance that ends when the timber turns to silt. 

Instead, I find myself hanging in the air between them. 

Not flying—nothing flies in this dead vault—but slung. 

I am the harvestman, or something that has come to the same conclusions as the harvestman. My body is almost nothing: a dry, flattened grain of matter no larger than a burnt lentil, holding only the cold, yellow droplet of a heart and a knot of ganglia that does not think so much as it balances. My real being is in the legs. 

They are eight inches across, absurdly thin, pale as boiled horsehair, and strung with tendons that do not fatigue. I do not stand on the rock. To stand on the rock would be death; the vibration would travel up through the joints and shatter the delicate fluid-valves of my core. 

Instead, I am anchored by three microscopic, curved claws to three separate surfaces, forming a tripod of tension in a dead corner of the dome:
One claw is hooked into a minute chisel-scar on the side of the split oak baulk, where an iron tool bit into the grain two centuries ago.
The second is lodged in a pore of dark brown limonite, resting on the cold slick of the iron-eating bacteria.
The third is wedged into an invisible joint-fissure in the limestone wall itself, where the rock is slick with bicarbonate sweat.

Suspended between these three points, my body hangs in the center of the acoustic space, held motionless four inches off the shelf. 

I am a biological bridge. The rock beneath me shudders with the fury of thirty tons of water per second driven through a throat of flint, but because my limbs are strung like the braces of a drum, I do not absorb the blow. I tune it out. By flexing the micro-tendons in the first three segments of my legs, I allow the vibration of the limestone to pass through the outer joints and dissipate into the dead oak and the iron crib, leaving my central body entirely, miraculously still. 

I have created a private node. In a chamber where the noise is a blunt, physical wall that bruises the ribs of anything with lungs, I hang in a pocket of absolute, manufactured silence. 

I have no eyes. The dim iron twilight that filters through the maple leaves sixty feet above never reaches this cleft, and it would be useless if it did. My world is entirely mechanical. Through the hair-fine sensilla along my legs, I read the room not as sight, and not even as sound, but as a map of shear and torque.

I can feel the exact weight of the cold air mass sitting in the sink, pressing down like mercury on the floor. 
I can feel the periodic drop of water from the flowstone boss thirty paces out—not the sound of its fall, which is swallowed by the river, but the microscopic air-displacement it causes when it strikes the chert pebbles. 
I can feel the slow, hydraulic displacement of the pale salamander, three yards away, its dense chin pressed to the bedrock, creeping like cold bread dough from one acoustic shadow to the next. It does not concern me; its small, thorn-like legs cannot climb the vertical split of the oak, and its wide mouth is meant for things that stay on the mats.

Mostly, I wait. 

Time here is not measured by sun or season. The air never warms; the stone never dries. Time is measured only by the micro-shocks that travel along my tethers. 

An hour may pass—or three days—in which nothing happens except the steady, hydraulic thrum of the river far below, an unchanging hum in the chitin of my knees. Then, three feet away on the black oak, a tiny white springtail lands, tossed into the air by the shivering stone, its microscopic weight registering against the wood like the impact of a falling leaf on water. 

My long forward leg does not strike. Nothing strikes in this room; hurry is an upland mistake. 

The leg simply lifts. It disengages its tiny claw from the oak grain with an imperceptible click, reaches out through the cold, dead air with a slow, hair-fine deliberation, and tests the wood an inch closer. It takes ten minutes to close the distance. My central body never moves; it remains suspended in its triangular cradle of tendons, an instrument measuring the mountain's slow digestion.

When the chelicerae finally close on the springtail, there is no struggle. There is only the cold, slow transfer of a microscopic drop of fat from one shell to another. 

And then the leg returns to its anchor. The claw finds the chisel-mark again. The tendons draw taut. 

Below me, the river keeps hammering at the meat of the mountain, scouring out the limestone, blind and frantic, rushing toward some outlet it will never see. But here, suspended by hair-widths between the old axe-work of dead men and the stone that will outlast them both, I am the only thing in the sink that is utterly at rest.
