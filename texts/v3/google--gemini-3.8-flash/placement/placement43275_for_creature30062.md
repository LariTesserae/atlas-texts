At first, you would think I am a bundle of split laths and grease-cured hide that has slipped into a crotch of the wood and stayed there until the frost glued it down. 

I would be wedged three miles out from the three huts, at the dead-angle where our home arch—the *Girth*, they call its rhythm, though it has no name that can be spoken without clicking the back teeth—grinds against a younger, paler rib that arrived twenty seasons ago out of the south-southeast. 

I am not in the huts because the huts are too loose; they rattle when the deep core sings. I am a listener, but not an official one. In the gulf, there are no titles, only tasks that have hollowed out a person until the person fits the task the way a bone wedge fits an auger-hole. 

I would be old, which here means forty, which means my fingers no longer open flat. If I laid my hand on a table—if there were an earth, if there were a board that had ever been cut horizontal—the knuckles would arch like the roots of a willow. My palms are yellow horn, thicker than the sole of a boot from forty years of biting into the hairy grain of the laths. When I sleep, slung in my basket of dried gut and willow-ribs two fathoms below the arch’s northern ridge, my hands remain closed in my armpits, hooked around the chest-band. If they loosened, I would wake in the white drop before my mind cleared enough to scream.

Here is what I am: I am the one who lives between the stay-lines.

The filament-spiders do not mind me. We have reached an engineering truce. Their silk—that thick, pearl-colored hawser that smells of cold tallow and crushed leaves—is anchored twelve paces above my basket. When a line hums too high, approaching the clean, bright *shing* that means the tensile limit is sixty heartbeats away, I do not cut it. I drive a horn peg into the bark just behind the spider’s foot-gland, giving the grain something to split against so the bark doesn't tear out in a twenty-yard sheet and take my wicker basket with it. Sometimes the spider is there when I do it. It does not lift its needle-feet against me; it merely shifts its three central joints, feeling the strike of my bone mallet through the wood, tasting the grease on my sleeves to see if I have died yet. 

My eyes are gone to slits. The light here does not allow you to keep an open face; if you looked straight up into the white ceiling for the space of a hundred paces, your tears would freeze into horns on your cheekbones and your pupils would scald into gray milk. I see the gulf through a crack between calloused folds of skin no wider than the thickness of a mica flake. 

Through that crack, the world is not beautiful. It is an immense, unyielding draftsmanship.

Everything is sharp. The rib that runs across my sky twenty leagues off is not a blue shadow; it is a pale bone rod, perfectly ruled, with its splinters and its hoarfrost needles as clean as if they were against my eyelashes. It hurts the brain, that lack of mist. Your mind wants to believe that what is small is far away and therefore soft, but here, the small thing ten miles off is merely small and hard, cutting into the eye like a grain of sand.

My work is to feel the *sst-prak* before it becomes a scream.

The huts rely on the sound carrying through the open void, but sound in the thin air is slow—it takes three long breaths for a fracture-crack at the junction to reach the settlement. By then, the spear-shoot has already burst through the bark like a lance through an ox’s belly, and whoever was anchored in the split is pulp and frozen mist. 

I live with my forehead pressed against the cambium. 

I spend twelve hours of each turn of light with my brow-ridge resting directly on the peeled wood, where I have scraped away the rind down to the slick, salty yellow fat of the inner grain. I do not listen with my ears; the thin air makes ears stupid. I listen with my skull. 

Through the frontal bone, the chord speaks:

First, there is the *thrum*—the slow, heavy pendulum-swing of sixty leagues of petrified starch carrying its load of frost. That is the breath. It never stops. It is like the pulse an infant feels in its mother’s throat, if the mother were made of frozen spruce and three miles thick.

Then, there is the *grit-grit* of the tallow-husks grazing below. They are five hundred cubits down, on the sheer under-belly where the white glare from the floorless drop reflects off their belly-plates. They chew without hurry. They do not know there is a void beneath them; they know only the hardness of the fiber and the salt of the slime. Sometimes, through the bone of my head, I feel one of them loose its hold—a small, dull *tick*, like a fingernail snapping, followed by nothing. No splash. No thud. Just the space where the weight was, and then the wood easing itself up a fraction of a hair.

And then comes the seizure.

It does not come with noise at first. It comes with heat.

When the hydraulic surge gathers forty miles back in the deep wall where the roots are jammed into the unseen furnace of the crust, the wood against my forehead warms by half a degree. The hoarfrost on my eyebrows does not melt, but it turns soft, like greasy salt. The dry, iron smell of the air thickens with something alkaline and sweet—the smell of crushed parsnips and marsh-gas.

That is when I must move. 

I do not stand. Standing is for those who are about to fall. I slide on my belly, my horny palms and knees finding the shallow notches I chiseled ten years ago, my safety-line of braided tallow-skin trailing behind me through the cold. I reach the horn wedge that hangs from the junction-post.

I take the bone mallet. I do not strike three times; three is a waste of grease. I strike once, with the weight of my shoulder and my neck combined, driving the wedge into the dead-seam between the two crossing ribs.

The sound does not travel through the air; it shoots through the marrow of the *Girth* straight into the spruce-pegs of the three huts. They will feel it in their hips where they lie in their baskets. They will know: *Twelve breaths. The southern spur is reaching.*

After the mallet-fall, I have nothing to do but anchor.

I drop my chin into my collar. I bite the horn bit that is lashed into my beard—the bit that keeps a man from biting his own tongue off when the shock wave runs through his jaw. I pull my knees into my ribs, hook my elbows through the twin loops of the root-stay, and wait.

The scream comes.

It is like six thousand yards of sailcloth being ripped down the middle by two teams of bullocks. The wood beneath me leaps—not an inch, but four feet upward in a fraction of an heartbeat. The air is slapped out of my lungs with a sound like a wet towel hitting flat stone. 

Through my knife-slit eyes, I see the south-spur bolt into the void.

It does not grow like a plant on the surface; it shoots like an iron bolt from an engine. A spear-head of wet, pale tallow-wood, sixty cubits round, lunges out into the frozen white light. It screams as its own bark fails to keep pace with the pith, the green fibers tearing themselves apart in their hurry to find something to brace against. A cloud of boiling sap sprays out behind it, blinding white in the glare, and instantly turns to hail—millions of translucent, pea-sized marbles of frozen brine that clatter down against my back and bounce off my skull like sling-stones.

The spear reaches thirty paces—sixty—a hundred. It is looking for the sister-arch five miles off. 

It does not make it.

I feel the shudder through my breastbone: the *vohl*—that sickening deadness when the wood realizes it has overhung its own strength. 

Two miles out, in the empty glare, the spear-head bows. It does not bend slowly; the frost hits it, the salt-water inside crystallizes into long, splitting daggers, and the whole five-hundred-ton tongue of new wood fractures with a sound that sounds like a cannon fired inside a small iron kettle.

*Crack.*

The severed head cartwheels. I watch it through my slits. It turns once, twice, three times, perfectly distinct in the shadowless white, shedding sheets of steaming peel that follow it down like white ribbons. It shrinks. It becomes the size of a lath. It becomes the size of a louse. It becomes a dot of frost on the lens of the void, and then the white swallows it entirely.

The trunk beneath me settles. It gives a long, groaning wheeze—the *khel*—as the remaining stump chills, stiffens, and joins the petrified spine of the arch.

The hail stops bouncing off my shoulders. 

I spit the horn bit out of my teeth. My mouth tastes of old iron and copper grease.

Slowly, using the hooks of my fingers, I drag myself out of the root-stay and crawl along the fresh fracture. The wood here is still steaming. A gallon of thick, gray-green sap is weeping from a split the size of a man’s torso, boiling faintly as the cold air attacks it.

Already, the lice are there. 

They are tumbling out of the grain, hundreds of them, their mica-shell legs clicking like dry seeds in a bladder. They do not care that the frost is thirty heartbeats behind them. They are climbing over each other’s backs, their abdomens already swelling with the salty brine, their tiny, glass-clear jaws buried in the foaming cellulose. Two of them are stuck together, mating while their lower legs are already turning white and brittle with rime.

I don't look at them long. I reach into the steaming fissure with my horn scraper.

I scrape out the warm, green jelly that lines the wood before the cold can turn it to flint. It is bitter, so bitter that it makes the glands under my ears ache until my teeth water, but it is warm. It is thirty degrees above the air. I pack it into the leather pouch at my navel, next to my skin, feeling the heat of the chord’s seizure sink through the fat-smeared hide of my belly.

Then I take my mallet and my bone drift-pin and I begin to clear the ice from my stay-line.

I will stay here until my hips no longer bend enough to fit the wicker basket. When that happens, I will not call out to the huts. They would not waste the sinew to come haul a stiff man three miles along the northern ridge. 

I will simply pull the pin from my basket-stay and drop the five cubits down into the wet fracture behind the junction. I will wedge my boots into the green grain while it is still soft from the last lunge.

The chord will take me. In three hours, the frost will have my eyes, but the wood will have my shins. The next seizure will run its salt through my femurs, crushing the hollow parts until the marrow is squeezed out to join the sap-boil. 

My knuckles will remain outside the bark for a year or two—yellow, hooked, and hard as horn—giving the tallow-husks something to click their plates against as they graze their way toward the drop. And fifty leagues off, through twenty miles of frozen white nothingness, someone with eyes narrowed to a knife-slit will see a tiny, dark knot in the arch and know that the timber held.
