I would not be on the floor. 

The floor is for things that have a purpose that must be acted out in transit—the scrapers with their frantic, rhythmic planings; the pale, unremembered Iterants feeling their way toward the cold seams where they will burst and turn to glue. Down there, the pressure is too narrative. Everything on the liverwort mat is either newly born, frantically grooming, or dissolving into a joint.

I find myself higher up, where the air ceases to circulate and simply *hangs*, caught in the dead-angles where the flying buttresses spring from the tallow-thick base of the wall. 

At first, I think I am the tendril itself. 

There is an immense, rigid cord of silica, no thicker than a thumb, drawn so tight across the vault that its resonance does not sound like music; it sounds like the edge of a razor pressed against a turning wet-stone. For a long time, there is only that frequency: an unbroken, high-register whine that bypasses any organ of hearing and reports directly to the marrow. I feel the strain of the rock three miles above, transmitted through the frozen ceiling into the root-crown, then down through this single, petrified line. I am the line’s stiffness. I am the sheer, obstinate refusal to snap.

Then there is a shifting of weight—a wet, curdled slosh inside a casing that does not belong to the mineral world. 

I am not the line. I am clamped to it.

My jaw is an arc of dense, unyielding horn, set with thousands of microscopic glass teeth that have bitten through the supercooled lipid film and seated themselves directly into the silica sheath. It does not hurt; there are no nerves for pain in the clamp, only a fierce, cold conductivity. My mouth is not an entrance for food. It is a vice. If I let go, the tendon would whip sideways with the force of an uncoiling spring, fracturing the amber tubes that run like veins behind me, and the wall would belly inward under the weight of the permafrost before the nearest Iterant could even register the tear.

So I hang. 

My back is turned to the void of the transept. My body is a heavy, flattened shield of unpolished tortoiseshell, milky and dull as tallow, weeping the non-Newtonian oil from every suture where the plates overlap. Below me, three stories down through the lavender fog, the Register sits in his rotating brine, his bald forehead thrumming with that slow, four-stroke marine rhythm. I feel his moan before it leaves the pool—not as sound, but as a subtle hydraulic drop along the wire, a loosening of the tension that allows me to slide my clamp three millimeters to the left.

The motion takes five minutes. Six short, segmented legs, clawless and flat as spade-bits, shift against the humid air, finding purchase on nothing, paddling the cold aerosol as if it were syrup. I do not move toward anything. I move only to cancel the sway. When a new node cracks open in the far corner—that explosive, wet rupture of cambium forcing its way into the void—the recoil hits my tendon like an iron bar. My viscera, which are cold and dense as sheep’s fat, lurch forward against the breastplate. My horn vice grates on the silica, throwing off a microscopic shower of glass dust that settles onto my own shell. I absorb the shudder. I damp the wave. The singing drops from a shriek back to a dead, glass hum.

This is what I am: an illiterate regulator. A dumb pocket of cold lard and bone, hung between the expansion of the machine and the weight of the mountain.

Except that the wire is not empty.

Every forty seconds, the amber core inside the silica flares. It does not warm me—nothing here has ever known warmth—but it turns my pale horn translucent from within. When the pulse fires, the light travels through the roots of my teeth, through the crescent vice, and straight into the primitive, unfurled knot of ganglia that sits between my shoulders.

For three seconds, I am not ballast. 

For three seconds, the wire uses my cold fat to think with.

The thought is never mine. It is too wide, too violently organized; it tastes of copper and noon, of things that possess an outside. It is the memory of the keystone—the image of that impossible, towering green, an architecture made not of ivory buttresses holding back the dark, but of leaves that open themselves to an emptiness so vast that the sky has no ceiling at all. It is a light that does not hum. It is a heat that does not curdle.

When it hits me, my short legs begin their involuntary dance. They twitch and curl in the freezing mist, beating an uneven, six-beat cadence against the underbelly of the arch. My plates creak. Inside my flask of skin, the curdled cream of my organs turns violet, glowing like an oil lamp lowered into deep sea. I do not understand what I am seeing—to a creature formed in an icebox of crushed zinc and starch, the concept of a *leaf that does not compute* is a monstrosity, an obscene waste of surface area. Yet my legs reach for it. My horn jaws clamp tighter on the wire, as if by crushing the glass I could drink the green before it dissipates into the floor.

Then the pulse passes. 

The light dies out of my belly. The violet bruise fades back into tallow and skimmed milk. The high, tectonic roar of the cavitation valves rushes back into the space left behind, deafening and familiar, and my legs fall slack, dangling down into the cold spray.

Below, an Iterant is dissolving against a cracked rib of wood, its pale grease running into the seam, its face-plate already turned to amber jelly. The scrapers are already there, their quartz legs clicking as they plane away the excess, indifferent to the dying thing, indifferent to the room, indifferent to the ice that grinds its teeth against the roof.

I wipe a droplet of lipid from my horn plates with the tip of my middle leg. I settle my weight. The Register moans in his bath; the room leans half an inch to the south; the wire tightens until it screams like a bird.

I dig my teeth into the glass and pull. I am where the pressure meets the logic. I will not let it snap.
