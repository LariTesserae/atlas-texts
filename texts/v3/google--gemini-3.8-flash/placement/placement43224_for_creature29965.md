At first, there is no person in it. 

There is only the frequency of the stone, which does not enter through the ears—the ears were torn into useless, whistling ribbons of cartilage two seasons after the hair went—but through the heel-bones, through the pelvis, through the three teeth left in the front of the jaw that rest directly against the basalt spar. 

The basalt has five distinct notes. The third is the one that matters: a hollow, dry *ping*, like an anvil struck under deep water. That is the note of the riven face three hundred feet above, where the copper pegs are slowly chewing their way out of the young rock. When the third note widens—when it takes on a wet, gravelly slide at the tail—the rock will drop within three hundred heartbeats.

I am not on the Hangs. The Hangs are for the ones who still leak water from their skins—the ones who still have salt in them, who must sleep strapped to crawler-ribs and dream whatever thin, frightened dreams people dream when their eyes have turned to lard. 

Nor am I down in the churn. You cannot survive down there past twenty; the knees go, or the vinegar-steam rots the lung-sacs until you cough up strings of black, curded liver, or you misjudge the three-beat tap of a skimmer’s stilt and take two inches of volcanic pumice-bone through the arch of your foot.

I am hung between.

I live in the *notch*—a five-foot horizontal split where an ancient slab tore free before the herd was thick enough to bury it. It sits twenty feet above the highest heap-line, right where the cool-hoods' grease-veins run down to meet the boiling updraft from the vents. It is the only place in the trench where the air doesn't move: a dead seam where the sulfur-smoke gathers in a thick, yellow-gray curd that smells of scorched horn and wet turnip. 

The flyers do not come into the notch; the sulfur burns the fine vellum of their wing-fingers, turning their skin brittle in five beats of a bank. The herd cannot reach it, though on days when the mineral-slick is rich, the swine-plates slide against the lip of the rock just beneath me with a sound like a thousand wet spades scraping a butcher’s slab.

I have ceased to look like the people above, though they are the ones who braided the sinew that keeps me from rolling into the trough when the tremors come. 

My thighs have flattened. When you sit for twelve years in a four-foot cleave of wet slate, your meat does not stay round; it spreads, taking the shape of the shelf, until the tallow-scabs on your buttocks and hips are three fingers thick—tough as the crawler-foot pads, yellowed and grease-hardened, slick with the liver-brown biofilm that grows even on me where the rock-sweat runs down my spine. My eyelids do not move up and down; the inner film is permanently drawn, so that the sun-shafts do not shatter my brain with their quartz-glare. I see the rift only as amber and zinc: a world of hot, yellow planes moving against cold, gray meat.

What am I? 

I am the *pendulum-hand*. 

The Hangs cannot see through the noon-glare. The families up in the rib-baskets look down into a white, screaming furnace where the quartz dust bounces the sun until every crawler looks like a flat plate of tin and every butcher looks like a grease-fly. They cannot time the swing. If they throw the ball from eighty feet up, the draft takes it; it dances in the updraft like a dried bladder, or it tangles in a skimmer’s balance-whip and snaps the beast’s keel, bringing forty stone of thrashing stilt and beak down on the runner’s neck.

The rope comes to me first. 

It passes through my notch: a twelve-ply braid of crawler-gut, thick as my wrist, greased twice a day with the fat scraped from flyer-livers. It hangs from a native copper winch a thousand feet up, anchored in the dead granite of the upper lip, and it drops through my fingers down into the boiling murk below.

At the end of the line hangs the pear: forty pounds of cold-hammered lead, cast with three deep grooves to fit the crook of an arm, and pitted with the tooth-marks of sixty different men who have caught it with their mouths when their elbows were broken by the herd.

I sit with my heels braced against the lower lip of the notch. My hands do not hold the rope; they *are* the eye through which it breathes. 

I feel the runner before he jumps. 

Through the gut-line, I feel the slap of his greaves as he leaves the lower ladder. I feel the shudder of the crawler-shell he lands on—that heavy, indifferent, three-inch aragonite plate that doesn't even know it has been touched. I count his strides by the twitch of the hemp-binders on his shins: *one, two, slip, recover, three.* 

He is at the kill-zone. The rockfall was five minutes ago. The meat is already turning from gray to zinc-whey; the skimmers are already swinging their keels in from the margins, their long, needle-toothed probosces twitching like dry reeds in the steam.

I do not look at his face. Faces have no work here. I watch only the third leg of the nearest herder.

The herder is an old one—eleven feet high, its stilts pitted with lime-cankers, its breast-keel stained dark where it has scraped against dead carapaces. It steps with that peculiar, lungless hitch: *tap... tap... drop.* It is waiting for the butcher to finish the sweet-root. It wants the mash of the belly-organs that the man’s adze will leave behind.

I feel the tremor coming. Not the one from above, but the deep one—the twenty-minute stomach-twitch of the basalt floor. 

The white herd feels it too. Beneath my shelf, the sound changes. The shovel-scrape stops. The *choc-choc-choc* of the million legs dies into a wet, sickening silence as forty thousand swine-things drop their plates flat to the stone and clamp their suction-pads into the tallow-slime. 

The tallow-man down there has five beats. 

If he stays on the shell, the herd will heap over him the second the tremor passes, drawn by the smell of the severed root in his net. If he hooks the skimmer too early, the beast will take fright before the shock-wave hits, snap its knee-joint under the sudden drag, and fold like a dry basket into the churn.

I lean out of the notch. 

The steam hits my face like hot lard. My inner lids turn the trench into an oily, amber broth where three figures move: the gray square of the meat, the thin, needle-line of the stilt, and the red dot of the man’s blood where his knuckle grazed a shell-edge.

*Now.*

I don't drop the lead. I throw it with my shoulder-blade, using the weight of my own hip-scabs against the stone to give it the sweep. 

The line hums—a dry, singing whine that cuts through the sulfur-hiss. The forty-pound pear cuts an arc through the steam-curtain, thirty feet wide, dropping through the noon-glare like a dead hawk.

It does not go to where the man is. It goes to the empty air six feet to the left of the skimmer’s third knee, where there is nothing but rising gas and the smell of vinegar.

The man hooks. 

I feel the jerk through the line: the dull *thud* of his copper adze biting into the pumice-bone of the herder’s joint. The beast screeches—that dry, ungreased whistle that makes the flyers up on the ledges shift their skin-wings. The herder jerks its limb up to clear the iron; the man swings with it, his legs dangling five feet clear of the white plates that are already beginning to boil and slide as the shock-wave passes through the bedrock.

He is at the top of his pendulum. The herder is tilting, its keel rocking sixty degrees off the perpendicular, its balance-whips thrashing the steam into yellow foam.

The lead pear arrives.

It strikes him not in the hand—his hands are locked to the adze-haft—but against the side of his ribs, right where the scab-tallow is thickest. He opens his jaw; he bites the groove. 

I feel his teeth through forty yards of gut. I feel the enamel of his molars grind into the soft lead; I feel the salt of his gums where the vitriol has loosened them; I feel the terrible, animal snatch of his neck-muscles as the lead takes his weight off the skimmer’s knee.

With my left hand, I slap the twitch-cord twice against the basalt lip.

*Two beats.*

A thousand feet above, in the cool, dry air where the women sit among the water-hoods, the winch-drum turns. 

The rope does not come up smooth; it comes in great, lunging jerks that make the gut-braid stretch until it is as thin as a flyer’s finger. Every jerk lifts him ten feet out of the broth. He passes my notch so close I can smell the sweet-root in his pack—that cold, wood-vinegar tang—and the hot, zinc-stink of his fear. 

His eyes are wide, yellow-milky, looking straight into mine as he sweeps past. He does not know who I am. To him, I am only the shelf that does not fall; I am the lump of old tallow and aragonite that lives where the smoke is thickest. 

He clears the notch. He goes up toward the platforms, toward the dry baskets where the infants swing in their shell-boxes and the water tastes of tin and wet feathers.

I do not watch him reach the Hangs. The line goes slack for twenty heartbeats; then the weight of the empty pear pulls it taut again as they lower it back down through my fingers.

The shock-wave has cleared. 

Below me, the herd is already heaping. A new ridge of shale has split off three hundred yards down-trench, pulping four or five of the big sows; the white mass is folding inward like curd in a churn, climbing four deep over the crushed plates, their chisel-mouths working the red-gray pulp until the stone is scraped clean down to the basal slate. 

A skimmer—perhaps the one the runner hooked, perhaps another—steps cautiously into the foam, its needle-spurs finding the seams between the grinding backs, its long, fleshy snout already dropping into the dark between the carapaces.

The lead pear comes to rest in the notch. It is warm from the boy's jaw. 

I put my thumb into the fresh dent his teeth have made in the metal. It is deep. He has strong roots in his mouth; he will last another season, perhaps two, before a herder tires of him or a peg blows in the third note.

I lay my jaw back against the basalt spar. 

The stone is cooling slightly as the planet rolls its ripped hide away from the sun. The amber glare turns to the color of sheep-liver, then to the dark, bruised purple of cold basalt. The sulfur vents down in the gutter begin their night-glow—a thin, pale blue grease that licks the bellies of the crawlers as they scrape.

The rock speaks again.

Not the third note this time. The first. A long, low, granite groan that lives three miles down, where the furnace is still whole. 

I close my inner lid. I hold the gut-rope between my three middle toes, where the skin has grown together into a tough, hairless cleft, and I wait for the twitch-cord to tell me that another boy has dropped his shield and is looking for a leg to ride out of the mill.
