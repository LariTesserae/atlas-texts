At first, I would have thought I was a Straggler. 

I still have the coat. It was good frieze once, heavy and greasy with lanolin from sheep that grazed on actual dirt three valleys back, though now its hem is frayed into something like gray moss, stiff with the district’s alkaline brine. I can still recall the iron-and-tin taste that floods the back of the mouth when the Quad-Bite sets in—that nausea where your molars ache because your left jaw thinks it is at sea level while your right jaw is convinced it has been dragged five thousand feet up a scree slope.

I remember crouching near the loggia fires. I remember the sound of zinc saws biting into the curing jelly, that wet, lardaceous *shluck-shluck* as we cut the slabs out before they set into permanent quartz, and how our thumbs grew pale and hard around the nails from handling the cold grease of it.

But you don’t stay a Straggler if you linger where the geometry leans.

I am not one of them anymore. When I walk down the arcade toward the brazier-smoke, the men who still have blood in their gums do not ask me to sit. They do not throw stones—throwing a stone here is useless; an object launched across three paces might drop straight down or drift leftward into an eave that isn’t there—but they pull their knees up to their ribs and look down at their zinc blades until I pass. They smell me, perhaps, but not the way they smell each other. I smell like damp paper and flour. I smell like an iron grate that has spent twenty years in cellar air.

Where I am is in the gap—not the physical gap between the buildings, because the buildings have no gaps, but in the margin where the arithmetic has been rounded off to make the surfaces meet.

You would find me on the fourth floor of the tenement, or rather, on the stone architrave of the portico that lives inside the tenement. 

It is the quietest place in the quadrangle. When the eyelid blinks and the administrative official becomes solid, his slate desk appears around me like a cold, gray frost. He does not see me; his eyes are the hazel paste of the Placeholders, unblinking, focused on the inventory of a harbor that was never poured. But when he sits, his quill-hand passes through my left forearm with the sensation of someone dragging a cold silk ribbon through my meat. 

It does not hurt. That is the first thing that leaves: the sharp, tearing hurt of being a biological thing trapped in an uncalculated space. The Quad-Bite left my teeth three weeks ago, or three miles ago—the two measurements have begun to mean the same thing. My jaw simply went loose, then light, then settled into a neutral density that no longer cares about the barometric slip.

I am what happens when the Gauger’s fingers don't finish the erasure.

He touched me near the conduit-riser. I had slipped on a skin of the sweat where a lateral drop had struck the glass-stone, and I was down on my knees, trying to keep my tallow-pot from spilling into the gutter. The paper of his skirts made a dry, dragging rattle, like someone turning the pages of an enormous ledger in a vault. I looked up, and there was no face—only the white, ruled grid running down his throat, the fine red coordinate lines blurring where the wall-mist had smeared them.

His hands were as long as birch boughs. Two pads of dark slate: one on the bridge of my nose, one on the point of my chin.

He didn't press down. It felt like being measured for an overcoat by an indifferent tailor. There was a tiny vibration behind my cheekbones—a dry, mechanical *hum-m-m* that lasted three pulses—and then he unfolded his knuckles, turned ninety degrees with that awful, smooth hydraulic sweep, and stepped away to check the angle of a lintel thirty paces off.

Since then, the pavement has been soft. 

Not soft like mud—it is still glass-dense feldspar, cold as an iron skillet—but it admits me. When I put my heel down, it sinks an eighth of an inch into the surface before the stone remembers to be solid. I leave no prints, but I can feel the structure under the skin of the floor: the cold, uncombed logic of it, running in straight, dead threads toward the center of the basin.

My coat has lost its weave. If you were to take a thread between your fingers, you could not pull it; the wool has become a single, matte gray surface, like the garments of the Placeholders, though it still holds the shape of the folds where I sat by the smoke three days after the deployment. My hands have no dirt under the nails now, because dirt requires friction to gather, and friction is one of the variables that has stopped updating across my skin.

My voice is the strangest part. I tried to shout yesterday at a Tucker that had wedged itself into the corner of the bunk-slot, drinking the grey void from a hairline fissure. 

I opened my throat, but the sound didn't come from my teeth. It came from thirty yards behind me, out of the dark opening of a drainage scupper down in the lane—a flat, hollow croak that sounded as though it had been spoken into an empty zinc tub and left there until it went cold. The Tucker didn't move. It stayed there, its horn carapace glistening with black grease, its snout buried three microns deep into the unrendered real.

Mostly, I sit where the perspective is crushed. 

The Stragglers stay away from that lane; they think it breaks the mind. But when your edges are already blurred, the compression doesn't pull at your retinas. You learn to lean against the invisible wall that sounds like slate when you knock it. If you sit there with your legs stretched out, the perspective foreshortens your boots until they look like two tiny lead pellets resting a half-mile away, while your knees remain large, greasy, and grey right before your eyes. 

From here, you can watch the moisture hesitate. 

It really does wait. It comes out of the clean, unpainted air like a thought that cannot quite be stated: a drop of water, clear as glass, holding itself four inches out from the architrave. It stays there for five minutes, perfectly round, perfectly still, while the blue-grey noon that never moves pours over it without casting a shadow. 

Sometimes, I reach out and touch it with the tip of my index finger. 

My skin does not break the surface tension. The drop simply recognizes that my finger belongs to the same class of unresolved decimals as itself. It slides sideways onto my nail, cold and greasy, and stays there, wobbling slightly, until the city lets out one of its flat, distant contraction-ticks. 

Then we both settle—just a fraction of an inch—deeper into the stone, waiting for whatever called this place down to remember what comes next, or to turn the light off for good.
