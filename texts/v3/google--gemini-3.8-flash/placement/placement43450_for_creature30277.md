At first the thought drifts toward the rim, because the rim is where the drama is cleanest: that paper-thin knife where thirty-below air touches the rising steam and turns into a crust of hoarfrost. It is tempting to imagine being one of the flyers wedged into a fissure up there, hanging by a claw with rime on the thorax, using the entire Arctic sky as an emergency brake against one’s own blood. There is a romantic vanity in that kind of burning—eating the grease of a forgotten engine, living at forty-two degrees, diving and tumbling.

But no. The flyers are too terrified. They are engines screaming toward their own seizure, and their life is mostly panic disguised as velocity. If you stay with them for more than a few breaths, the exhaustion makes your teeth ache.

So you drop lower, out of the wind. 

Down on the floor, the lime moss is soft enough to drown in, but the noise—the noise is an iron sheet. Millions of seed-sized jaws and wing-covers grating against the jade milk-stone until the skull feels like it is being scrubbed with pumice. To be a runner down there, an earless wedge of wet charcoal sprinting through sound-shadows, means living entirely inside a calculation. You would have no interior life; you would only be an acoustic vector, flinging a split tongue at changes in pitch, then collapsing into an imitation of a pebble every twenty minutes when the floor drops out from under your senses. 

The hollow fern-pipes are darker, cooler, perhaps. But the floating female in the stew of her own shed skin, held by the ribbed green walls like an embryo in a throat—that is too closed, too wet with purpose. It is a nursery that doubles as a digestive tract.

Keep sinking.

Past the fleshy stems. Past the lime frills where the thread-worms wait with their heads bowed, holding their seed like a held breath, praying for the catastrophe of quiet.

Beneath the moss there are the flagstones, cracked and shifted by a thousand centuries of roots and thermal creep. Between the flags run the gutters—shallow, barely an inch deep, where the water does not splash or leap, but moves with the sluggish, syrupy density of brine that has carried too much copper for too long. 

That is where the weight settles. 

I would not be the flyer, and I would not be the runner. I would be down in the gutter, beneath the acoustic roof, where the cicadas’ shriek does not arrive as sound at all, but only as a faint, prickling grit on the surface film of the water.

A crayfish, yes—but not a young one, not one of the ghostly, transparent things whose blue hearts show through their ribs like pilot lights. They are still too hungry; their whip-antennae are still wild with searching, snapping at drifting wings and flakes of dead thorax that fall from the ceiling like burnt foil.

I would be one of the old ones. The ones who have eaten enough of the sulfur-film from the submerged jade roots that the metal has stopped being food and has begun to replace the flesh.

My carpace is no longer calcite. It does not molt anymore; the seams have fused under layers of copper oxide, turned the dull, dusty turquoise of a temple bell pulled from a bog. The eyes were gone generations ago, and the sockets are filled with smooth, lime-colored silt that has hardened into mortar. I do not miss them. To see this depression would be to see only the cage—the quartzite walls rising fifty feet into the grey tundra sky, the three curdled columns that never blink. Without eyes, the world is not a shape; it is a gradient of salt and an oscillation of heat.

My legs move seldom. When they do, the joints don't click; they grind, heavy and deliberate, scraping against the basalt flags. I do not hunt the cicadas that drop; let the translucent young fight over those frantic, greasy husks. 

I stay where the milk-stone plunges into the rock gutter. 

There, below the water level, the machine has an edge that modern tools could never have ground—an interface so precise that the water molecules can barely wedge between the jade and the mountain. And along that seam, the yellow biofilm grows thick as lard. It tastes of dead stars and battery acid. I scrape it away with my spoon-claws, slow as a mason dressing slate, feeling the microscopic vibration of the reactor’s third harmonic buzz through the flat plates of my belly. 

The roar above means nothing to me. Down in the ditch, sound is just a current that ripples the fine hairs on my second antennae. 

Except when the pulse comes.

When the deep core heaves—that sub-audible knot of displaced pressure that travels out from the roots every twenty minutes—everything above goes into an agony of stillness. The flyers bank hard and tumble; the runners freeze into stones; the cicadas choke mid-rattle; the spores shoot upward like blown vapor into the shocking vacuum.

Down here, it is not a silence. It is an arrival.

The pulse hits the gutter like a dull mallet against lead. The copper water lifts a millimeter off the flagstones and settles back down. The current stops running. The electric hum that lives in my shell—the faint, prickling warmth that keeps the verdigris from freezing—flares white and clean for the span of two heartbeats.

In those two seconds, the machine is not leaking; it is speaking. It does not speak to the ferns, which only drink its leakage, or to the runners, which only borrow its warmth. It speaks into the stone, downward, toward whatever sister-device was left sleeping six leagues under the basalt knuckle of Mount Vraun when the sky was still a different color.

For those two seconds, through the metal plates of my chest and the calcified telson braced against the gutter’s seam, I am part of the transmission. I am the washer between the bolt and the nut. The ancient, impossible signal goes through my carapace, through the copper salts deposited in my sinew, through the verdigris crust on my claws, and into the dark root of the world.

Then the click returns. A single clatter high on the milk-stone. The roar comes down like a sluice gate opening. The runners heave their slick sides and bolt for the sound-shadows. The flyers shake the frost from their wings and dive for the hot oil.

I do not move. 

The current resumes its crawl over my back, tasting faintly of ozone and rain that fell an hour ago in the middle of a gale I will never feel. My spoon-claw reaches out, finds the yellow fat on the seam, and pares away another ribbon. 

Slowly, without haste, I am becoming stone that remembers how to breathe; and when the breathing stops, fifty years or a hundred years from now, there will be no difference at all between the machine’s foot and me. I will sit in the copper water, green and hard and heavy, listening to the mountain ring every twenty minutes until the fuel runs dry and the ice comes back to claim the bowl.
