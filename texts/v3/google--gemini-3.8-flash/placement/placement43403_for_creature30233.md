At first, there is only the contact point: the left zygomatic arch—the shelf of the cheekbone—pressed hard against the cool, sweating flange of a low-pressure discharge valve. 

It is not an uncomfortable posture. It is simply the only way to know what the works are doing two miles to the south, where the great syphon-wells plunge through the floor of the caldera. If I pull away, the world becomes an undifferentiated mash of warm fog and thoracic pounding. But with the bone anchored directly to the iron, the mountain organizes itself. I can feel the three-beat shuffle of the lower pumps; I can feel the minute, gravelly stutter of a silted bearing in the fourth condenser bank; I can feel the long, five-minute heave of the walking beam gathering its weight for the downstroke, like a bull setting its shoulder against an oak fence.

I am small, even among those of us who shelter in the draught-shadows of the cooling towers. My ribs do not arch out as widely as my mother’s did, though they are thick enough that when I cross my arms, my elbows cannot touch across my sternum. There is very little spring in my ribs; they have become like the hoops of a wet cask, thick and yellowed, holding the breath in against the pound of the mist.

I do not have a name that could be written, because there is nothing here that dries long enough for ink, and vowels rot in the throat before they reach the teeth. But the family knows the timbre of my rod. It is a nine-inch bolt of hard phosphor bronze, worn oval at the balance point where my thumb rests. When I strike the flange of the third return-pipe twice, wait the space of ten heartbeats, and drive the tip into the lime-rind with a sliding drag, they know it is me down in the crawl-ways beneath the tallow-skimmers. 

They call me—if they must tap it against my collarbone with their knuckles—the *Wedge-Watch*.

I live mostly in the spray-shadow of the Western Manifold, where the heat from the accumulator casings keeps the stones at the temperature of a fresh-killed sheep. It is good stone: coarse, grey sandstone that has drunk enough dissolved iron to turn the color of liver, but flat and relatively free of the black slime. 

My work is not large. If I were large, I would be useless. 

The walking beams do not care about men; they care about balance. When the long spruce laminations begin to drink water through their scarf-joints, they grow heavier on the northern sweep. Left to itself, a water-heavy beam will slip its clearance by half a thumb’s width over forty years, and when a beam slips, the leather packing in the accumulator pits tears like wet paper. 

So I spend the middle hours of the light—when the sky is the color of milk boiled in an iron pot—crawling along the trunnion-shelves with my bag of dry larch-pegs and my tallow-knife. 

I do not look at the sky often. It is too wide, and it gives the back of the neck a cold, loose ache to realize there is nothing above the head for miles except steam that has given up. The iron is better. It has corners. It has the smell of boiled grease and wet slate. 

I watch the clearances. Where the bronze sleeve of the main trunnion meets the pillow-block, there is a gap that ought to be no thicker than the fingernail of an old woman’s thumb. If the grey water sheeting off the cap carries a curl of green grease, I know the brass is scouring. I take a sliver of dried liverwort, wrap it around a wedge of larch-heart, and wait for the five-minute stroke to reach its zenith. 

There is an instant—three heartbeats, perhaps four—when the ten-ton beam hangs at the top of its swing, weightless, undecided, shuddering with the hydraulic back-surge from the lower valves. In that stillness, the sound of the Caldera changes: the low rumble drops half an octave, and the air smells briefly of cold brass and wet hair. 

That is when I drive the wedge. One stroke of the bronze mallet. The larch enters the gap; the liverwort takes up the tallow; the beam groans, chooses its descent, and comes down with the slow, terrifying dignity of a falling cliff. 

The wedge is crushed instantly into a film the thickness of a leaf, but it is enough. The stroke holds true. The spray rises forty feet from the needle-field, warm and sweet, showering my shoulders with orange grit that washes into my beard before it can dry.

I eat the tongue-fungus cold, peeled off the intake casings in long, tough ribbons that require half an hour of grinding between the back teeth. It tastes of zinc and fat, but it makes the gums hard. Sometimes, when the lower flumes are cleared of tallow-clots, one of the younger boys will bring me three or four blind crayfish, still kicking their legs in a gourd of warm runoff. I eat them whole, their shells soft as boiled horn, their bellies sweet with the fine river-silt that settles behind the weir-gates.

My knees are turning orange now. 

It happens to all of us after our fortieth year of the light. The lime-tufa doesn't simply coat the flags; it gets into the skin through the pores, replacing the soft salt of the grease with tiny, crystalline scales of spar. When I sit in my hammock—woven from my grandmother’s hair and the tough, fibrous runners of the black liverwort—my kneecaps do not bend with their old ease. They creak with a dry, internal scrape that I can feel in my molars. 

I know where I will go when the joints finally fuse. 

There is a chamber behind the sixth accumulator pit, where the casing has bulged slightly from the frost of some forgotten winter two centuries ago. The gap between the phosphor-bronze cylinder and the basalt footing is roughly the width of a small man's torso, curled on his side with his chin tucked into his knees. 

Yesterday, while the beam was at its lowest dip, I crept into the sump to clear out the crayfish shells and laid myself in the space to see. 

It is a good fit. 

The stone has been smoothed by eighty years of warm runoff, curved and dished like a saddle. When I drew my knees up and let my shoulder press into the iron flank of the cylinder, the vibration did not hurt; it passed straight through my ribs, through my liver, through the hard, yellow bone of my hips, and into the mountain beneath. I was not shaken; I was held. 

My skin was already the color of the tufa. The tallow-water ran over my hip with a soft, steady drag, neither warm nor cold, exactly the weight of the air when the mist is low. 

When my stroke slips—not the beam’s, but mine—the family will not have to carry me far. They will bring the grease-pot and the long rolls of fibrous liverwort to bind my arms against my thighs so they do not drift into the gears. They will wedge me into that curved bed behind the bronze, and they will pack the spaces around my shins with larch-shavings and mutton-fat. 

Then the great beam will come up, five minutes to the turn, and as it reaches the top, the accumulator will drink its full measure of boiling head. The iron will expand; the bronze will breathe outward by the thickness of a thumb; and the clearance will be closed.

I will stay there, beneath the three-thousand-meter roar, feeling the five-minute heave lift the mountain’s ribs and drop them down again, while the water goes on laundering the grey sky, and the plains below wait for the tub to fill.
