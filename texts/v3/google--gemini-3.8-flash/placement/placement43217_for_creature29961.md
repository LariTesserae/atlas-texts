At first, it is only a question of clearance. 

A fraction of a millimeter—the space between the bottom flange of a twelve-inch cast rail and the shaved timber-shelf beneath it. A space that should not exist according to the elevations in the draughting office, but does exist because the heartwood never yields uniformly. It has hard years and soft years, centuries of drought laid down long before the cloud ceiling formed, and where an iron-dense summer ring crosses the path of a gantry, the rail does not bite. It bridges. It hums with an empty air-gap underneath, thin as an adze-blade.

That is where the cold gathers first. That is where the condensed tallow-water hangs in long, greasy lenses that never break into drops because the surface tension of the resin is too high.

I would not be standing. Upright is an uncalculated posture here; to stand is to present eight square feet of surface to the dry draught that strips the pitch from the knuckles, and to invite the cambium to come up through the pegs of the boots. The Caulkers do it because their joints have already been cured by fifty seasons of kerosene fumes; their knees are essentially scarfed scarf-joints, pegged with gristle that has forgotten how to flex. 

I would be horizontal. Not dead, and not yet grafted, but wedged.

My belly would be to the shelf. The wool of the jacket would have long since taken up the black oil from the gantry-track grease-cups, so that the chest slides with little effort along the line of the fibers—always with the grain, never across it. Across the grain, the nap catches the weave like sharkskin, snagging the threads, demanding twenty pounds of lateral pull for every yard gained. But *with* it, one slips along the run of a single cell-wall, five hundred yards at a stretch, pushed only by the ambient vibration of the four-hundred-foot saws ten miles to the east.

What am I doing there? 

Not caulking. The Caulkers have their long-handled mops and their ash-sole clatter; they work in the dry light of the three-o’clock morning, moving in squads like fence-posts driven by a common hammer. 

My hands are smaller. The skin on the palms is not veneer yet, but it has yellowed at the calluses, turned translucent like the horn spoons they issue with the barley-tallow rations. In the left palm there is an indentation that never leaves: the hexagonal seat of a one-and-a-quarter-inch bronze levelling wedge. 

I am a *Shim-Man*, or something that took over the Shim-Man’s tools when he stopped crawling back to the bunk-lines at the turn of the shift.

The work is not complicated, but it cannot be done from an Och’s cab or from the seat of a gantry crane. The machines are too heavy; they crush the subtle discrepancies they are meant to measure. When an iron rail crosses a node—one of those prehistoric whorls where an ancient branch, three hundred feet in diameter, was torn away by some atmospheric sheer when the world was still vertical—the wood beneath the iron does not compress. It pushes back. The grain bunches, swells into a fist as hard as chilled car-wheel iron, and begins to lift the gantry an eighth of an inch every ten days.

If the gantry lifts, the reciprocating rods bind in their lignum-vitae journals. If the rods bind, the crossheads shatter. 

So someone must be under the timber. Someone whose ribcage can flatten down into the condensation-trough, dragging a leather roll of graduated brass leaves and a light, copper-headed mallet. 

You feel it with the forehead first. You lay the temple directly against the damp grain of the shelf, beneath the cold overhang of the rail-seat. 

Through the bone, the report comes in three voices:

First, the deep, hollow *drub-drub* of the arterial steam lines, three miles down in the subterranean pith-boilers. It has no rhythm you can count; it is a thermal heave, like the stomach of a bull that has eaten wet clover. It lifts the skull half a micron on every stroke.

Second, the shrill, dry chatter of the Slide-Ticks riding the crossheads above. It sounds like someone shuffling a deck of tin cards fifty yards away, repeated twenty times a minute, never varying by a fifth of a second. As long as that chatter stays bright and brassy, the grease is running. When it drops an octave—when it sounds like dry leaves dragged over an emery-wheel—the journal is starving, and you have about four minutes to back out into the aisle before the bronze melts and drops fifty pounds of white-hot slag into the sump.

The third voice is the wood itself. 

It does not speak in creaks. Green wood creaks; dry structural timber groans under snow. This petrified cambium has no air in its tubes. It is under five thousand meters of atmospheric compression and three centuries of mechanical torque. When it yields, it clicks. 

A sharp, brittle *tink*, like an oil-lamp chimney cracking from a cold draught. 

When you hear the *tink*, your fingers already know which leaf to pull from the roll. A three-gauge brass shim, four inches long, notched at the tail so the drift-pin can clear it. You do not drive it home with a swing—there is no room to swing an adze beneath the flanges. You set the copper face of the mallet against the butt of the brass, press your chest against the mallet’s ash handle, and use the downward heave of the Och passing three spans away to hammer it in. 

*Thung-chunk.* The forty-ton rocker hits the shelf. 
The shelf dips three millimeters. 
The mallet drives the shim an eighth of an inch into the gap between iron and heartwood. 

Then the Och lifts its foot; the shelf springs back; the brass is bitten. It will never come out. In forty years, the cambium will have grown through the screw-holes, wrapping the cold metal in a pale, bitter scab of resin until the brass is just another hard inclusion in the ring, like an arrowhead grown over in an oak.

***

After a long time under the rails, the light changes, though the cloud ceiling never lifts. 

It does not become day or night. It becomes *thick*. The sulfur from the leaking steam jackets mixes with the kerosene spray until the air between the ties has the color and weight of cold tallow broth. You do not breathe it so much as your mouth becomes lined with its grease. The tongue tastes exclusively of elderberries that have fermented in a brass pot.

I find that my pockets have filled with things that were not put there.

Small, curled shavings of lignum-vitae that have worked loose from the gantry journals, saturated with castor oil, smooth as sea-pebbles. A handful of lead rivets that have sheered from the steam lagging. And, in the cuff of the left trouser-leg, where the wool has been soaked with bitter wood-water until it is stiff as cardboard, a Gauger.

It is a young one, scarcely eighteen inches long, thin as a stay-busk. It does not try to bite—they have no mouth, only the flat, cartilage segments that match the year-rings of the shelf. It has crawled into the fold of the wool because the wool is warm from my calf, and it stays there, shrinking slightly when I crawl near a copper main, stretching out when I back into the long, drafty reaches of the billet lanes. 

We do not interfere with each other. It measures the heat of my leg; I measure the clearance of the rails. We are both things with long, flat bellies that understand the nap of the black wood and do not wish to be turned over.

There are weeks when I do not see a Caulker’s face. I see only their boots—those great, square-toed baulks of end-grain ash, shod with hoop-iron, coming down with a dead, heavy *slump* five feet from my head as they drag their mops. Sometimes a mop-head—a bundle of hemp line gray with tar and paraffin—will slap across the rail-gap, soaking my cap with cold fluid, but they do not look down to see what took the splash. To them, anything beneath the level of the flange is either floor or packing. 

And they are right.

If you stay down long enough, the difference between the packing and the worker ceases to be an engineering problem. My coat has six leather patches, each one held with copper wire passed through the wool with an awl; where the wire touches the skin of my shoulder, the flesh has not ulcerated. It has turned blue-gray, the color of a copper drift-pin that has been driven into wet larch and left to sit for a winter. 

Sometimes, in the silence between the shifts—that three-minute interval when the subterranean boilers blow down their mud-drums and the push-rods pause at the back-stroke, their great steel crossheads glistening like salted meat—I can feel the cambium tasting the iron in my knees.

It is not an attack. It is simply that this world does not permit an empty tolerance. 

A gap must be filled. If it is not filled with a brass leaf, it will be filled with a Gauger; if not with a Gauger, then with the cold, gray resin that weeps from the severed cell-heads. 

I take the mallet. I place the three-gauge shim into the dark between the tie and the grain. I wait for the Och to make its eight-foot stride, listening for the mercury pendulums to swing through their oil, waiting for the weight of forty tons of larch and iron to come down upon the sky-line and give me the fraction of an inch I need to drive the metal home. 

I am where the friction is measured. 
I am the thickness of three sheets of paper between the machine and the bottom of the world.
