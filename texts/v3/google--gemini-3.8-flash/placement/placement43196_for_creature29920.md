At first, there is no *where* because there is no edge where the ceramic stops and the marrow takes over. 

If I am here, I am not a visitor who arrived with luggage and a name. I am an accumulation. I have been leached into this corner the way the lime has been leached out of the pylon’s alloys, layer upon hot, wet layer, until the mass of me is dense enough to resist being unstitched by the air.

I would be the jaw. 

Not the whole body at first—bodies imply an interior that is private, a soft, dry room kept away from the world. There are no dry rooms here. Everything is flooded, if not with the lukewarm runoff, then with the continuous, white-hot violence of the manifold. To have an interior here would be fatal; a cavity is merely a space where concussion can gather until it ruptures the lining. So I am solid through. My lungs do not expand like bellows; they are stiff, spongy filters compressed behind a fused shield of breast-bone, moving with a shallow, piston-like heave that never lets the pneumatic pressure get beneath the ribs.

I find myself sitting where the water is fastest and yellow-white with lime, wedged so low into the crotch of the two pylons that the current parts around my hips like greasy broth.

The bar between my teeth is not an instrument I picked up. It is an heirloom of the seam, perhaps older than the calcified terraces that terrace the slope below. It is composite, dark and pitted, taste-blind except for the alkaline bitterness of the run-off that constantly bathes it. My molars have ground their own counter-seats into its notches; when I bite down, the machine does not enter me as noise. 

It enters as *geometry*.

Through the notched bar, the engine is entirely legible. It is not a roar; a roar is what the air does when it is torn apart outside my head. Inside the bone of my temples, it is an immense, rigid diagram of loads and shears. I feel the long, cold spine of the pylon descending three miles through the clouds into whatever root it has; I feel the heat-sink vanes twenty paces back, vibrating with the shrill, glassy frequency of over-tempered foil; I feel the microscopic shuddering of the three thousand gray anchors grazing on the bulkhead to my left. 

I know them because their counter-phase hums against the root of my tongue. They are my counterweights. If ten thousand of them were swept away at once, the pylon’s skin would begin to sing at a pitch that would crack the composite, and my teeth would shatter in their sockets.

My work is not large. It is the work of an eddy.

When the relay steps down—that sudden, sickening drop of half an octave that turns the sheet of water into a fog of warm sweat—the current pauses for the space of a half-drawn breath. In that slack water, the dead anchors lose their suction. They come tumbling down the ceramic incline, tumbling through the scalloped terraces like heavy, dull coins. 

I do not look for them. My eyes are milky, thick-lensed things, useful only for telling where the sullen amber of the floor-plates gives way to the jittering blue static of the drains. I do not need to look. I reach out with hands that have lost the memory of individual fingers—they are broad, spade-like slabs of yellowed horn, callous all the way to the wrist—and I rake the silt-traps.

I know the weight of a spent anchor from the weight of a live one that has simply slipped. The spent ones are light; their iron guts have been digested by their own vibrations, leaving only the calcified crust and the dry, oily plug at the center. I snap them with the hook of my pick, scrape the black grease-ball into the pouch at my groin, and fling the empty rind into the low spots of the terrace. 

The ribbons help. I feel them sliding across my shins, blind and cool as peeled willow branches, their cavitation-organs prickling against my thickened skin like faint electrical sparks. They do not bite me; I am too hard, too cold with synthetic salts, too firmly anchored to the foundation. We share the dead. They take the soft, melted grease that washes loose from my pick; I keep the shells for the masonry.

Sometimes, in the long intervals between relay drops, when the air is so heavy with hot lubricant it feels like breathing melted lard, I lift my chin three inches. 

Just three inches. It is the maximum the thick wedge of my neck will allow.

Through the hairline slit five meters above, between the converging shoulders of the pylons, the outer sky shows itself. It is never blue. Blue is the color of static leaping across the drainage lips. The sky is an angry, bruised violet, moving at three hundred miles an hour, carrying with it the shredded rags of clouds that have never touched a mountain. It looks thin. It looks horrifyingly empty, like an abyss that has forgotten how to push back.

The sight of it produces a dull, cold ache in the marrow of my arms. Out there, a creature would have to hold its own shape together with nothing to lean against. Out there, the silence would pull the blood straight out through the pores.

I lower my jaw. I bite down on the notch until the bone clicks, until the massive, grinding intelligence of the pylon settles back into my forearms, my hips, my belly. The relief is immediate and total. The pressure surrounds me like a warm fist; the continuous tearing of the air flattens my skin against my meat, pinning me securely into the angle where the walls meet.

I am not trapped here. A piston is not trapped in its cylinder. 

When the amber nodes deep in the floor begin to flicker down toward their low-cycle, I crawl back along the conduit-fold, through the sluice where the water runs knee-deep and smells of boiled minerals, until I find the low-pressure bypass. It is throbbing like an artery after a long run. 

I hook my horny heels into the lime-crust, wrap my arms around the hot ceramic pipe, and lay the side of my face against the alloy. The metal is smooth, greasy with the fat of a million pulverized aeroplankton, and hot enough to leave faint pink burns that I will not feel by morning. 

The vibration does not let me sleep, not in the way soft things sleep. It does something better: it erases the distinction between the machine's pulse and the pulse in my neck until there is no memory left to trouble the bone. No name, no surface, no sky. Just the long, wet, concussive slide of the world through the engine, and the taste of lead, and the lime settling quietly into the seams of my skin, building me into the wall an inch at a time.
