At first, there is only the measurement of a gap. 

Not a height or a length, but a thickness: the distance between two smooth, sheared plates of gray shale that have not yet had time to settle back together. It is a clearance of perhaps four-tenths of a millimeter. To exist here is not to inhabit a room, but to occupy an interface, like grease trapped between two ground surfaces of a valve-chest.

The first thing that becomes clear is that I am not round. There is no room in this world for roundness, except in the eggs glued to the boiling iron above, and they are privileged by their location. To be round down here would mean being crushed when the pump takes up its stroke and the bedding-stone shivers. 

I am flat. So flat that the blood—if it is blood, and not a pale, grease-thickened lymph that smells faintly of kerosene—must circulate without a heart, driven purely by the rhythmic, hydraulic pulsing of the shale itself. When the eight-ton piston above hits the cushion of its cylinder head, the siltstone compresses by some imperceptible fraction of a hair’s breadth; that compression is my pulse. It nudges my sides. It moves the fluid along my margins.

My back does not carry scrap metal. That is the labor of the broad ones, the carters, the ones whose plates click like shaken bone out in the amber light. I feel them through the stone: a vast, warm pavement of heavy bodies, grinding their hematite shields against one another, busy with their carpentry of swarf and saliva. 

I am not one of them. I do not have their mineral hunger. The thought of swallowing iron filings—of packing the gut with red rust until the skin hardens into ironplate—feels suffocating, an unnecessary weight. Why bolt iron to your flesh when the iron above will outlast you anyway?

No, my skin is thin. It feels like paper that has spent three months soaked in lard. It is parchment-colored, slick, and nearly without sensation, except along the edges.

Along the edges, there is too much to feel.

There are limbs, but they do not all do the same work. Four on the left, four on the right—short, flattened outriggers tipped with blunt, spade-like claws that can find purchase in the microscopic tooth of a fresh fracture. They do not walk so much as wedge. With them, I can pull myself deeper into the dark, back where the seam narrows until even the gray light of the amber work-bulb cannot cast a penumbra, back where the siltstone pinches down to a hairline.

And then there is the fifth pair.

They are not for walking. When I extend them, they unfold like jointed fishing rods, three times the length of my torso, so thin they cannot support their own weight in dry air. But the air here is not dry; it is soup. It is ninety-eight percent humidity, thick with suspended tallow and the sweet, hot steam that smells of dead ferns and wet brass. In this soup, the long whips float. They have no claws at the tips, only three fine, curved hairs that can taste the difference between pure water and water that has run over a rusted nut.

I push them forward, out of the cold knife-slit, toward the noise.

The noise is not a sound. The sound is eaten by the grease. The noise is a warmth, a heavy, rhythmic heat that radiates from the iron ceiling and the slow, living shingle of the gutters. 

One of my whips finds a margin. 

It is the lateral gutter of a mature worker, an elder whose dorsal plate is encrusted with the spiral turning of a brass bushing. The louse is stationary, pinned beneath two others, its leaf-gills fluttering in the warm, descending draft. It is choked. I can taste it through the hairs of my whip: the iron salts have precipitated too quickly around its third coxal joint, cementing the limb to the flank with a brittle, orange crust. It cannot tilt its gutter to catch the condensate; it is drying out from below, even as water weeps two inches above its head.

I do not crawl out to it. To go out under that work-bulb, into that amber, dry honesty, would be to invite the weight of thirty moving carapaces. I would be pressed into the slime like a fallen moth.

Instead, I draw myself forward only until my mouthparts clear the lip of the rock.

The mouth is not a rasp. It has no calcified teeth for quarrying the bedrock. It is a set of needles, fine as glass hairs, mounted on twin pedipalps that can articulate with the precision of a watchmaker’s tweezers. 

With the whip-leg, I tap the jammed joint—once, twice, in time with the engine’s stroke.

The louse knows the cadence. It does not flinch; it does not try to roll into an iron ball. It simply relaxes the tension in its ventral muscles, letting its heavy, swarf-decked shield lift by the width of a fingernail.

I work in that millimeter of clearance. 

My pedipalps find the orange scab. The iron here is soft, fresh from the condensate, crumbly as dried blood. Grain by grain, I shear the hematite away from the horn beneath, working the needle-tips into the hinge, lifting the scales of rust until the limb clicks free and kicks twice in the damp air.

And for this, the tax is paid.

From beneath the freed plate, a tiny, viscous droplet wells up—not water, not blood, but the rich, emulsified fat the louse has drawn from the tallow weeping through the pump flange, seasoned with the bitter copper-salts it scraped from the *11/16* wrench. It is hot. It tastes of forty years of machine-oil and ancient, compressed swamp.

I take it into the capillary groove between my jaws. It does not go to a stomach; it spreads through the paper-thin spaces between my organs, replenishing the slick film that keeps my skin from sticking to the slate.

The louse tilts its back. The brass shaving it wears catches the distant amber light for an instant, flashing like a submerged coin. Then it moves forward into the laminate, sliding under its neighbors, taking its place in the living gutter that leads down to the grubs in the fuse.

I pull my whips back into the dark.

Here, in the back-crevice, the stone is still cold. It has not yet learned the temperature of steam. It remembers only the weight of five hundred feet of overburden and the long, silent pressure of the mud before the iron men came and planted their pumps upon the world. 

I curl the long whips against my flanks, folding their forty joints like carpenter’s rules. 

Overhead, the stroke-rod descends. *Clack-whump.* The rock pinches. The drop falls: *tock*. 

I flatten myself against the gray laminae, feeling the machine's heart pump my blood, entirely content with the narrowness of things, waiting for the next joint to seize.
