I would not be on the stone. 

The stone belongs to those who need an edge—the Weavers who hitch their ribs to the alloy, or the Girth-Cattle whose wide, yellow-pegged tongues need something flat to scrape against. The rock is too hot; it has the persistent, demanding fever of an engine that refuses to cool. To stay on the face of the needle is to be in constant negotiation with the hum, answering it foot by foot, scar by scar.

I would be out in the sag.

Between the spire called *The Thumb* and the sister-needle two miles south—the one the Weavers call *The Unboiled*, because its veins run slightly cooler, the color of wet lead—there is a cable. It is not one of the footpaths. It is four hundred strands of the boiled wire-hair braided together until it is as thick around as the belly of an ox, dipped once in fig-pitch and left to swing. It droops six hundred paces at its center, bowing down toward the white floor of the Suet before rising again to bite into the gabbro horn across the gulf.

I would be the weight at the dip.

Not an animal that runs along it, and certainly not something that flies. To fly in this soup is to be a Slick-skin: perpetually half-frozen at the lip, grease-bellied, living on the garbage of strangers. 

I would be a *Sinker*. 

My body is mostly gut, water-ballast, and four short, unjointed limbs that have curved into solid horns of gray cartilage, shaped like adze-blades. They do not open and shut; they grow around the braid. When I was small—no larger than one of the Gristle’s dropped seeds—I was pushed onto the line by the older ones, my soft chest-plates fitted into the weave while the hair was still sticky with sap. The cable grew through my armpits. The braid has long since swallowed my ribs, and my hide—thick as three fingers of salt-cured lard, blue-gray and hairless—has calloused around the golden hemp until we are the same substance. If you were to cut the line, you would find my bone in the marrow of it: a dark, porous core of iron-soaked calcite that does not break, only bends with the groaning of the needles.

I hang upside-down, back to the sky, belly toward the curdled cloud-floor. 

The hair-line is a gutter. Because it is the lowest curve in two miles of sky, all the water that condenses on the upper leagues of the rope runs down toward me. It comes in a steady, scalding sluice along the braid: alkaline sweat from the rock, bitter brown grease from the fig-bark, and the dead ticks that lose their footing during the noon-scald and roll down the fibers like lead shot. 

My mouth is not for talking. It is an open, lipless gutter at the base of my throat, permanently unclasped against the belly of the cable. The drainage runs into me. My liver is enormous, cold, and slow; it turns the mineral brine into a thick, pale grease that I exude through the plates of my flanks, keeping the hair-rope from drying out and snapping when the nine-day breath pulls the atmosphere tight. 

I have no eyes. What is there to see? A league of yellow grease in the morning; a league of bruised liver at dusk. Sight is for the gliders who have to worry about where the cold begins. 

Instead, my skull is an anvil. The lower jaw is fused directly into the cable-clasp, so that every vibration that travels down the hair passes through my inner ear—not through a drum, but through three heavy pebbles of native bismuth that float in a pocket of oil behind my brain.

I know the Thumb better than the Weavers do. They know the skin; I know the teeth. 

When the Thumb leans an inch to the west, pulled by the slow thermodynamic knot in the deep crust, the braid hums high and thin, like a wire drawn through grease. When the Unboiled settles on its root, the cable slackens and rumbles in the pit of my gullet—a sound like a heavy stone dragged through butter. My mass—six hundred pounds of water, bone, and tallow—is what keeps them from tearing each other’s faces off. I am the damping-lump. When the needles begin to chatter in fourths—that dangerous, ragged vibration that makes the liverwort slough off the cliffs in thirty-ton carpets—my body drinks the shudder. I shiver it into heat. I boil the rain as it runs over my ribs, turning it into white plumes of steam that rise from my haunches, so that from the high ledges the Weavers look down through the mist and say: *The knot is sweating; the line will hold.*

Sometimes, during the third day of the breath, when the air is so heavy it feels like warm lard pressed against the nostrils, a Weaver will come down the braid. 

They do not come for me. They do not touch my head. They move with that hitching, spider-like grease-stride, their thirteen-inch fingers picking at the weave to clear away the sulfur-scabs. When they reach my flank, they pause. I feel the light, double-click of their knuckles on my hip-horn—not a greeting, but the way a cooper raps an iron hoop to see if the wood has swollen true. 

They bring little cakes made of tick-wax and crushed bismuth-shells, and they wedge them into the loose skin behind my neck. The parasites eat them; I don't care. In return, they take their horn scrapers and peel the gray blubber-crust from my thighs, collecting the sweet, yellow tallow that my pores secrete to grease their hair-spindles. 

We do not speak. They smell of dry twigs and boiled fig-leaf; I smell of the gutter and old bone. They are the kite; I am the stone.

The only time the weight leaves me is when the Gristle flowers.

It begins miles down, in the white Suet that no Weaver has ever reached through. A day before the bladder rises, the hair-cable goes dead. The hum stops—not because the needles have ceased their seething, but because the gas-pressure from the low abyss has risen so high that the air between the spires becomes buoyant as salt-brine. My four hundred pounds of bone begin to float. The cable straightens. The sag lifts.

I feel myself rising on the braid, hoisted toward the yellow canopy by the great, curdled breath of the lung below. My limbs, locked around the fibers for forty years, ease their pinch. 

And then the flower comes by.

It does not hit the cable; it is too vast, drifting between the needles like a pale, half-lit moon of skin. But it passes close enough that the hot cabbage-stench of it fills the gutter of my throat. The faint green phosphorescence soaks through my skin, lighting the veins in my chest until I can see them through my own gray lard—glowing like moss on a wet cellar-step.

When it pops, the air does not hit me as a sound. It hits me as a *drop*. 

The buoyancy fails in an instant. The cable snaps downward with the violence of a released bow-string. My ribs bite back into the braid; the bismuth-stones in my skull clatter against my brain with a dry, metallic ring like coins dropped into an iron tub. 

A seed hits my flank—wet, heavy, soft as a boiled sheep's eye. 

I do not eat it. I have no teeth to break it, and my throat is locked to the hemp. I simply let it rest against the hollow of my groin, where the hair is thickest and the tallow runs hot. 

Over the days that follow, while the Weavers sleep in their straps and the mountains clear their throats, the seed begins to dissolve. It does not grow; it melts into my grease. It tastes of cold cellar-floors and a sharp, metallic iron that is not like the iron of the seams—an iron that has never been hot, an iron that has known frost.

The seed goes into the braid through my horns. The hair drinks it. The Thumb drinks it through the hair. 

For three weeks, the cable sings in an octave so low that only the Girth-Cattle can hear it, their four-toed feet clamped to the bark, listening to the only thing in this whole, wet, boiling sky that remembers the taste of snow.
