I would not be moving. 

To skate across the slab—to kick out with the broad, spade-fused pads and plane with the grease-tide—requires a faith in distance that I do not have. Distance here is only more of the same cold iron-wash, an inch deep, running over an inch of rock, sliding toward a rim that simply repeats the fall. 

If you look for me, you will have to look where the current catches. You will find me at the throat of a weir, wedged where the two dry-laid stone arms narrow to a span no wider than a man’s ribs. 

I am not the hunter who tracks the wakes, nor am I the corpse sliding south-southwest with the drift. I am the *Wedge-Sitter*. 

My thighs are locked into a permanent, split-knee squat against the wet gneiss, my back humped low beneath the bruised wool of the clouds. The water hits the broad slab of my belly-apron—stiff, grease-cured skin peeled from an elder filter-feeder—and divides, foaming in two pale horns around my hips before spilling through the gap. Because I do not rise, the felt has begun to understand the seams of my boots. It has climbed the heel-leathers, blackish-olive and cool as curd, gluing the boiled stomach-hide to the bedrock with its millions of calcifying mouths. By the turn of the next cold swell, I will have to take the chert chisel and pare myself free of the floor again, shaving off curls of dead leather and rubbery slime before the colonial rot finds the living fat of my ankles. 

My knuckles rest in the thumb-deep runnel between my shins. They are calloused into round, horn-capped knobs, iron-stained to the color of wet slag. When the plate-skimmers come funneling into the weir—clattering against one another in a panic of lead-grey discs, their jointed oars shrieking against the gneiss—they do not break around me; they strike me. They hit my shins and the flats of my thighs like tossed flagstones. 

I do not use a gaff. With my forearms plunged into the freezing film, I reach into the churn, feel for the soft, gill-fringed margin beneath the dorsal dome, and flip them. 

Turned belly-up, their lead armor is useless. Their pale oars thrash the sluggish air, batting against my forearms with the dry, harmless rattle of dry reeds, while the current pins them against the weir-stones. I crack their liver-plates with a hammer-stone held in the palm of my left hand—one dull, wet *tock* that does not carry through the air, but shudders straight down into the slab. 

Behind me, in the eddy of dead-water my body creates, the livers float like yellow dumplings. The children of the rib-hut come skating out on their knees to ladle them into bladder-skins before the grease dissolves into the sheet-flow.

They do not look at my face. I do not look at theirs. 

My eyes are mostly tallow now, clouded over by sixty seasons of the bacterial milk that rises off the flats. I see the world only as shifting weights of pewter and wet grease. But I do not need the milky eyes. 

Beneath my left knee, directly under the foundation-stone of the weir’s eastern arm, there is a borehole. 

It is four inches across, round as a drilled coin, plumbed into the bowels of the iron-gneiss before the sea dried into this pan. Down in the dark of it, drowned in stagnant, iron-choked brine, a bull-worm lives. It is as thick as my calf, ribbed with rings of yellow cartilage, blind and heavy. 

I keep my left knee-cap pressed flat to the lip of that hole. 

I know the creature better than I know the people whose ribs smoke with the grease I catch. When the melt-swell gathers hundreds of miles to the north—when some unimaginable shelf of black ice calms and slumps into the sheet—the worm feels the hydraulic heave long before the current deepens by a thumbnail. It begins its work. 

*Thung. Thung. Thung.*

Its horn tail-plug strikes the borehole wall. The strike travels through the rock, through the three-inch callous on my knee, up the femur, and settles directly into the hinge of my jaw. 

It is a dull, oily ache. When it starts, I do not shout; the bitumen wads in my ears are pounded tight, sealed with mutton-tallow and river-grit, keeping out the screaming metal friction of the millions. Instead, I wait for the hunters to return from the runs. 

When the first one slides in, his knuckle-irons throwing up wings of silver spray, he drops before me. He does not take off his boots. He crawls into the churn between my thighs, reaches up with both hands to grip the horn-spurs of my collarbone, and pulls his forehead down against mine. 

The collision is hard—bone meeting bone through skin like wet leather. 

I grind my teeth. I let the vibration of the borehole pour out of my frontal sinus and into his. I give him the worm’s strike: *three pulses, a pause, five pulses.* The water is rising. The brine-spouts are uncoupling twenty miles out. The thong-hounds will be running high in the film, hungry and loose from the runnels. 

He feels it. I feel his brow buzz back his acknowledgment—a short, sour grating of his molars that tastes like copper on my tongue—and then he pushes off, his skate-pads kicking white water as he wheels around to warn the rib-shelters.

When they are gone, the quiet of the noise returns. 

The scream of the carapaces never stops. It is the skin of this world, stretched tight over the stone. Sometimes, a thong-hound, chasing the scent of the broken skimmer-livers behind me, will slide over my foot. It feels like eight feet of cold, wet rope, muscular and blind, its belly-claws rasping against the greasy hide of my boot. It does not bite. I am too large, too still, too much like the weir itself. It flows through the stones and is gone, whipped away by the current toward the south-southwest.

One day, the worm will not strike when the heave comes. Or my knee will soften, and the felt will drink through the hide and find the yellow lard beneath my shins, cementing me to the pan so deeply that the chisel cannot find the line between the stone and the meat. 

When that happens, I will not lean forward into the skull-touch. 

I will simply unwedge my shoulders from the weir-stones. I will take the bitumen plugs from my ears and let the sound of the plates—that continental shriek of horn on iron—pour into my head all at once, until the brain inside the thick bone rattles loose and turns to broth. 

Then the water will take the grease-apron. It will lift me out of the throat, roll me twice in the thumb-deep rill, and I will skate without legs, smooth and flat, down the long greased floor where everything goes, until the skimmers find the soft parts under my chin, and the bull-worms drum me down into the dark. 

Until then, the current hits my chest. It is cold. It never runs dry. And beneath my knee, the stone is beating like an iron heart.
