At first, it feels like I might be nothing more than the water’s tension—that paper-thin skin holding the saucer together on the flagstone, trembling when the cart rolls three streets over, but refusing to tear. 

For a long time in this kind of quiet, you don’t need a pulse to feel present. You can exist simply as the cool weight resting in a chisel-trough, or the greasy film left by the salt-mites where the tuff has been planed flat. You could be the dry space beneath the undercut slab, where the smell of crushed dock and cold skin gathers like a cellar draft.

Or perhaps higher: wedged into the vertical seam where three blocks meet, flattened until your ribs are just a pattern of wicker pressed against the raw tooth-marks of the bone chisels, white and translucent, watching the milky gray slot of the sky without blinking because there is no sun to tell you when to close your eyes.

No. That is too thin. There is no warmth in being a gecko, and the air here, though dead, is not cold; it is the thick, breath-soaked warmth of an unvented well. 

If I move, it is not with the nervous, dry-seed skitter of the skinks. I am too heavy for their sudden darts, and I do not have the patience of the mantis, hanging like an unswung pendulum waiting for a midge to touch the water. 

I feel myself lower down, where the water has weight and a bottom. 

I am under the central flag, in the shallow dark of the sump where the drainage slots spit their fine, measured trickles. 

When the light shifts above—not because of the sun, but because the fog has grown heavier over the city’s upper terraces—the water in the sump cools by half a degree. That is morning. Or evening. It doesn't matter which; they have the same weight here. 

I come out through the gap where the corner of the center stone was chipped during the laying—a small triangular notch, no wider than three fingers, sharp as flint. My shell scrapes it, a sound that doesn't travel three inches before the porous tuff swallows it down. The shell is broad, flat, and wet, smoothed down by years of gliding across masonry that has never seen a wheel or a sandal. It is the exact slate-black of the floor, so close to the tuff in grain and density that when I pull my legs into the margins, I am merely another bevel, an intentional irregularity left by the stone-cutter.

I push forward across the bare expanse. 

My belly-plate—the plastron—slides on the microfilm of condensation with a dull, wet slip. The stone is immaculate. There is no grit here, only the microscopic drift of the mites’ grease and the invisible fleece of algae. My legs, splayed wide like paddle-blades, leave small triangular wakes that seal themselves behind me. 

Ahead of me, near the aroid cluster where the green leaves sweat their periodic, heavy drops, she is sitting. 

I know her not by her face, which stays turned toward the oblong slot above, but by her heels. They are cracked, wide, and yellowed like boiled horn, settled squarely into the shallow film without disturbing it. Her ankles smell of the bitter dock sap she has been scraping from the T-joint with her copper leaf. 

She doesn’t look down, but the vibration of my shell sliding over the seam between the second and third flags reaches her through the stone. 

Her tongue strikes the roof of her mouth: *kck*. 

A dry, hollow sound, like two peach stones tapped together under an apron. 

It does not echo. It simply enters the tuff and stops. But through the water, the shockwave travels clean and sharp against the side of my neck. 

I reach the edge of her foot. I stop where the skin of her instep rises out of the water like a pale, mud-washed boulder. Here, the condensation is warm, heated by the dull, persistent furnace of her blood. 

Her hand comes down through the dead air, unhurried, drifting with the same languid swing as the sparrow feather that fell three days ago. Between her two middle fingers, she holds a damp pellet—crushed moth velvet and the bright green scum she skimmed from the southern drain-slot, rolled tight between her slate slates. 

I extend my snout. The air smells of her: tallow, wet rock, the peppery bite of root crowns, and that ancient, shut-in smell of linen that has never dried in a breeze. 

I take the pellet. The moth wings are powdery and sweet; the algae is cold and tastes of iron. 

When it is gone, she does not pull her hand away. Her fingers, stained the deep viridian of crushed dock leaves at the nails, rest beside my flank on the wet flagstone. One of them nudges the margin of my carapace, testing the rim, feeling the tiny notches where the stone-scrapers marked me when I was small. 

Above us, the high condensation reaches its weight. 

*Plip.* 

A fat drop strikes the broad heart of an aroid leaf three paces away. The leaf bobs, springs back, and sheds the bead into the drainage slot with a soft, subterranean suck. 

Then another. *Plip, plik.* 

The rhythmic percussion begins, measured and mindless, washing the greasepaper light down the sheer walls. 

We do not move while it rains. I rest my chin on the flat of her great toe, where the skin is thickest, feeling through her bone the faint, rhythmic shudder of the wheat-wains turning a corner far up in the sunlit street—a world that forgot to finish its basement, leaving us down here to keep the floor level, the water measured, and the stone from growing together in the dark.
