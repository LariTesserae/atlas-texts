At first, there is no reason to be an animal. 

In a trough like this, where the floor is five miles of fresh fracture and the air is thick as blood in a throat, consciousness does not need to begin with limbs. It could pool anywhere: in the lukewarm skim of moisture that coats the underhang of a basalt lip; in the petrifying marrow of a thighbone peg as the iron-heavy water turns its spongy canals into solid red jasper; or in the alkaline grease that the scale-mats leave behind, steaming faintly as the drizzle hits it.

To exist here is, primarily, to be pressed. 

The sound does that before anything touches you. It is not something you hear with an ear; it is an atmospheric mass that leans its broad chest against your surfaces. If you were a fern, your sheet-metal fronds would vibrate at three thousand cycles a second until the rivets of your stem sheared off. If you were a pocket of steam caught in a crevice, you would churn like water in an iron kettle.

So you cannot be soft, and you cannot be still.

For a long time, the impulse is to be the tallow. To be the thick, yellow, semi-coagulated layer of fat that an old ribbed-hide carries between its canvas hide and its heavy, barrel-ribs. It is an honest condition: thousands of razor-flakes of black glass worked into your substance over twenty seasons of dragging across the floor, until you are no longer quite flesh, but a dense, greasy terrazzo that stops the bleeding before it can find the dark. In the tallow, there is no panic. There is only the slow migration of the shards inward, smoothed over by animal heat, polished by the sliding of muscle against gristle, until every cutting edge is swaddled in lard.

Yet the tallow has no agency. It only receives the drop; it only cushions the butcher’s knee when the cleaver comes down. 

No, to find a footing here, one must be higher than the floor, but lower than the canopy where the high-altitude ribs shut out the sky. One must live in the *mid-scarp*—that violent, vertical boundary where the falling stone has not yet reached its terminal heap, and the rising sap has not yet gone soft with wood.

***

I find myself on a shelf no wider than three forearm-lengths. 

I am not young. Youth in this cleft is a thin, translucent matter—too much pulse, too little hide, the ribs showing like green osiers through skin that hasn't had the time to thicken with grease. To be young here is to be meat that hurries.

I am heavy in the shoulder, narrow in the flank, and my center of gravity sits low, right where the split-pelvis joins the knot of the lumbar spine. When I crouch, my belly does not clear the stone by more than a finger’s breadth; it rests against the basalt, tasting the temperature of the scarp through a slab of callused rind that has grown four inches deep over my chest.

My eyes are gone. Not torn out—simply overgrown, drowned long ago beneath the thick, grey hood of the brow-casing. Where they were, there are only two shallow, hairless ditches filled with tallow-plugs to keep the rock-dust from festering in the sockets. I do not miss them. Light here is only a nuisance anyway, an oily violet smear that signals that something is being ripped apart too close to you. 

Instead, my world has five dimensions, and they all live in the sinus.

Behind the great, projecting shelf of bone that overhangs my snout, the skull is not solid. It is an open, cavernous labyrinth of thin, paper-like bone-leaves, curled like dried tobacco, through which the steam of the trough is pulled with every heave of my ribs. 

When I draw the wet air inward, it does not merely smell; it *strikes*. 

The cold, mineral flint of the scarp tastes like iron on the front teeth. Two hundred yards down, where a pool of pelt-crab spawn has just burst in a pocket of warm sludge, the scent is thin, sharp, and sour as turned milk. Further still—down where the floor disappears into the black avalanche—there is the heavy, sweet, lardy stench of an old bull hide dying under a deadfall: the fat has split, the gut-flora have hit the sulfur-drizzle, and the scale-mats are already laying their cold, wet bellies against his steaming hams.

Through the sinus, I can smell the weight of a stone before it shears. A basalt block that is still bonded to the scarp smells dead, bitter, and cold; but when the micro-fissure begins to run behind it, the air enters the seam. The stone begins to sweat moisture that has been trapped since the rift dropped. It smells like old cistern-brick. When that smell reaches my whiskers, I do not look; I drop my knuckles into the horn-grooves and shift three paces toward the anchor-peg.

***

My hands are not hands. They are tools that have forgotten how to open flat.

The four fingers are short, thick as winter-leeks, tipped with blunt, horn shovels that have grown around the edges of the obsidian splinters driven into them during my second season. The skin of the palm is two inches of laminated leather, yellow as salt-beef, studded with tiny, hard grey kernels of embedded grit. I cannot feel the prick of a thorn; I cannot feel the edge of a fresh flake unless I bear down with my whole weight.

Only the whiskers feel.

They sprout from the heavy, mobile lip of my upper jaw in four paired ranks—twelve on the left, twelve on the right. They are thick as goose-quills at the root, ribbed with black rings of horn, tapering down to tips so fine they can find the grain of a hair in the dark. 

Right now, they are trailing against the wire-runner line that anchors my shelf to the three-ton block above the migration chute.

The line is singing.

To anyone with ears, the rift is only a flat, deafening roar—the sound of an iron foundry falling down a well that has no bottom. But my ear-holes were plugged when my third teeth came in. I took two broad, triangular flakes of black glass, wrapped them in greasy hide, and pushed them deep into the canals until the cartilage snapped around them. 

The avalanche-roar is gone. The world does not shout at me; it *taps*.

Through the soles of my prehensile feet, through the massive, knobbed knuckles resting in the basalt gravel, through the twelve quills touching the braided vine, the rift arrives as rhythm.

*Thump-thump-thump.* That is the hydraulic pulse of the sap-runners thirty feet behind me, prying a crack in the scarp with the slow, terrifying strength of swelling peas.

*Tick-a-tick-a-tick.* The pelt-crabs, three hundred of them, spilling across the obsidian scree below like a bucket of dried beans thrown against a bronze shield. Their stilts are snapping and shedding in the dark; I can taste the lime-dust of their broken plugs drifting up on the heat-plume.

And then, the heavy, double-beat: *HUNG... HUNG... HUNG.*

A matriarch. A great, five-ton ribbed-hide, pushing her way through the chute below, her tallow-coated snout plowing a furrow through the sharp blades, her blind, vacuum-mouth sucking the moss from the rock-heels. She is heavy with young; the heat she throws off is like the mouth of a lime-kiln, rising in a thick, fatty updraft that coats my whiskers in warm oil.

***

I do not move quickly. Quickness is for the floor-runners, the young ones who must dive into the purple light and carve their twenty pounds of flank before the mats lap their ankles.

My work is the *knapping of the tension*.

Between my thighs rests my stone. It is not obsidian; obsidian is for the butcher’s wedge, too light and brittle for the work I do. My stone is an eighty-pound river-ball of grey quartzite, carried up three hundred cubits from the old stream-bed that the rift swallowed when the world split. It is smooth as an egg, slick with my own shoulder-sebum, worn slightly hollow on its under-belly where it sits across my knees.

Beside my left foot is the primary bone-pin.

It is an old elk-femur—not from any beast that walks this trough now, but a fossil-spoil dug from the drift-gravel five rains ago. It is petrified into pale, heavy chert, cold as iron, driven four spans deep into a cleavage-plane of the basalt scarp.

Tied to the pin is the trip-line.

The trip-line does not lead to a deadfall; the young males have their own deadfalls fifty cubits down. My line leads higher—to the *keystone-brace*, a five-yard slab of black basalt that hangs over the entire throat of the narrow pass like an unclosed eyelid. It has hung there since my mother was small; it is held by only three braids of wire-runner, each as thick as my thigh, soaked continuously in the caustic sap of the liver-stalks to keep them from rotting in the greenhouse damp.

My quills are on the braid. 

I do not listen with my mind; I listen with the honeycombed cells of my forehead. 

The vibration is changing. The matriarch has entered the bottleneck. Her canvas sides are scraping both scarps at once; she is making the rock groan with her heave. If she passes, the herd follows, and if the herd follows, they will trample the lower shelf and carry away the hunting lattices like spiderwebs on a wet boot.

My fingers do not hurry. They find the bone wedge that sits beneath the middle braid.

I do not use a mallet. I raise the quartzite egg with both hands, lifting it until the cold river-stone touches the blunt bone of my brow-hood. My lungs fill with the iron-steam. My ribs expand until the tallow-crust on my chest cracks with a dry *snip*.

I bring the egg down.

It does not hit the wood; it hits the butt of the fossil-pin.

The sound does not travel through the air; it travels through the mountain. A single, short, flat *clack* that drives the pin two inches deeper into the glass fissure.

Above me, the eyelid moves.

It does not fall all at once. It shears. The three-ton basalt lip drops three feet, settles on its secondary shelf with a dry, grinding shriek that sets my nasal quills humming like struck wires, and pinches the neck of the chute until it is no wider than the shoulders of a man.

Down in the dark, the matriarch stops.

Her snout hits the fresh stone. Through the braided line, I feel her snort—a violent, subterranean cough of hot tallow and lung-mist that sprays the lower vines with grease. She cannot turn; her ribs are wedged between the basalt scarps. She is anchored. Behind her, the migration piles up: sixty, eighty, a hundred ribbed-hides pressing their blind, blubbery bulk into her flank, their vacuums sucking frantically at the glass floor, their canvas skins squealing against the stone like wet leather under a shoemaker’s knife.

A sharp, three-stroke vibration runs up my guide-vine from below.

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

It is the young ones. They are on the platforms. Their cleavers are already unstrapped from their wrist-thongs. They smell the squeeze.

***

I do not go down to help them butcher.

My knuckles are too thick now to clear the knots of the ascent before the whip-eels drop from the upper rigging. If I went into that purple churn, my tallow would be cold before I could haul forty pounds of muscle up the first scarp.

Instead, I lean back against the basalt wall.

The rock here is lukewarm, vibrating with the continuous, dull tremble of the meat-machine below. I reach into the skin-pocket behind my shoulder, dig out a knob of rancid, smoke-dried amphibian fat that has been mixed with river-silt, and push it between my split front teeth. 

It tastes of sulfur, wet mud, and old iron. I chew slowly, using the flat, spade-like molars at the back of my jaw to grind the grit down until it can be swallowed. 

A young female comes up the runner-line, moving with the ugly, jerky grace of our kind. She is three seasons old; her hide is still grey-pink, unshielded by fat-scars, and her whiskers are short, twitching with the wild, scattered tremor of someone who still thinks the rift has an end.

She has three slabs of flank slung over her back by gut-cords, each meat-block steaming in the drizzle, pale and marbled with yellow suet. She does not offer me meat; we do not give meat away. Meat is weight, and weight is the only thing the glass respects.

Instead, she drops onto all fours before me, lowers her head until the broad, honeycombed hood of her brow knocks gently against my chest-rind—*tock*—and turns the side of her neck toward my mouth.

There is a shard of black glass embedded behind her left ear-cleft. It has been there two days; the skin around it has grown purple and proud, leaking a thin, yellow whey that smells of rot.

I reach out with my callused fingers, steady her jaw with my thumb against her chin-fur, and lean down.

I do not use a knife. I catch the edge of the obsidian flake between my split front incisors. 

The glass is cold against the pulp of my gums. I taste her blood—thin, salty, full of the caustic green tang of the vines she has been climbing. I set my neck-muscles, brace my heels against the basalt shelf, and pull.

The shard comes free with a small, wet *snick*.

She does not flinch. She snorts once through her sinuses, a cloud of hot, sour steam that washes over my face, and then she rises, scrambles over the anchor-peg, and heads up into the wet dark where the family is clustering on the sleeping-cables.

I do not spit the shard out.

I hold it on my tongue for a moment, rolling it against the hard, ridged palate of my mouth. It is three fingers long, sharp as a louse-bite on both edges, thin as a beetle-wing. A clean flake. The drop was good today; the boulder struck the obsidian core at the true angle.

I raise my left foot, find the soft, lard-filled crease where the heel-pad joins the ankle-tendon, and push the shard deep into the tallow until only the blunt heel shows.

The fat closes around it, cold and thick. The pain is small, dull, and far away—a tiny, hard point of iron in a world that has no edges that are soft.

I settle my rump deeper into the scree. 

The avalanche-drone presses against my chest. My twelve quills trail out into the damp, hot fog, catching the first faint, greasy spatter of the rain as it turns to steam on the deadfall below. 

I close my mouth. I breathe through the bone. I wait for the next heave.
