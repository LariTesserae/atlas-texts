The draft comes first, because in a basin like this you cannot locate yourself by vision. The light does not change; it is always the color of cold suet, flat from rim to rim, casting no long shadows to give you an hour or an edge. You locate yourself by the direction the wet moves across your skin.

If the air is rising against your cheek, you are near a condenser vent, where the heat is slight and sour. If it is sliding downward, slick and heavy like a wet rag dragged over zinc, you are under the lip of the ashlar wall, where thirty feet of sodden granite cools whatever drifts over the town from the world above.

At first, there is the temptation to be the stone itself, or the grease: something that has no pulse to confuse with the walking-beam. You could be the tallow inside the glands, squeezed through split packing until you are gray and stringy, or the crust of dried whelk-cast that has turned into the foundation of the middle engine. There is an enormous, cold comfort in that—in being part of the four-millionth stroke of a cylinder that does not know its own diameter.

Or higher up: one of the kites of gristle roosting on the crane-rail, whose eyes are only slits of grey gelatine, whose belly is full of sweet fat and cold flies, whose entire life is the snap of a tendon when an up-draft tilts.

But those things have no names for the weight of an hour. They don't know the difference between three strokes and three thousand. To understand the basin, you have to be something that feels the cold through the sole of its foot and knows it will not end.

So the weight drops lower, down the rungs.

Not all the way to the flagstones—nobody belongs to the flags while they still have grease in their marrow. But four rungs off the wet, where the iron of the ladder is polished by palms until it feels like cold fat, and where the air has that slight, dead suction that pulls at the hems of a stiff twill smock.

I am not the child on the rung with the pin. That is too light; their knees are still pink, and their ears still ring with the high, whistling grease-whistle of the escape valves, the noise that drives them into corners with their fists jammed against their temples before the tallow sets in their heads.

I am past the setting.

The wax behind the drum has gone hard, like the cold fat in the bottom of a rendering skillet. The roar does not arrive through the ear; it lives in the ridge of the collarbone. It enters through the grip on the fourth rung, runs up the radius and ulna, sits behind the bridge of the nose like an old toothache, and then drops into the belly. When *The Weaver* strikes its leather arrestor out in the middle of the yard, the meat between my fourth and fifth ribs gives a small, involuntary jump, the way a horse’s skin shivers when a fly lands on it.

My shoulders are narrow. The twill of the smock has been soaked so many times in hot linseed that it doesn't fold anymore; it creases in white, chalky lines like cardboard. When I bend my elbow, the crook of the sleeve bites the skin.

In my left hand is the scraper: not horn, like the whelks have, but a leaf of discarded spring-steel from a pressure-gauge, ground down on the sill until the edge is square and blunt. In my right is the small wicker basket, lined with greased sheepskin, that hangs from my belt by an iron toggle.

This is the work: between the second floor and the stones, the condensation that bleeds down the brick doesn't run clear. It catches the fly-meal and the dry flakes of oxide that the gulls shake down from the stays, and it forms a yellow, butter-colored paste in the mortar lines. If you leave it, the paste swells; it rots the lime, and after forty years the chimney-stack leans toward the flags by half an inch. A half-inch lean means the drive-shaft to the domestic blower binds in its journals, and the hearth goes out.

So I peel it.

The steel goes in with a small, gritty *shuck* that I don't hear, but feel in the roots of my fingernails. A ribbon of yellow curd comes away, five inches long, curled like a shaving of cold cheese. It drops into the basket. It smells of lake-silt and sulfur, with that faint, sweet under-tang of the dead midges that have rotted in the gutter-boxes.

Above me, four floors up, my sister is likely at the press. I know this because every twenty seconds there is a dull, oily tremor through the black brick that is slightly different from the engine-pulse: the three-inch screw biting into forty pounds of wet flies, forcing the fat through the perforations in the iron drum. Down here, the drops from the I-beam hit the crown of my head—*one, two, three*—striking the greasy rag I have knotted there until the linen is plastered flat against my skull.

My teeth are rough with lime. If I run my tongue behind my lower incisors, there is a hard, sharp shelf of stone there that has grown since I was sixteen; everyone my age has it, a little white weir that tastes of the cistern and makes our tongues thick when we try to shape a word that isn't just an engine-tap.

We don't talk. If I need the salt-pan lowered, I take the heel of the scraper and hit the ladder rung: *tink, tink-tink*.

Two rungs above me, the iron vibrates in response. Not an answer, just the weight of someone stepping onto the platform to haul the coal-basket across to the switch-house.

From here, four feet above the whelk-floor, the basin looks wider than it does from the parapet. You see the bellies of the walking-beams from underneath—huge, rough castings with the foundry marks from eighty years ago still legible under the oil: *VANDERHOOF & SONS, LOW WATER.* They don't look like iron anymore; they look like wet meat, purple-black and sweating, rising and falling with that horrifying, patient indifference that makes you feel that if you put your forehead between the crank and the stop, the engine wouldn't even change its note. It would just take the grease out of your skull, pass it through the whelks, and turn your shin-bones into valve-grind before the cloud-ceiling had moved ten yards to the east.

A whelk is crawling on the third rung, just below my boot.

It is small, no bigger than a walnut, its three horn-plates clicking against the iron with a rapid, dry vibration that feels like a beetle inside a pillbox. It doesn't know I am meat; it thinks the linseed oil on my patten-leather is just another leak from a high-pressure line. It has laid its scrapers against the welt, testing the tallow.

I don't kick it off. It would only drop into the gravel and come back on the next tide. Instead, I wait until the ribbon of yellow grease in my basket is three fingers deep, then I lean my hip against the black brick, reach down with the blunt tip of the gauge-spring, and turn the creature over onto its lead-colored dome.

It lies there for three beats of *The Weaver*, its three grey legs paddling the cold cellar air, blind and greasy, reaching for the machine that made it.

Then I climb two rungs higher, into the sulfur-drip, where the house is still warm from the hearth, and pull the trap shut behind me.
