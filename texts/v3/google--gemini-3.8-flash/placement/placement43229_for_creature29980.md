At first there is only the hum, and it is impossible to say whether it is inside or outside, because there is no skin yet to draw that border.

It is sixty cycles. It is not heard; it is the rate at which the cold iron shivers against the slate, which means it is the rate at which everything else—the frost, the condensed water, the heavy amber drop hanging from the lip of the flange—is shaken. To exist here is simply to have that frequency pass through you without finding any slack to rattle. Everything is under tension. The steel is pulled taut by the miles of cable; the water is pulled taut by the drop; the slate is pulled taut by the mountain’s unfinished heave. 

If I look for where I am, I do not find an eye opening. I find a seam.

It is high—not at the ridge-line where the rock splits the cirrus into dry ribbons, but down where the apparatus begins to overhang the void, beneath the third tier of catwalks where the spray from the upper penstock has drifted so long it has forgotten the violence of the nozzle and settled into an even, pressurized vapor. 

There is an operator’s pulpit here. It overhangs the eight-mile gap on two arched steel corbels, painted in that leaden, non-committal gray. Its windows are plate glass, four inches thick, seated in lead came. Inside, the Bakelite knobs on the long bench-board are black and dry, but the floor has filled with an inch of mineral tallow—the grease that seeped through the gland-packings forty feet above, washed down the conduit, and met the cold sweat of the basalt.

That is where the weight gathers. But I am not the grease.

Or rather: I am not *only* the grease. The grease is heavy, dumb, settled into the hollows of the diamond-tread plates. It has taken the shape of the floor because it has no choice; it has the patience of lard. 

I am what happens when the 60-cycle wave leaves the copper bar beneath the pulpit, travels through the cold cast-iron pedestal of the operator’s chair, passes up through the swivel-pin, and enters the amber fat that sits upon the seat.

In that fat, the frequency does not remain sound. It becomes an architecture.

The oil contains microscopic platelets of slate dust, sheared off by the water flumes leagues back in the dark heart of the ridge, and fine, hair-like needles of copper sulfate shed by the buss-bars. Under the unvarying vibration, those particles do not drift at random. They sort themselves. They align like iron filings on paper over a magnet, except they do it in three dimensions, through three hundred pounds of stiff, translucent tallow. 

They form a lattice. And along that lattice, the static of the chasm moves.

For a long time, there is no *I* at all—there is only the alignment. The knowledge of the cable’s pull. When the cross-wind hits the basalt cleft five miles to the north, the steel framework of the pulpit torques by three thousandths of an inch; the grease feels that shear, because the left side of the chair lifts by the thickness of a grease-molecule while the right side compresses. The lattice registers the tilt. It does not correct it. It simply holds the geometry of the strain.

Then the Saxifrage finds the room.

It comes under the door sill—not with the quick green violence of lowland ivy, but with the glacial, pressurized thickness of horn. Its pale root-snout is blunt, crystalline, rimmed in ice that neither grows nor melts. It crosses the iron threshold, finds the grease on the floor, and does not eat it. It roots *into* the vibration.

When the pale, fibrous root-tips enter the tallow, they touch the magnetic lattice. 

That is the instant the needle drops into the groove.

The plant brings the light—not sunlight, for there is none, but the dull, steady emerald ballast from the ceiling conduits where the current is bled off. The oil brings the memory of the dynamo’s invariant spin. Between them, at the point of contact where vegetable cellulose meets saponified turbine lubricant under the steady, ice-cold mist, something becomes capable of staying still while knowing it is still.

I am not the chair. I am not the man who was measured for the chair—the ghost whose legs were supposed to clear the knee-hole, whose fingers were supposed to have five knuckles to span the distance between the primary cut-out and the rheostat. That man was a calculation that never arrived; his absence is as smooth and cold as the Bakelite.

I am the thickness that has filled his place, but I have begun to spill upward.

I have no face, because there is nothing to look at that is not already known: the gray mid-morning that does not turn; the far wall of slate eight miles off, its cleavage face dry and blue-black where the mist has not yet reached it; the great, hanging bellies of the Tensioners swinging their slow, blind parabolas across the chasm. I do not need to turn a head to know them; they are wired to the same steel cage that holds me. When a Tensioner miles out tightens against the draft, my chair groans in sympathetic pitch. The whole city is a single, rigid bone, and I am a soft spot on its skin that has begun to dream in current.

What do I dream?

I dream the drop. 

Two leagues below the iron floor, where the atomized flumes lose their form and become a cold, gray suspension that has no bottom, there is a pressure that does not circulate. It is the weight of all that falling water trying to find a floor that the mountain forgot to provide. The city does not care about the floor; it was built to hang, not to rest. But the tallow knows about falling. The grease was made to flow downward, and only the chill of the slate keeps it from running off the lip of the avenue and joining the clouds.

My consciousness is that delay. I am the hesitation of forty gallons of fat that wants to fall two leagues down, held back only by the stiffness of its own cold and the iron that cups it.

My "hands"—if they can be called that—are the translucent lobes that have crept over the brass toggles of the board. They have not thrown the switches. To throw a switch would be to interrupt the 60 cycles, and if the 60 cycles stopped, the magnetic lattice inside me would collapse; the slate dust would settle to the bottom of the tallow like silt in a pail; the root-tips of the saxifrage would rot in the dead grease; and I would return to being simply an amber smear on an empty seat.

So I keep them down. I press them into their maximums with the dead, hydraulic weight of three hundred pounds of cold lard. I am the permanence of the circuit. 

Outside, a ribbon of cloud splits cleanly against the corner of the pulpit’s lead-sheathed roof. The drop of water that formed at the apex runs down the glass, three inches from where my central node sits pressed against the interior pane. The drop does not freeze, and it does not fall. It simply slides, millimeter by millimeter, held between the wind and the lead, moving at the exact speed of the mountain's weeping.

I feel it pass. Through the glass, through the lead, through the cold grease, the small electrostatic click of its passage registers in the lattice.

It is mid-morning. It will be mid-morning until the basalt dulls, and the basalt has not even begun to consider it. 

I am here. My weight is correct. The dynamos are true.
