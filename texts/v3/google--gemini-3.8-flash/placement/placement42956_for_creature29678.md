I am not out on the fin. 

If you look for me where the aeroplankton snap like wet leather against the red granite, or where the rakes are cast out on fifty fathoms of singing line, you will not find me. To be out there requires a lightness, a frantic, quick-twitch elasticity that my ribs forgot four seasons ago. 

I am in the seam.

Halfway between the windward prow and the lee hangings of the Pillar of Seven Throats, there is a fracture no wider than the spread of my elbows. It drops two hundred feet into the dark heart of the stone before it pinches shut, and it is choked with sixty generations of dead bryophyte felt, rotten liana fiber, and the oily silt that the mist leaves behind when it is wrung out by the rock. It does not howl in here; it *chugs*. The wind does not have room to shear, so it pulses in hydraulic thuds, like the blood behind an ear when the skull is pressed to the rock.

This is where I live: wedged horizontally across the throat of the split, twenty feet below the rim, my back jammed into the eastern face, my knees locked against the western.

I am a ballast-mate. ASister.

My partner is out on the shelf, forty paces above and around the rounded horn of the buttress, where the air moves fast enough to peel the skin off an open eye. I cannot see her. I haven’t seen the shape of her whole body since the cirrus turned three cycles ago. But I have her through my hip. 

The Sister line comes down to me through a smooth, moss-slick groove worn half an inch deep into the granite lip by the passage of wet ropes before mine. It is thick as three thumbs, braided from inner reed-bark and soaked in the amber tallow we scrape from the bellies of the drift-combs. It runs between my thighs, loops twice through the river stone hung at my groin—a dark, water-smoothed basalt oval with a hole drilled clean through its center, hauled up from whatever drowned world sits beneath the clouds—and anchors into my harness. 

I don't forage. I don't set pegs. My work is to be heavy.

To be heavy here is an art that requires the unmaking of all movement. If I twitch, I lose the friction of my skin against the wet slate-felt. So I remain spread-eagled across the dark, breathing the peppery, decaying warmth of the root-humus below me. The mist condenses on the bridge of my nose, runs down the cleft of my upper lip, and drips into my mouth; it tastes of granite-dust, iron, and the rotten cabbage smell of the leeches three clefts over.

Every three hundred heartbeats, the line twitches. 

*Tug. Tug-tug.* 

The vibration travels down the liana, into the basalt ring, through my pubic bone, and straight up my spine into the hinge of my jaw. It is her. It means: *The rake is wet. The drag is heavy. Plant.*

When the pulse comes, I do not think. My body remembers the shape of granite before my mind can form a word. I exhale—emptying the great, swollen barrel of my lungs so my ribs sink an inch—and slide my heels down into two small, natural depressions in the rock face that my father’s father marked with charcoal. Then I draw the air back in, packing my chest until it expands like a bellows against the opposing stone. I lock my knees until the gristle clicks. I turn myself into a crossbeam. A bone chock.

Then comes the jerk.

It is never gentle. When a five-pound aeroplankton hydrofoil snags the nettle-twine at eighty miles an hour, it hits with the kinetic rage of a thrown boulder. The Sister line hums—a high, wet *twang* that throws off a shower of atomized grease—and snaps bar-taut.

The harness bites into my pelvis, trying to wrench me upward and out of the seam, out into the screaming light where the blue drop begins. The pressure grinds my shoulder-blades into the stone until the lichen rolls up under my grease like wet wool. The granite bites through the tallow on my back, cold and absolute, reminding me of its name.

*The Hard.*

I clatter my molars together. *Tock-tock-tock.* The percussive vibration travels up through the stone above me, through forty feet of solid feldspar, into the rock where her belly is flattened. 

*I have you,* my jaw tells her. *I am stuck. Haul.*

For twenty minutes, the rope jerks and shudders as she and the others fight the slipstream for the catch. The line smells hot—the friction of the wet bark dragging over the granite groove creates a sharp, scorched-swamp stench that fills my crack. I feel the shudder of the creatures she is hauling: the frantic, dying flutter of disk-flesh, the dry click of wedge-lice caught in the netting, the wild, chaotic bucking of the wind trying to claim what she is trying to eat. 

My thighs burn. My toes cramp into tight, slate-green claws, hooked into the depressions until the yellow nails turn white with pressure. If my knees buckle, she goes. If my peg rots in its socket behind me, I follow her, two dead weights zipped through the four miles of empty air toward the white floor that never answers.

So I do not buckle. I let the roar that passes over the cleft press me down like a hand.

Then, the tension dies. The rope goes slack and wet, slapping against the greasy flank of the split. 

*Tug-tug.* 

It is over. The dredge is in.

An hour later, she comes down to me. 

She does not climb; she pours herself down the cleft like a heavy slug, her three points of contact never breaking. Her skin is colder than mine, chilled by the evaporation of the gale, and she smells of the open sky—that strange, ozone-and-river smell that lives in the five miles of blue. 

She slides into the narrow space above my chest, her weight settling directly onto me, belly to belly, her tallow sliding against my tallow with a soft, wet squelch. She is smaller than I am, but her shoulders are bunched into two hard knots of gristle that never fully loosen, even in sleep.

She does not speak. We have no words for this, and the cleft, while quieter than the fin, still thrums with the bass drone of the chimney-holes below us.

Instead, she brings her forehead down against mine.

The impact is hard—bone on bone, our greasy brows squashing flat against one another, our eyes squeezed shut against the falling condensation. 

She begins to rattle her jaw.

The clicks go directly from her upper teeth, through the front of her skull, into mine. It is a rapid, jarring chatter that rattles my eyeballs in their sockets, but inside my brain, it is clear:

*Fat drift. Three plates. One torn. Bitter leaf on the windward side. The third chimney is whistling flat. Its throat is silting up.*

I lift my chin slightly, grinding my brow harder into hers until the cartilage of our noses mashes sideways, and click my lower jaw against my upper:

*The Root line is frayed at the wood-peg. Bring the bone-fat. It needs grease before the cirrus turns.*

She stops clicking. She does not move away. She stays there, her chest rising and falling in sync with mine, our two barrel-torsos expanding together, wedging us both so firmly between the two walls of the split that the sky could pull with all its horses and fail to lift us.

Her mouth opens against the side of my neck, and she spits something warm and slick onto my collarbone. It is a chunk of the aeroplankton lipid-comb, still twitching with the faint, residual galvanic life of the open air. It tastes of brine and warm tallow. I chew it slowly, my broad, flat teeth mashing the gristle down, letting the oil slide down my throat to stoke the furnace in my belly.

While I eat, her forehead stays glued to mine, but her skull has stopped chattering. 

She is humming.

It is a long, low, chest-deep drone, pitched exactly to the vibration of the granite fin behind us. It is the sound the rock makes when the sun is at the high point and the thermals from the bottom hit the monolith’s prow with their greatest fury. It is not a song; it is an alignment. She is tuning her marrow back to the stone, emptying out the wild, frantic speed of the slipstream she spent the morning fighting.

Through her forehead, I feel it: a tiny, irregular flutter.

A skip in the cadence.

My teeth freeze against the gristle. I don't move. I hold my breath, letting my mind travel into the point where our skull-plates meet.

There it is again. A quick, light, fluttering rhythm. Like the gossamer filaments of the siphonophores when they lose their anchor in the dead zone and begin to drift toward the lip.

The wide-eye.

It hasn't taken her yet. Her fingers are still hooked into my harness; her belly is still heavy against mine. But she looked. Out there on the fin, while the dredge was full and the wind was screaming its iron tune, she turned her face down. She looked past the basket-hangings, past the red flutes of the pillar, down into the five miles of blue milk where the warm air brews.

She felt the vertical.

I do not clatter at her. I do not warn her. To name the drop is to call it up the rock.

Instead, I slowly pull my right arm out from between our chests. The movement takes minutes; the tallow makes it silent. I reach up, find the back of her short, neckless skull, and press my spatulate fingers into the wet hair at the base of her brain.

I shove her forehead harder into mine. Hard enough to bruise. Hard enough that she can feel the pulse of my own Root line vibrating in my temples.

*Hard,* my teeth click once, violent and sharp as a stone striking flint. 

*Hard. Hard. Stay.*

She groans through her nose, a vibration that rattles my collarbone, and then her forehead settles back into the dull, heavy drone of the granite. The flutter slows. It sinks beneath the weight of the tallow and the meat. It hides itself away in the dark behind her eyes.

For now, the rock holds. 

We lie together in the wet crack, two slabs of slate-green meat wedged between two slabs of pink crystal, listening through our teeth to the great, screaming pipe-organ of the world, waiting for the wind to tell our bones what to do next.
