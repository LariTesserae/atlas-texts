At first, it is not an identity at all. It is only a density. 

A difference in pressure between the water hanging in the air and the water trapped beneath the hide.

If I begin anywhere, it is where the dry-stacked limestone wall meets the primary buttress of the fig, five feet above the floor of the pit. Not on the ground—the mud is too loud with the churning of the white isopods, an endless mill of hunger that never rests—and not up in the open drop, where the ribbon-cords hang like heavy grease and wait for a beating pulse. I am wedged into the architecture. I am in the seam where a human hand once leveled two courses of stone, though the space between them has long since been prised open by a finger of root the thickness of a thigh.

My back is pressed against the lintel stone above; my belly rests on the sill below. That is the first certainty: I am shaped by compression. 

For a long time, there is no need to move. The vault feeds itself to me. The steam does not merely touch my surface; it condenses along the heavy, vascular ridges of my flanks, running down grooved gutters in my skin toward the corners of my mouth. I taste the pit before I see it. It tastes of calcified bone, iron silt, and the bitter, yellow tannin of fig sap that has boiled in the dark for three hundred years. 

I do not have the dry, fluttering panic of the moths, nor the blind, star-nosed hurry of the things that trot the steps. My pulse is so slow that a liverwort can root in the fold of my shoulder without having its threads snapped by my breathing. Perhaps three times in an hour, my ribs expand—a broad, leathery creak against the limestone, displacing just enough vapor to cause the nearby orchid leaves to bob once, then settle.

I thought, perhaps, I was the root itself. There are hours when the distinction fails. The fig root has grown over my flank, pinning one hind limb in a permanent, wooden embrace; we have traded fluids through the damp. Where its gray bark presses against my hide, my skin has responded by throwing out fibrous, corky calluses that match the tree’s grain. But I am not vegetable. I have a jaw, broad and spade-like, lined with blunt, yellowed pegs of dentine, and deep inside the vault of my skull sits the dull, iron weight of an ancient intention.

When the dusk above the rift thins to the absolute charcoal of true night, the chamber’s circulation subtly shifts. 

The cold from the upper world reaches down just three paces further into the chimney, like a pale hand testing the neck of a furnace. The weeping stones weep faster. The *tock* from the stalactites quickens to the cadence of a trotting goat. 

It is then that I unwedge myself. 

The motion takes minutes. It is a slow, hydraulic peeling of tallow-colored belly from wet calcite. A film of white slime and gray grit stretches and parts with a quiet sucking sound. My claws—three to each foot, spade-wide, flat, and blunt from generations of scraping the softened faces of the blocks—find the small, thumb-sized depressions worn into the stone steps. 

I know these stairs. Not because I was there when the chisel bit, but because my ancestors were brought down here by the ones who carried the chisel, back when the air in the pit was still cool enough for a man to work without his veins bursting. We were their beasts of burden, perhaps, or their companions in the cellar, kept to root out the things that crawled from the vents. They died when the ceiling fell and the thermal water rose, their bones long since softened into paste and drunk by the ferns. But we stayed in the damp, growing low and thick, trading eyes for a thicker grease, dropping our ears until they were only pinholes lined with water-repellent wax.

I move down the three bowed steps to the rimstone pool. 

I do not drink with a tongue. I lower my wedge-shaped head until the snout enters the tepid iron film, letting the blind flatworms glide over my nostrils, grazing on the microscopic film of surface pollen that has settled across my snout. They tickle, but it is a familiar, communal sensation; they clean the algae from my gums, and in return, my heavy exhalations stir the amber silt they feed upon.

A jade lizard sits on the curb beside my forefoot, flattened until it has no shadow. It does not run from me. To the lizard, I am simply another block of limestone that has slid from the wall, warm and covered in edible lichen. Its translucent eyelids blink once, wiping away the steam. 

I reach down with my heavy, rubbery lower lip and strip a curtain of wet liverwort from the second riser, chewing with a circular, grinding rhythm that vibrates through my jaw into the stone. The taste is peppery, cold despite the heat, full of grit that grinds between my molars. It is not sweet, but it is dense with earth-fat. 

Above me, a moth passes, its downy butter-yellow wings pushing a draft of warm air against my face that smells of rotting melon and hot tallow. Then comes the silence again—the profound, subterranean smother that absorbs all things.

I will not leave this terrace. I will not climb toward the slit of sky; even if my limbs could find purchase on the sheer thirty-meter chimney, the air up there would be thin, dry, and cold as broken glass. It would shrivel the moist pleats of my lungs in three breaths. My world is eighty paces across, held between the sweating wall and the boiling vents. 

When my stomach is heavy with boiled stone-moss and the soft bodies of the white crabs I turn up from beneath the steps, I will turn around. The turn takes an hour. My hide scrapes the ancient wall, polishing the carvings that are already half-erased—the faint, rounded grooves of someone else’s forgotten gods, now worn down to the smooth curvature of my own ribs.

I will wedge myself back into the gap beneath the fig root. I will let the slime reseal the seam between my flank and the limestone. The drops will fall—*tock, tock*—from the ceiling. The ribbon-cord will glide overhead in its slow, mucus-sheathed circuit, sensing my blood, knowing it is too deep beneath the fat and stone to ever touch.

And there I will stay, neither asleep nor awake, simply continuing the breathing of the vault: half-beast, half-masonry, an old domestic animal that has outlived its masters by ten thousand seasons, slowly being digested by the garden it was left behind to tend.
