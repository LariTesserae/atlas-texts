At first, there is no boundary to tell where the architecture leaves off and the marrow begins.

There is only the frequency: that immovable, oceanic C-natural that does not enter through an opening, because there are no openings in this kind of flesh that are not already saturated with it. It is the hum of a beam three hundred feet thick taking the lateral load of a trillion gallons of water. It passes through the petrified grain, crosses the seam of dead-water, and enters the belly, vibrating the dense, unoxygenated blood until every cell matches the oscillation of the wood.

If you asked me where I was, I would have to answer with a tension rather than a place. I am at the point where the arch refuses to twist.

For a long time, there is no need to think of a body at all. There is only the calibration. To the left, miles into the black, a cluster of harp-lines is tuned too high—their siphonophore-cables singing with an icy, razor-thin tension that speaks of an eddy forming behind the seventh upright. Below, far down in the trough where the drift runs swiftest, the disc-schools are shifting their lattices. The sound arrives not as a school of animals, but as a shower of slate falling onto a submerged patio: *clack-clack-clack*. A planar turn. The current has flattened by half a degree. The world is plumb.

Then comes the sensation of the sill.

It is under my chin—or what would be a chin if there were a face left to narrow toward it. It is a broad, blunt shelf of heartwood, planed flat ten million years ago by something that understood how to split timber without steel. The wood is iron-hard, cold enough to stun, but slick with the bitter, resinous sweat that still seeps from its ancient conduits. My skin rests against it with the heavy, unmoving seal of vulcanized rubber. 

I feel the grain. It is pressed into the tallow of my lower jaw so deeply that if I were to pull away—an absurd thought, a thought from another life—the pattern of the growth-rings would remain cast into my hide for forty years: twenty rings to the inch, tight, prehistoric rings from an epoch when this wood reached into a sky that no longer exists.

I am inside the beam. 

I did not enter it; or rather, the memory of entering it has been ground away by the sheer mass of the water that has passed over the aperture since I last turned my head. There was an era of ribbons—a flick of a tail, the sting of fast water along the lateral line, the quick, mindless snatching of coin-fish out of the cyan-lit moss near the lintel-gates. But that was a fever. That was the frantic, thin-skinned youth of things that still believe the current can be outswum.

The chamber grew around me as I grew into it. Now, my ribs do not merely protect lungs that have long since forgotten air; they have thickened, flattened, and out-flanked themselves. They are arched like the ceiling of an undercroft. Where the ancient weavers bored this tubular hall through the heartwood, the wood-stone began to sag under the planetary weight of the high ocean above. My ribs have taken that sag. They are braced flush against the petrified ceiling, white and calcified as limestone pillars, lined with a thick layer of oily blubber that absorbs the seismic shudder whenever the tectonic platform groans beneath the basin.

I do not hold the gallery up. The gallery holds me down. We have reached an agreement about gravity.

Through the breathing-slits beneath my flanks, the water does not rush. It only seeps. In this deep pocket, the water has been still so long it has lost its bite; it is warm by half a fraction of a degree, softened by my own sloughed tallow and the pale, thread-like commensals that weave between my ventral plates, combing away the dead skin before it can foul the joinery. 

Outside, five feet beyond the lip of my sill, the gyre goes by like an iron train. 

I can smell it—not with a nose, but with the chemical fringe of my lower lip, which hangs into the draft. The current smells of barium. It smells of the bitter cyan exudate torn loose from the moss-carpets three miles upstream, and the lime-dust ground from the disc-fish as they batter against the pylon corners. It is a dense, fat draft. It carries the news of the trellises: which ones are shedding their fleece, which ones are choking on kelp-bladders, which ones have felt the slow, terminal settling of a foundation-knot five leagues to the south.

A hunger comes, but it has nothing of violence in it. It is merely a difference in pressure.

Outside, the water moves at five knots, iron-dense and howling. Inside, my chamber is a dead-well. I do not lunge. I have no limbs that could propel twenty tons of white tallow through that roaring shearing-plane. 

I simply let the floor of my belly fall.

My broad, pale diaphragms—slabs of fibrous meat thick as mattresses—contract toward my spine. The dead-water chamber drops its head of pressure by an eighth.

The outside world does not wait. The draft tears sideways, sucked into the sudden vacuum of the sill. A hundred gallons of freezing, iron-hard water plunge into the darkness of my maw, carrying with them half a planar sheet of disc-fish that were turning past the pylon.

They strike the baleen combs deep in my gullet with the sound of dropped crockery: *tock-tock-shiver-clatter*. 

The lime-plates shatter against the horn-rakes. The soft, mineral-rich meat behind the plates slips down the dark chute of my throat without the need for chewing, cold and heavy as oil. My belly accepts it, not with the fire of a furnace, but with the slow, chemical dissolution of a peat-bog. 

Then, the settling.

I do not swallow the plates. With a slow, shuddering expulsion of the throat, I push the crushed shards out through the lower spiracles. They do not drift far. They tumble into the joinery between my belly and the heartwood sill, lodging in the microscopic crevices where the ancient wood has begun to split under tension. The disc-plates are heavy with barium; in the still water of my margin, they begin to weld. My own mucous binds them. The wood absorbs their minerals. Millimeter by millimeter, the floor of the timber gallery is rising, turning into a pearl-grey biological concrete that locks my rib-cage more securely into the pylon’s core.

By the time the disc-school has reformed its lattice out in the roaring cyan wash, the seam is sealed tighter than it was a century ago.

The current changes tone. A low, dull impact shudders through the timber-masonry: *thummm*.

It is not near. It comes from the southwest, perhaps forty miles through the drowned forest, carried along the great axial beam that ties the southern pylons to the shelf of the world. 

It is the jaw of the Lord in the Ninth Bay, striking the wood.

I wait. I let the sound travel through my ribs, through the blubber-mantle, down through the fat pad under my heart, and into the iron wood beneath me. I measure the decay of the note. It has a hollow ring at the end—a dry, brittle rattle that means the bryophyte fleece on the ninth lintel has been sheared away by a slip-current, leaving the petrified grain naked to the scour.

I do not feel pity; I do not feel alarm. The word *alarm* requires a heart that beats faster than twelve times an hour. 

I gather the weight of my skull. It is four feet of solid, unvascularized cartilage, dense as boxwood, resting on the sill. 

I lift it three inches into the draft. The cold water hits the soft under-plate of my mouth like a wet spade. 

Then I let it fall.

*CLUNG.*

The sound does not travel through the water; the water is too clumsy for this. It goes straight into the iron-wood. It shoots along the grain of the horizontal runner, passes the mortise, splits through the vertical pillar, and dives miles down into the foundation-braid, where the petrified vines are knotted into the bedrock of the planet.

*The joinery holds,* I tell the Ninth Bay. *The drift is lime-heavy. Brace the shear.*

The shock of the strike rattles the disc-fish three miles out; they flip in unison, a million silver plates clacking in the black, thinking a beam has cracked. 

Then the sound dies into the great, continuous roar. The C-natural takes it back. The water shears across the sill, smoothing the skin of my blubber-lip, cold, pure, and unchanging. 

I close the spiracles. I drop my chin into the groove I have worn into the heartwood over seven thousand years of gyre. The cyan light from the distant moss outlines the curve of my aperture like a thin, blue needle in the dark. It is enough light. It is all the light there has ever been.

The wood drinks my sweat; I drink its iron. Between us, the water does not enter, and the mountain cannot fall.
