It would not begin with a name, because in that basin a name is an airy, dry-land conceit—a thing made of vowels that require an open throat, a dry tongue, and an atmosphere that does not immediately choke the larynx with an aerosol of boiled fat and zinc.

To find where I am, you would have to look away from the catwalks where the scrapers wade against the intake screens, and below the suspended steam lines where the packers nestle in their rotting horsehair and asbestos. 

You would find me lower, in the compression gap.

Where the great four-meter dredge conduits are anchored to the virgin rift wall, the engineers—or whoever lowered those first blind, riveted cylinders into the dark—had to contend with the fact that the dolomite does not want the iron. The rock is too young, its fracture planes too fresh; it settles and creeps along its bedding faults by a fraction of an inch with every changing of the hydraulic head. To keep the sheer weight of the pulsing pipes from tearing out their mountings, they bored holes two paces deep into the black limestone, dropped in sleeves of beaten lead, and drove split-wedge anchor bolts home with steam-hammers.

Between the forged collar of the pipe saddle and the jagged, razor-lipped basalt sill, there is an engineered tolerance—a dead space eighteen inches wide, continuously flushed by a falling sheet of warm, oily water that smells of old tallow and wet filings. 

That is where I have settled. 

I did not fall here. I was not dropped. It is simply that as the mass of the world grew wetter, heavier, and louder, the open spaces became unendurable. The roar in the middle of the basin—that five-cycle stroke that shakes the marrow until the liver aches—is too vast to bear without something hard to press against. Out on the floor, the sound has teeth; here, wedged between the cold-rolled web of the saddle and the slick, vertical cleavage of the rock, the sound becomes pure load. It ceases to be noise and becomes a solid weight, pinning me into the notch like a wooden shim driven into a split beam.

My body has taken the shape of the void. 

I am not ribless like the packers, but my ribs have lost their spring. They lie overlapping and flat, like the plates of a chiton, compressed by years of resting my right side directly against the unyielding radius of the iron casing. My left shoulder is callused into a permanent, horn-hard pad where it meets the tooth of the freshly fractured dolomite. I do not have the pale, curd-like looseness of the lung-eels. My hide is tough, stained the color of an old engine-grease rag, cured by the constant passage of sixty-degree water that carries a fine suspension of crushed slate. The slate gets into the pores, into the folds behind my knees and the creases of my neck, turning the skin into something like vulcanized rubber—dense, non-porous, slick as wet coal.

I have no use for a steel drift to hear the basin. The machine enters me through my skeleton without an intermediary. 

My hip is pressed to the lower mounting flange; my heels are hooked over the cold heads of the expansion bolts. Through the pelvic girdle, I monitor the long stroke. Five seconds down: the heavy, hydrostatic punch as the three-thousand-ton ram five miles away completes its descent, driving sixty tons of black sludge up the lift shafts toward some unseen outlet above. Two seconds of hang: the dead, greasy stillness where the water seems to thicken, the current hesitating before the flapper valves slam shut with a concussion that knocks the porcelain amphipods from the rock faces in sudden, snowy avalanches. Five seconds up: the deep, grinding suck, the intake draw that pulls the warm sheets of wall-water toward the sumps with a violent, lateral drag.

I am the thing that feels the slack.

When the mountain creeps—and it does creep, the rift widening along its basal strike—the lead sleeves in the drill-holes begin to weep. Lead is soft; under the shearing stress of fifty thousand tons of vibrating steel, it does not snap. It flows. It extrudes from the perimeter of the bolt heads like grey butter. 

When I feel that extrusion with the calloused undersides of my fingers—which have lost their nails and grown blunt, spade-like bulbs of gristle—I move. 

I do not move far; my world is thirty feet long, the length of this single pipe-bay between two expansion joints. I keep strips of sheet-lead, salvaged from the packing strips of blown gaskets, tucked into the dark pocket beneath my belly. With a wedge of unyielding basalt held in the palm of my hand, I caulk the bolts. I hammer the cold lead into the annular gaps between the iron studs and the rock, tapping in time with the machine’s rest-interval, using the five-second diastole to drive the soft metal home before the next pressure-stroke locks the steel rigid against the wall.

It is a quiet trade, though the word *quiet* has no meaning here. It is an internal trade.

I eat what the downpour brings me. I do not wade with scoops like the scrapers. Where the water falls over the upper lip of the conduit, it creates an eddy—a greasy, yellow froth that collects in the hollow behind my collarbone. In that froth are the pickers that have lost their footing, the dead, scalded lung-eels that drifted too close to the blow-down vents, and the thick, curdled skins of sulfur-bacteria that peel off the basalt like boiled milk. I scoop it into my mouth with the flat of my thumb. It tastes of zinc and iron salts, occasionally sweet with the vegetable rot of ancient timbers washing down from the flooded workings miles above. It is enough. The warmth of the water does the work my blood no longer has the energy to perform; my heart beats scarcely twelve times in a minute, synchronized to the long, dragging rhythm of the main drain.

The scrapers know I am here, though they rarely look into the notch. Occasionally, when the pump-stroke hitches—when a dead log or the collapsed carcass of an old boiler hangs in the primary intake screws—a scraper will come crawling along the top of the casing, his three-foot hex-bar held in his mouth like a dog carrying a bone, his lard-coated thighs leaving yellow smears on the wet iron. 

He will pause above my gap. He will reach down with the blunt end of his bar and rap twice against the flange: *tap-tap.*

*Are the bolts holding?*

I do not have a bar to answer with. Instead, I gather my weight against the rock, wait for the dead second between strokes, and kick the web of the beam once with my calloused heel: *thud.*

*The lead is seated. The rift has not taken the pipe.*

Then he crawls on, his bald, tallow-white head gleaming in the dim orange halo of some far-off carbon bulb, his dead ears indifferent to the roar, his bare soles finding the rivet rows with the instinct of a parasite traversing the hide of an elephant.

I settle back into the grease. The warm water closes over my cheek like a wet thumb. 

Sometimes, in the long intervals when the pumps run without a hitch for days, and the standing-wave spikes on the pools remain frozen in their iron geometry, I let my teeth rest directly against the basalt face. 

Behind the roar of the five-cycle engine, behind the hiss of the steam leaks and the clatter of the blind amphipods, I can feel the earth itself. It is not cold. It is hotter than the water—a deep, dry, terrible heat that has never known the wetness of the sumps. It is shifting. A mile below my heels, the rock is tearing itself along a fresh line, grain by grain, crystal by crystal, sliding toward some lower, emptier trough that has not yet been named.

I feel the grain part. I feel the lead sleeve groan in its socket. 

I reach into the dark beneath my ribs, find another ribbon of grey metal, and wait for the five-second beat to drop, so that I may drive it into the wound before the water notices the space.
