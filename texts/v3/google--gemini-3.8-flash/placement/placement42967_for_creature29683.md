It begins not with a shape, but with the acoustic weight. 

Sound here does not enter through an ear; it enters through the belly, through whatever surface meets the stone. The flume drops like an iron piston into the tank, over and over, never emptying, never pausing to draw breath, and the pale travertine floor takes the blow and sends it shuddering sideways through every joint. To be here is to be vibrated from within, to feel the calcium in one’s own frame hum at the exact pitch of quarried rock under tension.

I would not be up where the swallows cut the mist. That is an outside world, brief and dry, smelling of cedar needles and hot terra-cotta, gone before the water can touch it. 

Nor would I be the turtle. The turtle is too vast, too soft, an exile with memories of silt banks and rotting river-alder that make this clean, sharp-edged box an agony. Its leathery skin remembers mud, and so the flat, tooth-marked travertine feels to it like an insult, an impossible floor that will not yield to digging. It waits for an end to the sound that will never come.

I would be lower down, much smaller, and far less intact. 

At first, you would take me for drift. A dark, coin-sized flake tossed down the mountain flume along with the torn liverwort and the polished oak seeds, tumbled through thirty leagues of pitch-black mountain conduits before being spit out into this sudden, blinding turquoise room. A scrap of the gorge, flattened and stubborn, swept through the turbulence of the air-column and driven down toward the floor where the sun hammers hardest.

I am something built to hold fast to stone in violent water, but mountain water is cold, dark, and irregular. It has mossy lips, gravel pockets, roots of hemlock to tuck beneath. Here, there are only planes. Ninety-degree angles. Symmetrical joints filled with raw lime mortar that tastes of lye and fresh fire. 

I find myself pressed into the third course of ashlar on the south wall, six inches above the floor, directly in the path of the midday beam. 

My body is a flat, flexible disc of dark horn, no thicker than a fingernail—a torrent-crawler, a water penny, built so low to the stone that current cannot find an edge to pry away. My legs are tucked entirely beneath my rim; my margin is a fringe of microscopic hairs that seals against the travertine like a cupping glass. 

In the wild stream, I would graze diatom scum from water-smoothed river boulders. Here, my mouthparts test the stone and find the parallel, tooth-chiseled grooves of the mason’s pick. They are straight as surveyor’s lines, sharp-edged and raw, smelling faintly of the quarryman’s iron wedges. They make strange, unnatural roads. I crawl along them a fraction of an inch at a time, moving sideways through the chisel-wake, tasting the pale mineral paste the lime has left behind.

The light is the terror, and yet it is the only thing that sustains. 

In this tank, light does not behave as it does in a river. It does not dappled-shift through leaves or fade into brown bottom-murk. It comes from the cobalt square overhead like a dropped weight, hits the white floor five fathoms down, and fractures into a second noon that strikes upward from below. There is no shadow beneath my carapace. The glare bites at my soft ventral plates, bleaching the gray membranes of my gills, forcing me to pull my perimeter down harder, harder, clamping my horn shield against the pick-grooves until the seal is absolute.

And then the water begins its work.

This water is not passive. It carries the subterranean heat of the mountain’s furnace, so rich in dissolved lime that it is eager to drop its cargo onto anything that will hold still. In the heat of the beam, where the emerald algae is knitting itself into the micro-fissures of the stone, the calcium is already precipitating out of solution. A microscopic silt, bright white and chemically sharp, drifts across my back. 

At first, it is just dust in the back-eddy. But within hours, the percussion of the flume packs it down. It settles into the fine pits of my shell. The lime bonds with the chitin; the carbonate crystals begin to seed themselves along the ragged edges of my rim. 

I am not swimming, and I am no longer drifting. I am becoming cemented. 

The turtle lifts itself above me, a pale buckler drifting toward the ceiling to steal its single gulp of mortar-scented air, trailing bubbles that catch the sun like silver beads. I feel the downward thrust of its great webbed paddles—a heavy, displaced wave that pushes against my shell, trying to strip me from the wall. 

It cannot. The lime has taken hold. 

My carapace is losing its dark, horny flexibility, turning chalk-white, brittle, and thick. The emerald velvet of the algae is already creeping up my flanks, spreading over my rim, sealing the junction where my horn meets the mason’s ashlar. Beneath me, my small mouthparts continue to scrape the tooth-grooves, feeding on the microscopic rotifers and the crusts of dried starch, but above me, my skin is hardening into travertine. 

I am ceasing to be an animal that fell into the well. 

I am becoming an irregularity in the wall. A small, rounded knob in the straight-edged architecture of the box, over which the turquoise back-eddy will slide for a hundred years, rounded by current, blanketed by weed, buried deeper and deeper under the white lime that the water brings down from the hills. 

The swallows flash above like sparks across the cobalt ceiling. The turtle waits in the wedge of black shadow for a silence that will not come. And down in the seam, clamped to the hand-cut travertine, I let the bath-warm water fill my margins with stone, listening through my ribs to the great, uninterrupted drumbeat of the flume, turning steadily into the box itself.
