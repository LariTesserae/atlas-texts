At first, I would not be a person at all. 

It is too easy to imagine stepping onto those vibrating spars with a human mind, but a mind brought from anywhere else would curdle in three breaths out on the shelf. The glare alone would turn human thought into grease. If I look for myself in the first instant, I am only a point of pressure: the damp, fat-heavy knuckle of gut-rope where it bites into the notched driftwood; or one of those small, frantic *skirrs* crammed blindly under an armpit, its rib cage shivering at four hundred beats a minute, its bristle-feathers soaked in seal oil, wanting nothing except to bite through leather and dive into the froth.

To be the bird would be honest: to have no past, to be merely a fistful of fever and hunger tumbling through the silver halos, bouncing off wet slate, snapping at blind eels whose blood tastes of cold iron. 

But no—that is too light. The bird escapes the stone. The bird has the falling air.

If I remain here, let the spray crust over me, and wait for what settles, I find myself lower down. Not on the high hunting perches where the young leap into the mist with lead-weighted irons, and not inside the amber-lit sleeping baskets where the grease-stiff hair is braided together for heat.

I would be wedged into the seep-runnels behind the third tier of huts.

I would be one of the scrapers.

An older body, though "old" on the shelf is not a matter of winters—there are no winters, only the thickness of the rime—but a matter of lime in the chest. My ribs are broad and thick, packed in that dense, yellowish lard that never quite goes cold, but behind them my lungs make the sound of wet sand shifting in an apron. The voice went seasons ago; there is only the thick, gravelly click at the base of the palate that means *stand clear*, and the heavy mitts on my hands.

The mitts are what define me. They are stiffened with whale-glue until they are nearly like wood, the palms crusted with river-pebbles that have been ground flat against the slate. When I slap them against my hip—*clack-clack*—it is not an ornament; it is the only way the runner above knows I am still under the footing, clearing the sills.

My work is with the crust.

The water here is not water; it is liquid stone looking for an excuse to harden. If I stop for the length of three eel-turns, the spray that sheets over my curled, prehensile toes begins to glaze them, turning the grey skin into white spar, cementing my joints to the hemlock log. So I scrape. I hold a broad gouge made from the pelvic blade of an uncle or a brother—someone who choked on the lime two moons back—and I drive it along the joints where the huts meet the cliff face.

*Shhh-t. Shhh-t.*

The sound does not carry. It is eaten by the foam beneath my feet, where the blind lung-eels boil in their own yellow lather. But I feel the shudder of the blade in the bones of my forearms, all the way into the thick fat of my shoulders. The spar comes away in wet, sparkling ribbons, like cold lard curled off a wheel, and falls into the sluice.

Tucked inside my sleeve, against the bare crook of my elbow, a skirr is wedged. It is wedged so tightly it can barely twitch its wings, but its hard little beak pecks rhythmically into the meat of my forearm, a dull, repetitive prick that keeps the limb from going white and numb in the brine. I do not hate it; I do not pet it. It is my stove. When it pecks too deeply, I squeeze my elbow until its hollow bones give a faint creak, and it stills for a moment, panting its hot, sour breath through the greasy hide of my cuff.

Above me, the gut-lines shudder. 

I do not look up through the ivory slit. To tilt the head back is to invite a cupful of supercooled salt directly into the hood, and the ivory strip tied across my nose-bridge gives only a narrow, horizontal world: the wet grey curve of the rock, the greasy yellow wood of the walkway, the pale, squirming thrash of the eels in the baffle-troughs. But I feel the capstan turn. Three heavy, wet thuds travel down the spruce pilings into my knees.

*Thwack. Thwack. Thwack.*

A white thing has come through the siphon. 

Someone has jumped. I feel the rope take the weight—a long, agonizing groan that runs through the whole hive, the huts swaying three inches to the left as four hundred stones of blind fat drop out of the spray. 

The mitts above me begin their chatter. On the walkway over my head, three pairs of greased feet slap in frantic syncopation. *Clack-tick, pad, clack.* The sign comes down through the gaps in the timber: *Hold the sill. The haul is heavy. Clear the lower teeth.*

I do not drop my scraper. I reach out with one curled, stone-hard foot, hook the lower stay-rope, and pull myself deeper into the runnel. 

Here, behind the hut, lies the one who died of the rattle this morning. 

They have already stripped him. His sealskin was stiff with old fat, good for patching; the girl with the long jaw took it before the body was cold. His grease was scraped into the soapstone pot to keep the capstan-pins from seizing in the brine. What is left of him is grey-white, thick-ribbed, and quiet, lying in the shallow trough where the mineral water sheets thin as parchment.

My job is to wedge him true before the salt takes him.

I set his heels against the cliff-fluting, where the stone has been scooped out by ten thousand years of spray. I take my slate mallet and tap his broad, flat shin-bones until they sit flush against the timber framing of hut forty-two. He must not lie crooked; if he lies crooked, the calcite will build an uneven mound, and the spring-melt from the upper tube will catch the lip and tear the walkway out.

He must become a sill. 

The water sheets over his chest, perfectly clear, utterly soundless. Already, the tiny, glittering crystals of spar are catching in the stubble of his chin, filming the dull yellow of his unseeing eyes, turning the blue-grey tallow of his belly into something hard and frosted, like sugar left in the rain. By tomorrow, the skirrs will not be able to peck through him. By next moon, the people will lash the gut-stay for the new meat-rack directly around his lower spine, and it will hold better than any bronze peg dragged up from the old legends.

I work in the silver glare. My spit tastes of river-silt and ancient shells. 

The white beast is hauled up past my head, dripping great gobs of thick, grey blood that turn the churning foam below from yellow to a dull, bruised lavender. A hunter's foot slips on the wet spar three feet above me; a mitt clatters frantically against a hemlock strut—*tick-tick-tick*—and then rights itself. No one screams. There is only the wet, leaden slap of the carcass hitting the dressing-balk, the quick, rhythmic strokes of the slate adzes opening the blubber, and the steam rising from its opened belly, instantly flattened and murdered by the cold, rushing spray.

A chunk of raw liver, torn loose in the butchery, drops through the floorboards and smacks into the lime beside my knee. 

I do not clean it. I pick it up with my pebbled mitt, wipe the mineral grit against my thigh, and shove it into my mouth behind the ivory guard. It is still hot—that dense, metallic furnace-heat that does not come from fire, but from life that has never seen the sun. I chew it with my worn, flat molars until my jaw aches, swallowing the thick grease, feeling it run down into the yellow fat of my own belly to feed the stove.

The skirr inside my sleeve smells the iron. It begins to thrash, drumming its hard, bristle-coated head against my ribs, wild with the scent. 

I do not feed it. I hit it once with my elbow to quiet it, take up my bone gouge, and go back to the lime.

*Shhh-t. Shhh-t.*

I am the hinge between the meat and the cliff. I am the one who keeps the rock from closing its mouth, until it is my turn to be the tooth that holds the wood.
