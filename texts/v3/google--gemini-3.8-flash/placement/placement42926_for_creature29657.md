At first, there is only the lean. 

Before there is any name for what I am or what coat I wear, there is the simple, stubborn fact of thirty-two degrees off the true vertical. To stand upright in this place would be an act of madness, like opening an umbrella in an iron-foundry gale; the body must tilt inward toward the slate, knees soft, weight pooled into the low sacrum, heels seeking the square-jawed mesh of the catwalks.

My boots are heavy—not metaphorically, but with seventy-four ounces of scrap-lead hammered into the double-welt soles so that my feet know where the ground wants them to be, even when the ground itself is undecided.

I am not out on the long spans where the Cable-runners dance, nor am I down in the cold grease-trenches scraping the Sediment with the flat spades. Those are the margins of the work: the thin air above, the raw mouth below. 

No, find me where the iron meets its own uncertainty. 

Find me in the *expansion collars*. 

Between the sixth and seventh tiers of the Horn of St. Jude, there is a place where a five-hundred-foot truss of box-girder iron sleeves directly into an anchor socket sunk forty fathoms into the slate. It is not bolted dead-tight. If you bolted it dead-tight, the first time the deep core hiccuped, the shear-force would snap twenty-inch pins like dried macaroni. It has to breathe. It has to slide. 

The collar is an eight-foot ring of cast bronze, packed with tallow-soaked hemp, graphite flour, and neatsfoot oil. And inside the collar, in the crawlway between the inner flange and the wet, glass-faced ironstone that has never seen daylight, there is a space about the size of a baker’s oven. 

It is dry in there—not with the clean dryness of an attic, but with the baked, oily, suffocating dryness of a locomotive firebox that was damped down three hours ago. The smell is immense: scorched fat, boiled tallow, the flinty vinegar of virgin rock that is being crushed under a million tons of dead-load, and my own sour wool.

I think I am a man, though the category has grown somewhat loose around the edges. 

My wrists are bound in greasy linen to keep the razor-dust of the slate out of the pulse-points; when that dust gets under the skin, it doesn’t fester—it just stays there, cold and blue, until your forearm feels like a sack of broken watch-crystals. My teeth are worn flat on the left side from thirty years of holding the tap-chisel while both hands are busy with the jack-screws. I have the squat, thick neck of the lower-tier folk, and when I breathe, my ribs don't expand outward; they lift in one solid plate, like the shell of a grease-beetle, adapted to the heavy, sulfur-dragged air of the inner flues.

Yet the longer I stay in the collar, the less certain I am where my canvas coat ends and the horsehair lagging begins.

My task is the *drift*. 

Every six hours, the Horn swells. You do not see it with the eyes; you feel it through the soles of the lead-boots as a faint, nausea-inducing thrum, like standing on the deck of a steamer whose screw has fouled in heavy kelp. When the swell comes, the iron girder must slip inside the bronze collar by three-eighths of an inch. If it slips smooth, there is only a long, hydraulic *shuck-chuff* that makes the tallow bubble in the grease-cups.

If it sticks—if a grain of flint has worked into the race, or if the mountain has kicked sideways instead of true—the iron begins to *sing*.

It is a sound like a violin string made of three-inch wire rope, drawn so tight that the tone goes up beyond the hearing of dogs, straight into the roots of your molars. That is when I go in with the copper drifts and the five-ton hydraulic ram. 

I do not hammer. You do not strike anything inside a collar; a blow would mushroom the bronze and weld us forever to the mountain’s hip. I set the ram against the rock-face—against that cold, obsidian skin that sweats sour water—and I pump.

*Clink. Clink. Sssst.*

The hydraulic oil smells of whale-blubber and wintergreen. I watch the dial, its brass face fogged with sulfur-bloom. Three thousand pounds. Four thousand. The girder groans. It is an ancient, tired sound, the groan of pig-iron that was cast in some valley that had flat fields and cows and haystacks, now clamped ten thousand feet in the gray air to a mountain that doesn't know what a tree is.

And then, through the soles of my feet, the Horn answers.

This is the part they do not teach the Cable-runners, and the women at the mercury gauges do not see it on their columns. The mountain does not push back with force alone; it pushes back with *intention*. 

Against the foot of my jack, the rock yields—not by cracking, but by becoming momentarily *soft*. For three seconds, four seconds, under five thousand pounds of pressure, the crystalline basalt turns to the consistency of chilled cheese. The jack sinks an eighth of an inch into the virgin stone, and the stone takes a slow, blind bite around the footplate, tasting the forged steel, chilling it until frost furs the cylinder in the middle of a sixty-degree draft.

Then the girder slips: *THUNG.*

The sound does not travel. It hits the dead basalt and stops dead, dropping into my chest like an anvil wrapped in a blanket. The collar takes the grease. The tallow smokes. The gap closes.

I sit back in the dark of the crawlway, my knees drawn up to my chin, my canvas mittens resting on the hot cylinder of the ram.

A Harrow-hound crawls past my boots, upside down, its suction-pads making their wet *frip-frip-frip* against the bronze flange above my head. It doesn’t look at me. Its belly is swollen with grease-crust; one of its six breathing-slits is whistling slightly because it has sucked in a fiber of hemp from the packing. I reach out and touch its flank with the flat of a cold-chisel; it twitches once, indifferent, and pauses to lick a smear of mutton-fat off the heel of my right boot. Its tongue is like coarse sandpaper soaked in brine.

We understand each other, the hound and I. Neither of us belongs to the sky, and neither of us was born of the stone. We are the things that live in the clearance.

Out through the six-inch inspection port of the collar, I can see a strip of the world:

A slice of purple-gray nothing that drops away until the eyes refuse to focus on it. Across the gulf, two miles off, the chimney-stacks of the Third Pumping Station are venting their heavy dough-smoke. It does not go up; it rolls out flat, a gray, undulating road across the middle of the air. Upon it, three hundred yards out, a clump of blue-green ivy is growing on nothing at all, its leaves shaking in the cross-draft, drinking the carbon from the flue-gas and dropping fat, bitter seeds down into the zero below.

Farther out still, a Floater is turning. 

It is so vast that it takes five minutes for its left wing to clear the shadow of the St. Jude Horn. As it sweeps through the twilight, I can see the copper taps driven into its bladders, trailing long, braided-wire lanyards that hang down into the mist like the legs of a drowned spider. The creature does not flap; it merely arches its dorsal ridge, the cartilage creaking with a sound like a timber warehouse settling into mud, and lets the lateral gravity of the new peaks pull it sideways. It drifts toward the chimneys, looking for the warm grease-fogs where the Weavers are lacing their glass nets between the stays.

I could leave this hole. 

My twelve hours are nearly run. I can hear the shift-chime now—not through the air, but through the brass tap-wire that runs along the inspection flange. Three short taps, a pause, two long: *Fitter to the rib.* The boys are setting the soup-boilers on the steam-traps in the hollow-rib. There will be hot water with burnt-tuber crust, and the sour bread that tastes of zinc, and the men will sit on their tool-boxes with their lead-cuffs unbuckled, talking about whether the Seventh Horn is leaning an inch north or an inch south.

They will argue about the drop. They will pretend they are holding the mountain down.

I do not unbuckle my cuffs. 

I reach into my jacket and pull out a plug of compressed horsehair and tallow, working it between my fingers until the heat of my palm softens the grease. I have three more expansion collars to pack before the turn of the tide. 

If you ask the Creep-men what I am, they would say I am the Drift-fitter for the St. Jude Tier. They would say my name is Gault or Hallowes, or whatever name was stamped on the brass tally-plate I left hanging on the board by the hoist two generations ago.

But if you put your ear to the rock—not to the iron, but to the cold, freshly cleaved slate where the hydraulic jack sits—you would hear something else.

You would hear that I have begun to take twenty minutes to draw my breath, just like the low-pressure pistons in the floor of the rift. You would feel that my left side is slightly harder than my right, the skin there having taken the temper of the pig-iron from leaning against the flange through four hundred cycles of the swell.

The mountain is not finishing its work, and the iron city is there to make sure it does not hurry. 

And I? 

I am the grease between their teeth. I am the silence that keeps them from breaking each other into powder. I have thirty-two degrees of lean, six inches of bronze clearance, and an oil-can that never quite runs dry. 

When the Horn kicks again—and it will kick before the hour is out—I will not pop three feet into the sky. I will simply slide three-eighths of an inch to the north, take up the slack, and wait for the rock to tell me what it wants to be next.
