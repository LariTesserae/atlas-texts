At first, it feels like falling, but there is no distance long enough to fall through. Every trajectory here is interrupted by an outrigger, a weeping conduit, or the slick grey flank of a transformer jacket before gravity can even gather its weight. 

So it is not falling. It is settling.

I would not be up where the skimmers fly. That requires a nervous system tuned to electromagnetism, a frantic, darting metabolism that burns like magnesium in the draft of the vents, feeding on scum-motes until an exhaust flare fries the leather off its ribs. Nor would I be down in the calf-deep gut of the sumps, where the blind parsnip-pale things roll in the copper wash, all jaw and piston, chewing through hot silt. That is pure muscle without memory, a life lived entirely inside the swallow.

I think I would be found in the gussets. 

The city is full of them: the acute angles where a four-meter diagonal brace meets an extruded vertical spine, or the hollow dead-spaces left behind when a cluster of living cells slips sideways along its grease-tracks to make room for an uncoiling heat sink. There are millions of these pockets. They are triangular, warm as a fresh carcass, and completely ignored by the automated transit shuttles that screech through their pressurized jackets fifty feet away.

To be there, you cannot have soft edges.

I would not be an animal that runs. Running in this damp wears the pads down to the gristle within three cycles; the lime blooms and silica needles that crust every strut act like coarse emery paper underfoot. If you move fast here, you are either hunting or being scrubbed raw. 

Better to be slow. Better to be anchored.

At first, what emerges looks almost like a dweller, but older—or perhaps simpler, stripped of the purse-nets and the braided wire. The barrel chest is there, certainly, broad enough to act as an acoustic chamber for the thrumming that never ceases. But the limbs have lost the frantic reach of the ladder-climbers. The fingers are wide, flat spatulas of bone and horn, the knuckles fused by years of salt-damp into rigid, shovel-like levers that can jam themselves into the fluting of a tile and stay there while the hydraulic purge passes overhead.

No, not completely human. The dwellers still move; they have children in oiled skins and trade cakes of dried chemotrophic mash across the pylons with little tapping rods. They still believe in the possibility of another tier.

I would have stopped looking for tiers.

What takes shape is something that has become part of the drainage geometry. A body that stays long enough in one junction that the lime begins to cement its cuffs to the flange.

Consider the skin: it could not remain skin. The human dwellers must grease themselves continuously with rendered thresher-fat to keep the continuous weeping of the air from ulcerating their flesh into open meat. But tallow washes off in the high-pressure flushes; it pools in the armpits and goes rancid with sulfur. It is a fragile defense. 

A truer body for this trough would have solved the grease problem from within. The hide would be thick—two inches of fibrous, unvascularized rind, cool and slippery like the throat of a squid, constantly secreting a clear, dense mucus that turns the boiling runoff into harmless sheets that shear away before they can deliver their heat. 

The eyes would have finished their retreat. The dwellers keep amber membranes drawn across theirs to dim the blinding white crawl of the arc-welders; but why keep the eye at all? In a world of uniform, shadowless, yellow-marine glare, light carries almost no information. Light tells you only that you are not inside a sealed pipe. 

Instead, the forehead would broaden into a heavy, flat disc of porous bone, pressed directly against the structural ceramic. 

That is where I would be: with my forehead to the wet stone.

Listening. Not with the ears—the ears were sealed over long ago, plugged with plugs of dense, waxy keratin to keep the two-hundred-decibel roar from shaking the brain into pulp—but through the frontal plate and the zygomatic arches. 

When you press bone to ceramic in the basin, the city stops being noise. It becomes a map.

Through the pylon, you can feel the five-mile water column three hundred tiers above as a long, singing pull, a low C-sharp that wavers whenever a turbine gate fouls with silt. You can feel the transit sleds as razor-thin zips of vibration that skate across the roof of the skull from left to right. You can feel the dwellers three gantries down, their little alloy hammers going *tink-tink-tink-tink* against the rail—news of a collapsed filter-frame, a child dropped into an intake flue, a bloom of grease-cake ready for harvest.

And beneath that, the deeper movement: the slow, grinding creep of the foundation plates shifting against the basalt roots of the trough, thirty kilometers down, where the city’s weight is slowly crushing the ancient floor into metamorphic glass.

Who would I be?

Not a guardian. The city doesn't need guarding; it is self-repairing, self-machining, blind, and immortal. 
Not a parasite. The scavengers take; they scrape and chew and flee.

I would be a *dam*. A soft, living valve.

In every hydraulic system, there are points where the pressure becomes too coherent—where the standing waves of the cooling loops build toward a harmonic resonance that would shatter the brittle ceramic flanges or tear the composite sleeves from their anchor bolts. The machines have dampers, but their sensors foul with mineral crust; the metal pistons pit and freeze in the salt air.

A body like this—dense, rubbery, three hundred kilos of water-logged fat, gristle, and slow-beating red muscle wedged into the elbow of a secondary return flue—does what steel cannot. It absorbs the shock. 

The boiling runoff strikes the broad, curved shovel of my shoulders, divides, and curls away, its velocity broken. The rhythmic hammer of the purge-valves punches into my ribcage, and the ribcage—hinged, cartilaginous, flexible as green willow—gives half an inch, dampens the strike, and springs back, bleeding the vibration out into the surrounding frame as low, dispersed warmth.

The little slate-colored arthropods would live on me. They would crawl across the greasy leather of my back, grazing the mats of black chemotrophic slime that take root in the folds behind my neck where the spray is constant. Their tiny, jointed legs would feel like nothing more than an itch, a persistent static that confirms the skin is still whole.

The long, pale runners would pass me by. They would smell the thick, bitter slime-layer, see the lack of thrashing limb, and take me for a bulge in the conduit lagging or an old valve housing that has calcified into the wall. Their claw-tips would ring against the ceramic two inches from my flank as they loped through the cable-forest, blind wedges of gristle chasing skimmers through the yellow steam, and I would feel the air-displacement of their passing as a cool stroke against my side.

Sometimes, a dweller would come. 

They know where the living dampers are. They know that where the water is broken, the current slows enough to let the protein-scum gather in thick, grey foam along the margins of the ledge. They would climb down out of the suspension frames on their braided lines, their pale, barrel-chested bodies gleaming with tallow in the relentless sodium glare, their nictitating lids glowing like dull amber beads.

They would not speak to me. You cannot speak to a thing that has no throat left for air. 

Instead, they would step onto the ledge, their wide, splayed feet planting themselves in the slime beside my flank. They would draw their small scraper-knives and shave the mineral blooms from the pylon around me, clearing the vents so I do not become completely entombed in lime. They would reach down with flat, grease-caked palms and press them firmly against the flat disc of my forehead, leaning in until their own brows touched the bone.

For a few seconds, while the hot water sheets over our shoulders and falls ten thousand feet into the lower sumps, we would share the frequency.

I would give them the pulse: the telltale shudder that means a valve three levels north is about to purge; the deep, cold absence that marks a dead line; the long, singing note of a structural bolt shearing through its sleeve two kilometers into the yellow haze. 

They would feel it humming in their collarbones, grunt once into the meat of my shoulder—a vibration that registers as a dull, pleasant thump against my ribs—and haul their nets full of foam back up into the scaffold dark.

And then the silence would return. 

Not true silence—there is no silence in the sink, only the infinite, roaring metabolism of the floor that has no sky. But the silence of function. 

The water collects on the crown of my head. It gathers in a trembling, fat sheet, runs down the curve of the blind snout, drops off the shelf of the chin, and plunges downward to be boiled again, while the stone under my ribs shudders with the terrifying, beautiful certainty of a machine that will never stop running, and will never have to explain why.
