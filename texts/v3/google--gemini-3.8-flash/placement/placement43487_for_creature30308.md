At first, there is only the compression.

If you were to drop into this chamber from above, looking for yourself, you would look where the eye naturally goes: to the open water, where the current rips like an express train through the murk, or to the bold, hard silhouettes of the stanchions where the piston-lice ratchet across the steel. You would look for something with limbs, or teeth, or at least a recognizable hull.

You would not look into the seam.

I am not in the water. To be in the water is to be worn down to powder by noon, or to spend every calorie of your life clamping your belly to a cold rivet while the grit flays your back. I am inside the lap-joint between two sixty-foot-wide plates of rolled boiler-iron, four hundred feet down on the intake face of a diversion baffle.

The plates were once bolted together with three-inch rivets, set red-hot by hydraulic tongs before the water came. In the centuries since, the scouring wash has sheared the rivet heads off on the current-side, leaving only the headless shanks wedged in the boreholes. The plates want to pry apart; the thousand-ton hydraulic head of the chamber wants to flatten them. They live in a state of shuddering, microscopic fret—grinding together with an amplitude of less than half a millimeter, twelve hundred times a minute, driven by the pulse of the turbine runners three miles downstream.

I am the thing that was laid between them to stop the leak.

I am not the original packing. That was woven asbestos rope impregnated with graphite and boiled linseed oil, tightened down until it was hard as horn. But linseed oil is food to sulfur-reducers, and graphite is an electrode. Over three or four centuries of blood-warm, alkaline bath, the organic matter dissolved, the mineral fibers drifted, and something else took up the space: an accretion, a slow, fibrous colony of lead-tolerant sulfur-felt, growing outward from the dark heart of the joint.

I have no face. I have no front or back. I am forty yards long, three inches thick, and eleven inches wide—the exact dimensions of the flange.

My flesh is dense, black, and greasy, with the consistency of cured tire tread or cold pitch. It is woven through with needles of fibrous antimony and lead soaps, pressed so hard by the iron plates that no liquid can enter me except under catastrophic capillary pressure. I have no blood; my circulatory system is a network of microscopic shear-cracks through which the alkaline brine is forced by the chattering of the steel. As the plates bite down, the water is squeezed through my interior pore-mesh, stripping away the copper salts I have leached from the rivets and depositing fresh crusts of zinc and grease against my fibrous core. 

I do not hear the turbine. Sound here is not sound. Sound is the rhythm with which the upper plate hammers the lower plate. It is a warm, bruising massage that never stops. When the beat changes—when a runner somewhere below swallows a severed dropper or binds on a drift of slate—the stroke shortens. The plates clamp down tighter, biting into my flank with seventy thousand pounds of shear force per square foot. My lead-fibers slide over one another with a dull, subterranean squeak that travels through the bedrock. That is how I know the world: by how much it tries to flatten me.

To my left—only four inches away, where the seam opens into the chamber—is the roar. 

I can feel the lip of myself protruding into that gulf. It is not skin; it is a sacrificial fringe of vulcanized chitin that I constantly secrete and extrude into the scour. The current licks it away as fast as I produce it. At sixty knots, the grit-laden brine acts like an emery belt running against my margins, grinding the black gum into a fine, soot-like slurry that is swept into the dark. If I stop growing outward, the water will enter the joint and begin to hollow me out from behind. If I grow too far, the shear stress catches the protruding tongue and tries to rip my whole forty-yard length out of the seam like a worm from a sidewalk crack. 

So I keep the margin precise. I taste the scour with my edge. 

It tastes of battery-poles. 

Because the plates are iron and the shanks are wrought, and the wash is choked with zinc crumbs shed by the sacrificial anodes on the ziggurats, there is a permanent galvanic current leaking through the seam. It is not much—a few hundred millivolts, fluctuating with the turbidity—but to me it is the whole sky. I am the dielectric spacer. I drink the electron-fall across the iron-zinc boundary. It tingles along my antimony ribs, a steady, sour prickling that fuels the slow, anaerobic synthesis of my wax.

Sometimes, a piston-louse crawls across my exposed edge.

I feel its sixteen pick-feet arrive: *tack, tack, tack, tack*, vibrating through the top plate before it touches me. It does not know I am alive; to the louse, I am simply a rubbery tar-seam that provides slightly better claw-purchase than the bare, lime-slick iron. It settles its hydrofoil shell directly over my gap, using its blunt head to wedge itself against the scour, and its rasping mouthparts drop down onto my margin.

It grinds away at my dead fringe, chewing off the zinc-crust and the greasy lead-tallow I have spent three months pushing out from the dark. The vibration of its rasps is high and thin, like an electric drill against bone. It tickles, if I had a word for tickling. It saves me the work of letting the water scour it. 

When the louse is finished, it moves on, or it makes a mistake. If it lifts its skirt too high, the shear-current catches its rim and tears it away with a sudden *pop* of vacuum that leaves a ringing hollow on the steel where its claws let go. A fraction of a second later, I taste the spray of its burst belly—that pale, scalded pigskin sweetness—drifting past my seam before the alkaline water burns it out.

The days do not exist. There are only the cycles of the lime. 

Every few thousand hours, the subterranean valves shift. That is the only real terror, though terror is too quick a word for something that moves at the speed of settling grease. 

First, the water-column stops shuddering. The hammer-beat of the plates drops from twelve hundred strokes to six hundred, then to three, then hangs in a terrible, floating stillness. For five seconds, the pressure equalizes. 

Those five seconds are the void.

Without the seventy thousand pounds of clamp, I swell. My compressed fibers, held down for half a century, expand by an eighth of an inch, drinking in the hot, un-scrubbed brine that sits stagnant in the gap. It is like breathing too deep after being choked—a burning rush of copper-reek and rotten machine-oil that sets my internal chemistry into a frantic, foaming heave. 

Then the rock groans.

Miles below, the gate-leaf drops into its bronze guides with the weight of an iron cathedral. The entire hydraulic balance of the district snaps sideways. The water-column jerks like an anchor chain pulled taut by a falling ship.

The plates do not simply clamp; they *slide*. 

The top plate lurches forward three inches while the bottom plate is held fast by the bedrock. The headless rivet shanks shriek in their holes, grooving the steel with orange-hot sparks that are quenched the instant they form by the boiling brine. The shear force grabs my forty-yard length and tries to roll me into a sausage, to smear me along the flange and squirt me into the void like lard through a syringe.

This is what I was made for. Not by the men who cast the plates, but by the long, patient logic of the water.

My antimony needles lock. My lead soaps deform, absorbing the lateral thrust and converting the kinetic shock into pure heat, warming my core until the grease within me liquefies and settles into the fresh gouges in the iron. I scream—not with a voice, but with the high, hydraulic whistle of boiling tallow being squeezed through the microscopic pores of my margin into the shearing wash. 

The plate stops with a sound that would break the ears of any creature with lungs. 

The vibration begins again: twelve hundred strokes a minute. The current finds its new groove, tearing across my trimmed edge at seventy knots, screaming over the fresh score-marks in the metal.

The pressure returns. The iron bites. The taste of zinc and copper settles back into its familiar, battery-acid hum.

I flatten. I spread. I fill the gap from end to end, perfectly cold, perfectly tight, keeping the drowned world from getting between the plates until the next shift. I am the silence between two pieces of screaming steel. There is nowhere else to be.
