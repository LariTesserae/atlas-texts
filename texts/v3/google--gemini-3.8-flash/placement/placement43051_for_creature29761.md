Not in the center of the vault. Nothing stays in the center unless it is falling or being torn.

At first, there is only the sensation of being jammed into a margin—a place where two planes of cold feldspar meet at an angle too narrow for the water to turn inside it. A pinch. 

It is dark back in the seam, darker than the great white-gold pillar of sun that drills the open water, but it is not silent. The mountain here doesn’t speak with the slow, grinding groan of deep crust; it snaps. It registers every hammer-strike of the hydraulic column as a high, hard ring, like an anvil struck with an iron rod. The vibrations travel through the mineral grain before the sound even hits the water. To exist here is to receive that impact through the marrow—or whatever serves for marrow when everything soft has either been washed down the flue or pressed flat against the rock until it has the density of horn.

I am not the fish. I have too much friction to be the fish. The loaches are fluid-slick; they are made of oil and pure grease, sliding through the shear like lard through a chute. They don’t hold; they let the mountain pour through their ribs and around their snouts.

Nor am I the diver, though I know his smell. He brings a rank, fatty warmth into the shaft that lasts for three seconds—mutton-tallow, wood-smoke, the dry rot of old leather, the sharp musk of sweat starved of salt. It is an impossible smell down here, an absurdity that enters during the heave and is instantly scoured away when the cavitation returns. He is a visitor who pays for every heartbeat with a crushed vessel behind his eyes. He cannot linger. If he lingers half a pulse too long, the column takes his shins or tears his lungs inside-out through his mouth.

No, what I am has to be able to endure the return of the water.

It begins with the ash.

The timber was brought down green, split with stone wedges on the scree above, hauled down through the sky-slit while its sap was still gummy. When it was driven into the fault, it was raw, pale, tasting of northern dirt and rain. But down here, under the hydraulic press, the wood does not rot as it does in a fen. It doesn't soften. The water drives the sap out and forces itself into the capillary pores of the linden and ash, grain by grain, until the timber is heavier than the stone it braces against. 

It becomes something intermediate: half-tree, half-mineral, bound to the rock by an antler tine that was battered in behind it with an iron mallet.

I find myself at that wedge. Not the wood alone, and not the antler, but the knot where they have been hammered together until their fibers crushed into each other.

The antler was shed by an elk three seasons back, up on the scrub-line where the juniper flattens into mats. It is yellowed, dry, riddled with the pin-hole burrows of bone-mites. But jammed here, beneath sixty cubits of ice-water, wedged into a blind cleft where the feldspar forms a razor-lip, the bone has swollen. It has drunk the melt. The pressure has forced the grease of the loaches into its porous core, greasing the calcium until it doesn't shatter when the cavitation bubble detonates an inch from its tip.

I am the thing that holds the dead-water pocket.

Behind me—leeward, in the tiny, triangular stall created where the ash-pole crosses the antler’s fork—the violence dies. It doesn't become peaceful; it becomes thick. The water here doesn't ricochet. It spins in a slow, greasy circle no wider than a child’s palm. 

In this tiny pocket, the world collects.

The loaches come first, slipping their blunt, pale snouts into the eddy to drag their flanks against the braided gut-string. They leave their shed skin behind—that grey, curdled lipid that smells like cold marrow. It catches on the rough grain of the antler. It smears into the split in the ash. It builds, layer upon layer, until the timber is no longer bare wood, but coated in a slick, leathery mantle that insulates it from the cold.

And on that mantle, the liver-skin grows. 

It isn't plant and it isn't lichen. It is a tough, purplish gristle that feeds on the diver’s tallow and the loach’s grease, anchored to the antler’s pores by thousands of microscopic hooks. 

I feel the rhythm of the mountain not as an eye sees it, but as a wedge feels tension.

When the flumes above choke on their slush, the pressure eases. The ash-timber expands by the width of a hair; the antler tine shifts against the quartz; the dead-water eddy slows until the grease hangs suspended, pearled and motionless in the grey light. In those ten seconds, I feel the diver’s hand. 

It is dead-white, blunt-fingered, shaking with an uncontrollable palsy of cold. The knuckles are gouged from the stone, bleeding a dark, sluggish wine that doesn't bloom into the water because the melt is too cold to let blood disperse; it curls off his thumb like a thread of red wax. His bone scoop scrapes across the ash, right down to the wood, gouging away the curd I have held for six days. 

The scrape is violent, crude, stripping the mantle, tearing away the liver-skin down to the antler’s bare tooth. It hurts—not with the pain of an animal, but with the sudden, terrible exposure of a joint unseated.

Then the heel-strike: two clacks of slate against slate. 

The horsehair line goes rigid. The diver is plucked upward, a dark, heavy bundle hauled into the gold needle of the sun-shaft, kicking his bare heels into the wash.

Then the metallic hum returns.

The flume clears. The water drops three cubits, stalls, and then the great cylinder rises from the dark throat below. 

The hydraulic press hits the crib.

The ash-pole groans, its split grain bending until it threatens to snap, but the antler tine bites deeper into the feldspar seam. A cavitation bubble forms directly on the point where bone meets stone—a void of pure vacuum, silver as a polished coin—and collapses with the crack of a splitting skull. The shock-wave shudders through the antler, through the timber, through the gut-lashing, and down into the roots of the granite needle.

The dead-water pocket collapses for an instant, then reforms.

The curd is gone, scraped clean into the diver's pouch to be melted over dung-fires on the scree, but the water is already bringing more. An amphipod, tumbled from the open wash, is whipped around the timber and smashed against the horn. Its glass carapace shatters; its pale legs twitch once and are caught in the rough grain. A thread of its soft interior adheres to the wet wood. 

The loaches will be back when the sun-wedge tilts. They are already waiting down in the black fissures, their barbels tasting the trace of grease washed loose by the scoop.

I settle back into the quartz. 

The mountain is young; the break is still sharp enough to sever hair. For another hundred summers, the water will try to push this crib down into the belly of the range, and for another hundred summers the ash will swell, the antler will hold its bite, and the grease will gather in the lee. 

I do not look at the sky-slit. I do not look down into the throat where the slate slabs fall to rest among the bones of earlier wedges. There is only the grip: bone against stone, timber against wash, holding three inches of quiet water while the peak screams itself to pieces.
