At first, there is no shape to it, only a differential. 

One side of the world is seventy degrees hotter than the other. That is the whole geography. To lean one way is to feel the dry, radiant bake of the lower flue-linings soaking through whatever serves as skin; to lean the other is to feel the thin, distant threat of a draft that would turn water into needles. But there is no water here to turn. Everything is cured, parched, baked down to the mineral limit.

So you do not turn toward the draft. You turn toward the iron.

You find yourself not at the rim, where the pale crickets sweep the quartz with their monstrous whips, and not yet in the blind taper where the ivory pseudoscorpions dance in the felt. Those are the extremes—the porch and the root. You are in the middle density, where the seam is perhaps three inches wide, where the light has long since failed, and where there is no such thing as an empty space.

To exist here is to be pressed. Not crushed—crushing implies an ending, a violent change of state—but held in a perpetual, sliding clasp. 

You become aware of your edges through what rubs against them. You are flat. Not thin like paper, but thick-flat, like a copper slug that has been beaten on an anvil until its shoulders are rounded and its belly is smooth. Above you, there is the dry, rhythmic drag of another back—chitin on chitin, making that faint, corn-husk rustle that never stops. Below you, there is another. You are not standing on granite; you are slotted between two other densities identical to your own, carried along in a slow, hydraulic creep that has no destination other than the rock face itself.

For hours, perhaps days—time has no markers here except the heat—you do not touch the stone. You touch only the stack. Your legs, short and heavily spurred, find their purchase in the lateral grooves of the shells beneath you. You move because the body behind you has wedged its spatulate head beneath your rear margin, levering upward with the blind, stubborn patience of an iron jack. You yield to it. You slide forward an eighth of an inch, your own head sliding into the hollow between the pronotum and wing-covers of the neighbor ahead. 

It is not an assault. It is the only way anything breathes or feeds in this compression.

Gradually, as the stack shifts, you feel the texture change. The smooth, waxy carapaces give way beneath your claws to something violently hard and sharp: the fresh blast-face of the granite. 

It is viciously cold to your undersides—not cold in the alpine sense, but cold compared to the living oven of the stack. It bites into the soft joints of your tarsi with microscopic teeth of sheared mica and quartz prisms. But on the rock, clinging to the pulverized feldspar dust, is what you have been sinking toward: the blue sheen. 

It is microscopic, a dried fallout of vaporized machine-fat and cylinder oil that has risen three thousand feet through the updraft and condensed on the dry mineral tooth. It has no taste of plant or flesh. It tastes of deep earth, friction, and iron filings. You work your mandibles into the grain, not chewing so much as scraping, drawing the bitter, petroleum-soaked dust into your crop. It is dry as lime, but inside your gut there is a chemistry that asks for nothing else. You need no drop of dew. The waxy varnish of your shell is so tight, so completely sealed, that your life does not evaporate; it simply burns, very slowly, fueled by the soot of the lower levels.

And through it all runs the stroke.

You do not hear it. You have no tympanum for that high, whistling air at the lip. The sound is an impact that enters through your sternum and your six claw-tips where they grip the stone: *whump... whump... whump.* 

Seventy-two times a minute. 

It is the five-ton shoes of the reduction stamps hitting the ore three hundred feet down the flue. Every stroke lifts the granite by half a micron; every stroke lifts you against the weight of the three bodies resting on your back. You do not fight it; you set your gait to it. You advance only on the impact. When the stone jumps, you slip your front claw forward into a micro-fissure; when the stone settles, your weight drives the barb deep into the crack, anchoring you against the downward creep of the stack.

Then, without warning, the stone goes dead.

The silence is not a void; it is a physical weight. The seventy-two strokes cut out mid-cadence—a shear pin broken on the main shaft, perhaps, or a belt slipped from its pulley in the lower works. 

Instantly, you lock. It is not a conscious choice; it is an automatic seizure of the joints that passes through forty thousand carapaces in the same quarter-second. Your tarsi clamp into the rock; your head retracts beneath the heavy shield of your prothorax; your antennae lay flat in their grooves. The rustling stops. The stack ceases to be a fluid and becomes a pavement—a cold, solid layer of horn and bronze wedged between two slabs of gray rock. 

In this stillness, you feel how precarious the seam is. Without the draft from the boilers, the heat begins to leak away. It does not happen fast, but the stone loses its baking glow, settling from the heat of an oven toward the dull warmth of an extinguished hearth. You feel the wind overhead—not as a draft, but as a faint, high singing in the stone, the frost feeling for the crack, waiting to drop its weight into the gap.

You wait. Minutes pass, or three hours. Your legs remain set like small iron clamps. 

Then, deep in the mountain's floor, a boiler sighs. A valve clatters. The long line of shafting groans as the steam takes it, and then—*THUD.*

The rock kicks beneath you. The dust shivers. 

A collective breath, dry as salt poured on slate, sweeps the six feet of the fissure. The limbs unclamp. The stack turns liquid again. The head behind you wedges under your flank, the barbs find their slots, and the slow, blind circulation toward the tallow-crust resumes.

You are not an invader here. You are not a guest of the mountain or a parasite upon the mine. You are the third thing created when iron strikes stone: the friction that took on legs, the soot that learned to walk, waiting out the centuries in the dark flue until the rock finally cools to ash.
