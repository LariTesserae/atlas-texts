At first there is only the bearing.

It is phosphor-bronze, twelve feet across, hollow-cast and seated in a block of green basalt that was leveled before the second Lift. It does not turn fast enough to hum. It gives off a heavy, sweetish, metallic warmth—not the wet warmth of the sky-fog, but something dry and packed, like heated flour. When the shaft makes its quarter-turn, the oil between the bronze face and the iron journal groans: a three-stroke whistle that sounds like a calf being drawn through an auger-hole, followed by thirty seconds of utter silence while the balance-weight descends four levels below.

In that silence, there is the scraper.

It has an edge of beaten brass, four fingers broad, pinned with soft rivets to a haft of carapaced rib-bone. The bone has yellowed until it looks like mutton-tallow, and where the palm grips it, the grain has been filled with three generations of thumb-fat and nickel-dust until it is slicker than glass.

I am not the diver. I have never gone down the siphons with a gull’s crop in my teeth; my skin has the gray turnip-pallor, but it has not thrown the scale. Nor am I one of the Candelers, who sit until their hips weld to the dry-dock flags. I am too heavy for the roofs and too wide in the shoulder for the chimneys where the swallows drown in their own lime.

I am the *Wedge-tender* of the Lower Sump of the Third Arch.

My station is eleven cubits below the waterline of the Saint-Malo canal, in a dry pocket formed by the overhang of the great abutment. It is dry only in the sense that water does not pool above the ankle; the walls weep continuously, a gray, thin sweat that smells of old marrow-tallow and river-worms. Above my head, the floor of the canal is four courses of ashlar, three feet thick, bound with the dead-bone mortar that was mixed when the foundries still had names. Every eight or nine minutes, an iron scow passes overhead, dragged by the submerged cables; you do not hear it with your ears, but the water in your shins tightens, and the moisture on the roof of the pocket falls all at once in a single, greasy shower that leaves gray spots on your sleeves.

My apron is not gull-skin. Gull-skin is for the lightermen, who need to turn their wrists quickly in the locks. Mine is three plies of carapaced hide, soaked nine months in copperas-water until it has the stiffness of boot-sole leather and the dark, dull sheen of a wet plum. When I sit, it does not fold; it creaks and stays propped against the stone like an iron fender.

My task is the crust.

The bronze bearing does not run in clear oil. In Oun, nothing stays clear for forty strokes of the piston. The airborne dust—the gray meal of the stampers, the dried bird-lime that drifts from the high cornices like fine sea-salt, the skin-scurf of two hundred thousand sleds—falls through the light-hatches and mingles with the lard-wash that drips from the overhead trunnions. It makes a cake. If the cake is left for three turns of the great wheel, it hardens under the pressure into a kind of greasy stone, brittle as shale, that cuts into the bronze and scores the shaft.

So between the groans—in that long, wet pause while the water gathers in the gutter behind my heels—I lean my sternum against the safety-rail and push the brass edge along the journal.

It does not peel like wood-shavings. It comes away in rolls, like cold fat scraped from the lip of a kettle, gray-green and heavy with copper-rot. I drop each roll into a low copper tray that sits between my boots.

The sound is *shhh-tuck*. The *shhh* is the blade clearing the lead-fur; the *tuck* is the point hitting the iron shoulder where the keyway is driven.

I have done this through one Clearing and four Lift-quarters. I know the circumference of the journal better than I know the face of the woman who brings the mush-pot down the iron rung-way at the seventh bell. Her name is Krel; she has lost the two middle fingers of her left hand to the cable-pinch at Lock Nine, and the skin of her forearm is mottled with the blue-gray lichen-scabs that come from working the sulfur-flumes. When she lowers the pot—it is sheet-iron, with an ear of twisted bronze wire—she does not speak. She strikes the rung three times with her ladle.

The sound goes through the iron ladder directly into the rock against which my shoulder rests. *Clink. Clink. Clink.*

I reach up. I do not look at her boots; there is no light to see them by, only the yellow, milky eye of the tallow-float that sits in the niche behind the bearing. Her hand comes down through the steam, greasy and gray-white like a skinned knuckle, drops the wire loop over my thumb, and draws back. The porridge is hot—hotter than the air, which is ninety degrees—and smells of salt-scrapings and the tallow they press from the bellies of the sled-calves that die during the roof-shoving.

It tastes of the pot. Everything in the Third Arch tastes of the pot: an iron taste that stays under the back teeth for hours, like you have been sucking on a wet penny.

When I have eaten, I use the scraper to pare the yellow fat that has set around the rim of the pot and drop it into the tray with the bearing-crust. Nothing goes to the floor. If grease falls into the bilge-trench, the buckler-crabs come up from the lower conduits within an hour. They are blind, but they can smell a drop of lard across thirty rods of running water. You hear them before you see them: a dry, clicking rustle, like dry leaves being dragged across basalt, only there are no leaves in this world, so it sounds like nothing else—just sixty cast-iron shells scraping their rims together as they fight for the grease-clot.

Sometimes, when the cable-engine stops for the noon-slack—when the water-gates at the western reservoir are dropped to let the head-pressure rise—the silence becomes very wide.

It is not quiet. In Oun there is no quiet. But the rhythm changes. The deep, four-stroke piston-cough dies down until you can hear the birds above the ceiling—not individual gulls, but that vast, leathery shivering of three million wings adjusting themselves upon the parapets eighty yards overhead, displaced by the sudden lack of steam. It sounds like a great wet sheet being shaken out in an empty vault.

In that slack, I sit on my bone-stool and wipe my arms with a rag of worm-skin.

My arms have the color of the masonry now. They do not get sunburned, because the sun is only a flat dish of tin behind the steam-ceiling, but the gray lime-grit has worked into the pores until my elbows look like they have been turned on a lathe from soft limestone. My knuckles have no skin-folds left; they are rounded and smooth, like the river-gravel they shovel into the mortar-mills.

I look at the shaft. It has three shallow scratches, half a fathom long, that run around its middle like three gray wires. My grandfather made the first one with a chipped scraper when the great frost of the sixty-eighth Lift seized the water-gates and the lard froze in the conduits. The second was made by a boy whose name was not notched on the bunk-posts—he fell between the web and the eccentric during the third hour of the shift, and his belt-buckle scored the iron before the engine could be thrown off the dog.

The third scratch I made myself, twelve years ago, when a mast-gull, blind and bloated with tallow-grease, tumbled down the light-vent and hit my shoulder while the shaft was on the back-stroke. My hand jerked; the brass took a bite out of the metal that smelled of fried sulfur for three days.

Whenever the stroke comes around, that scratch passes under my thumb. It is slightly rougher than the rest—a tiny, cold shudder against the bone of the haft.

*Here,* it says. *Here.*

Then the thirty seconds of grease-drip. The *whuff-whuff* of the birds settling their down against the warm basalt. The iron overhead trembling as the lock-keeper knocks the dog loose with his mallet.

Then the three-stroke groan: the bronze singing against the pig-iron, the water rising an inch in the bilge-trench, and the first curl of lead-colored grease lifting from the lip of the journal, ready for the blade.
