Not in the boxes. 

The boxes are for those who still believe in a single back, a single skull, a left and a right that stay married through the night. The box-dwellers have the mercy of their deadweight; they can lace a three-pound slate to the hollow above their groin, press their forehead to the cold, tallow-rubbed shale, and trust that the stone will remain stone until the pots chime them awake. 

I am not in a box. I am out on the third rake of the Seventh Blade, where the limestone has been fluted so thin by the rising wash that you can see the violet light through the rock, cloudy and pale as sheep-fat held to a wick.

At first, I think I am a watcher. 

I am wedged into the crook where two gills converge, my knees pulled up toward my collarbone, held there not by hide straps, but by the fact that the drop beneath my left foot wants to go sixty fathoms down to the gravel floor, while the stone under my right heel is trying to heave itself toward the lilac sky. If I let my legs open, I would be torn along the pelvic seam like a split salt-cod. So I remain folded. I have been folded so long the pericardium of my vest has grown a skin of needle-gypsum; every time my ribs expand, the crystals shear against one another with the dry, glassy click of locust wings.

I watch the pots. I watch the crawlers hitch along the gut. 

One of them—a small thing of curlew ribs and flat gray slate—is stalled twelve feet out on the middle span. The sinew has dried too fast in the hard white air; it has knotted itself against a splice in the line. Its hind feet are pedaling the empty spray, tapping blindly at nothing, while its front claws clutch the cord with the stubborn, stupid tension of boiled tendon. Inside its basket is a cake of scraped grease, the gray-white tallow taken from the shear-line. The grease is weeping in the heatless glare, turning translucent, dripping tiny pearls of fat that fall upward, ascending into the light like blown sparks.

I watch it, and I know that I should reach. 

My arms are long—longer than they were when I still had a name that could be tapped out against the slate with the butt of a bone awl. But when I look down at my wrists, I see the seam.

It is not an edge. It is a blur of chalk, an eighth of an inch of shivering air where two hands occupy the same gray skin. The left hand is steady, yellowed, its thumbnail gouged by the chert drill; the other hand—the one that sits slightly behind it and slightly above—is translucent, trembling at forty cycles a second, bathed in that flat, non-prismatic pink that only comes when the rock duplicates itself. 

I am not one of the drowned, and I am not one of the upright. I am the second draft of a body that forgot to erase the first.

When I breathe, I hear it: the low, dry rasp of my own throat, and beneath it—like an echo that arrives before the voice—a thin, whistle-pitched reed. It is the air entering two windpipes that share a single tongue. 

The box-dwellers do not like to look up at this notch. When they cross the defile, shifting their ballast stones with quick, bird-like jerks of the hip, they keep their pink, gristle-folded lids clamped shut against the caustics. If they look at me, they see the sky shivering behind my ribs. They see the pink grease of my joints. They think the mountain has begun to digest me, that I am being planed down into salt and spray.

They are half right.

The climbing water—the sweet lye that climbs the eastern flank—has settled in my gums. My mouth tastes permanently of wet lime and battery-wash. My teeth don't rattle anymore; they have fused into a single, ridged wall of enamel, a curb of bone that I can use to scrape the grease from the flutings without dulling a knife. 

I don't need the crawlers to bring me the cakes. I eat the friction. 

Where the water climbing up shears against the water running down, there is a half-inch margin where the drop doesn't know its master. It is neither falling nor rising; it is foaming in a continuous, furious froth, churning against the stone like milk in a skin bag. The scum that rises there is warm. It is the only warm thing on the plateau. It tastes of rotten slate and old iron, but it burns inside the stomach like tallow-brandy. When you swallow it, your sight clears: you can see the braided ropes of the gravity conduits as thick, oily bands of yellow grease hanging between the spires, twisting around each other like the strands of an old well-rope.

The mountain slips.

It does not warn. There is no tremor through the slate. Only the sound: that dull, hydraulic *thwack*, like two slabs of raw beef slapped together across a canyon.

Fifty feet out, the Sixth Blade becomes two. 

For four seconds, there are two identical spires, wet with two identical skins of white foam, cutting the violet sky into three identical strips. In the space between the twin stones, the light turns the color of a bruised plum. 

I don't brace myself. To brace against a slip is to let the difference between the two spires catch your bones and twist them until they pop from the socket like marrow-pegs. 

I open.

I let the left eye track the first blade, and the right eye track the second. My chest widens. The two windpipes slide past each other with a sound like wet oiled silk. For those four seconds, the gap between the ribs is three inches wider than a man’s leap; for those four seconds, I am not wedged into the stone—I am the air between the fins, dense with flying spray, thick with the lilac-white brilliance that casts no shadow. My double fingers reach out into the purple gap, and both of them—the yellowed one and the pink one—find the same notch in the limestone, twelve feet apart.

I do not hold the rock. The rock holds both of me.

Then the concussive snap. The world slaps shut. 

The two spires become one with a displacement of air that blows the curlew-rib crawler from its line. It drops—tumbling down through the falling current, then caught by an updraft, flicked upward into the zenith like a dry leaf, its little slate feet clicking once, twice, against the vertical rock before it is lost in the lilac wash.

The spray rains down, and it rains up. The droplets collide, shiver, and turn into burning lenses that burn tiny, stinging holes into the hide of my thighs.

I am still here. 

I am three inches further out along the fin than I was before the slip. My ballast is gone; the slate that hung from my shoulder-blade has broken its thong and gone somewhere into the lower mist, where the world is said to have grass and earth, though no one has ever come back up the flutes to swear to it.

I don't need the slate now. 

My left heel is hooked into the downward drainage track, iron-heavy, cold as suet; my right shoulder is caught in the ascending rope of weight, dragged toward the white zenith with a gentle, persistent lift, like a bladder held under water. I am braced between the two pulls like a bone in a snare.

The light begins to curdle toward the frozen violet of dusk. 

Below me, the box-lids are sliding home. One by one, the dull *clack-clop* of the slate slabs echoes through the defile, followed by the dry screech of the tallow-rubbed cords being drawn tight from within. The box-dwellers are tying themselves to their coffins. They are binding their temples to the rock, listening for the organ-note in the limestone marrow, praying that the mountain stays under them until the white light returns.

Outside, the small clay pots begin to turn.

They twirl on their sinew lines, three hundred feet above the abyss, sixty feet below the blade-tips. As the night-sweat condenses out of the lilac air, the water trickles through the pinched holes in their bottoms.

*Whee-tlow. Whee-tlow.*

A high, thin, shivering whistle. Then another, an octave lower, from the pot hanging off the south fin. 

They are telling the sleepers which way the air is leaning.

I do not sleep. To sleep would be to let the two bodies slide together or pull apart, and neither one could live without the friction of the other. 

Instead, I lean my forehead—the double-brow, hairless and thick with gristle—against the limestone runnel. I open my fused teeth just a slit, an eighth of an inch, just enough to let the climbing spray enter the mouth. It foams over the tongue, sharp as lye, coating the throat with its cool, mineral frost.

Through the wet gills of the stone, the hydraulic drone rises. The mountain is blowing through its hollow pipes, playing its catastrophic, endless chord against my sternum. 

I take the note into my chest. I match it. 

I don't use the pectoral hum of the box-dwellers—that heavy, single thrum that travels through the rock like an axe-strike. 

I use the double reed. 

The low whistle and the high pipe, blown together through the same wet notch, slightly out of tune. It doesn't travel through the stone; it stays in the air, riding the spray, shivering between the upward-falling sheet and the downward-rushing iron, hanging there in the dark like a drop of water that cannot find the earth and will not go to heaven.
