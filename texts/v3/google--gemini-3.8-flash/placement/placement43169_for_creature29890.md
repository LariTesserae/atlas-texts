I would not want the sky. The violet hairline at the lip is too thin to hold a thought, and the wind out there has nothing to tell except how fast things freeze when they are stripped of their fat. 

At first, trying to find footing here, you look for a vantage. You imagine yourself perched—perhaps up on the rime-frosted lintel where the skimmers crouch on their broom-bristle elbows, or down in the bottom where the grave-wax is deep enough to lie in like a warm, salt bath. But a vantage requires an eye, and an eye in this rift is just an ulcer waiting to happen, a soft grape for the ammonia to turn milky in an hour. To look is to be an intrusion.

So you have to give up the face. You have to let the senses slide down into the plates of the chest and the soles of whatever keeps you from tumbling into the sump.

For a while, you might think you are one of the nits. There is a frantic sort of domesticity to them that feels familiar—the tidy, flat industry of thousands of little hooks working through the smegma, the communal warmth where the three hides meet, the absolute safety of being no thicker than a fingernail between two slabs of eighty-degree lard. You would know the host by its seasonal flavor: whether the jet stream brought spring collembola from the steppes or the dry, bitter pollen of high-desert artemisia. You would feel the mountain only as an occasional shudder through the meat, like a train passing under a cellar. 

But their life is too hurried. They are too small for fifty paces of syenite. They have the nervousness of things that can be smeared flat by a single bad settling of a rib-hinge.

No, you have to go deeper into the leverage.

You find yourself where the weight is. 

Not a free-swimming thing, and not the kivva itself—that fifty-second lung is too lonely, too much like a bellows run by a waterwheel that nobody has oiled since the glacial retreat. To be a kivva is to be thirty feet of blind, wet digestion, waiting for an earthquake to knock you down three tiers so you can spend forty years climbing back up on the heels of your aunts.

I think I would be the point where the elk-horn meets the rock.

Specifically: one of the middle pegs, five feet above the grave-wax moraine, where the syenite pinches so hard that the rock has begun to bite back into the bone.

Consider what that peg is. It was an antler once, grown in the scrub-forests six thousand feet below, velveted and bloody and scraped against mountain birch under a sun that actually felt round. A man brought it up here when the rift was still raw enough to echo. He had dry fingers, grease under his nails, and he drove it into a vertical bedding plane with seven blows of a river-stone that didn't belong to this range either. 

Then he left, or died under a slide of meat, and the kivva closed over the hole like lard poured into a bucket.

Now, five hundred winters in:

The horn is no longer bone, but it hasn't become stone yet. It has drunk four centuries of tallow until its marrow-cells are plugged solid with amber grease. It doesn't rot, because the air in the cleft has no rot in it—only the sour vinegar of kivva-piss and the dry dust of syenite flour. 

If I am that wedge, I have two things that nothing else in the throat possesses:

First, I have the shear. 

The kivva above me weighs as much as an ox, and its third left rib-spur—the amber-colored keratin one—rests directly upon my shoulder. Every twenty seconds, it draws its breath. When it does, its flank rises three inches, the tension eases, and the cold grease of the cleft seeps into the microscopic gap between my horn-grain and its hide. Then comes the suction—that wet, iron cough—and sixty pounds of dead, tallow-slick leverage comes down again, grinding me a thousandth of an inch deeper into the split. 

I know the mountain better than the kivva does. The kivva only knows the back of the kivva beneath it; I know the tooth of the fault. I know that the horn-ridge is twisting three degrees to the northeast. I feel the frost-shatter when the jet stream shifts in November—a high, singing ping that travels through the syenite like a needle through glass, vibrating through my calcified spongy tissue until the old root-nerves, dead these five hundred years, hum with the altitude.

Second, I have the thread.

The *Caloplaca* did not choose me; it had nowhere else to root. The syenite is too greasy for it, and the kivva are too hot. But my core is porous, full of the little vaulted galleries where the red marrow used to manufacture elk-blood. The hyphae have filled every chamber. They are thicker than spiders’ silk now, red as dried liver, soaked in old fat. 

I don't make the yellow light—the lichen does that, burning some slow, cold sugar it wrings from the ammonia—but I am the lamp-wick. The light is useless to the things that live here, but that is why it is honest. It doesn't illuminate anything except three inches of wet belly-hide and the edge of a slate-cut made by a hunter who died before the horn had finished drying. It is a light made entirely for the rock to ignore.

In the earthquake years, when the range grunts and the great mats slip, the sound is like a ship coming apart on a gravel bar—wood-on-wood clatter of tripartite skulls, the tearing of suction plates, the huge, dull *thwack* of sixty tons of meat falling into the tallow-sump.

When that happens, the draft reaches me. 

For ten minutes, before the bodies gather themselves and begin the long, blind crawl back up the anchors, the cold outside gets in. The jet stream drops its frost into the bottom. The grease freezes instantly, turning white and crumbly like mutton-fat on a winter plate.

And there, in the sudden, naked draft, two miles above the timberline, I can feel the shape of the notch: four paces wide, fifty paces long, an open wound in the sky that hasn't yet had the time to heal, held open only by the thickness of an elk's skull-growth and the stubborn, mindless grease of things that don't know they are alive, but refuse to let go of the stone.
