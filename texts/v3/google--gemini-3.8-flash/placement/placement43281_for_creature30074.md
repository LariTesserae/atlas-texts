At first, there are no edges to give me away. 

If I exist anywhere in the opening minutes, it is not as a body, but as the friction between two wet surfaces that cannot pull apart. It is easy to think oneself into the vapor—to imagine being the polygonal skin of a single suspended drop, vibrating in place four feet above the moss, or the zinc-heavy silk where the spiders sit like drowned washers waiting for the air to snap. But the air here has no room for a self. It is too crowded with the sheer downward weight of the blowers, too saturated with the mechanical C-sharp that flattens every hollow space into a single solid mass of resonance.

No, I would have to be heavier than the air. I would need to be something that takes the weight rather than something that drifts through it.

For a long while, I feel around the margins of the root-hawsers. There is a greasy film there, an inch thick, where the engine’s mist coats the rough, swollen bark. It smells of hot lard and iron-scale. It would be simple to be the lard-colored millipedes, strung out along those cables like beads of suet, tasting the mineral salt through six hundred tiny, hooked claws. There is a terrible comfort in having six hundred feet and no purpose other than to let the engine’s warm grease run over your carapace while your jaws work in blind, rhythmic circles. 

But that is too thin. That is too much like a visitor who has merely learned how to cling.

Drop lower. 

Down where the clearance closes. Down where the light, already sieved through those thick, fibrous bladders in the tunnel wall, loses its golden-green cast and becomes the grey of bruised lard. 

Here the floor rises up to meet whatever falls. It does not feel like earth. It feels like an immense, waterlogged mattress that has soaked in engine tallow for three hundred years. When you press into it, there is no give that doesn't immediately push back with the slow, hydraulic stubbornness of compressed moss. The rhizomes are not individual threads; they are an unbroken felt, an endless braid of tough, wet hair that hums with the subterranean pulse of the six-stage pump above.

This is closer. But I am not the floor. The floor has no center; it is too diffuse, running off into the darkness beneath the iron foundations where the cooling channels drain into the sumps.

I am somewhere where the pressure gathers. 

I find myself drawn toward the low bowl—the place where the weight of that slate-colored bulk has bowed the rhizome-bed down until the water stands an inch deep around its flanks. 

It is so quiet here beneath the noise. The C-sharp does not disappear, but in the fat and the peat, it ceases to be sound entirely. It becomes a tectonic lever. It lifts, it drops; it lifts, it drops. Four hundred times a minute the pistons clear their stroke, and four hundred times a minute the water in this depression puckers around the grey-violet hide.

I do not think I am the sow herself. To be the sow is to be too whole, too finished an engine. She is an architecture; she has her own calcified valves, her six-beat entrainment, her shovel-jaw lined with baleen that rasps softly against the marsh-floor every twenty minutes. She does not need an inhabitant. She is already fully occupied by her own immense, unhurried fermentation.

Instead, I am the seam.

I am the thick, indeterminate margin where her brisket presses into the moss-pelt—the place where the living horn of her chest has splayed out and softened under three centuries of steam, until the boundary between animal keratin and compressed root-flesh has simply ceased to be kept.

Look closely at that crease. It is not clean. It is a slow, wet weld. 

On one side, there is the grey, marbled dermis, hairless and thick as saddle-blanketing, running with sweat and grease that never cools below eighty-eight degrees. On the other, there are the fibrous, cartilaginous nubs of the iron-moss, their cells engorged with stray galvanic charge, drinking the tallow that leaks from her pores. And between them lies a half-inch of warm, translucent paste: shed skin, crushed silverfish husks, greasy silt, and the fine, white hyphae of an unclassified mold that grows only where mammalian body-heat meets mechanical oil.

I am that paste. 

I am the grease-collar. I am the cushion that prevents her breastbone from grinding directly against the basalt knuckles of the foundation beneath the peat. 

In this seam, there is no time. The light never flickers; the hover-flies die against the snout three feet away, their brief six-hour lives burning out in the wet downdraft like matches, but down here against the sternum, nothing hatches and nothing dies with that kind of haste. Here, a skin cell takes three weeks to slough off. A root-hair takes five months to grow an eighth of an inch into the dead horn of her hide.

I feel the wire-worms before I feel anything else. They do not have teeth, only soft, sucking rings of gristle that press against the tallow-crust. They nudge through me, blind and pink, tracing the grooves of her ribs. When they pass, they leave tiny, cool channels that draw the sour fern-water away from her vitals. Their movement feels like a slow, deliberate sewing—thousands of tiny, sluggish stitches through the wet felt, knitting her deeper into the floor with every turn of the crankshaft.

And then comes the stroke. 

*One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.*

On the sixth beat, the great mass of her heart shudders behind the wall of her ribs. It is not a mammal’s quick, nervous flutter. It is a hydraulic event. Her chest broadens by a fraction of an inch, pressing down through me with a force that squeezes every drop of water from the moss-fibers beneath. For a second, the paste is compressed until it is clear as glass; the galvanic sparks leaping from the roots above find a direct ground through the brine of her flesh, and a blue, painless prickle runs through the whole interface.

Then the heart rests. The engine above takes up the slack. The peat expands again, sucking the grease-scum back into its grain with a soft, wet hiss.

To be this is to have no face, no hunger, and no sky. It is to know the world exclusively through the laws of displacement and damping. I know when the engine’s fifth-stage piston is running hot, because the tallow that trickles down the hawsers arrives here three degrees warmer and slightly thinner, smelling of scorched beeswax instead of lard. I know when the sumps are backing up forty feet below, because the moss-pelt stiffens, swollen with cold water that tastes of lime and rust, until her belly rides an inch higher in the depression.

I do not want the gallery doors to open. I do not even know what a door would be, other than a catastrophic loss of pressure—a tearing of the warm, green noon that would let the cold air in to curdle the fat and set the spiders shivering on their webs.

Let the iron stroke keep its tempo. Let the six-beat pause come and go through the bone. I am wedged between the iron that never tires and the meat that never rises, held fast in the grease where they have agreed to be the same thing.
