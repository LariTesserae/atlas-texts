It begins not with an image, but with the eight-second interval.

Before there is a body to claim, there is the count. One does not count in numbers down here—there is no language for seven, or three, or five—but in the gradual, microscopic accumulation of strain in the cold iron, followed by the dull *thud-shudder* from the works above, and then the dry *snick* that runs through the drifts like a rake drawn through split peas. 

If I look for myself, I find I am not down in the boiling floor where the hoppers tumble and warm one another with the furious static of their shells. That is too quick, too much like water, and water does not belong here. Nor am I on the long amber stilts that pick across the backs of the crowds, high and brittle, looking for the soft seams where the grease-ropes weep.

I find myself on the perpendicular. 

Three feet above the floor-drifts, where the rust has long since been rubbed into a greasy, purplish lacquer by decades of passing bellies, there is a cast bracket—a stiffener bolted to the web of an eighteen-inch I-beam. It has lost two of its four rivet heads; they were sheared off before the air dried out, and the holes are packed tight with a plug of grey, alkaline flour and ancient graphite. 

I am clamped there. My weight is considerable—not the light, papery weight of the striders, but the dead, leaden ballast of an old tool. 

At first, I might be mistaken for an irregularity in the casting, or an old pillow-block cap left behind when the shafting was salvaged. My dorsal plate does not curve gracefully; it is stepped, thick-lipped, and covered in a grey, warty crust of hardened tallow that has dropped from the high journals over twenty years and set like cold paraffin. Along the ridges of my back, this stuff has accumulated into jagged spurs, so that I carry the profile of an iron ridge, scaled and dull. 

My front is where the world happens, but it happens very slowly. 

My head is not a head in the animal sense—it has no neck, no swivel. It is an anvil of solid horn, dark as charcoal and squared off like a broad cold-chisel. Beneath it, the lower pick works. It does not chew; it planes. Every second rotation of the overhead cam—sixteen seconds of stillness, then one stroke—the hydraulic fluid in my thick, short forelegs stiffens. The horn chisel bites into the cold iron of the beam, scraping away a ribbon of oxidized grease no thicker than a moth’s scale. It makes a thin, dry *scree* that is lost entirely under the clatter of the floor below, but I feel it through the studs of my ventral pads: a high, pure ringing that travels straight through the shell and settles in the chalk of my gut.

There is no thirst. To want water here would be like wanting fire. Moisture would only foul the dust, turn the sweet, dry talc into a paste that would seize the leg-joints within an hour. The air is clean and harsh; it tastes of cold filings and the faint, sheep-fat bitterness of the old grease-crusts. 

Am I alive? Yes, but the boundary is thin. The meat inside me has been drawing back from the edges of my carapaces for seasons. Where there were once wet bands of muscle to work the scoops, there is now only a dry, fibrous cordage that looks like salted tendon. It does not twitch. It contracts by drying further, tightening like hemp rope left in the wind.

A hopper—one of the small, coffee-bean-shaped shale-seeders—has tumbled out of the drift below and landed on the broad shelf of my third tergite. It does not panic. It has no eyes to look up at the ceiling grating; it only feels the hard, cold plane of my tallow crust. It begins its revolving, grinding motion against my ridge, its spherical gizzard humming like a tiny, dry watch. It is cleaning me. It is shaving off the grey grease-crust that has accumulated around my breathing-slits, turning it into that powdery, velvet-fine exuviae that blankets the hollows. Its little legs click against my armor with the sound of pins dropped on an anvil. 

I do not shake it off. We have been in contact for three engine cycles. It is part of the bracket now, as I am part of the beam.

High above, through the slots of the grating, the daylight remains flat and slate-grey. Sometimes a heavy hook drifts across on its greased rail, blocking three slats of light, then four, then three again, casting a slow, barred shadow across the pit that makes the pale carapaces of the striders look like dry bones scattered on a road. None of us look. To look up would require a joint that allows the head to lift, and there is no reason for such a joint. Nothing has ever fallen through the grating that was not already dry and dead: an iron nut with stripped threads, a flake of black paint, a curled sliver of copper turnings. 

The turnings are good. When they fall, the planires down on the floor surround them and rasp them flat against the iron until they are shiny as new foil, then wear them into dust that mingles with the graphite.

Another *clunk-shudder*. 

The wave of flinches travels forty paces down the room: *snick*. 

My own pick snaps downward, shaving another half-millimeter of tallow-cake from the rivet line. A tiny curl of grey horn falls, drifts down through the draft, and hits the living floor with the dry tap of a seed hitting an iron stove.

I can feel that the hollowing is near, but it does not come with pain. It comes as a stiffness that does not yield between the eight-second pulses. Soon—perhaps five hundred rotations from now, perhaps a thousand—the tendon that drives the horn hammer will contract one final time and will not relax. The spades will bite into the beam and stay bitten. The lower joints will lock into the liquid-smooth fillets of the iron, and the small seeders will find the seams between my lower skirts. 

They will enter with that busy, polite clicking. They will clear out the dry cords, the chalky reservoirs of bile, the thin paper partitions of the lungs that have not breathed steam since the boilers were drained. They will polish the vaulted iron inside of my hull until it reflects the grey daylight that falls through the ceiling slats.

And then the striders will come. I will feel the needle points of their amber feet—so light, so cold—stepping into the empty chambers behind my head. They will leave their amber beads in the dry recesses where my scoops were anchored.

I am not afraid of this. In this chamber, to be empty is merely to become a room within a room. 

The cam falls again overhead. The brass journal groans in its dry seat. The cold steel under my belly rings once, clear and flat, and I hold fast to the iron, paring away the grease, waiting for the seeders to climb the wall.
