At first, there is only the refusal of the water to give way.

It does not part when an edge enters it; it shears, slowly, leaving a temporary seam in the grease that takes hours to heal behind whatever has moved. To exist here is not to swim or to rest, but to be held in a vise lined with cold oil. 

If I look for my limbs, I do not find fingers or fins. I find edges. Flat, bevel-ground planes that feel the cold south-facing slate not as a surface to stand upon, but as an intolerable, magnetic ache—a sideways floor that wants to become a wall, that wants to become the sky. 

I am not the Litigant. The Litigant has a destination, a terrible, six-inch certainty that draws it toward the keyway like an anchor sliding through grease toward an eyelet. I do not have that kind of forward mass. 

Nor am I one of the turners whose needle-knees tap the herringbone tiles into their stiff beds of saliva and silt. I hear them, though. The sound does not enter through ears; it enters through my plates. Three dry clicks: the slate rings at the pitch of a struck teacup, then the vibration dies in the liverwort, swallowed instantly by the bristles. A pause. Four weeks of sawing. Another click. They are too frantic, too small, too full of a mechanical terror that calls itself masonry. They believe the split can be cured if only the angle between the clay and the rock is made square to the tenth part of a hair. 

I am older than their tiles, but younger than the split itself. 

Perhaps I began as a shaving—a curl of slate scraped loose when the mountain broke open three winters ago, or a sliver of lead peeled from the skirt of that reliquary as it tumbled down through the quarry’s dark to wedge itself in the jaws of this fracture. But lead does not watch. Slate does not wait for the tap to return.

I find myself wedged in the dark zone behind the arch, where the sideways gravity ceases to pull simply south and begins to twist—a torque like the wringing out of a heavy, soaked cloth. 

Here, my body has settled into the shape of a wedge. Not a tool laid down, but a tool in the act of being driven.

I am flat—perhaps three scales thick, overlapping like the shingles of a drowned steeple—and my front is not a mouth, but an unyielding, chisel-shaped lip of cold iron-grit, bound together by the dried secretion of the pale worms. I don't breathe the water; I displace it, molecule by molecule, pressing myself into the hairline fissures that the turners haven't yet reached with their paste.

My work is not their work. They pave the floor because they fear the wound; they want to hide the raggedness of the split under a neat herringbone skin. 

I do not want to hide the split. I live inside its grain. 

I am the shim.

When the white, glass-hard roots of the hornwort drive their tips into the bedding beneath the tiles, I feel the strain five inches away. The stone does not groan—slate has no elasticity for groaning—it merely tightens its microscopic teeth. And in that tightening, I feel the hydraulic gel shift. The mint grease drifts against my flanks, thin and sharp as camphor, coating my edges in a cold, amber frost that never solidifies.

I spend entire seasons levering myself forward by the breadth of a single diatom. My needle-feet—only two, shorter and thicker than those of the turners, more like the pins of a rusted lock—find the pits where the iron has leached out of the stone. I hook. I set my bevel against the cleft. And with the glacial, indifferent momentum that belongs only to submerged things, I press.

I am widening it.

Not quickly. The turners do not even know I am here; when their knees click against the tiles, they think the microscopic tilt they measure against the hanging bellies of the white worms is the mountain shifting above them. They think the quarry is settling its immense, drowned weight. They tap three times, listen to the deadened echo, and hurry to scrape another root flat, never suspecting that behind the three-inch arch, anchored in the recursive depth where their saliva cannot cure, something flat and heavy is leaning back against the south slab, prying.

Why? 

Because the Litigant must not fit.

I have seen its chisel-cut flanks through the curdled green light. I have tasted the wintergreen of its sweat as it drifts past my bevel. It is a plug, yes, but it is an ending without a release. It wants to pin the slate forever into a dumb, airtight silence—to seal the seam before the stone has finished breaking, to trap the cold grease and the young thorns in an eternity of perfect, unbreathing contact. The Litigant is the law of the reliquary: preserve, encase, drown.

If the archway holds, if the herringbone floor remains true to the thousandth of a millimeter, the lead needle will seat into the keystone, and this world will turn to iron-stone in an instant, frozen midway through its first, crowded breath.

So I work the seam. 

A root dies; I take its place before the turners can scrape the socket clean. A tile lifts; I drive my iron-grit lip a fraction of an inch under its skirt, holding the angle open, keeping the fracture raw, uneven, and alive. 

The water here is heavy as tallow. It presses against my plates with an intimate, unyielding weight that would crush a creature of air. But under that weight, there is a rhythm. 

*Click.* A turner taps the floor. 
*Click-click.* The answer from the south wall. 
A long, dense silence while the capillary creep moves three molecules of oil from west to east.

Then, from the center of my own small, plated mass, where the iron-grit meets the lead: a tiny, unhearable shudder. 

The slate yields by a micron. The archway leans half a degree out of plumb. 

In the hydraulic gel, the Litigant drifts an imperceptible fraction of a hair to the left—and for another forty winters, the keyway will remain an open door.
