At first, the instinct is to look for a vantage—a perch above the writhing tiers, or a foothold on the cold side of the sill where the mountain air might grant an outline to the body. But there is no exteriority to borrow from here. To arrive in this crack is to be immediately shorn of perspective; there is no distance across which to look.

So I am not standing outside looking in. And I am not, I think, one of the scrapers near the light.

Their existence is too bright, too frantic, consumed by the simple arithmetic of the scald. To be one of those thumb-sized engines near the mouth would be to have no past tense at all—only the continuous, violent *now* of the nail against the glass, the hot ribbon of tallow passing through the ring of the mouth, the blind scramble over three wet backs to reach the brine-pulse before the forty-minute burn of life runs dry. If I were there, I would not be an *I*; I would be a muscle twitching inside a collective spasm, dropped into the frost before I could even register the edge.

No, my presence settles deeper, where the frenzy begins to slow under its own unbearable weight.

Perhaps, for a moment, I am the nematode—suspended in the rising steam, neither sinking nor rising, drunk on atomized lard. There is a strange, drifting peace in that: hair-thin, tossed on the breath of five hundred pulsing gills, watching the bruised-violet pits of the scrapers flare into raw pink below me. To feel oneself stiffening, minute by minute, as the silica leaches from the mist into one’s skin, trading flexibility for sharpness, waiting for the split-second when the thread of flesh snaps into an instrument, dropped into a worker’s palm as a chisel. 

It is tempting to linger there, in that brief interval between vermin and tool. 

Yet even that has too much motion. The crack has an appetite for stillness, tucked away in the places the light cannot reach. 

As I move inward—drawn not downward, but sideways, along that unnatural, horizontal drift where the eggs roll like oiled beads toward the back wall—the air changes. The wet, greasy heat gives way to a pressure so dense it feels crystalline. The acoustic deadness deepens. The wet *clop* of peeling skin and the glass-chisel clicks fade into a single, low, hydraulic groan: the mountain trying to close its teeth.

This is where I find myself. 

Not in the breeding shelves. Not among the newly hatched, tearing their way out of parchment envelopes with embryonic nails. 

I am wedged into the corner where the geometry slips.

I am one of the anchors, but not an old one—not yet the hard, amber resin that has completely forgotten what blood felt like. I am in the long, slow middle of the transformation. 

My skull is broad, spade-flat, pressed hard into the junction where two plates of dacite meet at a sickening, impossible shear. The pressure on the crown of my head is absolute; it is the entire tectonic mass of the spur, millions of tons of black volcanic glass trying to collapse inward to heal this wound, held back only by the resistance of my cervical joints and the flat wedge of my forehead. 

My limbs are long, spidery, double-hinged, tucked tightly into the flutes of the stone like folded calipers. I do not scrape. I do not gorge. The cartilage ring of my mouth is dry, pressed shut against the cold mineral seam. Through the translucent parchment of my ribs, my dark organs churn with an agonizing, glacial economy, processing not the fresh tallow of the front tiers, but the dry, iron-heavy vapor that leaks from the mountain’s folded root.

It smells of ancient dust, crushed pyrite, and dried albumen. 

From here, looking forward along the impossible shelf—a perspective that somehow stretches back twenty yards through a boulder that measures three feet wide from the sky—I can see the back of the entire machine. 

I see the red, pulsing wave of their heat-pits, thousands of them, like coals stirred in a deep stove. I can feel the collective vibration of their feeding traveling through the rock, arriving at my ribcage not as sound, but as a fine, microscopic tremble that keeps my drying marrow from settling entirely into stone. I see the pale drift of the nematodes floating like ash in the mid-steam. I see the discarded ribcages at the far mouth vitrifying into white porcelain.

I know what will happen to me, and the knowing is purely physical, devoid of dread. 

The heat in my belly is cooling, turning from the raw, wet boil of the front tiers into a stiff, sluggish warmth. The tallow under my skin is not sloughing off to feed the others; it is thick, polymerizing under the mountain's shear, turning heavy and clear. My eyes—if they had ever existed—would be useless here, but my heat-pits can feel the precise lines of force running through the dacite. I can feel where the mountain is weak. I can feel where the void wants to fold shut and crush the nursery into grease. 

And so I lean harder into the non-existent angle. I spread the flat plate of my skull wider into the seam.

I am the gasket. I am the thing that holds the room open.

Eventually, the last pulse of the brine will miss my shelf. The vents will shift an inch to the left, diverted by some fresh glass-borer near the front, and the dry, iron-cold air will finish its work. My limbs will lock in their double-hinges. The skin over my ribs will stretch until it hardens into a clear, resinous varnish, through which some future scraper—crawling back into the dark when the press becomes too great—will see my motionless, amber heart. 

I will remain here long after the mountain forgets why it opened. A wedge of petrified meat, holding apart two walls of glass that should have touched before the clouds were formed.
