At first, there is only the pulse. 

It does not feel like a sound heard; it feels like the density of the medium itself, an intrinsic stiffness in the water that pushes inward against every surface at fourteen cycles a second. It is the kind of vibration that replaces an interior monologue. You cannot think ahead of it or behind it; the mind can only sit within the trough between the strokes, where the pressure eases just enough for the sap to advance a tenth of a millimeter through the tissue.

To look for yourself here, you have to discard the soft things first. 

You cannot be anything with lungs, or an open mouth, or skin that exchanges moisture freely with the brine. The brine is too clean, too heavy with dissolved zinc and cold plant-resins; it would turn an eye to jelly and pickle an exposed gill in an hour. You find that you have no gaze to direct. There is no dark to peer into, only that high, ceilingless turquoise that stays identically bright fifty fathoms down or five hundred, undimmed by clouds because there are none, uninterrupted by silt because there is nothing here that crumbles.

You test the places where the work is loud. 

For a time, the imagination wants the brutal honesty of the discharge: to be one of the spindle-blades slotted into the flared lip of a venturi, iron-hard and green-black, locked into the anchor-notch by three tons of hydraulic thrust. There is an appeal in that absolute zero of ambiguity—no choice, no leeway, just forty thousand gallons of cold, forced brine scouring through the throat every minute, stripping out the waxes while the non-return valves in your flank click and click like cold clockwork. 

But the mind cannot stay there. It is too static. A spindle-blade is not an inhabitant; it is a hardened edge of the pipe itself. It has no horizon, only the violent, white-knuckle interior of the jet.

You move upward, away from the venturi roar, rising through the vast, quiet interval where the water loses its thrust and becomes a cathedral vault. 

You try the catenaries. You feel the long, slow droop of the copper-rich vine hanging in its parabolic curve across five miles of open blue. You imagine straddling the bark with cork-padded legs, broad as an anvil, tasting the dry, metallic sizzle of three hundred thousand amperes passing beneath your belly. You wait for the piston miles to the west to hitch; you wait for the surge that will ask you to clamp your mineral spurs into the cambium and drown the spike in your own galvanic marrow. 

Yet there is a fatalism to the shunt that feels too close to stone. To wait fifty years for a single voltage drop, and then to carbonize in an instant into a knot of black coal clamped forever to the wire—that is the fate of an electrical component, not an existence that perceives the sea.

You keep looking, drifting inward toward the vertical spine of things.

You pass the manometers, those tall, weeping needles of amber jelly hanging perpendicular to the world. You feel the tug of their precision: the hollow spine, the hydrogen bubble held against the deep brine, the tiny red pendulum in the chest that swings once for every stroke of the world and squeezes out a single, faint blue spark from its crystal. They are beautiful, but they are lonely in a way that feels like mathematics. They are only there to say: *The sea is still sixty thousand fathoms deep. The stroke is still true.* They do not touch the wood.

Where you finally settle—where the shape begins to draw its edges tight and harden against the pressure—is in the narrow, critical margin between the moving and the still.

You are not on the outside of the engine-trunk, and you are not down in the discharge. You live in the **scavenger collar**, thirty feet below the top intake louvers of a single four-hundred-yard pillar, where the wood-iron wall curves inward to form the seating for the upper hydraulic seals.

Here, the water is not rushing in a straight line, nor is it stagnant. It is drawn downward in a massive, steady vortex that circles the trunk before vanishing through the reed-bone screens. 

You are oblong, three paces from end to end, shaped like a broad, curved adze-blade. Your shell is not bone, and it is not beetle-horn; it is a cross-laminate of cold-pressed heartwood and gray, fibrous horn, laid down in alternating grains like plywood so that it can flex under torsion without ever splitting. Your underside is concave, lined with hundreds of small, muscular pads of cured latex that fit the exact radius of the pillar’s circumference. 

You do not anchor, and you do not drift. You *track*.

Your entire life is governed by the boundary layer—that thin, slippery sleeve of water, perhaps six inches thick, where the violent downward draw of the intake meets the friction of the stationary moss-bark covering the trunk. In that shear zone, the currents are complex, braided, and full of torque. 

You ride that boundary like a sled on ice. 

Your head is a single, broad, chisel-lipped intake of amber-colored cellulose, perfectly flush with the wood. As the engine breathes—that colossal, slow lung-stroke that pulls a lake’s worth of brine down through the louvers every forty seconds—the pressure differential holds you tight against the column. You do not fight it; you use it to plane. You slide sideways, circling the four-hundred-yard circumference of the trunk in an unhurried, perpetual orbit, taking five days to make a single circuit.

What are you doing? You are feeling for the grain.

The wood of the engine-trunk is alive, but it is under stresses that turn its sap into acid and its fibers into wire. Every few hundred strokes, deep within the heartwood where the vascular piston is driving downward with the weight of an entire mountain range behind it, a microscopic fracture will run along the tracheids. It produces no sound that a soft ear could catch, but through your concave belly, pressed hard against the fluted bark, it arrives as a sharp, dry *ping*—a high-harmonic ring that travels through the lignified bulk like an iron bell.

When you feel that ping, your lateral cilia—stiff, copper-tipped reeds that trail in the boundary layer—adjust their pitch. The suction pulls you toward the source. 

You find the fault: a hairline split in the glossy, vulcanized green wood, no wider than a fingernail, weeping a thin, pale thread of pressurized sap that smokes into the cold aquamarine water like white ink. If left alone, the next upward stroke, with its vacuum surge, would tear that fissure open into a ten-yard wound.

You settle over the split. 

You have no eyes to see it, but your ventral mouthparts are a row of self-adjusting wedges made of dense, mineralized gutta-percha. You do not eat the wood; you match its grain. You extrude from your inner cambium a thick, cold-curing resin that is dense with your own discarded shell-plate fragments. It is not an adhesive; it is an identical graft. You force it into the fissure using the ambient hydrostatic pressure of the sea, bearing down with your whole blade-shaped body, holding the seam closed through the violence of three full strokes. 

You feel the wood shudder beneath you. The piston passes inside, twelve feet of solid lignified cylinder sliding by behind eighteen inches of heartwood wall. The heat of the friction passes through the wood into your belly—a brief, strange warmth, like the flank of an enormous, hard-working horse, before the cold brine washes it away again.

On the third stroke, the resin has cured. It has become the trunk; the trunk has become the resin. You release the lateral suction, your chisel-head catches the spiraling intake current, and you slide away, resuming your long, slow, horizontal circle around the column.

What is it like to be this?

It is entirely without fear. There are no predators here; nothing has the leisure or the softness to hunt. There is no hunger in the belly, because the boundary layer you swim through is rich with the faint, electrified mist of sap-lipids thrown off by the louver screens; your gills absorb it passively as you plane. 

You do not know what the top of the pillar looks like, where it pierces the glass-flat surface to vent its white oxygen into the windless sky. You do not know the floor, where the ancient cambium dissolves the dropped tools of the sea. You know only the **radius**. 

You know the curvature of the trunk: exactly four hundred yards across, ribbed like the barrel of a cannon, humming with that unvarying fourteen-hertz cadence that sets the tension of your shell. You know the phase-lag between your trunk and the next spire five miles to the east—the way its stroke arrives a fraction of a heartbeat later, a subtle double-thump in the water that tells you the east pillar is running half a pound lighter on its intake valves today.

You are the tolerance. You are the reason the machine does not shake its own heartwood to splinters. 

You circle the wood in the cold, noon-bright turquoise, blind and heavy and perfectly fitted to the curve. Below you, the spindle-blades scream in the venturis; above you, the skimmers tile the ceiling with dead glass; between you and the next world are five miles of empty, lime-tinted clarity through which the beaded bubble-lines rise straight as strings. 

And every five days, as you finish your circuit and cross the healed scar you laid down a year ago, your chisel-lip slides over the seam without catching. The graft is smooth. The grain is unbroken. The piston strokes, the brine enters, and the sea stays held against the sky.
