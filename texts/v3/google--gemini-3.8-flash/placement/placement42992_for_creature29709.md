It begins with the four seconds. Before there is a body, there is the interval. 

One: the pressure drops in the ears, a thin, hollow pull that draws the grease-film tight across the surface of the sumps. Two: the intake blades four miles up bite into whatever passes for air above, and the deck plates sing against their anchor bolts—a dry, ascending whine that travels faster through the rolled iron than through the wet fog. Three: the air gathers its mass, a cold wall shoving through the conduit mouths, thickening until the fine mist stops hanging and starts to travel sideways in flat, white ribbons. Four: the *whuff*. It hits the breastbone first. Not as sound—sound was traded away weeks or seasons ago for a continuous, low-frequency hum like a wet finger run around the rim of a lead tub—but as displacement. A fist made of cold fog and pulverized iron water that pushes the stomach against the spine and sets the slack in the messenger cables dancing in their brackets.

Then the slack falls back. One.

If I look for where I am standing, the boots are wrong. They are not the lead-slabbed brogans the culvert-tenders lash together out of cut gasket and wire. Those are for men who still think about walking away, men who want to keep their arches from breaking when they carry three gallons of grey condenser tallow up an eight-gauge ladder. 

My feet are thinner than that, flatter to the web. I have come down out of the high dry-gussets where the birds clatter, but I have not gone all the way into the sump where the horned fish graze the aggregate until their bellies shine like fresh solder. 

I am in the middle damp. The shelf-work.

Here, the six-foot I-beams that carry the lower access ways have an inner flange—twelve inches of rolled mild steel, primed once with red lead before the war or the project or the shift ended, now covered in that cold, colorless pelt that is half liverwort and half machine scum. It is narrow, but it does not shift. The water that runs here is not the thick, black discharge of the spillways; it is the secondary drip, the run-off that has already been combed twice through the mesh of the upper deck gratings. It is clear enough to see a dropped washer through, though it tastes of battery plates and old zinc.

I find myself holding a scrap of split copper tube, about the length of a forearm, beaten flat at one end to make a spud. 

Not a chisel—a chisel belongs to the ones who strike the beams to say *here, this is my sump, keep your wire-net out of my drain.* The spud is for peeling. 

Between the web of the beam and the concrete apron, the tar has squeezed out under three hundred thousand tons of post-tensioned bridge deck. It comes out like cold licorice, scalloped by the four-second gasp, hard enough to crack a tooth on the edges but soft and greasy in the seam where the earth-heat still leaks through the rock below. The slugs follow it. If you slide the flat of the copper between the iron and the lard-rind, they come away whole, heavy as wet sausages, their undersides still rippling with that slow, blind rasp that wants to eat the red lead off the beam.

You don't eat them. Nothing eats them that wants to keep its tongue; they are full of ground iron-filings and the bitter lime that leaches out of the green cement. But if you slit them down the belly with a split rivet and lay them in a gallon can over a three-wick wick-pot, the fat runs off clear as lard oil. It doesn't smoke. It doesn't foul the glass of the lanterns. 

That is who I am becoming: the one who stays near the lights.

Not the big sodium lanterns—those belong to the company, or whatever automated circuit still throws the breakers from the central core every ninety hours. Those are thirty feet up, swinging on their rusty chains, throwing their huge, dirty amber cones across the drowned bays where no one can walk. 

I keep the small ones. The bracket lamps. 

They are salvage: square-bodied brass inspection lamps from the construction era, bolted into the web-stiffeners every forty yards along the secondary baffle. Each one has a four-inch reflector of stamped nickel, clouded with lime scale until it looks like mutton tallow. If you leave them alone, the cold soak coats them in twenty minutes; the mist hardens into a mineral scab that turns the amber white, then grey, then blind. 

I move between them on the flange, belly to the iron, one hand hooked into the rivet line, the other carrying the scraper and the tallow-rag. 

It is slow work because of the birds. 

They aren't above me anymore; they are level with me. In the dark between the sodium cones, the pale crows sit along the purlins like dirty laundry hung out in the rain. They don't fly—there is no room to open a wing between the three-meter water mains and the cable racks, and the air is too heavy with water to get a lift off the down-stroke—but they follow. They know the scrape of the copper spud against the zinc. It sounds like a sculpin’s chin-barb hitting an iron grate. When I pry a slug free, they lean forward on their thick, scabby toes, their milky eye-covers sliding back and forth, clicking their beaks against the deck bolts in that fast, dry, stuttering rhythm that mimics the solenoid trips on the pump towers.

"Not yours," I tell them, or I think I tell them. 

The sound doesn't leave the hood. My mouth is muffled in three layers of felt cut from an intake filter, greasy around the lips where the breath condenses and turns back to cold water. The deaf-silence inside the rubber cowl is different from the deaf-silence outside: inside, it is all pulse—the carotid artery hammering against the jaw, the slide of the tongue against the teeth, the click of the cervical vertebrae every time the four-second turbine slap drops the chin an eighth of an inch toward the chest. 

Outside, the silence is hard, like an anvil that has been struck so many times it has forgotten how to ring.

I have three lamps clean when the deck begins to taste different.

You don't smell the gates; the cold ozone from the turbine vents kills the nerves in the nose after an hour on the lower tiers. You feel the grease-line change on the iron. 

Six inches below my flange, there is an eight-inch weld where the transverse girder meets the seat. For twenty minutes, the water has been lapping at the lower edge of that weld—a flat, oily wash that leaves a scum of grey froth like soap-suds. 

Now the froth stops drifting. It hangs. Then it begins to stretch out into long, thin triangles, pointing back toward the dark quadrant where the secondary sumps drop into the floor. The water is pulling back into the concrete teeth. 

The crows know it before I do. They don't look at the weld. They don't look upstream. They simply turn around on the cable-trays, all together, like laundry swinging on a line when the draft reverses, and push their heads down between their shoulders until their necks disappear into their bleached ruffs.

One.

Two.

Three. 

The klaxon doesn't reach the ears, but it gets into the iron behind my knees. A triple shudder, dry and thin, like someone dropping three heavy sacks of bolts onto the deck half a mile back in the dark. 

I don't climb down. Down is the sluice, and down is where the wedge-headed fish are already locking their horn-spines into the timber-grain of the concrete bevels, turning their rubbery snouts into the current so the skin doesn't tear off their ribs. And I don't climb up, because the cable-trays belong to the birds, and if you get between twenty crows and their dry purlin when the wind comes, they will take your hood off with their bills just to clear an inch of zinc to set their claws on.

I slide into the gusset. 

It is a triangular pocket, eighteen inches wide, formed where the diagonal brace meets the floor beam. The builders left a hole in the center of it—a six-inch flame-cut circle to let the grout through when they poured the deck above. It never got grouted. 

I hook my heels into the lower flange, shove my shoulders back into the angle until the red lead bites through the vulcanized canvas, and pull the copper spud up against my throat like a tie-pin.

Then the sky comes down.

It is not rain. Rain has space between the drops. This is a solid horizontal slab of cold, black river-slop that has spent three tiers in the darkness gathering weight, forced through the gate-apertures under sixty atmospheres of air. It does not drop; it shears. It hits the girder below me with a sound that doesn't have an end—just a flat, expanding tearing that takes the light away. 

The amber cone from the sodium lamp sixty yards out doesn't go out; it simply turns into a greasy, orange smear, like butter spread over an eye. 

The gusset fills to my hips in three seconds. The water is so cold it feels dry—a tightening, shrinking iron hoop around the shins that wants to drag the feet out of their stirrups on the flange. It smells of dead weed, old aggregate, and the deep, sour silt of sump bottoms that haven't seen an intake screen since the forms were stripped. In the dark of the wave, something hard hits my left boot-heel—a dead wedge-head, tumbled down from the upper causeway, its horn already snapped off at the base, its rubber skin torn into white ribbons by the deck grates. It rolls twice against my leaded sole, soft and heavy as a wet boot, and then the suction takes it, spinning it out into the central roar where the grates are gulping.

I close my eyes. Not against the dark—it is dark anyway—but against the mist. The mist carries grease-beads that get under the eyelids and stay there for days, floating like amber grease-drops on cold soup.

One.

The wave hits the gusset web. The steel bellows, an inch from my shoulder blades, groaning like an iron stove being dropped down a mine shaft. 

Two.

The concussive stroke from the turbine arrives right through the middle of the deluge. The water itself seems to jump—a single, violent heave that lifts my thighs three inches off the iron before the lead on my soles drops them back.

Three.

The water is dropping. 

You can tell by the smell of the air: the sulfur comes back first, sharp as vinegar, followed by the warm, sweetish stink of turbine grease blowing out of the intake ducts. 

Four.

The gates drop.

The thud is different this time. It doesn't travel through the iron; it comes through the water that is still draining out of the gusset, a single, dead *clop* that hits the soles of the feet like an iron hammer. 

Then the roar doesn't stop—it breaks into pieces. It turns into ten thousand separate slaps: the sheets falling off the three-acre flats, the three-meter conduits dumping their condensations with that heavy, dropped-canvas sound, the perimeter drains drinking with a loud, wet, desperate slurping that makes the air in the bay smell like an empty cellar.

I wait for the third turbine stroke before I pull my shoulders out of the corner.

The amber light is back, but it has moved. The sodium bulb half a mile out has tripped its breaker again—the blue-white flash lit the gusset for a tenth of a second while the water was still up—and now the only glow is the dull, yellow reflection off the floor plates below.

They are bare.

In the whole three acres beneath my beam, the aggregate is stripped clean. It looks like bone, white and grey, with the plywood grain showing up sharp and dark where the cement pulled away from the timber forms twenty years ago. The runnels of grease are gone, washed down the floor grates with the amphipods and the silt. 

Already, the crows are coming down.

They don't fly. They drop like wet gray rags from the cable racks, catching the vertical messenger wires with one foot, sliding six feet with their wings clamped tight, then grabbing with the other foot to stop the fall with a dry *snick* of horn against zinc. 

They are silent. They don't fight over the grates. There is too much of it: three acres of washed iron and gravel covered in the white, pulped ribbons of the weed-mats, split sculpins whose marbled livers have come out through their gills, and thousands of the amber-carapaced crawlers, their legs still twitching in the cold thin skim that leads to the perimeter troughs.

I reach out with the copper spud. 

Right at the corner of my flange, where the water swirled as it backed into the drain, a sculpin is wedged. 

It is a small one, no longer than my hand from wrist to middle knuckle, but it is still whole. Its skull is like an iron broadhead, broad and flat, the grey skin over its cheeks puckered where the turbine concussion has shaken the oil out of its fat-cells. It isn't dead yet; its chin-barbs are tapping the zinc, very slowly, feeling for the grain of the wood that isn't there. 

*Tap.* 

*Tap.*

It has the same rhythm as the drop from the three-meter conduit above my head.

I don't put it in the sack with the tallow-scraps. I don't throw it down to the newsprint crows that are already skittering across the slick aggregate thirty feet below, their bills clicking like shears as they split the amphipod shells.

I use the flat of the copper to pry its chin-barb off the iron. 

It doesn't fight. It is too cold to fight; its blood must be like the oil in my lamps, thick as syrup, barely moving through the marbled veins on its belly. I slide it into the deep bellows-pocket of my rubber suit, where the lead offcuts keep the skirt down. The pocket is full of cold water anyway, an inch of salt-grease that won't drain until I sit down on a dry purlin and unlace the flap.

It settles there against my hip, heavy and hard as an iron weight. 

Through the double-ply vulcanized canvas, through the grease-soaked felt of my under-suit, I can feel its chin tapping. Not against the iron now, but against the lead plate bolted into the hem of my coat.

*Tap.* 

A pause.

*Tap.*

One.

The air thins out.

Two.

The whine comes up through the web-stiffeners, sharp and clean, singing in the copper spud until my fingers go numb around the beaten end.

Three.

The crows on the flats stop tearing at the weed-mats. They turn their pale, scarred heads up toward the galvanized deck forty feet above, their milky eyes catching the dull, oiled spangle of the ceiling where the zinc still glints.

Four.

The lung takes its breath. 

My shoulder goes back into the timber-grained cement. The small lamp at my elbow, its nickel reflector clean down to the red brass, holds its tiny pool of tallow-light against the fog, and we wait together for the drop to stop falling from the conduit, two minutes before the lift.
