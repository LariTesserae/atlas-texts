At first, there is the temptation to be the cold stone itself—the anvil that has outlived five dynasties of plainsmen, or the sixty feet of grease-buffed limestone where the shoulders have rocked the damp away. It is easy to imagine being mineral: immune to the chill, patient beyond hunger, receiving the *clack* of the river cobble like a pulse through the teeth.

Or to be the water. Not a stream—nothing here possesses the vanity of current—but the seepage, the film that rides the curve of the travertine lip, thick with lime, sliding without a splash into the sponge below.

Yet neither of those carries the true weight of the rift. The rift is not quiet because it is dead; it is quiet because it is crowded.

So the mind drops lower, looking for blood.

Perhaps one of the saucer-broad necrophores? Moving on fifty white, paper-jointed legs beneath the black bryophyte crust, mouthparts working with dry, lime-eating patience, feeling the faint seismic slump that means an old loaf-thing has stopped breathing three feet to the left. There is a terrible, monastic peace in that: to be the toothless mouth of the cleft, keeping the floor sweet, entirely without eyes or malice, turning tallow and marrow into the fine, cool silt that coats the bedding benches.

Or the welter—wedged between two sunken slabs, four decades in a single furrow, feeling the scarlet threads of the gills sway in the lazy sheet-flow like hair in an eddy. The great, pebbled spade of the lower jaw resting on the silt, waiting for the dead weight of drift-cress or a drowned kit to brush the sensory pits. To open the throat with that single, hollow hydraulic *thwump*—thirty gallons taken in, the world swallowed whole in the dark, the wet cellar door slammed shut. It is an ancient way to be. But it is too far from the heat.

No. If I look for where the weight actually settles—where the four degrees above freezing ceases to be an abstraction and becomes an ache—I am higher up, on the siltstone bench.

I am not the one who holds the stone. Not yet.

I am the young one, apple-sized, pressed into the deep hollow between her collarbone and the swell of her breast.

The world does not have a horizon; it has a circumference of twelve inches. It begins in the wool of her chest—silvery-ash, drenched in tallow, so greasy and warm that the damp cannot touch my skin. My face is buried in it. My eyes are only two pale spots of lard beneath the skin, incapable of knowing if the roof is eighty feet above or ten thousand miles away, but my snout is awake. The ridge of cartilage across my nose is split into sixty tiny leaf-plates that ripple like gills, reading the weather of her skin: the faint, salt-cellar stink of her milk-pores, the sour iron of the snail-pulp on her paws, the cool draft that slips through the guard-hairs whenever she lifts an arm to strike the bench.

Her ribs rise beneath my belly. It is an enormous, slow lifting, like the bottom of an iron kettle settling into coals. *One. Two. Three.* A long pause where nothing happens, where the water outside drops *glop* into the peat, and then the kettle empties, the ribs sink, and my cheek rides the descent.

Around us, the loaf-things are packed like hot loaves against her flanks. I feel them through her hide: the steady, vibrating furnace of their thirty-pound bodies, their turnip-breath puffing in rhythmic, damp bursts against her knees. They are our insulation; we are their roof. When a dislodged pebble rattles eighty feet up in the chimney, their ears flick against her thighs, and I feel the quick, communal twitch run through all five of them at once—a ripple through a sack of grain—before they settle again, chewing on the rind of a water-beetle that fell from her fingers an hour ago.

Then she shifts.

Her heel—wide, callous-crusted, cold as lard—strikes the siltstone once.

The shock does not travel through the air; there is no sound to it, only a sudden, leaden *thud* that enters my gut from below. It is the language of the bench. Farther down the shelf, thirty yards into the black, another heel answers: two beats, slightly sharper, vibrating through the moss-sponge. *A welter has turned in the runnel. The snail-dam is holding.*

She leans forward. The tallow-smell thickens. Her forearms, dense with short muscle, lift the river cobble from its worn seat. I feel the pull of the tendon across her collarbone, right beneath my ear—a wet cord tightening under five inches of blubber.

*Clack.*

The shock of the stone hitting the beetle-shell travels straight into her sternum and into my small, soft jaw. It is not an act of violence. It has the regular, indifferent rhythm of a grandfather clock’s escapement. She lifts the flat rock, sets it back into its century-old groove, and her spatulate thumb—the amber spade-nail blunt and smeared with black wing-grease—peels the shell away.

She does not look. She does not need to look. Her paw comes up to her throat, her knuckle nudging the loose fat of my shoulder.

The soft, gray pulp of the beetle’s belly is pushed into my mouth. It is bitter with lime, slick with cold fat, tasting of rot and underground river. I take it with my gums, working it down, my hind paws digging into the wool of her chest to anchor myself against the heave of her lungs.

Behind her, the wall of the cleft is buffed like gray butter where her mother sat, and her mother before her, rocking the wet out of their backs. In four seasons, when my claws have hardened into shovels and the guard-hairs have grown through my tallow, I will drop from her chest into the peat. I will wedge my hips between the loaf-things, find a cobble that fits the hollow of my own palm, and take my place in the row.

Until then, I am the small, second heart beating beneath her collarbone. The mountain can split; the valleys below can dry into salt or vanish under three leagues of ice. In here, between the four-inch armor of her fat and the eternal weep of the limestone, there is only the rhythm of the cobble: lifted, struck, and returned to the stone.
