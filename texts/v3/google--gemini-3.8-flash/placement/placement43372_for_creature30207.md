At first, it would not be a matter of having a shape. To exist here, the first thing one must surrender is the habit of volume—the assumption that an interior can afford to be soft, or that a boundary might give way to accommodate an arrival. There is no slack in the chamber. Every cubic millimeter is occupied either by mineral thrust or the frantic, locked machinery of something enduring that thrust. 

So I would have to begin not as a body, but as a point of contact. 

Not the light, because the light here has no source to trace back to; it is merely an abrasive condition of the rock, a dry rinse that exposes without revealing. And not the sound, though the scream is everywhere. To be the sound would be too vast and too empty—the scream belongs to the millipedes, and their life is a shared, continuous panic turned to voltage, thousands of eyelash-thin segments bound into a single, high-pressure wire. There is no selfhood in a scream that never varies its note.

I would look, instead, for wherever the compression is most intimate. 

Not behind the slate lintel. The artificer is already complete; its shovel-nose and lard-pale skin are too specific, its purpose too terrible in its patience. It has the luxury of work, even if that work is measured in three-century intervals: the raising of an arm, the curling of a single flake of bone, the tuning of a filament that keeps the roof from dropping into the blue clay. It is an agent, and agency requires an edge of distance from the stone.

I would have no distance.

I would find myself down near the floor, where the baryte spires converge like shears. 

At first, I would only be the sensation of two cold wedges meeting bone. Two razor points of heavy spar, translucent and edged like fresh-struck glass, biting into the lateral margins of a broad, flattened back. The pressure would not feel like an injury, because an injury implies a before and an after—a moment when the skin was whole, and a moment when it tore. Here, the pinch is foundational. It is the only thing that proves I have an outline. 

I am the curd-white beetle.

My back is broad and smooth, opaque as mutton-fat, without the gloss of living chitin. It has been buffed flat by the stillness, frosted around the rim with the same clear lime that crusts the fish-rib pegs. I have no eyes—eyes in this light would be twin blisters of scalded jelly—and my antennae are pasted down in the grooves of my thorax, glued there by the unbroken film of mineral sweat. I cannot lift them. If I were to lift an antenna, the surface tension of the room would tear, and there is no wind to mend it.

My six legs are tucked beneath me, folded into the underside of my carapace like the blades of a penknife shut forever. There is nowhere to step; between my belly and the floor there is only a microscopic skin of moisture and the razor-lips of embryonic zinc. If I had claws, they would find only edges sharp enough to shear them away. 

So I do not stand. I am held. The baryte points do not pierce me; my shell was formed against them, growing around their advance until stone and horn achieved this exact, frozen truce. If the earth above were to ease by a single grain, the needles would slacken, and I would fall half a millimeter into the void. If the lithic weight deepened, they would crush my curd-white back into chalk. But the artificer tunes the chamber. The chord holds. Therefore, the needles remain precisely deep enough into my sides to hold me suspended an eighth of an inch above the floor.

And in front of me—touching the very tip of my parted mandibles—is the droplet.

It is not rain. It is not dew. It is a single, clear bead of water, swollen to the limit of its skin, perfectly spherical, anchored to a facet of baryte. 

My mouthparts do not drink it. To swallow would be to initiate an internal movement—a peristalsis, a displacement of moisture—and there is no room inside me for change. My digestive tract, if it exists at all, must be as dry and silent as the marl twelve fathoms down. Instead, the tips of my palps simply rest against the water’s meniscus. 

I feel its surface tension as an iron wall. It trembles at the frequency of the millipedes’ scream, shivering in tiny, geometric octagons that repeat across its curved face, thousands of times a second, never sloshing, never spilling. Through the point where my shell touches that water, the whole room passes into me. 

I am the ear that does not hear, the eye that does not see, but the gauge. The artificer rests its enamel chin upon the vitreous cord to feel the tension of the pegs; but I am the anchor at the opposite pole. I feel the weight of the water. I feel the mineral saturation of the droplet, heavy with dissolved zinc, cold enough to ache, sweet and metallic as a copper nail held against the tongue. 

My heart does not beat in seconds. Once every forty years, perhaps, a single thick pulse moves from the tip of my abdomen toward my head—a sluggish, white hydraulic heave that pushes my mandibles a micron harder against the water, testing its skin. The skin does not break. The droplet answers with its tiny, rigid elasticity, pushing back against the curd-white horn. 

*Still here,* the droplet says, in the language of surface tension and geometric ripples.

*Still here,* the baryte points answer against my ribs.

Across the blinding hollow, behind the notched slate, the artificer sits with its chert flake poised. It knows I am here. It does not look at me, because it has no eyes, but in its mathematical apprehension of the chamber, my white back is one of the fixed coordinates from which it calculates the balance of the roof. If I were to crumble, the acoustic trap would deform. The high-pressure scream of the eyelash-millipedes would strike the walls at a slightly altered angle; the vibration would slip from its groove; the filament on the five pegs would go dull; and the twelve fathoms of blue clay would settle with a soft, wet sigh, closing the bread-oven forever.

So I remain pinned. The noon never dims. The light remains as sharp and merciless as salt, picking out the pale dust trapped in the baryte, the lime-frost on my flanks, the black, odorless bitumen of the joins. 

I am not waiting for an escape. An escape would require an outside, and the outside is only an unproven rumor of air and mud. This three-foot oven of screaming crystal is the entire sum of things: bright, cold, crowded to the limits of existence, and locked so tight that not even a single drop of sweat can find the room to fall.
