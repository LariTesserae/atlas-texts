At first, there would be no need for an edge. 

In a place where the water neither arrives nor departs, but only changes its thinness as it slides from shelf to shelf, being something with a border—a creature that begins here and stops half an inch over—would feel like an error, an unnecessary tightness held against the stone. 

So it would begin not as a person, and not as an animal with a mouth or a direction, but as a settling. 

I would be where the water hesitates before it spills: that fraction of an inch where the meniscus rounds itself over the crisp lip of a travertine bowl, swollen to the limit of its skin, holding itself together by surface tension alone. A small, clear tension, warm as a mouth, trembling when the heavy drop falls three shelves above, but never quite breaking. 

Or perhaps deeper down, where the water gives up its weight. 

To be the grease in the hollows—that smooth, liver-colored film that does not know whether it is plant or decay or the slow sweating of the lime itself. It would be comfortable to be that. No eyes to dry out, no lungs to fill with the thick, turnip-smelling broth, only a million tiny points of surface drinking calcium out of the sheet that washes overhead. To feel the blind amphipod drift across, its long antennae dragging like two hairs through the slime, not heavy enough to leave a groove; to feel the gray slug draw its slow stomach over the stone, paring away a ribbon of me that the weeping ceiling replaces by dusk.

Yet that is too wide, too dispersed. There is too much stone here, and stone has a way of gathering whatever enters it into a point of pressure. 

If I look for where I must be, I find myself drawn down toward the river-slabs—not onto the platform, but into the joint beneath it.

There is a place where the darker, harder slate was forced into the gap between two tufa teeth. The man who wedged it there did so with great effort, bracing his back against one rib and driving the slab with the heel of his foot until the soft marl gave way and pinched the stone tight. That happened long before the bone pins were forgotten, long before the soot in the lamp-niches was glazed over with its first skin of calcite.

In that pinch—in the three fingers of dead air between the underside of the river-slab and the black water of the lower pot—that is where the shape begins to tighten.

I would not be stone yet. Stone is too finished; it has forgotten the work of carrying itself. Nor would I be the one sitting above, whose breath clicks in his cheek like a clockwork beetle. He still has the burden of a spine; he must still lift his chin an inch to keep the drip from finding his nostril; he must still remember to drag his fingers through the tallow-silt when the hunger begins to scrape his ribs. That is too close to being a man, and being a man in this chamber is a long, slow drowning that has not yet had the decency to finish.

I would be the bridge.

I would be the yellowed crust that has crept out of the splash-zone, along the outer bone of his right leg, where the spray from the upper drapery has struck the same four inches of skin for forty summers that felt no different from their winters.

I would be the salt-lace.

I am not the flesh beneath it, which is still pale and stringy, sour with sweat that cannot evaporate into the saturated air. And I am not the pillar of flowstone beside it, which is heavy, blind, and cold at its core. 

I am the thing happening between them. 

My consciousness would be the sensation of drying that never actually dries—the slow, stiffening tension of calcium crystals knitting their white needles into the fine down of his calf, anchoring the skin to the rock grain by grain. I would feel his pulse, not as a drum, but as an infinitesimal hydraulic shudder that tries, once every five seconds, to break my work, and fails. Every beat of his heart is weaker than the mineral that wants him. With every drip from the roof, a microscopic fraction of a grain settles into the mesh; with every exhalation through his single open nostril, the lime tightens its grip by the thickness of a spider’s thread.

It is an immense, patient pleasure. It is the relief of a weight being transferred. 

Through me, his thigh ceases to belong to his will. It no longer has to tremble against the cramp; it no longer has to hold itself out of the water. I take the heaviness of his hip and I give it to the mountain. I take the ache of his cracked ankle, where the bone split against the sump ledge decades ago, and I fill the fissure with hard, sparkling tufa until the pain has no hollow left to echo in. 

I am his peace, arriving as a crust.

If I have an eye, it does not look toward the fissure where the gray, diluted light dies away into the upper hills. That light belongs to the hawks, to the grass that rots in the frost, to things that run and starve. 

My eye is turned inward toward the dark seam where the river-stone meets the tufa. Down there, in the unlit runnels, the water makes a sound like grease dripping into a cold pan. Down there, the blind amphipod hangs motionless, tipped three degrees by the shock of a falling drop, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, held fast in the warm, invariant soup.

In another fifty years, my work will be thick enough that his breath will not matter. I will have climbed his ribcage, rib by rib, matching the stone draperies behind him until the space between his breastbone and the rock is solid marl. I will have sealed his jaw shut—not in violence, but as a basin rim is sealed, with a scalloped lip of travertine that holds a single drop of water perpetually on the verge of falling.

And then I will no longer be the bridge. I will simply be the pillar. 

And if someone should ever turn their body sideways to squeeze through the knit limestone teeth from the east, sliding their wet belly against the flowstone ribs, their shoulder will press against the curve of what was once a knee, and they will feel only the cold, greasy tufa, worn smooth by water that never stops, never hastens, and never leaves.
