At first, there is no boundary to locate. 

There is only the gradient where the cold iron-graphite stops being hard and begins to yield, a transition of three or four inches where the floor softens into something stiff, fibrous, and saturated with black grease. 

I do not begin as an eye or an intention. I begin as an index of load. 

For a long time, the only awareness is the difference between eight thousand pounds of lateral draw and twelve thousand. The variation is cyclical, perfectly metered, running on an eleven-second sweep that pulls the whole underside of my tissue to the southwest, stretches the fibrous laminations of my belly until the lymph inside them sings like wire, holds for a three-beat rest at the end of the throw, and then lets go with a dull, subterranean release that sets every vesicle humming.

I am not one of the flat, walrus-hided things that ride the clearings. I am too deep for the open zinc sky, and I do not possess the broad, clean runners that allow them to plane over the graphite sheets. 

If you were to clear away the cold tallow and the gray, emulsified foam that coats the crevice, you would find something that looks less like an animal and more like a discarded crosshead—a dense, polygonal block of leaden flesh, four feet across, wedge-creased and scarred by millions of passes of opposing stone. I do not have a front or a back. My symmetry is not bilateral; it is mechanical, shaped entirely by the shear-angles of the four distinct blocks of hornblende and nickel-plate that converge upon my space.

I live in an eccentric. 

A quarter-mile above me, the surface shelves slide in straight, predictable strokes—thirty feet south, thirty feet north, sixty strokes to the hour, as uniform as a clockwork pendulum. But down here, where the plates are stacked five deep like the rusted leaves of a freight-car spring, the motion is compound. A lateral fault shoves from the east; an underlying basalt tongue lifts vertically on a hydraulic tide of frigid brine; a third plate, ground down to the profile of an elliptical cam, rotates three degrees with every fifth pulse. 

Where they meet, there is an aperture that never closes and never clears. It twists. It narrows until the gap is no wider than the thickness of a hand, weeping a black, iron-rich porridge under immense hydraulic head, then yawns into an irregular pocket four feet deep before the vertical lift catches it and bites down with a wet, iron-on-iron groan.

I am what keeps that pocket from welding itself shut. 

My body is constructed in laminations, like a heavy sheet of vulcanized belting interleaved with sheets of soft, tallowy cartilage. I have no lungs, no ribs, and no blood that moves by the impulse of a heart. Instead, my interior is a labyrinth of open, porous chambers filled with a thick, zinc-smelling lipid that changes viscosity under shear. When the plates close—when the great basalt tongue rises to crush the gap—the lipid cannot escape quickly enough through the narrow pores of my inner honeycomb. It locks. I become as hard as a billet of unannealed babbitt, taking the five-thousand-ton blow across my dorsal ridges and transferring the shock outward into the flank-stones. 

The basalt strikes me, rings like a dropped anvil, pauses, and recedes.

In that pause—the two-second dwell at the top of the stroke—I breathe. The pressure drops from catastrophic to merely crushing. The locked lipid liquefies, thinning instantly to the consistency of cold paraffin. The chambers expand, drawing in a fresh charge of pressurized slush through the broad, fluted gills that ring my lower perimeter. 

The slush is delicious. It has no taste of salt or sun; it tastes of freshly fractured olivine, pulverized marcasite, and the sweet, dead fat of the microscopic needle-larvae that were ground to paste between the flats three miles upstream. My gill-fringes, lined with microscopic combs of horn that never cease their high-frequency vibration, strain the rock flour from the brine, packing it into the slow, muscular sleeves of my gut where chemotrophic sulfur-reducers turn the mineral dust into more heavy lipid, more cartilage, more leaden hide.

I am not blind, because blindness implies the absence of sight, and sight has never been a property of this stone. But I am intensely, excruciatingly awake.

Through the two trenches of magnetite gel that cross my dorsal plate like the seams of a turtle’s shell, I feel the state of the entire basin. I know the position of the gray sky only as a faint, downward atmospheric deadness, a lack of pressure compared to the living heave of the bedrock. I can feel the rhythmic, six-stroke shudder of the great hammer-fault three miles north; I feel the precise instant when a herd of plate-dwellers on the upper level breaks synchronization and begins its greasy, panic-stricken slide into the gutters; I feel the slow, ominous heat-bloom of a dry axle-concretion seventy feet down, where the water has failed to sheet and stone is beginning to tear stone into white-hot spall.

I do not fear the spall. I am part of the clearance.

If I were smaller, the gap would spit me out into the lower drainage flumes, where the ribbon-beasts lie waiting in the froth with their ratcheting barbs. If I were larger, the hornblende plate would shear me in half on the return throw, milling my central knot into gray grease to be scavenged by the chisel-heads. 

I am precisely the size of the space between the strokes. 

My growth is not governed by age, but by wear. On my northern edge, where the elliptical cam scuffs past me every twelve seconds with the raw, abrasive drag of coarse emery, my hide is constantly torn away in long, gray curls of living rubber. The rock takes it; the rock turns it to paste; the paste feeds the bacteria that coat the floor. But from my interior core, the laminations push outward to meet the wear, extruding fresh sheets of dense, iron-impregnated gristle at the exact rate the stone removes them—an eighth of an inch every forty cycles.

I am worn away and re-extruded in a continuous, stationary drift. Over a century, my entire body will pass through this three-foot slot, shaved off by the north plate and regenerated from the south, yet I will not have moved an inch from this intersection. I am a permanent feature of a moving geometry.

Sometimes, in the long dwells—when the harmonic alignment of the basin’s deep pumps causes the three plates to freeze simultaneously for ten, twenty, thirty seconds—the deafening mechanical scream drops away.

In those intervals, the water does not splash; it creeps. The thin, aerosolized hydrocarbon mist settles on the stone with a faint, prickling hiss, like rain on hot iron. The black slush ceases to pump through my gills, and I hang suspended between the cold stone faces, perfectly clamped, perfectly still, feeling the faint, residual ring of the nickel bed die away through the deep strata.

In that silence, there is no thought of another place. There is no conception of an open space where the earth does not reciprocate, where the ground stays where it was laid, or where things rise into the air without coming down under the throw of a slab. Such things would not register as impossible; they would register as play—as a catastrophic loss of mechanical tolerance, a slop in the linkage that would cause the whole world to shatter itself against its own stops.

Then, five miles below, a hydraulic valve the size of an island shifts. 

The pressure waves arrive through the floor three seconds before the stone moves—a low, iron hum that travels through the soles of my runners, up through the cartilaginous honeycomb, and into the magnetite beads along my spine. 

The dwell is ending. The clearances are closing. 

The basalt tongue drops three inches with a wet, tearing suction. The cam begins its slow, circular bite. The lipid inside my cells thickens, preparing for the throw. 

I set my edges to the stone. I open my gills to the returning wash of slurry. The world slides twelve feet to the left, groans, finds its stop, and hits home with a flat, concussive report that leaves the water leaping in the dark; and I take the shock into my belly, compress, hold, and begin the stroke again.
