At first, there is no reason to possess a spine. 

It is tempting to think of being the air here—the cool, tallow-scented volume that hangs trapped between the vault and the sheet-flow, carrying the hiss of the ceiling joints from one red wall to the other. Or to imagine being the water itself, that thin, running skin that has no will of its own, merely sliding along the gentle grade of the baked floor until the turquoise throat drinks it down.

But the air is too diffuse; it has no purchase on the room. And the water is too hurried, too constantly evacuated through the bronze slots to ever know where it has been. 

To exist here with any permanence, you must have weight. You must have something that can take the impress of the earthenware.

So the awareness drops lower, past the midges that churn in the updrafts, past the chalky swifts whose wings never touch the tile. The swifts are too fragile, their bones like dried grass, their whole existence an exhausting panic of flight above a floor that would drown them in its slime. There is no rest in being a bird beneath this vault.

The floor is where the reality of the room gathers. 

You sink toward it. Not as a watcher standing ankle-deep—for a standing creature would be an absurdity here, a foreign vertical pillar dividing the acoustic planes—but as something that lies horizontally, broad and heavy, along the curve of the tile.

You do not begin with eyes. To imagine seeing this place is to bring an unnecessary light that ruins it. The tallow dusk filtering through the square shafts is an accident of the upper world, a faint, irrelevant grease that does nothing to warm the clay. The room does not exist in light; it exists in percussion.

You feel it first in the breastbone.

A flat plate of dense, pliable gristle, wide as a baker’s peel, pressed firmly against a square of dark-red earthenware. Through that bone comes the map: the heavy, continuous *thwack* of water falling fifty feet from the vault crown; the micro-tremor of the scissor-tailed swifts banking against the roof corbels; the perpetual, hollow churning of the bronze grate sixty paces to the south, where the water whirlpools down into the flooded troughs below.

You are not the water. You are the thing the water flows against.

The ribs are flexible, arched low and soft like the wicker frame of a coracle. When you draw in the damp, mold-thick air, your chest does not lift upward into the room; it expands laterally, spreading your milk-pale flanks further along the slick red glaze. The mucus your skin produces is cool and thick, an alkaline oil that creates a hydraulic seal between your hide and the tile. Where your belly touches the clay, there is no air, no friction—only a seamless, suction-held continuity. 

You are a crawler, but not one of the yearlings skittering through the shallows with their claw-pads clicking like castanets. That frantic, hungry scurrying belongs to the small, whose ribs have not yet learned the acoustics of the hall.

You are older. Your tail is a thick, rudder-like spade of muscle tucked into the seam where the floor curves up to become the wall. You are wedged into the perimeter, where the water is only a quarter-inch deep, smelling of river marrow and the bitter, chalky wash of swift droppings. Beside you, pressed so tightly against your flank that your pale papillae interlock with its own, lies another adult. And beyond that one, another, all the way along the northern curve—a long, sleeping chain of white, lard-thick bodies, breathing in a slow, communal rhythm that matches the swell and fall of the air pressure in the flues.

Your broad snout rests flat on the wet terracotta. 

The whiskers along the lip-margin are not hairs; they are stiff, cartilaginous needles, trembling continuously in the sheet-flow. Through them, the floor speaks. 

Three hundred feet away, near the fifth drop-shaft, a clump of earth-worms has just fallen from the pipe. It hit with a dull, wet *plop*. 

You do not move. You feel the instant detonation through your sternum: the immediate, sharp scratching of a dozen younger crawlers launching themselves across the open dish; the frantic splashing of their hind limbs kicking fans of grey water against the low vault; the hollow, echoing snaps of their jaws as they roll together over the slick tile. The vibrations travel through the red clay directly into your jawbone, a clear, percussive narrative of teeth meeting gristle and horn pads skidding on wet glaze.

It is too far. The expenditure of grease is not worth the mouthful. 

Instead, you remain flat, feeling the subtle, barometric thickening of the room. Above, far beyond the eight venting shafts, the sky is darkening. The river valley is filling with rain. You know this not because you have ever seen a cloud—you have never known an open horizon or a dry wind—but because the pitch of the organ-pipe hiss from the ceiling joints has dropped half an octave. The air is growing heavy, sitting like lead upon your moist back. 

In an hour, the conduits will vomit. The carcasses of moles, the drownings of field mice, the washed-out clusters of beetle grubs will fall through the flues in a continuous, bloody hail. 

Until then, your business is simply to hold the tile. 

You feel the slow, steady pull of the current against your left flank—that perpetual two-inch tide sliding downhill toward the turquoise throat at the center. It nudges at you, an ancient, mechanical persuasion that wants to slide your lard-pale bulk down the slope, down toward the spoon-worn bronze rim where the dying go to be folded and drawn down into the drowned channels. 

Your spade-shaped toes, tipped with their hard pads of horn, grip the tongue-and-groove seam of the tile with effortless, hydraulic certainty. Not yet. The suction holds. The cartilage yields to the curve of the ancient brick, your skin drinks the fog, and deep in your chest, your heart beats once—slow, heavy, cold—echoing the single drop of water that just struck the empty bronze fifty paces away, ringing like a copper bell in the wet dark.
