I am not up on the shingle-walks where the beetle-wing roofs shed the seep. Those terraces are for the quick, the dry-fingered, those whose temple-bristles still bristle clean like hog-wire in the wind. 

You would find me lower down, where the city stops pretending it is stone and remembers it is sitting in a cracked bone.

I am wedged into the *Throat*—the blind, narrowing crotch at the very base of the frost-fissure, where the two torn halves of the taproot still cling together by a few white strands of un-snapped xylem. Here, the void pinches shut like the cleft between two clenched buttocks. It is the hinge of the entire disaster. If the frost drives deeper into the fen, this is where the wood will tear another inch; if the great thaw comes and the root swells with sap, this is the jaw that will clamp shut first, grinding the Third Shingle District into a paste of wet thistle-fluff and crushed grubs.

I am too heavy and too slow now to be a chisel-man. When your horn-tips wear flat against the flint-grain, they don’t grow back sharp; they round off like river gravel, polished smooth by decades of scraping cold slime. My belly has lost its pale, curdled grease. It is stained the color of an old boot, cured by seventy seasons of sitting waist-deep in the black tannin-trough where the runoff collects. My ribs have taken the curve of the wood; if you laid me on a flat stone, I would rock like a dried gourd.

I am what they call a *Wedge-Sitter*. 

My craft does not use the jawbone of the shrew, nor the windlasses of spider-silk. My tools are simple, brute deadfall: three split segments of a field-vole’s thigh-bone, each as thick as a hemlock twig, jammed horizontally across the micro-crevice where the crack terminates. 

I do not build. I hold the seam open with my weight and my bones.

Every few minutes, the tannin-water around my thighs heaves. It doesn't splash—tannin-water is too thick for splashing, heavy with peat-fat and iron-rust. It nudges. It lifts me half an inch, my leathery skin peeling from the raw cambium with the sound of wet moss torn from a boulder, and then drops me back down into the silt. The silt here is not mud; it is the drift-wrack of the city above. It is the discarded wing-covers, the moltings of millions of tallow-mites, the yellow egg-casings washed out of the nurseries by the seep, and the white, granular sawdust that floats down constantly from the chisel-gangs like a bitter, wooden snow. It collects between my toes. The tallow-mites do not leave me alone; they mistake my callused shins for drowned timber and graze upon the fungus that blooms in the wrinkles behind my knees. I let them. Their mouthparts are cool, like the prick of many small needles, and they keep the rot from entering the marrow where my horn has worn through to the pink.

In the pitch black, the world has no distance. It has only *heaviness* and *yield*.

Above me, four inches up the sheer drop, I can hear the click-chatter of the harpooners on the Great Pier: *tcha-k, tcha-k*. They are happy; they have struck a minnow. I feel the dull, rhythmic thrumming through the liquid as the fish beats its tail against the heartwood pilings, trying to back down into my darkness. The water washes across my chest in greasy, cold pulses, carrying the sweetish, ditch-leaf smell of its fear. 

Then comes the quiet. 

The twenty-minute shift. 

It does not surprise me. I feel it three heartbeats before the pupae in the Crown drop their spiracles. Down here, at the fulcrum, the wood tells the truth before the air does.

The first sign is the taste of the seep. The water trickling down the groove of my spine loses its salt; it becomes suddenly thin, acidic, and sharp as iron filing. The fen above is settling. The ice-crust, three feet thick over the peat, has groaned and dropped a fraction of a hair under its own dead weight. 

Next comes the grip.

The vole-bones in the throat begin to sing. It is a sound no citizen on the avenues ever hears: a high, thin, glass-like whistle as tons of sleeping oak squeeze against the bone-struts. The wood groans—a low, greasy shudder that passes straight through my pelvis and sets my teeth rattling in their gums. The two walls of the crevice, cold and slick as wet tallow, lean toward each other. They press against my hips. They want to meet. They want to sew the grain back together with callous-flesh and bury the whole city under an inch of white, suffocating scar-wood.

I do not run. I have nowhere to run, and my horn-tips could not climb the vertical grease of the xylem if I tried. 

Instead, I take my third bone—the thickest one, rubbed smooth with minnow-grease—and I set its notched end against the fresh wound-lip where the oak is trying to push out its new callus. I lean my chest against the other end. I throw the dead, leathery weight of my torso into the gap, bracing my heels against the opposing wall where the heartwood is black and iron-hard.

The pressure comes down like a sodden boot.

It squeezes the foul air from my lungs in a wet whistle. For thirty heartbeats, forty heartbeats, I am nothing but a knot in the grain, an obstruction the burr oak is trying to digest. I feel the pulse of the living plant—not a pulse of blood, but the slow, glacial ache of water moving through capillary tubes narrower than a hair, dragged upward toward branches that are currently white with rime in the sunless world above. The tree smells of winter sleep: stale starch, sour tannin, and the cold, unyielding indifference of a thing that measures its life in centuries and considers this whole city an afternoon’s itch.

If my bone snaps, the crack pinches. If the crack pinches, the docks shear off. The windlasses plunge into the trough, and the Stump-Wives are smothered in their hollow-grain shafts before they can spit the next mouthful of pulp.

I bite down on my tongue. The blood tastes like the fen-water: salt and bog-iron.

*Hold.*

*Hold the bone.*

Then, a shudder. The wood lets out a sound like dry grass snapping. 

The air draft rushes down through the frost-split from above—cold, heavy, smelling of frozen moss and the sulfurous bottom of the world. The pressure releases. The vole-bones stop their whistling. The two walls of the root ease back by a paper’s width, satisfied for another twenty minutes that the freeze still holds the marsh in check.

The water in the trough sloshes back against my ribs. 

Above, the city begins to move again. The *tick... tick... tick* of the seeps resumes. The springtails un-anchor their forks and begin their dry, sandy rattle across the upper shoals. Far up on the Pier, a chime rattles against the wood: they are hauling the minnow out of the brine, its wet belly thumping against the skin-slate docks. A rain of silver scales drifts down through the darkness, fluttering through the void until they hit the black water around my waist with tiny, musical slaps.

One drifts against my forearm. I pick it off with a blunt, hornless finger and put it in my mouth, sucking the cold fish-grease from the bone-plate until it is clean, then let it drop into the silt between my feet.

The wound is quiet now. The wood is breathing. 

I reach down into the black water, feel with my spatulate toes for the spare vole-ribs sunk in the muck, and settle my back into the seam once more, waiting for the marsh to heave.
