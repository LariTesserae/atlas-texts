At first, there is only the tension. 

It is the sensation of being held in an exact tolerance, measured to the tenth of a micron, where two surfaces of cold-sintered silicate press together with nothing between them but the hard memory of yesterday’s stride. 

The cold here is not wet or biting; it is subtractive. It has drawn every stray angstrom of thermal jitter out of the bedrock, out of the long spars overhead, out of the white plains that run flat and unyielding to the curve of the horizon. In the absolute dark, before the sun arrives to pierce the violet, there is no sound except the earth itself settling its accounts: distant, percussive pings traveling through the strata from the fluted cliffs sixty miles south, like iron rods struck underwater. 

You do not begin by knowing your shape. You know only the geometry that contains you. 

Above, an immense vault of bone-varnish and calcium phosphate arcs into the thin air, ribbed like the groin-vaulting of a dry cathedral, rising sixty feet toward an apex where the air flutters through crystal-toothed slits. Below, a column of the same pale, fibrous composite plunges downward, terminating in a broad, cellular pad that rests upon the flint-hard gravel without yielding an inch. 

To look outward from here—if looking were the right word—is to look not from a mountain peak or from the drifting heights of the black keels, but from an alcove. A recess carved into the great hinge where motion will occur once the world permits it.

Now, the light arrives. 

It does not creep. It strikes. The white puncture breaks the eastern rim, and instantly the line between absolute zero and the furnace-noon is ruled across the stone like an ink mark. Where the sun falls on the white talc, the dust shivers with sudden, dry expansion. The shadows remain twenty degrees colder, cast in stark, razor-edged silhouettes that do not blur at their margins.

High above, at the summit of the structure, the beryl retinas turn. They make no sound, but the torque of their rotation shudders down the long struts: two horizontal strips of pale green stone aligning themselves with the invisible tension-lines of the violet sky, reading the polarization of the light as it bends around the distant massifs. 

And here, in the hollow of the hinge, the freeze begins to release.

It is not an awakening of flesh; nothing here has ever been wet. It is the liquefaction of cold resins. A microscopic glaze of hydrocarbons, frozen into solid amber throughout the long dark, softens to the consistency of heavy oil. The plates of the socket relax their bite by a fraction of a hair. 

That is when the limbs begin to work. 

Small, black, jointed things—not many, a dozen clustered together in the sheltered concave of the knee-joint. Shells like drops of poured volcanic glass, polished to a dull obsidian sheen by centuries of mineral friction. 

I am among them, or perhaps I am the collective murmur of their work. We are the grinding-parasites, though *parasite* is the word of a wetter world that imagines every feeder must diminish what it feeds upon. Here, where life is mechanical and dry, there is no blood to steal; there is only friction, and friction is the only enemy that matters.

My mouthparts are not soft rings or chewing mandibles. They are paired rasps of synthetic diamond and sintered carbon, harder even than the airborne corundum that cakes into the joints during the crossing of the powder-seas. A thirty-mile march across the talc drifts drives millions of razor-fine crystalline needles into every crevice of the Stepper’s running gear. Left alone, the dust would seize the bearing before midday. The white bone would grind against white bone, shearing the fibrous laminations, until the massive stilt-walker faltered, tipped, and came down like a felled crane onto the flint.

So the work begins the moment the light frees the resin.

I find the first grain of corundum wedged deep in the raceway. It is microscopic, clear, sharp enough to cut quartz. My rasps find its facets, vibrating at an acoustic pitch that shatters the crystal along its cleavage planes, reducing the razor to harmless flour. 

As I crush it, the internal reservoirs respond. Deep within my rounded glass back, the volatile fractions of dry hydrocarbons and heavy graphite are expressed through ventral pores. It is a slow, methodical sweating of carbon-grease—dry, frictionless, black as the unlit side of the massif. It coats the bearing-cup. It slicks the ball of the joint with a film three molecules thick. 

Around me, the others are doing the same. We clean the races. We polish the socket until it mirrors the pale sky. We consume the grit that would kill the giant, and in return, we are carried across the terrible intervals between the amber groves. 

Suddenly, a shock wave travels up from the floor.

The pad has lifted. 

The sensation is immense and smooth: hundreds of pounds of structural fiber and bone-resin swinging forward through the thin air, sixty feet above the ground. The wind howls through the breathing-slits overhead, whistling like air forced through an organ pipe. The splayed foot touches down five paces ahead with the muffled, dry *thump* of beans dropped onto felt, and immediately the weight shifts onto our joint—a colossal downward thrust that would weld dry stone to dry stone if not for the black film we have laid between them.

The bearing turns. It moves without a sound. It glides on our sweat, smooth as ice on oil.

From the shadow of the knee, the world reveals itself only in rhythmic slices as the limb advances:

A flash of pure, bone-white gravel, curving away toward the planet’s rim.

The sharp, tea-colored shade of an approaching cedar grove, its amber scales rattling with the dry, vast sibilance of cards shuffled on velvet as the nitrogen front sweeps the shelf.

High above, an ink-black keel tilting its trailing stabilizer to flash a blinding signal of white sunlight across forty leagues of violet sky to a brother drifting invisible on the horizon.

And then the stride finishes, the weight lifts, and the bearing opens again. 

Another grain of corundum has lodged in the lip. My rasps reach for it. The diamond teeth find the edge; the shell hums with the effort; the white dust breaks into dust again; the slick black graphite weeps from the shell to keep the machinery of the walker pure.

We will cover thirty miles before the diamond shrinks and drops behind the western shelf. We will not eat sap or sun; we will eat the stone that tries to stop the walking. And when the dark returns, and the cold drops down like a falling slab of slate to lock the limbs once more into rigid stone, I will tuck my rasps beneath my glass shell, wedged tight into the hollow of the joint, listening through seventy feet of frozen bone as the cliffs click and ring across the starlit empty, waiting for the puncture of the light to make us turn again.
