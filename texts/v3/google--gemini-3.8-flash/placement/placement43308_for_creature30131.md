At first, the instinct is to look for yourself in the air—because the terrace is so small, and the sky above it so pale and wide, that consciousness feels like something that ought to drift down on the updrafts from the valley, like the winged ants or the poplar fluff. You want to be the hawk that never cries, or the cold moisture sliding across the northeast face of the basalt, touching everything without having to occupy a mass.

But the terrace does not tolerate spectators. The air here has no meat in it; it does not hold a thought. If you stayed in the sky, you would simply be blown over the drop-off and lost in the three thousand feet of gray vapor below. 

To be here, you have to accept weight. 

You look down, into the dark, rectangular cisterns. It is tempting to imagine being the isopod—to be pale as quartz, devoid of memory, scraping microscopic dust from the copper-bruised rock while the mountain tastes of lime or sulfur. There is an ancient, undisturbed comfort in that corners-only existence, pressed flat against the stone, insulated by three feet of dead-calm water. 

Yet there is no room for *knowing* down there. The isopod does not know the plan. It only knows the balance of salts and the shockwave of the cold drop falling from the lintel. 

You move up out of the water, across the dressed lip. You pass through the dry gravel backfill between the double basalt walls. You feel the temptation to be the gravel itself—thousands of small, round river pebbles trapped forever in the dark, settling by fractions of a millimeter over four centuries, carrying the mother’s chest-hum from her heels through the bedrock to the elder’s tailbone. That would be an honest thing: to be the transmission of a voice that has no syllables.

Still, you are drawn outward, into the light that does not warm.

You are drawn to the alleys that are two shoulders wide. You find yourself standing at the southern lip, where the pavement meets the drop-off, looking back across the neat, low roofs. 

You are not an observer who arrived here with boots and iron tools. If you were, your skin would split in an afternoon, your tongue would dry into a leather strap, and your lungs would burn for the thick mud of the lowlands. 

You are one of the six. 

Perhaps the oldest of them, though *old* has very little meaning when the dwarf willow has stayed four spans wide since your grandfather’s grandfather was wedged into the retaining wall. 

You are inside the stiff wrap. It is heavy, smelling faintly of old marmot fat and dried river silt. It does not touch your skin everywhere; it stands around you like a dry, unbaked chimney of felted seeds, holding a pocket of air that your own broad barrel of a chest has warmed to a flat, bloodless lukewarm. 

Your feet are bare. The soles are hard as dried hoof, curved to fit the shallow, saucer-like hollows that centuries of rolling footsteps have worked into the river flags. You are standing near the eastern corner of the communal circle, holding the copper scoop. 

The copper is cold in your hand, but it is the only thing here that shares a temperature with a living body, because you have held it for so long that the metal and your skin have traded salts. The edge of the scoop is thin as a willow leaf, worn down by scraping dead midges from the stone troughs and nudging quartz-pale isopods away from the dry curbs. It is older than you are. You can feel the small, irregular dent near the shank where someone four generations back set it down too hard against a block of gneiss.

The sun is at its high, motionless noon. 

The others are inside the corbelled rooms, their chins on their knees, waiting for the shadows to lengthen by a finger's breadth. You can feel them through your ankles: three slow heartbeats in the middle room, two in the northern vault by the cliff face. Their pulses do not synchronize; they strike the basalt at irregular, heavy intervals, like water dripping in separate caves. 

You do not go inside yet. 

You walk. You do not hurry—hurry is for creatures whose blood runs fast and thin, creatures who think the mountain can be outrun. You take forty paces along the main drainage rill. Your heel sets down first, wide and cracked like the crustose lichen on the masonry, then the ball rolls smooth, pressing down into the stone without a scrape. 

You find a dead dragonfly in the groove. It was carried up an hour ago on the thermal from the marshlands five leagues south, killed by the freeze before it could fold its wings. Its body is blue, a fierce, wet, violent valley-blue that looks obscene against the dull basalt. 

You drop to one knee. The stiff felt wrap cracks softly as it hinges at your hips. With the leaf-thin copper scoop, you lift the insect from the channel. You do not crush it; you do not eat it yet. You lay it on the flat palm of your left hand, where the gray skin is cross-hatched with pale, mineral lines. 

The color is already fading in the thin air. The blue is turning slate. 

You look toward the southern retaining wall. 

There are twelve vertical expansion joints cut into the outer face to allow the terrace to breathe when the winter ice heaves the gneiss shoulders. Eleven of them are full. 

In the eleventh joint, your uncle stands. He has been there for forty summers. His skin is the exact color of the wall now, dry and taut over the broad barrel of his ribs, his empty eye sockets turned inward toward the alley where you are kneeling. The cloud-sweat has washed all the salt from him; he does not smell of rot, nor of man. He smells only of wet iron and basalt dust. When the morning mist comes, the moisture glazes his forehead precisely as it glazes the lintels, and the rill cut beneath his heels carries the water straight down into the third cistern. 

The twelfth joint is open. It is two spans wide and plumb from top to bottom. The back of the slot is gray gravel; the floor is a single slab of cleaved gneiss, polished flat.

You look down at the blue insect on your hand, then across at the empty space in the stone. 

You do not feel fear, because fear requires a future that looks different from the past. You feel only the exact, glassy balance of the cistern water, three-quarters full, waiting for the drop to break from the roof with that dull, wooden *tink*. 

You put the dead insect into your mouth. You chew slowly. It tastes of bitter chitin and cold mountain air. You swallow, and you feel the weight of it settle into your ribs, becoming part of the pump that drives your gray blood through your wide shoulders.

Then you stand. The felt wrap settles around your hips like an unmortared wall. 

You turn your face toward the northern vault, press your tongue against the back of your front teeth, and strike the roof of your mouth once: a dry, flat *clop* that travels three paces and drops dead onto the flagstones. 

From the dark of the doorway, thirty yards away, a low, chest-resonant hum rises out of the gravel, vibrates through the flags, passes into your calloused heels, and settles behind your collarbone. 

*The water is clear,* it says. 

You nod once to the empty alley. You tuck the copper scoop into the stiff band of your wrap, turn your back on the drop-off, and walk toward the shadow of the corbelled roof to wait out the noon.
