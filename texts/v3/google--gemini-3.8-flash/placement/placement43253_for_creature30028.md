At first, there is only the weight. Not a burden, but a lateral holding—the way silt holds a dropped wrench until the shape of the jaw is recorded in the grey clay long after the iron has softened into carbonate.

I am not swimming. There is nothing to kick against that would not give back a dull, yielding slide of black velvet-ivy, and besides, the fluid has no margins that invite travel. To move across this borough would be like trying to push through a warehouse packed floor-to-ceiling with wet blankets and uncoiled cable; one does not travel through it so much as adjust one’s displacement within it.

I am somewhere in the middle tier, where the water is thickest with suspended tallow—neither the scald of the deep mud near the heat-exchangers, nor the cool, dead downdrafts that lick the mortar from the outer ring-wall. 

My back is against something that does not give. It has the flat, unyielding curve of an expansion joint, three feet of cast iron with an eight-inch flange held by hex-headed bolts. The bolt heads are missing their corners, rounded down by the constant, greasy licking of the convective ribbons until they feel like river pebbles. I can feel the vibration of the sixty-cycle hum through the base of my skull—or what serves for a skull now. It does not enter through the ears; it enters through the sutures where bone has had to make terms with lead.

For a long time, I think I am a valve.

There is a rhythm to the thought. Every five or six hours, when the deep jacket seeps its pulse of warm saline, the pressure behind my ribs climbs by an eighth of a pound per square inch. It is an exquisite sensation, like the slow filling of an eye with blood. It pushes me slightly outward from the flange, stretching the pale, fibrous bands that anchor my hips to the rusted stays. I lean into the amber current. The soup parts around my shoulders—smoothly, because I have grown the same coating of petroleum jelly and paraffin-eating fungus that blankets the pipe casings—and then, as the heat-plume diffuses into the ferns above, the pressure slacks. I settle back against the cold lap-joint with a tiny, soundless *thunk* that displaces three flakes of orange rust. 

That is the first hour of the day. Or the tenth. Time here does not notch itself into minutes; it is measured by how far a bubble of sewer-gas can climb before the ivy leaves pinch it to death.

Wait. Not a valve. A valve has an iron heart, and when the current drops, a valve stays shut.

I am softer than that, though not by much. When I draw my hands inward—a movement that takes the better part of an hour, so as not to stir the red flour that has settled across my forearms—my fingers do not end in nails. They end in the flat, spatula-like calluses of something that has spent forty generations levering itself into narrow clearances. The pads are black and slick, impregnated with powdered graphite and the gray grease that leaks from the packing glands of the stationary engines. When I touch my own throat, I do not find skin; I find a throat-latch of overlapping, horn-colored plates, like the belly of a water-snake, but stiffened with the sulfur-ash that drifts from the drowned flues.

Between the plates, the water goes in and out. 

It does not burn. It tastes of cold tea, zinc shavings, and the sweet, rotten marrow of old cattle-bones boiled down before the streets fell in. It is very calm inside me. My lungs—if they were lungs—have collapsed into long, pleated pockets lined with the same carmine velvet that fringes the salamanders downstream, but shorter, tighter, tucked away inside the ribs where the iron-dust cannot foul them.

I am wedged into the space between a feed-water heater and the collapsed ceiling of what was once an apothecary’s cellar. 

Above me, through a tangle of lead pipe that has folded like cooked macaroni, is one of the amber bulls-eyes. It is three yards away, no larger than an orange, but in this broth it is a sun. It does not flicker. Its filament is thick as a broom-straw, a loop of carbonized twine glowing a dull, sleepy saffron behind three inches of cast plate-glass. The glass is crazed with thousands of hairline fractures, held together only by the hydrostatic pressure and the black slime that has caulked the fissures. 

In that beam, I can see what I am made of.

I am long—perhaps eleven or twelve feet if I were to uncoil from the pipe-cradle, though there is no reason to uncoil. My middle has the broad, depressed girth of a sturgeon, white and tallowy on the underside where it rests against the silt, but along my spine I have taken the imprint of the ironwork. The rivets have left indentations between my vertebrae; the coarse diamond-tread of an engine-room floorplate has embossed itself into my dorsal hide, a permanent criss-cross of black scars where the skin has grown around the iron oxide.

I have no eyes to speak of, but I can see the bulls-eye because my skin remembers yellow. 

It does not remember blue—there is no blue here, not even in the deep dynamos—but it knows the yellow of the indicator lamps the way an old leaf knows the direction of an cellar grating. When the lamp dims—when the water-table shifts three miles away and the galvanic cells in the foundations lose their salt—my skin tightens. It feels like frost on a pond. When the glow returns, the hide relaxes, the grease thins, and I can let my head sink another half-inch into the soft drift of boiler-slime.

I am not alone here, but the others are not companions; they are benchmarks.

Ten feet below me, where the feed pipe enters the brick foundations, one of the pale salamanders has hooked its hind limb through an open drain cock. It has been there since before the ivy reached the ceiling. Its gill-plumes drift in the eddy made by my ribs, vast red ferns that brush against my side with every half-hour heave of my belly. Sometimes, when a rivet gives way in the municipal water-tower far above us—a sound like a pistol shot fired into a feather bed—the salamander’s gills flare until they turn the amber soup around us to the color of raw liver. Then we wait together, four feet apart, until the vibration has traveled through the marrow of the pipe, through my ribs, through its three remaining fingers, and died in the mud of the basement.

When it dies, we both sink back.

Who was I? 

The question has the same consistency as the paraffin beads that rise past my chin: greasy, hollow, drifting upward until they smash against the brick vaults and become part of the roof-scum. A name here would be an obstruction. A name has corners; it would catch on the flanges. To exist in this basin, one must have the cross-section of a gasket.

I think I was something that worked with brass. I have a memory—not in my mind, which is small and dark and smells of pond-weed, but in the muscles of my forearm—of turning a threaded stem until the hemp packing bit into the rod. I remember the sound of steam weeping through a gland before the water took it: a thin, high *fittering* like a bird in a chimney. 

Now, there is no steam. There is only the memory of its heat, stored in these millions of tons of iron like the warmth in an oven three days after the fire has been pulled.

A bubble breaks four rooms away. 

I feel it through the diamond-plate pattern on my back: first the *thump* against the cast iron, then the shudder of the floorbeams, then the slow, three-minute wave that lifts the ribbon-ferns off my belly and drops them back down, reversed. 

I open my mouth. It is wide, toothless, lined with tough, white gristle that has never known bread. I take in a long, slow draught of the amber liquor. It carries the taste of a dead crustacean five yards above, the sulfurous tang of an accumulator battery that has finally shorted through its rubber case, and the faint, sweet trace of mutton fat that has been forty years working its way out of an eccentric-strap.

It is enough. The convective ribbon is turning; the high cyclopean wall is cooling the upper soup, and the slow breath of the borough is beginning its downward curve. 

My tail—flat, thick, broad as a coal shovel—shifts an inch to the left, wedging itself deeper into the flange. The indicator lamp above me hums its sixty cycles, a dry, warm wire in a cold wet world. I close the plates over my throat. I let the red dust fall until it covers my nostrils, until my outline is indistinguishable from the lap-joint, until the iron and the meat are the same temperature again, resting at the exact weight of blood.
