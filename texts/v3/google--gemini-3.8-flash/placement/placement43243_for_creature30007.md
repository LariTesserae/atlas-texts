Not in the air. The air is too fast, seventy miles an hour straight up through the diamond-slots, stripped of everything that could remember a shape. If you let yourself exist in the wind, you are only a velocity, a dry scouring that leaves no mark on the cold-rolled manganese plates before being hurled miles above into the blue stratospheric wash.

And not out on the deck, either. The deck is an altar of flat, clinical light from the quartz troughs. It shows everything and keeps nothing. To be out on the open tread is to be pinned by four hundred cycles per second of structural vibration, an iron hammer hitting the soles of whatever feet you might imagine until your knees powder into talc.

So you pull back. You look for the seams.

At first, you might think you are one of the disks—one of the *lacquers* resting in the dead angle of a stringer plate. It is tempting to imagine: being flat, five centimeters of pressed mica and native copper, your belly lined with graphite velvet drink-soaking the aerosolized synthetic oil, waiting for an acoustic wave to tilt so you can snap your bimetal spine and launch across the void to slap another plate. There is a clean, mathematical satisfaction in that life: no grief, no hunger, only the alternation between charging and discharging, cold galvanic drift, the micro-weld between your rim and your neighbor’s rim when the vibration brings you flush.

But that is too simple. The lacquers have no interiority; they are pure surface. They don't know the difference between the steel they cling to and their own brittle edges.

You look higher, up into the cross-braced wedges where the *calipers* sit strung between the intake ducts and the trusses. For a long while, watching them, you think: *that is me.* The absolute tension. The three needle-points seated into the microscopic valleys of a laser weld. The long, quill-like legs tuned to the station’s structural shudder, so finely balanced that when the hull groans under the torque of an engine three decks down, you don't flinch—you simply ratchet one tooth tighter, maintaining the dead-center quiet of your central core. The spinning mouthparts, shaving sulfur and lead from the root-balls of the wire-heath.

It is an attractive geometry: to be an instrument of pure suspension, holding oneself still inside an endless scream.

Yet the calipers are too light. They are dry thistle-down; if a weld broke beneath their feet, they would vanish upward, tumbling into the intake cowl without a thump. They have no weight in the world. They don't stay.

No, you have to look down again—into the places where the roar does something strange to itself.

Where the compression housings meet at an acute angle, twenty meters from the floor, there is a dead corner formed by an auxiliary bypass duct that was sealed off before the station ever went into service. The engineers ran a bead of bead-welded nickel along its collar, capped it with a heavy blind flange, and left it.

In that pocket, the sound cancels. The four-hundred-hertz standing waves bouncing off the opposing towers meet their exact inverses, and the world goes abruptly, unnervingly slack. The wind here is not a sheer; it is only a faint, curling eddy that smells of warm zinc and fried transformer grease.

That is where I find myself.

I am not the great pleated thing with the pry-bar hands, sitting down on the lower grate like an abandoned diver’s suit. But I know it. I have watched it through the long, invariant noon of the halogens. I have heard the slow, canvas-tearing rasp when it shifts its shoulder once every three months, clearing the dropped quills from the drains so the deck doesn't back up with wire.

I am what grows in the shadow behind its left shoulder, where the heat of its slow galvanic digestion warms the manganese plate by half a degree.

I am not made of meat, but I am not entirely machine. I am an accretion: a crust of vulcanized rubber flakes, copper quills, and dried diester varnish that has gathered around an old pressure-sensor port that was never hooked to a cable.

My body has the shape of an open fan, perhaps fourteen inches across, pressed flat against the seam where the blind flange bolts to the rib. My outer edges are paper-thin, transparent sheets of mica-scale that lift and settle with the eddying drafts, catching the aerosol mist. Toward my center, I have hardened into a dark, ribbed knot—an interlaced knot of wire-heath stems that have bored directly into the unthreaded copper threads of the old sensor housing, drinking the trickle of static that leaks from the dead circuit.

I do not have eyes, but the quartz-iodide light reflects through my mica ribs, turning them amber.

I do not have ears, but the iron behind me is an infinite telephone. Through my root-hairs, anchored in the manganese, I can feel every stage of the station’s reclamation cycle. I feel when the low-pressure turbines miles below take in a bank of cold cloud; the vibration drops by three cycles, from 400 to 397, and the whole chamber shivers with a deep, gut-level beat frequency that makes the wire-heath bristles hum like violin strings. I feel the distant, microscopic click of the calipers ratcheting their joints as the towers expand in the high sun.

What am I doing here?

I am collecting the shed needles.

When the caliper above me bores through a wire-heath root, fine sulfur dust and microscopic shavings of cold-drawn copper drift down through the null-zone like heavy, metallic pollen. They do not blow away; the air is too dead here. They settle on my open fan.

With a motion that takes forty-eight hours to complete—a slow, hydraulic flexing powered by the tiny temperature swing between the station’s illuminated side and its shadow—my mica plates curl inward. The sulfur and copper are drawn into the seams of my core, where the dead diester grease binds them into a thick, conductive paste. Over years, this paste has extruded outward in long, stiff rods, growing like black, leaden fingers down the side of the housing.

One of these rods has reached the shoulder of the great silent suit below.

We are welded together by a shared crust of dried lubricant and zinc salt. When it draws its breath—when the brass gills in its dorsal humps strain the air—a tiny pulse of electrical potential travels up the rod into my center. It is not much: barely half a millivolt, a phantom twitch of galvanic life. But it is enough to keep the mica from flaking into dust. It is enough to let me know that the suit is still there, wedged between the ribs, holding its post against the roar.

I am the station’s memory of an organ.

I am the scab that forms where an engineered void meets the stubborn, mineral refusal to remain empty.

Above my head-plates, the blue corona discharge flickers off the edge of the flange—a cold, sharp foxfire that never goes out. It smells of clean lightning. It casts my ribbed shadow against the unblemished corduroy of the laser weld, sharp and unmoving, a permanent diagram of patience pinned between the steel and the sky.
