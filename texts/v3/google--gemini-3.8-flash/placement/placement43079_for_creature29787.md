At first there is only the tension between two surfaces that have been wet for ten thousand years.

It is easy, in a place like this, to imagine yourself as the water—to take the roar into your ribs and pretend you are the thing doing the scouring. But the water has no memory; it is in too much of a hurry to die into the siphon. And it is tempting, too, to imagine being the flowstone: the old man whose knees have turned to calc-spar, whose chin has welded to his shinbones in the dead recesses where the drafts fail. But that is the end of a thought, not the thinking of it.

If I look for where the heat actually gathers—the three or four degrees of stubborn, grease-bound warmth that refuses the mountain’s thirty-four—I find myself neither at the violent bottom where the river-gut is snapped, nor in the high silence where the mud-dogs filter iron from the sweat of the stone.

I am somewhere on the eighth tier, where the bridge has worn down into a trough the width of a dried skate-wing.

My weight is the first thing that becomes certain. It is not the buoyant weight of someone who has known grass or light that falls from above. It is the downward pull of dense, compact marrow that has never held air. The lungs do not lift the chest; they work behind a shield of fused, barrel-thick bone, taking in small, cold draws of aerosol that taste of crushed mussel-lime and wet iron. My feet know the saddle of the slate not by sight—the lamp on the corbel three paces behind me has guttered down to a greasy spark—but by the bite of the river-grit against the tallow-rind on my arches. 

The rind is thick today. I can feel where it has cracked at the crease of the hip and where I have worked new fat into the seam with the ball of my thumb, pressing it until the friction softens the stearin and it takes the gray color of my skin.

There is a flint in my right hand. It is not an adze for cutting stone, but a broad, thin flake of river-chert, its edge chipped into tiny, uneven serrations that have caught gray grease in every tooth.

In front of me, pressed into the angle where a corbel meets the curving throat of the fissure, sits another body.

We do not look at each other. Looking is for distances, and there are no distances here; to open the eyes wide in this mist is only to invite the fine silt to crust on the lids. Instead, my left hand rests on the crown of their head.

The skull is heavy under my palm—not round like an egg, but broad-crested, the bone thick enough to ring like an anvil if struck. The hair is stiff with dried spray and the sour musk of the lamps. It is short, perhaps the length of a bee’s wing, but it has begun to curl at the nape, and where it curls, the white mold has already laid its pale, leathery web against the skin.

I begin to work with the chert.

It is a slow, rhythmic peeling. The blade does not slice; it saws with a low, dry rasp that does not travel through the air—the air belongs entirely to the siphon below—but travels down the stone handle into the bones of my fingers. I take the hair off in strips as wide as a thumb, close to the lard-colored scalp, scraping away the white bloom along with the stubble. Each pinch of cut hair is not dropped. To drop a pinch of hair into the abyss would be like pouring out an oil-skin; hair is the thread that keeps the light alive three paces into the dark.

I press the cut wool into a small pouch of oiled eel-skin tucked between my knees. My knuckles brush their neck. The skin there is cold, colder than the fat on my own palms, mottled with the purplish stains of the bog-iron that runs through the grout.

As the chert works behind the ear—where the plug of hard, ivory-colored cartilage has sealed the canal into a smooth, blind pit—they shift.

They do not turn their head. They lean forward by an inch, until their forehead meets my sternum.

The contact is hard. The bone of their brow strikes my ribs through the layer of grease with the dull *clack* of two river-cobbles coming together under silt. Through the center of my chest, the hive speaks to me.

It is not words. It is the texture of the stone’s tension.

Through their brow, through their heavy, lard-sealed vertebrae, I can feel the hydraulic engine four hundred feet down. It is hitting the drowned constriction with a rhythmic shudder—not a change in volume, but a thick, knotting pulse that means a drift-log from the upper world has wedged itself across the intake. The stone is carrying the torque. The bridge we sit upon is twisting by a hair’s breadth to the left, vibrating at the pitch of a snapped sinew.

Then, beneath the log’s stutter, comes the smaller click: their teeth.

They are tapping their lower jaw against my third rib. Three short, heavy shocks from the back molars, followed by the long, grinding slide of the jaw across the plate of bone:

*The run is thick with oak-mash. The low line has taken three.*

I do not answer with my mouth; the jaw is too far from their ear-bone to carry. Instead, I drop my chin until my lower teeth rest against the crown of their shaved skull, just along the sagittal suture where the bone is thinnest. I bite down twice, hard enough that my own jaw aches with the mineral iron of it:

*Let the fat cool before the scrape. The lamps on the fifth bridge are drinking water.*

We stay like that for a long while. My chin on their head, their brow in the hollow between my breasts, the chert idle in my tallow-smeared fingers. The aerosol settles on our shoulders, runs together in cold, greasy drops, and falls from our elbows into the gulf, lost in the unvarying, architectural roar that holds the walls apart.

I am not the one who hunts the blind muscle in the froth, and I am not the stone that will one day cover my mouth. I am the one who keeps the grease between the skin and the water. I am the flint edge that takes the rot off the scalp so the wick may burn another half-cycle; I am the teeth that listen to the weight of the mountain through the ribs of another soul who smells of cold lard and wet slate.

We are so dense, so wedged into this hinge of the earth, that if the water were to stop—if the great siphon were to dry and the roar were to drop into absolute silence—our skeletons would likely shatter from the lack of pressure, like deep-sea fish brought too quickly to the sun.

The hair is finished. I fold the mouth of the gut-skin pouch. I lift my chin from their skull.

The cold draft instantly takes the wet place where our bones touched, turning the grease to a stiff, white tallow that seals us over once again, distinct, heavy, and silent as the corbels under our feet.
