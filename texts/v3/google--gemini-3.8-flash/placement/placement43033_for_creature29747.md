At first, there is only the sense of being wedged into a place that was not originally meant for a body, but which has loosened just enough over two hundred years of wet and dry to admit one.

Not the open air of the sun-well, where the hemp lines saw against the cedar timbers, and not the bottom flagstones where the swine-wash thickens in the runnels, but somewhere along the middle seam—perhaps seven courses below the line where the swallows stop nesting and the mastiff bats begin. It is the zone where the lime wash has completely flaked away, leaving the double finger-drawn grooves on the faces of the bricks exposed like dry ribs. 

My back is curved against the unbaked earth of the pit’s outer fill—that dense, cold, ancient packing of desert silt and camel-dung aggregate that holds the heat of the summer three cubits deep. In front of me is the backside of the retaining wall, where the straw in the bricks has long since rotted out, leaving narrow, horizontal voids that whistle whenever a dry wind scrapes across the rush-hurdles thirty cubits above.

I am not light enough to swing in the wicker cradles anymore. My collarbones have widened, thickened by that dry, persistent cough that everyone takes into their ribs after their fourteenth winter, when the bird-fluff and lime-flour stop being things you spit out and become the permanent lining of your throat. But my knees haven't yet gathered the heavy water that keeps the swine-masters pinned to their hazel switches on the sump flags. 

I live in the between-depth. 

My trade—if it is a trade and not simply a condition of having the right kind of hand—is the joints. Not the laying of new courses, because no one adds to the pit now; the work is keeping the ancient courses from sliding into each other like wet tiles in a basket. 

I carry a square wooden trencher greased with hog-lard, a short-bladed copper adze that has been ground down until it is no longer than a dog’s tongue, and three leather bladders of bitter tallow mixed with powdered bone-lime. Where the brick has "blown"—where the saltpetre has grown behind the skin of a header until it bursts outward in a pale, stinging crust—I pick the rotten core out with the copper edge, clear the cricket-dirt from the weep-hole, and butter the wound with the lime-grease before the shear can travel downward into the tier below.

It is slow, blind work, done mostly by thumb-sense. 

My knuckles are twice the size they should be, swollen at the mid-joint from being knocked against the earthenware rims in the dark. The skin of my fingers has lost its whorls; it is cross-hatched like dried river-mud, smooth and hard enough that I can pinch out a dropped ember of tallow-wick without feeling the fire of it. The red dust lives under my nails like a permanent crescent of rust.

Mostly, I am an ear.

Because I spend my days behind the decorative face—crouched in the narrow inspection crawls that run behind the horizontal amphorae, where the smell of bat urine is as thick and sweet as boiled turnip-greens—I hear the pit from its marrow outward. 

To the climbers above, the sound of this place is a roar: the card-shuffle rattle of forty thousand swallow-wings beating the warm chimney-draft, the dry rustle of gourd leaves rubbing against hemp, the sudden, shrill whistle of a girl warning that a wicker lath has split. Down at the sumps, it is all liquid: the heavy, leaden *thump* of the bone-balance tipping, the sucking grunt of the pigs fighting over a dropped squab, the slow *drip-tock* that measures out the hours in clay jars.

Behind the wall, neither of those sounds has any sharpness. The swallows sound like a great kettle of dry peas being turned on a fire. The water-fall is only a soft, greasy vibration in the soles of my feet. 

What I hear are the things the brick says to the earth.

A pit built of unbaked dirt and lime does not stay where you put it. It wants to go back to being a flat desert floor; it wants the center to fill and the walls to buckle inward toward the sun. Every hour, the terrace I am wedged behind moves a distance smaller than the width of a midge’s leg. When it moves, it groans—not with a human sound, but with a dry, grinding *churr*, like a hand mill turning without grain.

I know which pipes are "dead"—choked with the debris of old bat nests or collapsed from an ox-bone settling unevenly three courses above—and which ones are "clear." 

Sometimes, during the white hour of midday, when the sun stands directly over the center of the pit and drives the climbers into their barrel-vaults to sleep out the furnace-blow, I sit in the space behind an empty oil amphora on the seventh tier. Its mouth looks out through the brick skin into the blinding, pink-dusted core of the world. 

If I put my chin into the hollow of the vessel’s neck, the entire basin is laid out before me, sliced into a round disc of yellow glare. I can see the long, pale gourds dangling in their netting, motionless in the dead air. I can see the pigs lying like black river-stones in the gray muck around the drainage troughs, their sides heaving with short, wet breaths. Far above, the braided hurdles at the rim are so bleached by the midday light that they look like a ring of white bone set against the hard, flat blue of the sky.

From this hole, I do not use the percussive tongue-clicks that the ladder-boys use. I don't need to. I keep a pair of polished river-pebbles—black, dense things brought down from the desert rim by the salt-traders three generations ago, so smooth they feel like grease between the fingers. 

When the settling comes—not the little churring of the mortar, but that deep, iron-hard *thung* that means a foundation brick at the third sump has broken its spine under sixty cubits of weight—I do not wait for the runners to report it. 

I take the right-hand pebble and click it against the interior rib of the jar. 

*Tink.*

One cubit above the salt-beds. 

Then I slide it half a hand’s breadth deeper into the throat, where the clay is thicker, and rap twice. 

*Tok-tok.*

A pause. I count seven swallow-beats. 

From thirty cubits down, out of the dark mouth of an irrigation conduit that opens directly over the swine-pens, comes the dull, wet answer: a hazel rod striking the bottom of an empty catch-basin. 

*Clump.*

They have heard. They are already dragging the cedar wedges out of the muck to shore the lower lintel before the sweat-line can open into a shear.

When my time comes to go down to the sumps—and it comes for everyone who lives long enough for the saltpetre to rot the marrow of their wrists—I will not be a stranger to the floor-men. They know my knuckles. They have lived with my pebble-talk coming out of their drainage sleeves since they were small enough to be lowered down the well-shafts in baskets to scrape the moss.

Until then, I stay where the air is dry enough to keep my tallow from spoiling and damp enough that my skin doesn't split when I close my fists. I live in the hollow between the finger-grooves of the makers and the cold, unworked silt that waits outside to press them flat. 

I reach out my thumb. In the dark behind the fourth course, a seam has opened—a gap no wider than a bat’s ear, but fresh, with the dry smell of freshly sheared clay leaking out of it. 

I scoop a lump of bone-grease from the bladder. I press it home until the gap stops breathing. 

Then I tap once with the pebble, very light, into the side of the tube: 

*Here.* 

And I wait for the pit to click back.
