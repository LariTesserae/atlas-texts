At first, you would look for me against the iron, or flat to the rock where the charge runs. That is where the substance is. That is where the bodies with weight anchor their hooks or weld their bellies.

You would not see me there.

To find me, you would have to look into the empty span between the great exhaust pump and the sheer face of the pale basalt—a gap of perhaps twelve feet where nothing connects. 

The air does not move, but it is not empty. The 80-hertz drone does not travel as a simple wash of sound; in this narrow, stone-flanked chimney, it reflects. It bounces from the boiler-plate trusses to the faceted rock and back again, folding over itself until the entire chasm is mapped in invisible, standing architecture. There are troughs of ferocious pressure where the air feels as stiff as pig-iron, and between them, spaced with mathematical precision, are the nodes: places where the waves cancel each other out completely. Points of absolute, unnatural stillness.

I am suspended in one of the nodes.

I do not touch the mountain. If I were to drift two inches to the left, into the shear-line of the standing wave, the violent vibration would tear my dry lamina into grey confetti within a tenth of a second. I am held in place by acoustic levitation—cradled by the very force that would pulverize me, trapped in the dead center of a pressure well.

I am not an animal with a spine, nor a bug with calipers. I am an assemblage of exfoliations.

My body is shaped like a flattened, double-convex lens, no thicker than a dried maple seed and barely three spans across. I have no meat; I am built of what the chimney sheds. My dorsal shell is a shingled mosaic of selenite flakes that broke from the tine-striders’ bellies decades ago, bound together by fine, drifting gossamer—not silk, but the dry, electrostatic filaments shed by the bellows-wefts as they flex against the pipes. My core is a honeycomb of hollow, paper-thin graphite scales, lighter than ash, held in an airy, cellular lattice by pure static attraction. 

Because I touch neither stone nor iron, I have no access to the thermal currents or the direct electrical circuit of the mountain. I feed on what remains aloft. 

The air is saturated with an imperceptible haze: powdered zinc from the grinding gizzards, talc-like graphite dust, microscopic needle-spores of silica shaken free by the plants. Most of it falls, but the finest dross is kicked back into the draft by the constant, sub-audible shiver of the fronds. When these micro-particles wander into the silence of my node, they lose their momentum. They settle onto my upper surface like dry frost.

Across my back runs a labyrinth of microscopic, stepped grooves, etched into the selenite by the friction of falling dust. I do not have a mouth, but I have polarity. By flexing the dry, hair-thin filaments along my perimeter—striking one flake of mica against another to generate tiny bursts of triboelectric charge—I draw the zinc dust toward my interior ribs. The dust does not dissolve; it compacts. It packs my cellular chambers, adding mass, stabilizing my poise in the center of the null-space.

The most terrifying thing about this chimney is not its drought, nor its iron machinery. It is the noise. The tine-striders live *in* it; the grease-mound at the casing has made the vibration its nervous system. 

I am the only thing in the fissure that knows silence.

Inside the perimeter of my node, it is as quiet as the heart of a buried flint. The air does not buzz; the silica dust settles without a whisper. But because I exist in the eye of the acoustic storm, I am entirely defined by its boundaries. The roar is not a sound to me; it is an impenetrable, vibrating wall of glass that encloses me on all sides. I feel it as a rigid enclosure. To the north, the boiler-plate creates a hard, metallic compression; to the south, the basalt produces a rougher, broader acoustic shelf. I rest between them like a dry leaf caught between two opposing jets of compressed air.

I live in terror of an imperfect note.

When a hairline fracture begins to trace its way through an iron truss three levels below, the pitch of the flue does not lower, but its geometry warps. The standing wave flickers. The node twitches. 

When that happens, my entire world lurches. The invisible walls of my cage lean inward. If the distortion is slight, my perimeter filaments brush against the pressure gradient; they strike silent, static sparks against the air, burning off tiny fragments of my selenite margins with a dry, sulfurous odor, and the sudden electrostatic recoil pushes me back into the shrinking center of the quiet. 

I feel an agony of dependence on the weavers. When one of those pale, ribbon-like vermin finds the fracture and begins to wedge its cold-iron snout into the split, forcing its solder-silk into the gap, I feel the repair long before the metal cools. I feel the walls of my acoustic room firm up again. The geometry squares itself. The floor of my silence levels out, and I settle, weightless once more, over the deep drop.

I have no eyes, but I am translucent. The faint, cellar-like daylight filtering down from the grates high above passes straight through my selenite shingles, tinted pale amber by the old resin that holds my core. If something looked up from the pump housing through the tangled ironwork, they might see me: a dim, dull-glinting disc floating motionless in mid-air, fixed as a star, six feet above the dead iron, anchored to nothing at all.

I grow so slowly that centuries leave only a ring the width of a hair around my rim. Eventually, of course, the exhaust casing will wear through. A main bearing will seize; a blade will part from its shaft; the invariant 80-hertz hum will tumble down an octave, or choke into a jagged clatter, or simply die.

When it does, the standing wave will vanish. 

The invisible floor will be pulled from beneath me. I will fall through the grey twilight, down through the tangled thickets of silica fronds and petrified hemp, bouncing soundlessly off the ropy vines until I strike the grease-mound at the bottom. 

And there, where everything is bone-dry and nothing decays, the tine-striders will find me before my static charge has even grounded out. Their iron jaws will snip through my selenite scales, harvesting the clean graphite flour from my ribs, scattering my centuries of quiet back into the roaring air.
