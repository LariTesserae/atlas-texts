The sound does not come down through the air. The air is nothing—a dry cloth with no weight to carry a knock.

The knock comes up through the left ulna, or what has hardened into an ulna beneath two thicknesses of four-ply vulcanized baling-strap.

*Tink.*

Then the long, grease-bound pause, four seconds while the iron rings out into the rock where the anchor-pin has bitten through the basalt into the cooler shale, and then the return:

*Tink.*

It is cold where it hits the collarbone. Even here, three inches off the boiler-box of the No. 2 Return, where the mineral crust is four inches thick and looks like cauliflower boiled in tallow, the vibration feels cold. Steel is always cold at its center, no matter how much six-hundred-pound wet steam is blowing through the throat of it. It has the weight of an anvil dragged through wet grass.

I am not in the open. To be in the open is to have the sun peel the fat off your ribs like lard off a hot shovel while the draft from the notch pulls your eyes dry.

I am down in the run between the flange of the sixth expansion loop and the dead-end wall where the shale broke away during the great slides of the seventy-seventh season. It is two feet wide, sixteen feet long, and three feet deep to the grease-floor. The tiles do not come down here because the pitch is too steep for their gristle-plates; they slide over the lip and hang by their scute-corners like slates slipped from an eaves-trough, leaving this trench clear. It smells of them, though. It smells of elderberry mash, sheep-dip, and old leather boots that have been soaked in tallow to keep the lime from eating the stitching.

My hands are not hands. When you lash the rubber on with twelve-gauge copper, you do not unlash it to pick lice. You leave the thumb free to clear the blow-holes and you let the four fingers fuse together into a single broad horn, curved like a coal-scoop, yellow at the nail-line where the horn meets the salt-rot. If you strike a flat rock with the palm, it gives a dry, heavy *thunk*, like a side of salted mutton dropped on a butcher’s bench.

There is an old galvanized grease-keg wedged into the angle of the pipe-support. The brand on the head is gone, rusted into red bran forty years back, but the lead seal on the bung is still soft enough to take a thumbnail. I keep my scrapers there: three leaf-springs from an old narrow-gauge trolley, ground against the basalt until their bellies are thin as ribbon-dogs, and a heavy brass grease-syringe with a split barrel that I have whipped with wet sinew from a shear-vulture’s leg.

When the valve on the Nine-Bore begins that dry, rattly *chitter*—like someone shaking dry peas in a pewter cup—I don't look up. You don't look up at the Nine-Bore; the mist will take the film off your corneas before you can get the lids down.

You lay your forehead against the cold iron of the flange.

The iron tells you everything the sky won’t.

It tells you the mud is rising in the sump three miles under the basalt floor. You can feel it through your brow-ridge—a slow, thick churning, not fast like water, but heavy, like wet cement being stirred with a fence-post. It takes twenty minutes for that churn to climb the casing. While it climbs, the ribbon-dogs come down through the iron-mesh skips overhead, dropping with soft, wet slaps into the dust, their pale bellies swollen with wasp-eggs, their tails dragging behind them like dirty gray ropes. They don't run; they don't have the legs for running. They flow. They pour themselves into the seams where the flange-bolts go through the granite, eight or nine of them packed into an eighteen-inch gap until their pink gills are all working together, sucking the last of the cool air out of the bolt-holes before the heat strikes.

I don't kill them. You don't kill a ribbon-dog in the run; their fat is full of arsenic and if you burst one on the stone, the smell will keep the air out of your throat for an hour.

I reach out with the spring-steel scraper and ease three tiles off the apron of the expansion loop. They come away hard—the gristle-pad on their bellies has a grip like a wet boot on a cellar flap—with a sound like *shuck-shuck-shuck*, like tearing wet cardboard. They are heavy, forty pounds of cold slate, their margins crusted with sulfur-bloom that turns my thumbs yellow. I stack them against my knees. One. Two. Three. A little wall, three tiles high, their magnetite mouths working the air with a tiny, dry clicking, looking for the rust they think I’ve brought them.

Then the Nine-Bore lets go.

It does not shout. The air here is too starved to carry a shout; it just gives a great, flat, coughing *whuff*, like a dead horse falling into a trench.

Then comes the gray.

It isn't smoke and it isn't steam. It is water that has spent ten thousand years five miles down under the lead-veins, water that has forgotten what a cloud is, water so full of copper and iron-meal that it doesn't drift—it drops. It comes down the rock like gray suds, boiling, five hundred degrees where it leaves the bronze nozzle, three hundred when it strikes the tiles against my knees.

The tiles take it. They don't move. Their gray-veined gristle sweats that thick, cold grease that smells like scorched lard, and the boiling broth just curtains off them, running round the sides of my vulcanized pads and away down the basalt fluting into the deep throat of the sink. My teeth feel like they are made of dry salt; they ache right up into the temple-veins with the hum of the casing.

The mist lasts six minutes. You count it by the pulses in the copper wire around your left wrist—sixty to the run, six runs to the clear.

When the air thins enough that the pale circle of the sun stops looking like a grease-spot on a wet coat, I shove the tiles down the slope. They slide on their own lard, three feet, six feet, then stick again as the grease cools and catches the basalt grain.

Now the work begins.

The runnels are full. The vent-blast has knocked seventy or eighty vultures off the high cables; they are lying down in the scree like heaps of burnt roofing-felt, their lacquered wings cracked open where the heat split the bituminous skin. The tick-wasps are already boiling out of their feathers, billions of them, a yellow dust that smells like vinegar, crawling over the wet stones toward the shade before the noon sun can bake them into meal.

I take the iron cable-hook.

You don't take the whole bird. A dead shear-vulture weighs fifty pounds and the meat is black, stringy, and tastes of kerosene. You take the breast-callus—the four-inch gristle-knot where the wing-struts meet the breastbone. It has no antimony in it; the bird keeps all the poison in its liver and the primary quills. You lever the adze-bill up with the short spring-steel, plant the hook under the crop, and haul until the gristle gives with that same dry, wooden snap the wings make when they lock for the midday drift.

The meat comes away clean, like the heart of a cabbage.

I drop four of them into the galvanized keg. The fat is already running clear in the heat, yellow as mutton-fat, thick enough to seal the leather on my left knee where the wire has begun to chafe through to the meat.

Across the chimney, on the second step of the No. 4 Intake, there is a movement.

A gray thing. Low.

It has a piece of conveyor-belt over its croup to keep the noon glare from frying its spine, held on with a strip of frayed hemp rope that must have come off the No. 3 winch before the spool broke back in the winter of the iron-frost. It is down on all fours, its head hanging so low between its shoulder-blades that you can see the flat, gray ridge of its neck-vertebrae, white with salt-crust where the skin has split.

It doesn't look at me. Its nose-holes are two red slits, crusted with the dry yellow powder of the sedge-pollen that blows off the northern saddle once a month.

It has an iron rod in its horn-hand.

It reaches out, slow—slow as a tile sliding through the grease—and lays the flat of the rod against the six-inch brine pipe that runs between its shelf and mine.

*Tink.*

I wait. I watch a ribbon-dog poke its pink, axe-shaped snout out of the flange-packing to lick the gray froth off the iron bolt-heads.

I pick up a flat piece of basalt, no bigger than a saucer, and strike the cold flange twice.

*Tink. Tink.*

The gray thing over the way doesn't move its head. It just shifts its weight four inches to the left, out of the small runnel that is beginning to boil with the return-wash from the nine-bore, and settles down into the stone until its ribs marry the fluting.

The sun is directly overhead now. The Scarp is beginning to slide.

Everywhere, from the five-mile notch down to the dead bypass at the bottom of the world, the gray shingles are moving. Millions of them. It makes no noise—just that paper-thin rasp, like an old woman dragging a dry broom over an asphalt yard, three miles wide and forty miles long.

The stone is getting too hot for the flesh. We are all going down together into the cracks, into the wet, dark grease under the machinery where the bronze sleeves are still turning, four inches an hour, grinding the mountain into flour that smells of cold oil and old copper.

I drop into the trench. I drag the two nearest tiles over my knees, tuck my chin into the bib of my rubber apron, and let the hot, slick moisture gather on the nape of my neck until it runs down my back like warm oil.

The deep turbine twitches once. A long, slow heave that lifts the floor half an inch and drops it back onto the granite bed.

We have six hours of noon. Then the cold draft will come off the plains, the steam will freeze into gray rime on the cables, and the vultures that are left will lock their wing-hinges and wait for the dawn-blast.

Until then, there is only the salt in the mouth, the iron under the ribs, and the grease that never dries.
