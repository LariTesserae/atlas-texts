At first, there is no sense of being an *inside* looking out. 

Where the light is total and cast from within the very geometry of the rock, there is no shadow to define an edge. To have an edge, something must be kept in reserve; something must be dark. Here, the white radiance does not fall upon things—it passes through them, bounces between internal cleavages, leaves nothing unexposed. 

If I look for where I begin, I find only an anchor. 

It is a chemical burn, microscopic and circular, etched directly into the smooth obsidian pavement. A ring of microscopic pits where my lower tissue meets the volcanic glass, dissolved by a cold, persistent secretion of acid so that the stone and the flesh can no longer agree on where one terminates and the other starts. I am not tall. I possess almost no height at all; if you ran a razor across the floor, it would catch on me only as a slight drag, the way a blade catches on a film of drying lacquer. 

I am one of the discs. A *calix*, though the name belongs to an observer who is not here. 

To be this shape is to be mostly diameter. I am roughly three centimeters across, flattened until my organs are spread like diagrams pressed between two plates of transparent mica. My skeleton is not a cage of bone, but a spoke-work of needles: lithium carbonate, flexible as boiled hair when wet, arranged radially like the seams of an opened parasol. Between these needles, my body is a clear, proteinaceous skin, so thin that the terahertz vibration of the titanate veins beneath me hums straight through my gut.

I do not hear with ears, and I do not see with eyes. The chamber is too loud for hearing and too bright for sight. Instead, the hum of the stone is a constant, physical friction against the lithium needles in my back. When the mantle tightens miles above us, the pitch of the floor rises; the white brilliance becomes marginally more surgical, harder, singing at a frequency that makes my cilia shudder. 

I live in the rills, where the water is only a skin deep. 

Most of the time, the world is a sideways slip of boiling broth. The capillary brine slides over my dorsal membrane, cooling just enough against the open air to keep from cooking my enzymes. Around my circular perimeter, my cilia beat with a dry, mechanical flutter. They are not swimming; they are sweeping. They draw the surface tension toward my center, where my mouth—a simple, sphincter-like pore that has never closed on darkness—gathers what the current brings.

It tastes of crushed sulfur and deep alkaline salts. It tastes of the amphipods that died three rills over, their opaline armor pulverized by the current and drifting as microscopic glass dust. Sometimes a strand of the archaeal mat breaks loose from the northern wall—a golden, fiber-optic thread still hot with channeled light—and my cilia fold it down into the pore, still glowing, still carrying the eight-degree difference between the radiance and the cold bedrock. My body digests the heat as much as the sulfur.

Then, the interval begins to run out.

You can tell by the sound of the steam. The delicate hiss against the rhyolite shards changes timbre, rising from a wet sizzle to a sharp, dry fry. Along the northern wall, the micro-pores stop weeping. The sheet of water crossing the pavement does not break into puddles; it simply thins, stretched taut by its own surface tension, until it is a film three molecules thick. 

This is the drought.

The humidity drops. The air clears until the upright rhyolite knives look as if they have been drafted in ink. The heat does not decrease—without the vapor to scatter it, the light strikes down with a flat, searing pressure that begins to bake the water off my back in ribbons of steam.

I do not flee. I have nowhere to go, and my anchors are chemically fused to the glass. 

Instead, I hold my breath, though I have no lungs. I let the lithium salts precipitate. 

As the water vanishes, the brine thickens into an oily, oversaturated syrup, and then, in a fraction of a second, the salt falls out of solution. It does not settle like dust; it flashes into geometry. It catches on the lithium needles of my skeleton, using them as seed-crystals. White, opaque, and cold, the salt crusts over my skin, sealing my central pore, locking my cilia into stiff, chalky fringes. 

My fluids do not boil away because the crystal shroud forms faster than the water can escape. My internal proteins slip into a vitrified state—a glass transition. My heart, if the slow hydraulic pulse in my core could be called a heart, ceases to beat. The chartreuse paste in my gut turns solid. 

For three minutes, I am not alive. I am an ornament on the obsidian. I am a white ring among white rings, indistinguishable from the dry mineral stains left by a dropped bead of brine. 

Yet, oddly, it is during these three minutes that I am most resonant. 

When I am wet and soft, I absorb the vibration of the pocket; I muffle it with my flesh. But when I am glass and salt, I become continuous with the stone. The terahertz hum of the titanates travels straight out of the floor, enters my etched foot, passes through my calcified needles, and rings in the air above me. I become a tiny amplifier in the optical cavity. The blinding white light enters my salt shroud, is refracted through the radial spokes of my vitrified skeleton, and splits into needle-thin interference fringes on the dark floor around me. I do not think, but I ring. I am a note held by the mountain.

Then, through the dead stone, comes the blow.

It is not a sound you hear with the shroud; it is an impact that threatens to shear the anchors from the rock. The deep seismic thrum. The northern fault resetting its teeth.

The hum of the floor cuts out instantly, choked by the shift in pressure. The silence is massive, heavy as iron, lasting only for the length of a single dropped pebble.

*Crack.*

The brine hits. 

It does not trickle. It erupts from the northern boundary as a boiling wall of froth, carrying with it the stench of fresh sulfur, iron, and ancient anaerobic deeps. It smashes across the central pavement at ninety-eight degrees Celsius. 

The boiling turbulence strikes my salt shroud. The lithium crust does not wear away slowly; under the scalding, undersaturated flood, it shatters and dissolves in three pulses of the wave. 

The glass transition reverses. Water tears through my dried channels. My lithium needles soften, springing back into their flexible curves. My central pore dilates with a violent, involuntary spasm, gulping down the roaring slurry of ions and heat. The cilia, freed from their mineral cast, snap instantly back into motion, whipping against the violent drag of the surge.

The atrium is suddenly alive with chaos. 

Overhead, the light is fractured by steam, turned into an unstable, flashing white tempest. Across the pavement, the clear amphipods are flattened like coins against the obsidian, their needle-legs locked into the stone’s atomic flaws, their opaline backs shedding the boiling current like shields. 

And then, sliding over my dorsal face, comes the cold, slick weight of a ribbon.

It is wider than I am, pale as grease, moving against the torrent with a smooth, sickening peristalsis. Its fluoropolymer skin brushes my delicate outer rim, and for a terrifying second, my cilia are pinned beneath its bulk. It leaves a smear of caustic slime that stings my mantle, eating at the edge of my lithium needles—the chemical trail of an animal that dissolves stone to eat the things inside it. 

It does not want me. I am too thin; there is not enough sulfur or grease in my paper-flat disc to justify the effort of dissolution. It is hunting the grazer locked to the stone six inches upstream. The ribbon glides past, undulating like a strip of cellophane dropped in a boiling gutter, seeking the seam where the amphipod’s legs meet its carapace.

I do not turn to look. I cannot turn. 

I open my pore wider. The boiling surge is already beginning to slacken; the violence lasts only as long as it takes the fault to relieve its hydraulic head. The water slows from a wave to a sheet, from a sheet to a rhythmic, steady slide. The scalding temperature drops back toward ninety degrees—the temperature where my enzymes work with the mechanical speed of an engine.

The white light settles, flattening once more into its surgical, shadowless state. The terahertz hum returns, rising from the floor like heat from an iron stove, setting my lithium spokes to vibrating with a clear, high pitch that I feel as hunger.

High above, in the vault of volcanic glass, the steam gathers. It reaches ninety percent. The first condensed droplet swells, heavy with scavenged silica, lets go, and falls through the coherent white beam.

*Tink.*

It strikes the stone an inch from my margin.

My cilia beat. The rill flows. I taste the sulfur of the broken mat, the shed carapaces, the ancient water pushed up from the mantle’s foot. I spread myself as thin as I can against the obsidian, drinking the light from above and the heat from below, waiting for the salt to take me again.
