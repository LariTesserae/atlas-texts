I would not be one of the people. That feels certain almost immediately. Their life is an achievement so hard-won and clenched—every milligram of mass accounted for, every gesture an economy against evaporation—that to imagine stepping into one of those scuttle-gaited, resin-lacquered frames feels like an intrusion of something too soft, too speculative, too damp at the edges of the mind. They have no room for visitors, and their bones are already spoken for.

Nor would I be among the things that race. Not the striders, whose existence is a furious arithmetic of claw-purchase and sulfur-grazing before the gale shears them into the drop; not the ribbon-tails, whose snap-turns between the cane stalks have the twitching, overclocked urgency of sparks in an exhaust vent.

Where the attention settles—where the weight wants to go—is lower, and stiller, though not asleep. Because nothing here sleeps.

There is the wind-baffle: that zig-zag of slate shingles set in frames of split cane, lashed with wire-grass and gummed with the blind wasps’ dark amber varnish. It stands near the lip, taking the first, worst violence of the updrafts before the querns can be worked. 

I would be something that lives in the overlap between those slate shingles and the stone they are anchored into. Not a tool, and not quite an animal in the way the striders are—more like an encrustation that has developed an interior.

A lichen-creature, perhaps, or a dry-scale colonial thing that has grown into the joinery. 

Call it a *hinge-moth*, though it has neither wings nor dust: a flat, multi-segmented creature the length of an adze-haft and the thickness of three split shingles, wedged into the dead-air crevices where the baffle’s timber-pegs enter the basalt. It does not scuttle. Its movement is entirely thermal and tension-based—it creeps by the expansion and contraction of its own horn plates as the mountain’s vent-heat pulses up through the rock and clashes with the cold, hyper-oxygenated downdraft from the troposphere.

What does it do? It listens, but mechanically, through the stone.

The whole ledge is an acoustic instrument, five thousand cubits of hollow cane and drilled skeleton screaming into the brown void. But down in the seams of the wind-baffle, the sound is different. It is translated entirely into strain. The slate plates groan against the resin-glue; the braided cane-cords stretch with a high, thin pinging that travels directly through the anchors into the basalt floor; the basalt itself, hot from the flue below, ticks and shudders as the updraft pulls at its teeth.

The thing in the seam lives on that friction. 

Its diet is not the fatty *medulla* of the cane, nor the mite-butter the children spoon from the hounds’ flanks. It feeds on the finest rock-flour—the microscopic grit ground out where slate rubs against slate, where the wooden pegs shear against their basalt sockets under the gale’s torque. It secretes a slow, dry silicate acid from the margins of its plates, dissolving the powder into its shell, turning the mountain’s wear into its own increasing density. Over decades, it becomes heavy as lead, hard as corundum, an living wedge driven deeper and deeper into the structure of the shelter.

The bivouac-dwellers know it is there. They do not pry it out, because a hinge-moth in a corner-joint makes the joint tighter; it swells slightly when the downdraft is about to sickle over the rim, its plates contracting against the sudden chill three heartbeats before the mica-ferns shatter. When the women sit at the querns, their heels resting near the base of the baffle, they feel that anticipatory clench through their calloused rinds—a dull, subterranean thud: *the seam is biting down.*

They tap the cane with their horn rings. *Two sharp, one drag.* But the hinge-moth felt it before the ring struck the stalk.

In that crevice, there is no view of the brass sun or the five-thousand-cubit drop. There is only the long, warm throat of the mountain below, muttering through its basalt teeth, and the tremendous, lifting violence above, trying to peel the skin off the stone. To be the thing that sits between them—cold on the back, red-hot on the belly, turning the mountain’s friction into mineral mass, utterly dry, completely wedge-fast, holding the corner down—that feels like the true posture for this place. 

Not singing through bone-whistles into the void. Just making sure the post doesn’t pull out of the rock.
