It is tempting to think of arriving from above—to imagine dropping down through a four-inch sleeve pipe on a grease-slicked cable, carrying a lens or an eye or some small, stubborn knot of human attention. But the updraft from the vault is too thick; it smells of wet lime and hot marrow, and anything coming down from the yards would drown in the breath of it before reaching the third rung of the ladder.

No, if I am here, it is because I was already poured.

At first, there is only the sensation of being held in an alkaline jaw. The hydration reaction has not finished. Deep inside the four-inch skin of shotcrete that pins the fractured gneiss, where the water has not yet figured out whether it is liquid or crystal, there is a low, chemical fever. It does not fluctuate with the trains. It sits at fifty degrees Celsius—a dry, internal hum, surrounded by wetness.

To be there is to have no joints. It is to be spread thin between the aggregate, a gray-green lime-slurry that listens through thirty yards of solid rock to the heavy, double-beat of freight bogies hitting the points overhead. The sound doesn't come as noise; it comes as a shear stress. A microscopic shifting of the sand grains against the crushed quartz. A tiny give in the bond.

Then the steam starts to bleed through the flanges.

Where the rock meets the air, the crust is soft. The shotcrete doesn’t dry—it sweats out its hydroxide, and in that sweat, the boundaries get imprecise. You begin to slip outward through the weep-holes. 

You do not stay rock for long. Not when the jute is pinned directly against your face.

The curing blanket is where the lime stops being chemistry and starts being meat. It is four layers of quilted burlap bound in punctured green plastic, anchored with lead expansion shields that have already begun to ox-blood with corrosion. The burlap has rotted past the stage of thread; it has become a dark, warm curd, wet as a dog’s mouth, packed tight between the cold stone and the hundred-and-ten-degree air.

I would be in that seam. 

Not the rat pup, entirely—not the little, bald pear of milk-skin and blue veins twitching in the pocket—and not the centipede that works its long, dry comb of legs through the rotting mesh. 

Rather, the place where they overlap.

You have to understand how soft everything is here. Without winter, without the dry edge of frost to define a snout or a claw, the edges of things lose their temper. When a rat pup dies—not from violence, but from the simple, flat failure of a heart that cannot pump heat out of its ribs fast enough—it does not go cold. It cannot. The ambient wetness keeps it at blood-warmth for days. Its small, gray tallow melts into the jute; the jute’s cellulose is already half-digested by the sulfur-colored molds; the molds are grazed flat by the blind nymphs before the horn has even cleared on their backs.

I find myself thickening in that pocket. 

A body that has forgotten how to be narrow.

I have ribs, but they don’t meet in a keel; they lie flat and splayed against the shotcrete, like the hoops of a barrel that has been dropped in the mud. There is no hair, only that mottled, purple-gray skin that has spent three generations pressed against the caustic weep of fresh cement, so that the epidermis is slick and hairless as an eel’s belly, calcified in patches where the lime has entered the pores and turned them into little white studs, like the heads of pins.

My eyes are there, but they are unnecessary luxuries, small as coriander seeds and sunken beneath folds of thick, water-logged tissue that keeps the sulfur steam out. I do not look into the amber sodium glare. I don’t need to. The world is delivered through the belly.

Through the sternum, I feel the four-inch pipe that carries the six-hundred-pound return from the terrace foundations. When the condensate trap clears—that heavy, mechanical *clank-shudder* that travels the iron every seven minutes—the tremor passes directly into my jaw. 

It tells me where the puddles are deep. It tells me which anchor-bolt has stripped its threads in the soft rock.

My paws are wide, pale, five-rayed stars with soft, spatulate pads that never harden into horn. They are always wet. They have the gray, water-wrinkled look of a washerwoman’s thumbs, but the skin is tough as boiled tripe. With them, I do not dig—the rock is too hard, the concrete too bitter—I simply peel.

I peel the greasy moult-shucks from the damp undersides of the steel flanges where the roaches congregate in their millions. The skins have the texture of wet onion skin, saturated with mineral oil and rat grease; they slide down the throat without a chew, cold and alkaline, leaving a taste like stale lard and plaster dust.

Sometimes a pup slips down behind the blanket, too weak to find the nipple in the tangle of damp cloth. It doesn't cry. In this heat, nothing wastes breath on a squeak. It just paddles its short, red limbs against my flank, its tiny, translucent snout nuzzling into the soft hollow where my shoulder blade should be. 

I do not turn to lick it. I do not crush it. 

I simply let it lie against the broad, damp slab of my side, where the blood pools close to the surface in wide, swollen sinuses. We pant together. Our lungs work at the same speed—that high, shallow, bird-flutter that never stops, the double-time rhythm of things whose marrow is constantly on the verge of curdling from the steam. 

My tail is the longest part of me. It is not an appendage for balance; there is nowhere to fall in this pocket. It is an organ of disposal. It lies stretched out behind me for three feet, fat as a sausage, bright coral-pink and scaleless, trailing down the rock face into the alkaline sump below. 

Down there, in the lime milk, the small, white isopods—the ones carved out of tallow and chalk—crawl over it. They nibble the gray slough of dead skin that comes away from my tail in the hot current. They clean me with their small, frantic mouthparts, their legs ticking against the meat like tiny pins, and in return, the heat of my blood keeps the puddle around my rump four degrees higher than the rest of the cistern.

We are all plumbed into one another.

The trains cross the diamond three hundred feet up. A shudder. A rain of lime-dust—warm, dry flour that sticks to the sweat on my snout and turns into gray paste in the corners of my lips.

I open my mouth slightly. The air doesn't enter; it falls in, a heavy, wet drop of grease and water that hits the back of my tongue. 

I do not wish for the night. I have never known a door. The sodium light leaks around the edge of the blanket in a greasy yellow line that shows the silhouette of a thousand roach legs twitching in their sleep, but here behind the quilted plastic, it is black enough to let the skull dissolve. 

The concrete is curing. It will take forty years for the core of the vault to finish its crystallization—forty years of stone giving off its fever, inch by inch, into the steam. 

I will still be here when the blankets rot to nothing. By then, the lime will have finished its work on my belly; I will be stuck fast to the wall, a gray, breathing knuckle of meat and calcite, fixed between the girder and the bedrock, panting softly into the dark while the trains roll on through the cold air above.
