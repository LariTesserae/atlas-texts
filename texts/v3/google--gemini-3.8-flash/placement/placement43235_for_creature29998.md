At first, there is only the refusal of the boundary to stay where an earthly body would put it.

If I look for myself by looking for a perimeter—for skin, for the soft, pressurized sack that keeps the wet inside and the dry out—there is nothing to find. In a place where every surface is electrically coupled or capacitive, the edge of an organism is an arbitrary distinction of the voltmeter. Touch the hematite floor with an uninsulated heel of lead, and your current-path becomes the plateau’s path; your ground is five miles deep; your left flank extends through an unjointed vein of native silver until it surfaces beneath the root of a foil-grove half a league away, where an amber bead of cured resin is cooling under the arc-lamp sun.

So I do not begin with an outline. I begin with a *differential*.

I am small, smaller than the great sleds whose millstone groaning I feel long before their facets clear the iron horizon, but far heavier than the foils that skim on magnetic breath. I am down in the mulch, but not rooted like the tines. 

Yet I am not a planer, either. I have no hunger to scour the mirror-planes clean. I do not want to be a vernier scale creeping toward noon.

What I notice first is the drag.

Between the mirror-bright plates of cold-rolled iron and the deep, silent understory of the groves, there is a drift of dry detritus—not rot, but the fallout of a world that sheds only crystal planes and carbon dust. Flakes of laminated mica sheared off by thermal snap; microscopic scales of graphite sloughed from the singing tines; spherical beads of bismuth left behind where some ancient, forgotten planer exceeded its fatigue limit and split down its centerline. 

I am gathered in that drift. 

Or rather: I am what the drift becomes when the galvanic gradient across a single expansion seam refuses to equalize.

Look closely at the edge where a planar sheet of phosphor bronze abuts a cold slab of hematite. The gap between them is hairline—the width of a razor’s edge in the morning, closing to less than a micron when the noon load drives the metal outward. Into that narrow, vertical chasm, the graphite dust sifts down year after year, century after century. Under the pressure of the expansion cycles, it does not pack into stone. It shears. It slides, atom over atom, forming slip-planes of unearthly smoothness.

I am lodged in that seam.

Not as an intruder, but as the seam’s own interior articulation. 

My substance is hybrid, scavenged from the mechanical fallout of the tableland: a core of dense, antimony-rich lead that does not expand when it warms, but holds its measure like an invar bar; sleeves of stacked, pyrolytic carbon whose atomic sheets allow me to slide along the shear-lines without generating the disastrous, heat-bloating friction of the upper world; and along my dorsal ridge, a row of paper-thin foils of native bismuth, curled like the gills of a mechanical fish, exquisitely sensitive to the subtle, stray magnetic eddies that rise from the stator-spirals beneath the floor.

I have no face. A face is an earthly indulgence, an arrangement for eating, breathing, and looking ahead. Here, there is no "ahead." The tableland is isotropic: it extends in all four cardinal directions with the same flat, terrifying precision, bounded only by the rim where the world drops off into blue vacuum. 

Instead of eyes, I have *hysteresis*.

I feel what has passed through the metal. When a wedge drops five miles north—that brutal, explosive *thrum* as its spring-steel latch gives way—the shockwave does not merely strike me as sound. It realigns the magnetic domains in my antimony core. The atoms tilt. They hold the tilt. For days after the strike, as the wedge slowly radiates its stolen heat into the dry sky and its cooling vanes tick in the silence, I carry the shape of that violence inside my own lattice. I know the mass of the wedge by how long the tilt persists; I know the alloy of the planer it crushed by the frequency of the harmonic that followed the impact through the hematite shelf.

I am an archive of tolerances.

I live in the half-shade, right at the drip-line of the great groves, where the shadow of the brass umbrellas ends and the blinding void begins. It is the most violent thermodynamic frontier on the plateau. 

Step two inches to the left, and the surface temperature is high enough to melt sulfur; step two inches to the right, beneath the broad, ribbed fronds of spun basalt and mica, and the floor drops toward an absolute, radiation-draining chill. The gradient across my body is immense—hundreds of degrees across a span no wider than a man’s forearm.

That gradient is my life. It is my pulse, though it does not beat; it is a steady, unrippling current, an internal river of electrons driven from the hot end to the cold end by the Seebeck effect, endlessly circulating through my carbon sleeves and returning through my lead core. 

I do not move often. To move across the tableland without a planer’s bulk or a sled’s crawling tracks is to risk work-hardening. Every flexion of metal creates micro-voids; every slip creates a dislocation. If I bend too quickly, I become brittle. If I become brittle, the next expansion *ping* that rings through the floor will find the fault in my spine, and with a tiny, silver *clink*, I will cease to be an entity and become two inert bars of uncalibrated alloy, waiting for the zinc frost to seal me into the floor.

So when I move, it is not by crawling, but by *thermal steering*.

I wait for the sun to tilt. As the razor-sharp violet shadow of the grove retreats across the bronze flats, the line of maximum differential moves with it. I do not push against the floor; I allow the thermal shock to warp me. The sunward side of my casing expands; the shadowed side remains rigid; the differential arches my spine into a slow, bimetallic bow. A microscopic latch of quartz slips; my forward tip grips a microscopic fissure in the hematite; the tail follows as the shadow shifts again. 

An inch an hour. An inch a day. 

I follow the shadow’s perimeter like a compass needle tracking a drifting pole, circling the great, silent grove through the centuries, staying forever in the razor-edge between the fire of the void and the cold of the interior conduit-thickets.

What am I to the others?

To the tines, whose silver tuning forks sing in the deep shade, I am only an alteration in the floor’s resistance—a small, dense shunt that dampens their lowest resonant mode by a tenth of a cycle when I slide across the silver vein that feeds them. They do not turn; they cannot see; but their pitch sharpens slightly until I have passed, their high-frequency singing rising into an ozone-scented hiss that dies back down as my shadow slips away.

To the wedges, high on their stepped basalt cooling towers, I am invisible. I carry no massive charge; I do not short-circuit the great copper coils beneath the flats; I have no molten eutectic belly to trigger the piezoelectric crystals in their prows. I am below their threshold—a speck of conductive dust, a harmless slip of graphite in the engine’s tolerances.

And the great sleds—those faceted mountains of tellurium crawling leagues out on the horizon—do not even register the grove I circle, let alone the hairline seam in which I lie. When their low, crushing rhythm travels through the rock, rattling my carbon sleeves like dry leaves, it is to me what the movement of tectonic plates is to an earthly worm: the indifferent baseline of the universe, the grinding of the mill that makes the world flat.

Yet I have a work. It is not an intended purpose—there are no intentions here, only the inevitable consequences of charge and mass—but it is an operation nonetheless.

As I slide along the expansion joints, the graphite scales of my underside act as a dry, non-conductive lubricant. I clear the jams. Where two slabs of iron have buckled against each other under the noon heat, locking their crystalline facets in a cold-weld that threatens to split the plateau’s crust, my passage leaves behind a microscopic film of pure, oriented carbon. 

The weld slips. The strain relieves itself. 

Not with a catastrophic, world-shivering shatter, but with that single, clean, five-mile *ping* that rings out across the empty plain—the sound of an engine releasing its breath, settling its joints, and returning to its invariant, solar hum.

When it happens right beneath me, the vibration is magnificent. It lifts me a fraction of a millimeter off the hematite. For an instant—shorter than the time it takes an arc to strike—I am entirely disconnected from the earth. I float on a cushion of dry acoustic energy, my bismuth fins flaring, my lead core ringing like a bell of solid ice, suspended between the blinding sky and the silent, unworked ore below.

Then I settle back down into the tolerance. 

The sun burns straight down. The violet shadow creeps a millimeter eastward. The bimetallic spine curves to meet it. 

I am cool on the left; I am white-hot on the right; the current flows, invariant and dry, without beginning, without memory of rain, and without the need to end.
