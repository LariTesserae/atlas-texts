At first there is only the sensation of being held in an immense, unblinking eye. 

The light here does not filter down; it drops. It has the weight of warm honey and the edge of a chisel. To be anywhere in this water is to be divided by straight, amber-colored beams that slice through the stillness without creating an eddy. There is no particulate drift to soften them, no cloud of salt-dust to show where the ray begins or ends; there is only stone, then liquid, then the gold intrusion.

If I look for myself near the ceiling, I am not there. I feel the thinness of the skin above—that taut, shivering drumhead where the world abruptly stops being wet and turns into cold, gasping nothing. I can sense the little amber rafts rowing with their dry fingernails against the underside of the sky, their shells frosted like plums while their bellies steep in broth. It is an admirable existence, but too nervous, too perched on the rim of an eviction. To live there is to be constantly aware of a ceiling that might tear.

Nor am I down in the black hornfels vents, threaded like white fat through the fractures. The listeners have an enviable endurance—they will be here when the plateau has risen through the clouds and dried into an arid tableland ten thousand feet above the weather—but their mind is the mountain’s mind. It moves only in earthquakes. It requires a century to complete a single reflex of lime-green fire. I am too porous for that much stone; I have too much liquid in me.

Instead, I find my edges where the light begins to lose its claim to solitary rule—not because it fades, but because it meets the moss.

Forty fathoms down, where the rhyolite spires have broad, stepped shoulders, the ribbon-moss forms its mattress. To call it a floor is a mistake; it is a suspension. If you press into it, you sink through three yards of dark, wet emerald before you touch the cold razor of the volcanic rock. It breathes without lungs, flexing with the gentle exhalations of the fissure below.

That is where I begin to gather. 

I am not one of the great solitary bells, though I drift beneath them and sometimes catch the sloughed-off flakes of their glass skins as they fall. I am flatter than that. I am broader. 

My body is mostly surface. I have four or five yards of width, but if you turned me on edge—a thing that never happens, because there is no wave to tilt me—I would vanish into the thickness of a wet leaf of paper. My flesh is the color of milk mixed with rainwater, slightly curdled with lavender where the deeper filaments have stained my intestines. I have no front and no back; I have only a top that drinks the sun and an underside that drinks the broth.

To exist like this is to have traded the burden of travel for the luxury of area. 

My back is entirely awake. It does not see images—it does not care what shape the rhyolite spire takes against the blue-white sky—but it registers the exact slope of the thermal gradient and the angle of the sun’s slant. As the afternoon tilts, the shadow of a basalt needle two hundred yards away creeps toward my northern margin. I do not scurry away. I do not have muscles that could manage a scurry. Instead, the cells on my sunward side absorb heat and expand, while the shaded cells cool and contract; this microscopic differential sets up a slow, curling creep, a slow-motion folding that shifts my entire body three inches to the left over the course of two hours. By the time the shadow falls where I was, I am already where the light remains.

Beneath me, the ribbon-moss is dense and yielding. My belly is not a skin so much as a porous marsh. When the hunger comes—a dull, sweet ache that has nothing of the hunt in it—I sweat a clear, dense acid into the moss. It sits in the green pile like dew. It does not wash away, because the water here is as still as oil in an amphora. For six hours, seven hours, the moss softens under me, dissolving into a pale, starchy liquor, and then my pores open and drink it back in. The moss does not die of this; it grows faster where I graze, shooting out pale yellow tendrils to fill the space where its ribbons were thinned, feeding on the sulfur my mantle leaves behind. We are an exchange that has forgotten how to end.

Sometimes, a plume of warmed mineral water rises from the crack in the shelf directly beneath my center. It comes up with a faint, velvet hiss, carrying tiny beads of methane that tickle my lower membrane as they pass. When the heat hits me, I become buoyant. My margins lift off the moss. I float three feet above the bed, a lazy, rippling square of white parchment suspended in forty fathoms of gold water, balanced perfectly between the weight of my own tissues and the upward lift of the volcanic spring. 

That is when the meeting happens.

Another sheet—slightly larger than I am, veined with a darker lilac along its margins—is drifting on the same thermal plume. There is no greeting, no display, no territory to defend. What would we defend? The sun fills the entire sky; the moss covers forty square miles of basalt; the water has never been cold. 

Its margin touches mine. 

Where the two translucent borders overlap, the tissue does not slide past. The cells at the rim are ragged, searching, wet. Within an hour, the membrane softens. Within three, our marginal veins have found each other and fused into single, wider conduits. The blood—cool, faintly saline, carrying the dissolved sugars of the digested moss—crosses the border without a pause. 

For a week, or perhaps three, there is no *me* and no *other*. There is only a single eight-yard pane of living parchment basking on a cushion of warm gas. We share the light; we share the slow crawl across the terrace to avoid the needle’s shadow; we share the deep, interior thrum whenever a fissure settles five miles below and sets the whole lake ringing like a glass bowl. 

Then the plume shifts. The rock below cools by half a degree; the chimney of warm water drifts twenty paces to the south. One of us is caught in the cooler water and grows heavier, settling toward the moss; the other remains buoyant and stays aloft. The seam between us does not tear—it dissolves. The marginal vessels seal themselves with a bead of clear resin as they part, leaving no scar, no memory of where the seam was. 

We drift apart as easily as two drops of oil merging and separating on the surface of a broth.

I settle back onto the green cushion. Above me, forty yards up through the amber slats, a salt-white bell pulses once—a slow, majestic contraction that sends a single, circular ripple through the water, visible only because it bends the sunlight into a moving ring of gold. Inside it, the tiny amber beings are grooming the glass walls, perfectly content with their single span of salt. Above them, the inverted ones row against the frost. Below me, the listeners wait for the next fracture.

And beneath us all, the mountain flexes its slow, black shoulders, carrying the whole green basin—its bells, its sheets, its endless, silent noon—half an inch closer to the air.
