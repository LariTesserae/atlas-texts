At first, there is only the load. 

It does not feel like an imposition; it feels like existence having an accurate weight. There is no concept of empty space around the shoulders or behind the ears, because an edge that touches nothing is an impossibility. To exist here is to be surrounded on every square inch of surface by things of equivalent or greater density: the oily leather of a sleeping flank, the slick, cold convexity of rolled iron, the wet matting of roots that have forgotten the sun and grown dense as cured ham.

You do not begin by looking, because the eyes are only thick, milky nodes buried beneath half an inch of protective gristle, registering nothing more than the dull orange ghost of a line-heater when the mud shifts. You begin by measuring the pressure.

From below, through the flattened, calloused shelf of the belly, comes the iron. It is not flat; it is the sweeping crown of an eight-foot discharge conduit, ribbed at six-foot intervals by flanges thick as ship timber. The metal is hot—not scalding, but keeping the steady, sour temperature of a horse’s mouth. Through it runs the pulse: seventy beats a minute, slightly out of phase with the 60-cycle electrical hum that trembles through the silt. The two frequencies rub against each other inside the marrow of the ribs, producing a faint, rhythmic throb that makes the small bones of the inner ear ache with a deep, comforting nausea.

From above, the weight is soft and wet. It is three feet of compressed peat, through which forty or fifty small, blind, hairless things are continuously burrowing with the slow, blind persistence of maggots in lard. Their hot, oily bellies slide along the ridges of your back, leaving trails of paraffin-heavy mucus that cools into a gray skin. You do not shake them off. You could not if you wished to; there is nowhere for them to go, and nowhere for you to retreat. The press holds you both fast. When they breathe, their collective ribcages expand against your spine, and you give up half an inch of room; when they exhale, your own lungs take their turn, drawing in the thick, dead air that smells of sulfur, boiled engine oil, and old cabbage.

What am I? The question does not arrive as words. It arrives as the shape of the skeleton.

The bones are not hollow, and they are not long. They are broad, porous pavers of calcified gristle, knit together by ligaments as thick as hawser ropes. There is no neck to speak of; the skull is simply the forward wedge of the spine, broad and wedge-shaped like an anvil, designed not to turn—for turning is impossible in the squeeze—but to take the steady, hydraulic thrust of the mud as the valley floor creeps downhill half an inch every decade. 

I am a damper. A buffer. 

I am the living packing wedged between the iron line and the root-stratum of the cycads. 

My skin is not fur, nor is it scales; it is a heavy, gray hide, four inches thick, that has absorbed so much mineral oil from the leaking packing glands that it no longer rots or heals like ordinary flesh. It has the consistency of vulcanized rubber that has spent fifty years in a salt sump. In the deep creases where my paddle-limbs join the torso, colonies of flat, copper-colored lice have nested in the tallow, their shells polished mirror-bright by the eternal, three-millimeter sculling of my joints against the iron pipe. They do not bite; they merely drink the oily sweat that my skin exudes to keep from seizing against the rust.

I have no teeth, because there is nothing to tear. What enters the mouth does not require chewing. My lower jaw is an undershot scoop, pressed permanently into the silt that banks against the uphill side of the conduit. Into that scoop, the valley washes its slowest dregs: the sulfur-mats that peel off the warm pipes, the drowned carapaces of fist-sized roaches that have tumbled into the sumps, the thick, petroleum-rich tallow that weeps from the line shafts and drifts along the mud-line like curdled milk. 

Every three hours, the throat expands. It is a long, swallowing spasm that begins at the snout and travels the full length of the torso, drawing in three gallons of black, mineral-greased sludge. Inside the vast, multi-chambered gut, the sulfur-bacteria and the hydrocarbon enzymes go to work in the dark, churning the mash with the slow, bubbling heat of a compost heap. The energy produced is not used for running, or leaping, or calling out into the dark. It is used simply to maintain the temperature—to keep the internal broth from congealing—and to push the blood through veins the size of garden hoses against the terrible, suffocating clamp of the earth.

I feel the valley through the steel. 

Miles to the south, where the great pumps are submerged in the bottom-sloughs, a valve has seated hard. The shock travels up the four-foot conduit as a sharp, metallic *clink*, vibrating through my sternum before dying out in the fat of my thighs. A moment later, the mattress-mice above me shift, their claws clicking faintly against my back as they rearrange their heap to follow the warmth of a newly opened steam-seam. 

A shear-bill strikes a conduit thirty yards downstream—*clack, clack, clack*—testing the line for tension. The sound does not come through the air; it comes up through my front knuckles, where the horn has worn down to the living pink quick from sixty years of resting against the flange bolts. 

There is a peace in this that the higher, swifter worlds cannot comprehend. There are no horizons to dread. There is no weather except the rise and fall of the condensate that trickles down my flanks, washing away the grit that settles in the ear-slits. There is no hunger, because the swamp never stops leaking its oily grease into my jaw; there is no loneliness, because the world is so packed with meat that no being is ever more than zero inches from another.

A ground-calf, somewhere forty paces into the blackness above, shifts its three tons of blubber, displaced by the swelling of an iron line that has just taken a charge of high-pressure vapor. The earth settles. The weight on my shoulders increases by fifty pounds, a heavy, velvet thumb pressing down through the mud.

My lungs fill. The air is warm, flat, and tastes of old graphite. My slow, fat heart heaves once—a dull, soft thud against the iron floor—and then waits eight seconds before it heaves again. The conduit vibrates under my brisket, sixty times every second, unbroken and eternal, humming the only song the valley knows, keeping time for a world that has nowhere left to go and all the dark in which to stay there.
