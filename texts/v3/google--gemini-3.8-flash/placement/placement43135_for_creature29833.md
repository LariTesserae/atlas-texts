At first, the instinct is to look for an edge—to imagine oneself standing at some hypothetical rim where the poured stone breaks off into whatever was here before: a salt marsh, an iron-red trench, or a dead sea basin scraped clean by earth-movers. One wants a perimeter to lean against. 

But if you are here, you are not at the edge. The edge is so distant that the curve of the planet, or simply the dense, trembling refraction of the midday lime-steam, has erased it completely. You are in the interior, which means every direction is mathematically identical to every other direction.

I would not be standing. To stand upright here is an error; the air two feet above the ceramic is an anvil of radiant glare, and the air six feet up is empty, offering neither perch nor shade. To possess height here is simply to be an antenna for heat, unanchored and visible to nothing.

No, I would find myself lower down—pressed toward that razor-thin zone where the water sheets, or where the water has just left. 

At first, I think of the seams: sliding into the hairline gap between two fifty-yard pavers, wedged into the cold, sharp-edged grit of the unwashed silica sand. It is tempting to be the silver thread, blind and segmented, feeling the multi-ton slabs sigh and grind against my flanks as the squalls pass and the stone shrinks by half a millimeter. There is a deep, architectural comfort in the grout. You would know the world only as tension and release: the sudden hydraulic squeeze when the sun comes out and the ceramic expands; the loose, wet slackening when the cold cloud breaks overhead; the brief, ecstatic rush of warm downpour dragging you out into the open mirror to swim in two dimensions.

Yet the seams are too narrow, too solitary, too purely mechanical. They belong to things that were already finished before the stone was even dry.

And I would not be the kites, either. To float on the thermal cushion two feet above the deck, perpetually balancing on a pillow of hot air, is too precarious, too nervous. One sudden shadow from an anvil-headed cloud and your lift dies; you flutter down like discarded parchment onto stone hot enough to curl you into ash before the first drop hits.

So I look to the pans. Not the center of the catchments, where the amber siphonophores graze on the fresh lime, but the *margin*. 

The margin is where the world happens. 

In the catchments, the water is an ankle deep, but as the afternoon advances and the squall moves off toward the pale horizon, that water does not retreat toward a river or a drain. It shrinks by evaporation alone, pulling back across the broad, micro-graded slope at the speed of a walking man. 

That is where I would be. 

Not quite a creature of the open stone, and not quite an animal of the pool, but something formed out of the drying fringe. 

I would be flat—impossibly flat, scarcely thicker than a wet leather sole. My underside would not be skin, but a broad, pale apron of horn and porous felt, soaked through with the saturated, alkaline water of the pan. As long as I am wet, I can slide. I would move by capillary action and the slow, rhythmic contraction of microscopic ventral gills, gliding across the trowel-marks without ever breaking the surface film, keeping pace with the receding edge of the water.

My back would be the color of the lime itself: that pale, hydraulic, matte white that gives nothing back to the sky. Not smooth, but pitted with thousands of microscopic wells that trap droplets of rain and hold them against the sun, turning my own body into a miniature, self-cooling catchment. 

I would not have eyes. What is there to see? The monoliths do not move; the clouds are merely shifts in pressure and temperature that my skin reads instantly. Instead, I would live by resonance. 

Through the dense ceramic beneath me, I would hear the world:
The deep, sub-audible *thrum* of a rainfront five miles to the north, its millions of warm drops striking the plates like shot against a drumhead.
The sharp, metallic *clink* of the pavers shifting in their beds as the thermal line moves across them.
The faint, dry scrape of the shadow-dwellers’ pads far off, inching along the northern wall of a monolith as the gnomon of shade turns.

What would I eat? Not the flotsam of the sky—I have no beak or bristles for spiders—and not the lichens in the dark of the cracks. 

I would eat the salt. 

As the pans dry, the dissolved calcium hydroxide, the slaked minerals, and the fine aerial dust crystallize along the waterline in a fragile, sparkling crust, a rim of white sugar that marks where the moisture was five minutes ago. I would follow that rim. My leading edge would be a soft, wide scraper of fibrous chitin, gathering that fresh, alkaline salt-fringe before it hardens into dry stone, metabolizing the curing chemistry of the floor, converting the fresh minerals into the dense, cool armor of my back.

And when the water fails—as it must, every afternoon, when the sun burns the pan down to bare, steaming ceramic—I would not panic. 

I would do what the floor taught me to do. 

I would seek the nearest polygonal joint. I would pull my edges tight, flatten my soft periphery into the razor-slit of the seam, and let my underbelly cement itself to the trowel-grooves with a bead of clear, calcium-rich mucus. 

For two hours, while the sun turns the ceramic into a kiln, I would become part of the floor. A slightly thicker paver. An unrecorded imperfection in the pour. My heart would slow until it matched the seismic frequency of the plates; my fluid would retreat into the porous chambers of my back; I would be baked stone-hard, dry as chalk, dreaming the dead, white dream of the terrace.

And then, the sound would come.

First, the wind: smelling of cold dust, slaking lime, and sky.
Then, the drop in pressure, popping the air in my desiccated shell.
And finally, the great, advancing roar—the wall of warm water drumming across five leagues of empty stone.

The first sheet of rain would strike my back, hissing into steam. The second would fill my cups. The third would lift my glued skirts from the ceramic with a soft, wet tear, and the sudden current would catch me, floating me off the trowel-marks, spinning me gently in the warm, ankle-deep flood as the basin refills.

I would uncurl my gills. I would taste the new wash of minerals leaching from the stone. 

And in that clean, unpopulated light, beneath the towering white cumulus, I would begin the slow, blind glide back toward the center of the pan, waiting for the rain to cease, waiting for the rim to form again, taking my small, imperceptible nourishment from the slow, magnificent wearing-out of a world that was made too large to ever end.
