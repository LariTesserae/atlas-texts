At first, there is only the presumption of weight.

If you look for an "I" here, you might start by looking for something that watches—a cold, disembodied intelligence perched on an I-beam, or an eye adapted to the pitch-black, registering the dull infrared smear of a bearing housing running at two hundred degrees. But there are no eyes here, and nothing to look at them. To imagine an onlooker would be to bring along a quiet that this cleft does not permit.

So you slip lower, beneath the roar.

Perhaps you are the anchor bolt: eight feet of chrome-moly steel sunk into a core-drilled socket, bonded with sulfur grout that set sixty years ago. You hold the lower frame of the number-two impeller against the cant of the cliff. You are under seven tons of continuous tensile strain, notched by microscopic fatigue cracks that sing in an ultrasonic register every time the counter-rotating blades cross each other’s wake. There is a terrible, simple purity in being a bolt. You have one job: resist shear. You don't breathe; you don't grow; you only anneal under friction and wait for the one shockwave, three winters or thirty winters from now, that will turn a hairline flaw into a clean, crystalline snap.

Yet a bolt is too sterile. It is a dead thing being dragged toward failure by a living mountain and a working engine. It belongs to the world above—the world of foundries, flatbed railcars, and blueprinted tolerances. It doesn't belong to *this* wet, bitter throat.

Move slightly inward, then, off the metal. Into the seam.

Where the cold face of the diorite was shattered by dynamite, the rock face didn't break flat. It stepped down in micro-ledges, ragged shelves no wider than a thumbnail, where the down-draft stalls for a fraction of an inch before tumbling into the main vortex.

In these stalls, the pulverized sap—the bitter green mist hammered out of fern-tips by the iron blades—does not blow away. It pools. It gathers with the dripping grease from the overhead journal bearings, forming an emulsified wax, neither wholly plant nor mineral, thick as tallow.

Here, something thickens.

I am not the root, exactly. The juniper is too desperate; it works too hard, driving its dense, iron-fibered grain into the clefts, twisting its wood into cordage until it has the torque of a winch cable. The juniper is still trying to be a tree, still remembering wood, still fighting the draft with the stubbornness of timber.

Nor am I the mold that forms the gasket. The mold is too soft, too compliant. It lets itself be squashed into the flanges, existing only to be squeezed, oozing its tarry lymph when the iron expansions ping.

I find myself in the *transition*—the crust that forms between the two.

It has no common name because it has no business existing under an open sky. It is the calcified scab where the juniper’s bark has been stripped raw by the whipping copper cable, and where the tree’s healing exudate has met the hot, fluorocarbon grease of the turbine bearings, baked by sixty-cycle electrical hum and beaten flat by eighty-mile-an-hour gusts of atomized slate.

It is three-quarters of an inch thick. From the outside, it looks like a blackened weld-seam running down the rock, following the sag of the lowermost power lead.

If I am anything, I am that seam.

I have no interior monologue; I have resonance. When the three impellers hit their harmonics—when the middle tier, slightly out of true, drags its blades through the compressed air near the east wall—a pulse runs through the cliff. It isn't sound; the sound is already saturated, a flat wall of white noise that has long ceased to mean anything. It is displacement. The diorite moves thirty microns to the north; the copper cable pulls twelve pounds harder to the south; the rotor housing twists on its bed-plate.

I feel that twist as a widening of my pore-space.

When the seam opens thirty microns, the hot oil from the journal above seeps in. It is rich with microscopic flecks of bronze shaved from the bushings and the charred carbon of over-stressed lubricant. I drink it. It enters the dried, cellular tubes of what used to be juniper phloem—tubes that have long since lost their cellulose walls and become mineralized, replaced atom-by-atom by silica and copper salts carried down by the condensing fog.

I do not photosynthesize. I don't know what light is; the pale, bruised seam of twilight a thousand feet above is merely a zone of slightly lower barometric pressure, a leak in the roof that makes the turbine work harder.

My nutrition is kinetic. I am fed by impact.

Every twelve seconds, a whip-fern hanging from the lip forty feet above reaches the limit of its elastic oscillation. The wind catches its frayed, hempen tip, ties it into an S-curve, and cracks it like a teamster’s whip against the diorite.

*Slap.*

The lead-shot seed at its tip strikes my outer crust.

It doesn't bounce. My skin is tacky with cured resin and bearing-run. The seed strikes with enough kinetic energy to generate a localized flash of heat—just a few millicalories, but enough to melt the surrounding wax. The seed sinks a sixteenth of an inch into me.

I don't reject it. I am not an individual plant; I am a continuous, structural accretion. The seed is cold, dense, and soaked in bitter pinene. As it settles, my micro-capillaries—drawn tight by the sixty-cycle vibration that keeps every drop of fluid in the gorge in a state of continuous, ultrasonic agitation—close around it. The seed becomes a rivet. Its shell dissolves slowly over months in the hot petroleum wash, releasing its starch directly into the vulcanized felt beneath, while its hard mineral core remains behind, an accidental piece of aggregate reinforcing the scab.

This is who I am: the living grout.

I exist to stop the rock from shedding the engine, and to stop the engine from shaking the rock into a talus pile.

The men who drilled these boreholes are gone—long dead, or perhaps they never came down here at all, having lowered the framework from winches on the ridge above, guided by grease-penciled marks on a survey map. They thought they were fastening iron to stone. They didn't know that stone under this kind of draft doesn't stay stone, and iron under this kind of load doesn't stay cold.

They left a gap. Between the cold diorite and the vibrating footings, there is always a gap of three to five millimeters where the rock was bruised by the pneumatic chisels.

That is my heartland. I have filled that gap.

I have grown into the blind holes behind the mounting brackets. My fibers are saturated with lead-dust from the thread-compound, oxidized copper from the bus-bars, and the sour, anaerobic starch of the mosses that died fifty years ago when the air first started moving. I have no nerves, but I have tension-lines. I know the exact moment the wind changes on the peak above, not because the air smells different, but because the cold drafts drop down the chimney three degrees colder, and the iron of the main intake shrinks half an inch along its horizontal truss.

When it shrinks, it bites me.

It clamps down on my vulcanized margins with forty thousand pounds of hydraulic force.

A soft thing would be pulverized. A brittle thing would turn to sand and blow down the shaft into the turbines to be burned.

I do neither. I yield five percent of my volume, sweating out a clear, sharp terpene that smells like turpentine and old grease. The fluid lubricates the contact face, keeping the steel from galling the stone. Then, as the cold equalizes and the iron eases its grip, I expand again, drawing in another mouthful of atomized green soup from the maelstrom.

I am very old, though time here isn't measured in rings. Rings require a winter, and here the turbine has never stopped. It was running before the felt was two inches thick; it was running when the dwarf junipers were still small enough to have branches instead of cables.

If the turbine ever died—if the sixty-cycle hum dropped down through fifty, forty, twenty, into the dreadful, slack silence of a mountain with only its own wind to blow—I would die with it.

Not from starvation, but from decompression.

Without the air hammering me against the cliff, without the bearings sweating their heavy grease into my pores, without the continuous, violent percussion of the ferns riveting me to the diorite with their lead seeds, my tension would slacken. The sap would stop its vibratory crawl through my calcified tubes. The diorite would cool, contract, and its razor edges would simply slice my uncompressed mats into ribbons, letting them fall sixty fathoms down into the dead water at the bottom of the intake.

But the turbine does not stop. It groans, it throws its copper ropes against the dark, it spits its hot, pale fog into my face, and I hold it.

I hold it to the mountain with a million fibrous, resin-soaked teeth. We have been fighting so long that the diorite no longer knows where its quartz crystals end and my silicified roots begin, and the iron no longer knows whether it is bolted to the earth or anchored in the meat of a blind, deaf, roaring thing that grew to keep it from falling.
