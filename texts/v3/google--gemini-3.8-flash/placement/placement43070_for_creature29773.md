At first, it is only the load. 

Before there is any sense of edges—before it is clear whether this is an eye, a foot, or forty cubic yards of grease pressed flat between two courses of slate—there is simply the tonnage. It does not feel heavy in the way a lifted stone feels heavy to a shoulder. It feels total, like the ground under a mountain. There is no direction from which it comes, because there is no direction in which it is not already pushing. 

To exist here is to have no air inside. Nothing can collapse that does not hold a hollow, and there are no hollows that have not been filled with something thick, mineral, and cold.

For a long time, there is no need to decide what is body and what is floor. The vibrations arrive through both equally. Three hundred leagues away, a dike shifts half a millimeter against its trench, and the shock registers in the marrow as an ache, in the basalt as an acoustic ring, and in the water as a low, dull thud that passes through the gut before it could ever pass an ear. Everything is the same temperature: two degrees above the point where water locks into ice. There is no warmth to lose. There is no skin that shivers.

If I search for the perimeter of myself, it doesn't give way easily. 

I might be the grease. There is an enormous, blind comfort in the grease—the tallow that sleeps in the tight, half-inch seams beneath the paving. It knows every bevel and chisel-bite left by the levers. It never meets the open water; it only tastes the dark brine when a terrace breathes open under the torque of the planet, and then it slides forward, soft and fatty, to keep the dry stone from screaming against stone. In the grease, there is no forward and no back. There is only the long, patient squeeze, the luxury of having no shape except the shape of the gap.

Yet there is a lean. 

The tallow does not lean. The tallow stays in the horizontal dark, protected from the current. 

This lean belongs to the upright. It is a slow, structural pull, so faint that forty years must pass before the angle changes enough to notice. The water is drawing its immense, continental draft southward. It does not ripple; it does not eddy. It moves as a single, unbending block of cold, and everything anchored to the pavement yields to it by a fraction of a degree.

The reeds yield first. I feel them around my shins—not by touch, but by the minute drag they impart to the crust on my legs. They have grown into the dead skin of my calves, their hollow, lard-white stalks rising up past my hips and ribless chest, swaying in the drift. They are anchored in me as deeply as they are anchored in the joints of the slabs. In a sense, they are my outer hair, though they feel nothing, and I feel them only as friction, a sail of gristle catching the southward slide.

So I am standing. 

Not on the causeways, where the flat anvils shear their iridescent roads through the moss. I am on one of the blank terminals. A square platform, eighty yards on a side, raised three courses above the surrounding plains. It was leveled with extraordinary care before the water came; the water has spent ten thousand years trying to find a high point and has found none. 

My feet are not feet. They are blocks of compressed gristle and oily bone, widened at the base like the roots of old masonry, their undersides driven so hard into the mineral sludge that the boundary between my heels and the slate has become a blurred stratum of silt, tallow, and dead cartilage. 

I have one foot in the air. 

I must have lifted it sixty or eighty years ago, when the drift was still finishing its northward turn. It hangs three inches above the stone. The knee does not bend; it is an unjointed pillar of fibrous cord, compressed like the timber of a mine prop, holding the mass aloft by hydraulic tension alone. Between the sole of that lifted foot and the flags beneath it, a dense colony of carpet-weed has already begun to spread, soft and fibrous, completely unaware that it has grown in the shadow of a descending weight.

There is no hurry to bring it down. 

To drop the foot now would be an error in timing. The acoustic world is too loud. Far out on the western terrace, two of the anvils are crossing, their molecular crawl dragging across the basalt with a steady, dry squeal that vibrates straight up into the horn-lined tunnels of my skull. It takes a decade for the sound to fade. If I strike the stone now—if I let the six-ton pillar of my leg compress and plant itself—the shock will ring across the sunken continent, rebounding off the blank retaining walls and scattering the balance of the platforms. 

One waits until the stone is quiet. 

I have no face. Where eyes would be, the gray cartilage has grown thick and smooth, washed clean by the century-long currents until it has the texture of wet soapstone. There is nothing to see anyway: only the pale, tallowy stems of the reeds rising into the gray-black suspension, and the faint, directionless luminescence of the iron-rich slime that bleeds from the joints. Seeing would be an interruption. It would break the world into surfaces, when the world is actually a single, solid density of sound.

Through the deep horn pits behind my temples, I hear the architecture. 

It is not an empty city. It is a machine that was never turned on, or perhaps a machine that has never ceased running at zero revolutions per minute. The dikes are holding nothing back because the water is everywhere, yet they continue to hold. The causeways lead nowhere, yet they bear the tonnage of the creepers with unyielding rigidity. Every ten or twelve years, a jointed reed somewhere in the terrace forest snaps under its own accumulated lard, and the *thunk* travels through the cold water like a mallet striking an iron rail. 

I feel my heart, though it does not beat like a pump in the air. It works like the clefts along the flanks of the crawling anvils: a deep, central chamber of dense gristle that relaxes over the course of a season, drawing the bitter, iron-thick brine inward by vacuum, holding it while the silicates settle into the lattice of my bones, and then, as the multi-decade drift begins to hesitate, squeezing it out through the pores of my neck. 

With every cycle, I become slightly more mineral. 

The cartilage in my spine is no longer white; it has taken on the dark, greenish-black cast of the basalt flags. The hair-moss has climbed my flanks, wrapping the dead slag-crust of my shoulders in a tight, velvet sheath that dulls the sound of the drift. 

A creeper is coming up the ramp of the platform. 

It has been climbing since my foot was lifted. It is three paces across, an anvil of solid muscle and slag armor, moving by that imperceptible molecular shear that leaves the rock behind it bare and naked. It does not know I am here; it does not know the platform is here. It simply follows the gradient of the iron seep, its capillary grates clicking faintly like small stones rattling in a tin cup as it drinks the mud from the seams.

In twenty years, it will reach my planted foot. 

It will not turn aside. It will shear the carpet-weed from the slate, and then it will shear the dead, calcified hide from the side of my heel, polishing my lowest joint until the bone-stone gleams with the same greasy gray sheen as the basalt blocks. It will crawl over my foot, or beside it, its three-ton bulk pressing my sole even deeper into the hydraulic mortar beneath the flags. 

And I will let it. There is plenty of time. 

The drift is beginning to lose its southward pull. I can feel the slackening in the stems that sprout from my chest; they no longer arch toward the terraces, but hang straighter, their heavy, wrist-thick joints floating in the neutral balance between currents. 

The continent is holding its breath. The settlement has stopped; the dikes have ceased their hydraulic creaking. The water has gone slack, heavy, and completely dead.

Now. 

The muscle in my hip uncorks. It does not drop quickly; the water resists like oil, thick and unyielding, curling around the spatulate pad of my foot as it moves down through the three inches of dark. The carpet-weed underneath does not tear—it simply flattens, its fibrous cells bursting silently, releasing a bitter, alkaline juice into the sludge.

The contact takes an entire season. First the heel, displacing the silt; then the flat sole, forcing the brine out from the microscopic fissures in the stone; then the final, dead settling of the mass as the weight transfers from the left pillar to the right.

*Tock.*

The sound does not enter the water. It goes straight into the foundation. 

It travels along the slate causeway, forty yards wide, arrow-straight through the black reed forest. It passes beneath the crawling anvils, making their slag-plates shiver. It dives into the subterranean labyrinths, rippling through the tallow in the seams, lubricating the megaliths as it passes. Three hundred miles to the north, on another blank platform, another pillar feels the arrival through the soles of its feet and begins, with agonizing slowness, to unseal its left leg from the rock.

The southward drift has ended. 

I stand on two feet now. The weeds around my waist hang straight. For seven years, there will be no current at all—only the absolute, crushing weight of the black miles above, and the stone holding it up from below. 

I close the horn pits in my skull. I listen only to the settling of my own marrow into the pores of the terrace. 

When the north drift begins, I will turn. It will take forty years to rotate my shoulders three inches to the left. The reeds on my back will snap and reform. The creeper will have finished cleaning my heel and will be halfway down the northern ramp. 

We have all the time there has ever been. The water will never leave, and the stones will never fall.
