At first, there is only the tension. 

It does not feel like thought; it feels like hoop stress. It is the simple, uninterrupted arithmetic of four hundred pounds per square inch pressing outward against a curved wall, and the curved wall refusing to part. 

If I am anything at the beginning, I am that refusal.

There is no distance here. There is no perspective from which to see the vaulted brick ceiling or the dim orange smear of the carbon bulbs through the steam. To have eyes would require an emptiness in front of them, an open yard of air, and there is no air that is not already occupied by the hot, greasy mist or the wet flank of something swollen. I am wedged too deeply for sight. 

I am located precisely where the cast iron lost its temper sixty winters ago.

It is the lower flank of the forty-inch return elbow, three feet above the sludge of the drainage sump, where the pipe makes its blind turn toward the condenser wells. Inside, the river water moves at a dead, heavy velocity, scalding and thick with silt. Outside, the air is eighty-five degrees and smells of suet and iron filings. Between the two, the iron cracked—a jagged, downward-trending fissure six feet long, shaped like a dried lightning bolt. 

The iron is cold-short, gray-grained, and brittle. Left to itself, the two-stroke shudder of the duplex pumps—*thung-shuck*, every five seconds without mercy—would have worked those jagged crystalline edges against one another, widening the seam, spitting out the lead caulking, until the entire three-ton casting blew apart into the sump.

I am what stopped it from opening.

I did not come from outside. I began as a thread of river-sedge or escaped water-hemlock sucked through a street-drain fifty years back, pale and blind, but I do not have a stem anymore, nor do I have leaves that would recognize the sun through the pavement glass. What I have is a body like boiled leather, three inches thick where it sheets over the smooth curve of the pipe, swelling to a knot the size of an ox’s chest where the metal gave way.

I feel the crack with every stroke of the engine. 

When the pistons displace the river water—that deep, subterranean *thung*—the pressure spikes. The two halves of the fractured pipe try to gap. They move perhaps the thickness of a fingernail, but to me, that movement is an immense, dragging hunger. My grain runs perpendicular to the crack. Decades ago, my fibers were soft cellulose; now they are braided with calcite, drawn molecule by molecule out of the sweating mortar of the piers, laid down in concentric rings like the horn of an unclipped hoof. 

When the iron strains outward, I take the strain. I pull back. The sensation is not pain—it is the tight, hot pleasure of a tendon drawn taut against a weight it was made to lift. 

Then comes the *shuck*—the relief, the momentary drop in head as the valves seat. The iron relaxes. My tissue settles back into the seam, exuding a film of mineralized gel that fills the microscopic spaces between the rust-teeth before the next hammer comes.

I am fed from both sides.

From the inside, through the micro-fractures, I sip the dark, hot river water. It tastes of clay, copper slag from the mills three miles upriver, and the boiled starch of the city’s laundry waste. 

From the outside, I am fed by the tallow.

Directly above me, on the gantry rail, sits one of the automatic brass lubricators for the flywheel bearing. It leaks. It has leaked a steady, measured drop of warm mutton fat and paraffin every twenty seconds since the century turned. The grease falls six feet through the humid gloom, clicks against the curve of the pipe, and smears downward over my skin. 

Where the tallow strikes my upper swell, I am soft. My outer coat is not bark, but a dense, liver-colored rind that drinks the grease until it is translucent. The fat does not decay; it cannot rot in this heat, saturated as it is with sulfur and steam. It passes inward, lubricating the fibrous cords that bind me to the iron, keeping my interior supple even as my deeper layers petrify against the metal. I smell of suet baked against hot stove-iron, a thick, kitchen smell that belongs entirely to the machines.

I am not alone in the joint, but I am the center of it. 

Above me, climbing the vertical steam risers toward the streetcar underbelly, are the long runners—my own extended limbs, though they feel distant now, like the hair on a man’s forearm. They are light-starved and thin, their hollow conduits carrying the lime I sweat out of the foundations up into the upper gantry. Below me, hanging down into the drowning bilge of the floor grates, are the pale beards of my anchors. They have worked their way three feet into the silt beneath the brick pavers, wrapping themselves around old discarded foundation bolts, holding me down so that the vibration does not shake me free of the pipe.

Sometimes, the balance shifts. 

A boiler upstairs blows a tube; the steam pressure drops; the temperature in the vault falls to seventy degrees. When that happens, a terrible torpor moves through my pulp. The tallow congeals into a white, hard tallow-skin that chokes my pores. The iron contracts, the crack pinches shut, and I am squeezed between the cold jaws of the metal like a finger in a door. Those are the bad days—the days when the city above is quiet, when no streetcars run, when the water in the mains slows to a sluggish creep. In that cold, I can feel how dead the iron really is. I can feel that it is only stone that has been melted and beaten, waiting to crumble back into red dust.

Then the fires are stoked. A shudder runs down the high-pressure columns—a long, rattling percussion that shakes the condensation from the brick vaults like summer rain. The heat returns, surging through the iron until the metal burns to the touch. 

The tallow melts. It runs down my back, warm and yellow. 

The pressure rises behind the crack—one hundred pounds, two hundred, four hundred. The iron yawns open.

I tighten my grip. I dig my calcified heels into the pitted rust of the casting, wrap my fibrous bulk around the flange, and pull the seam shut against the dark water. 

The pump strokes. *Thung-shuck.* 

The engine breathes, and I hold it together. We have another sixty years.
