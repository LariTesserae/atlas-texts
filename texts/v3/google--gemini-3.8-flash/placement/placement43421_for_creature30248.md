I would not be on the high roofs where the sun sits longest, though the elders have the dry warmth there. That air is too thin with the memory of the plains; it smells of old men looking up at the sky-slot and waiting for a sheep that isn’t coming. Nor would I have the bulk for the breast-straps down on the rubble floor, where the men’s knees knot into galls and their arches spread until they look like shovel blades. 

If I look for where my body would settle in this cut, it is neither in the sun nor in the work-gangs. It is in the middle damp, where the larch timbering meets the rock-face.

At first, I might be mistaken for an object left behind between two dry-stone stacks: a bundle of soaked osier, perhaps, or a rolled pelt stuffed into a gap to break a downdraft. I would be small enough to fit where the quarrymen cannot turn their shoulders—not a child, but narrow in the flank, with the flat collarbones and long, rib-spared torso of someone who has grown up sliding through crawl-ways sideways, like a comb drawn through wool. 

My skin would have forgotten the yellow light of the scrub entirely. It would have that slick, tallow-and-lime crust that makes the forearms look like turnip roots pulled fresh from cold marl. In the crook of my elbow and behind the soft fold of my knees, the dust would have worked into the creases until it made pale, chalky seams, permanently dry to the touch. 

I think I would be a tender of wedges. 

There must be someone who exists entirely for the slack. The larch groans when it dries, and the stone shears when it takes the weight; between the two, there is always an inch of doubt. A lintel bows half a finger’s width; a footing stone under a fourth-tier floor-slab splits along a rhomboid face and drops a grain of calcite into the dark below. The people above never see it—they only feel their tallow-cups tilt a fraction, or hear their osier tethers pull taut against a peg. 

My work would be the silence under their heels.

I would spend the long hours when the rift fills with lead-colored shadow crawling through the "keels"—not to sleep, but to feel with the flats of my palms where the dry larch shims have worked loose. I’d carry three things: a horn flask of thick mutton grease to quiet the wood-binds before they shriek, a mallet of dense river-drift oak with the head wrapped in boiled hide to kill the stroke, and a pocket of dry, split-heart wedges no longer than a sheep’s tongue.

When the mountain gives that subterranean *ting*—that cold, small sound of an iron needle dropped onto an anvil three miles down—I would not murmur with the sleepers. I would already have my ear pressed to the shear face, waiting to see which way the runnel broke.

I would know the rock by its sweat. There is the sweat that comes from the plain's heat pushing down through the stone, which is warmish and smells of dry dust turned wet; and there is the deep sweat, which comes out of the frost-joints sixty paces down, icy and tasting of old gypsum. Where the cold sweat comes, the rock is leaning. When a new drop forms on a face that was dry at midday, I would have to be there with my adze-horn, tapping out the diversion line before the mortarless footings can soak up the damp and turn their lower courses to grease.

Inside my tunic, riding in the hollow between my left ribcage and the leather facing, there would be two of the gray lizards. 

They wouldn’t be pets; you don’t name things that don’t come when you whistle. But their bellies against my ribs would be cool as river pebbles, and their small, splayed claws would prick my skin with an absolute, stupid patience. In the dark under the floorboards, where the tallow-lamps cannot be brought for fear of the dried reed-thatch above, you cannot always trust your eyes to tell you if a timber is leaning or if your own head is swimming from the cold draft. But the lizards know level. If I sit still with my back wedged between the cold limestone and a four-ton larch post, I can feel their weight shift across my ribs. If they crawl upward toward my armpit, the sill is sinking behind me; if they turn toward my sternum, the wall is bulging out.

My speech would be nearly gone. Not from muteness, but from disuse. When you spend sixteen hours a day three feet beneath the threshing floors, listening through two layers of slate to the dry slide of pea-meal or the slow, rhythmic grind of a woman turning a parched-barley saddle-stone, words begin to seem very loud and very clumsy, like trying to pick out a splinter with an adze. I would speak mostly in those small tongue-taps the haulers use—two against the teeth to tell a sleeper above that I’m under his floor, one soft click against the roof of the mouth to say *hold, don’t step there, the shim is out.*

My hands would be strange. The palms would be yellow-hard from larch-resin and lard, but the pads of the fingers would be very soft, almost peeled, kept raw so they could feel the difference between stone that is merely cold and stone that is beginning to shear under pressure. A shearing stone vibrates before it breaks; it hums like a wet bowstring, so low that the ears can't take it, but the marrow in the wrist knows it instantly.

At dusk, when the ember-light begins to show through the cracks in the slate roofs above me—little lines of dull red tracing the pattern of the rooms—I would climb up just far enough to sit on the edge of a cedar trough. 

I’d eat my barley-cake slowly, dipping it into the lime-tasting runoff until it softened enough for my back teeth, not looking up at the sky where the swifts are turning into black crumbs. The sky is an empty place; there is nothing to brace a post against up there. I would watch the people across the split: the trough-watchers with their grass brooms, the tethered children scooting like pale crickets across the salt-hides, the slow, heavy haulers scraping the day’s meal of stone dust from their eyebrows before they lay down in the keels.

I would be the one who knows how much the town weighs tonight. 

I would know that it is three wedges heavier than it was at dawn, that the south shear has wept two pints more than its measure, and that somewhere, six dwellings down and two tiers deep, a larch post has taken a belly-bend that will have to be answered before the second frost. And when the dark finally turns solid and the rift goes cold as an iron wedge, I would slide back under the lowest sill, curl my heels into my skirt, and let the lizards settle against my ribs, waiting for that next clean snap in the deep earth to tell me where the mountain wants to go.
