I would not be up on the ledges where the swifts turn, and I would not be down where the char breast the current in the lowest flumes. I would be in the middle tiers, where the weight of the city presses hardest against the mountain’s hip.

You would find me folded into a corner where a three-family dwelling meets a dry buttress of living rock. My back would be pressed flat to the shale, my knees drawn up near my breastbone, keeping my center low and small. The duck-skin tunic is old—three winters of molted down stuffed between the inner vole pelts and the greased outer feathers—so thick that the cold rock against my spine feels only like a cool palm held against the woolly heat of a sleeping animal. 

My hands would be resting on my shins, palms upward to catch the mist. 

They are the hands of someone who has handled ten thousand broken edges: the skin across the pads is yellowed, cross-hatched with pale, healed split-marks from shale slivers, and the nails are ground flat, crescent-less, hard as horn. The tallow we press from the autumn ducks is worked so deep into the grain of my wrists that the water weeping from the lintel overhead never touches me; it strikes my forearm, balls into clear, heavy beads, and rolls off the edge of my wrist to drop into the drainage notch between my heels.

I would be one of the peg-tenders. 

It is quiet work, and slow, suited to someone whose knees have begun to thicken with the cold fluid that settles in our joints as we age. When the daylight bar slides down the eastern face and strikes the wet shingles with that sharp, shadowless silver, I am already crouched before a hairline fracture in the cleft’s shoulder. 

The fracture is narrow—barely the thickness of two fingernails—and it smells of fresh, sour mud, which means the bedding plane three fathoms behind the wall has shifted its weight since the moon stood high. If left alone, the frost and the water will blow the whole face off into the flume by midwinter, taking two hearth-shelves with it. 

Beside me is a birch-bark basket of dried alder pegs, cut short and baked hard in the draft-chimneys until the wood is thirsty as bone. My fingers know them by weight. I select one, set its tapered nose into the seam, and tap it home using a river-cobble wrapped in vole-skin to keep the strike from ringing. 

*Thnk. Thnk.* 

Not a crack. A dull thud that travels into the stone rather than through the air.

Then comes the waiting. With a slate ladle, I draw water from the cold runnel that rushes between my feet—tasting it first on the back of my tongue to check for lime grit—and pour it drop by drop over the head of the peg. 

In the silence between the falling drops, I lean my ear against the wet rock face. 

This is who I am: the one who listens to the wood drink. 

Through the cheekbone, the mountain is never still. It does not speak in words, but in continuous, microscopic complaints: the *hiss* of sheet-water working through hidden chimneys, the distant, muffled *tump* of a boulder settling six miles up on the tundra rim, and the slow, agonizing yawn of the peg as its dry fibers swell, gorge on the runnel-water, and push outward against the fault. 

When the rock gives, it doesn't shatter. There is only a deep, subterranean *clack*, felt first in the teeth and the balls of the feet, followed by a thin sigh as the crack opens half an inch wider, releasing a small gush of cloudy gray silt.

A younger man, short-backed and wide-ribbed, appears silently beside me. He does not greet me; his knuckles simply brush the wet slate near my left knee—a dry rustle that asks: *Does it part clean?*

I hum once, low in the diaphragm, a vibration that doesn't rise above the hiss of the flume. *Clean.* 

Together, using bone levers smoothed by decades of fat and river-sand, we ease the loosened slab away from the living wall. It comes down into our greased forearms without a scrape, heavy and dripping with ancient, cold clay. It is five feet long, four fingers thick, flat as frozen water—a lintel stone that will outlast our grandchildren’s grandchildren in the dry under-tiers where the small lamps burn.

When the daylight bar begins to narrow overhead, turning from silver to that pale, slate-blue that signals the retreat of the sun from the upper jaws of the rift, I do not go up. 

I crawl through the vole-skin drape into the low, moss-chinked dark of the shared room. The air inside smells of duck tallow, wet earth, and the sweet, rotten-leaf warmth of eight bodies nesting together in dry sedge. 

I take my place near the rear wall, where the weeping rock forms the room’s inner boundary. I rub fresh fat into the hard skin of my soles, working out the stiffness where the stone has bitten during the long crouch. Beside me, a child with pale eyelashes reaches out in the blue light of the liverwort wick, curls three thick, spatulate fingers around my wrist, and falls asleep to the slow, wet rhythm of the drop-line falling into the stone trough outside the door.

I know nothing of a world that stretches wide to a horizon. If you showed me an open prairie, my heart would stop from the terror of falling upward into the blue. Here, the earth has walls; it holds us between its ribs like char tucked into the crevices of a fast stream. The rock is cold, but it is certain. The water is heavy, but it always knows where down is. 

I close my eyes, feel the stone behind my skull vibrating with the steady, quiet work of the flumes, and let the darkness close over like wet slate sliding into place.
