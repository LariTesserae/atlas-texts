At first, there is no boundary between having a body and being caught in a vise. 

There is only the shear.

It is not an empty space; an empty space here would be crushed flat in twenty seasons. It is a tolerance—perhaps three-sixteenths of an inch between the machined lip of an outer sleeve and the pitted neck of the main expansion conduit, forty paces from the red-glowing heat-pipe, suspended ten feet above the drowned flywheels. 

To be here is to be flattened. Not dead—dead things are brittle, and brittle things turn to dry graphite powder under this kind of torque, falling out of the seams to float upon the verdigris pools below. To stay here, you must be wet, but not with water; water boils off or turns to lime-crust. You must be wet with tallow, with cylinder oil that has broken down into heavy, chain-linked hydrocarbons, with the dark, yellow fat pressed out of dead scale-weavers by falling rock.

I am not the Accumulator. I have no room for convolutions, no cavernous cylinder in which to coil a cerebral mass. I am thin—scarcely thicker than the sole of an old boot, but twenty feet long where I wrap, collar-like, around the circumference of the joint. 

I do not think with a center. I think in arcs of hoop-stress.

Around me, the collar of cast iron holds sixty tons of cold-bent tension, trying perpetually to close that three-sixteenths gap. Inside me, the inner conduit vibrates with the 40-hertz hum of the thermal tap, a ceaseless, scouring tremor that acts upon my underside like an emery stone. If I were flesh as the sky knows it, I would be ground into meal in an afternoon. But the flesh that has grown into this seam is an emulsion: flax fibers that were tamped into the packing gland three hundred years ago by a man with a wooden mallet, infused season after season with the liver-colored fungal gristle that travels through the grease-lines.

I am the packing. But the packing has learned to digest the galling of the metal.

My belly—the side turned inward toward the vibrating neck—is coated in a slick, regenerative paste of black lead and sulfur-tolerant amoeboid matter. As the conduit trembles, it rasps away microscopic shavings of cast iron. I drink them. My cells do not divide outward into the open air; the air is an enemy that would dry them to asphalt. They divide horizontally, creeping around the bolt-shafts, weaving a dense, fibrous felt that matches the microscopic tool-marks left by long-dead lathes.

The acoustic roar of the vault is not sound to me. It is the blood-pump. The deep *tock-tock* of the condensate drops falling from the roof registers only as a faint, localized tickle along my upper flange. But the muffled, zinc-tub hammering miles below—the blind valve knocking in the thermal tap—sets up a standing wave within the collar. It compresses me, relaxes me, compresses me, relaxes me, twelve times every minute. With each compression, the dense mineral grease in my tissues is driven through capillary sieves toward my edges; with each relaxation, it draws back in, rich with dissolved copper-salts and paraffin scum weeping from the cracked flange above.

I smell—if the chemical registration of my skin can be called a smell—the approaching fatigue of the iron. 

Cast iron does not warn the eye before it shifts, but it warns the skin. Two days before a joint yields, the surface grain changes. The crystalline lattice of the inner pipe begins to hum at a higher pitch—not forty hertz, but forty-four, forty-eight—a dry, shrill micro-vibration that makes my tallow froth. 

When that happens, I stiffen.

I cannot leave. There is nowhere to go that is not either too cold or too loose; outside the seam, a body has no purchase, no hydraulic resistance to push against. To exist is to be wedged. So I sweat out my reserves. From my lateral pores, buried deep within the bolt-threads where the nuts have rusted into solid nodules, I exude a heavy, dark lard—calcium soap bound with sulfur-wax. I flood the interface. I become the lubricant that prevents the two faces of the machine from tearing each other to pieces, even as they prepare to crush me.

Then the vault catches its breath. 

There is always an instant—a fraction of a second before the *CLANG*—when the acoustic mass of the chamber seems to fold back down the flues. The 40-hertz roar drops an octave. The caliper-crawlers on the girder above freeze their hydrostats, locking their horn wedges into the bolt-holes. The Accumulator in its cylinder eight yards away stiffens its greasy liver-folds, holding its breath.

The static friction breaks.

It is a violence that has no sky-equivalent. The inner pipe leaps three millimeters forward along its seat. 

For the length of that three-millimeter stroke, the heat inside my body spikes past the boiling point of lead. The sheer force smears me across the lathe-lines, dragging half my substance into a thin, translucent film no thicker than an onion skin, while the rest is heaped like cold butter against the retaining lip. The sound is not heard; it is a mechanical hammer that strikes every cell at once, rupturing the larger oil-vesicles, flattening my fibrous nerves until they register nothing but the absolute, blinding presence of work done.

And then it stops. The iron bites into its new seat. The rifle-shot crack echoes away through the wet rock, bouncing off the rubber fronds, dying into the soft, waxy carpets of moss.

In the silence that follows—that momentary slackness before the deep earth begins to wind the spring again—I begin the work of recovery.

My torn margins, bruised to a dull, tallowy purple by the friction, begin to flow back together. The heat of the slip, lingering in the sheared metal, is food; it keeps the heavy paraffins liquid enough to crawl. I send pale, thread-like tendrils out through the weep-holes of the gland, questing into the warm, falling condensate, seeking the run-off from the dead dynamos to replace the iron filings I have burned.

A pipe-leech, sluggish and thick with sulfur-water, crawls blindly across the outer face of the collar, its flat belly dragging over my exposed margin. It does not know I am alive; to it, I am only a softer seam of iron-scale, a bead of machine-sweat that does not wash away. It grazes upon the grease I have extruded during the slip. I let it. Its slow, rasping mouth-lip stimulates the repair of my outer margin, drawing the fresh wax up from the bolt-threads.

Far down in the Western corner, the Accumulator lets out its slow, flatulent bubble of gas. The sulfur-cloud drifts over the flywheels, rising toward the bruised blue light of the fissure, and with it comes the familiar, suffocating warmth of the earth's gut, steadying the chamber back into its 40-hertz groove.

The conduit begins to cool a fraction, shrinking back against its stops. The hoop-stress tightens. 

I feel the load settling back into my grain. The lathe-marks bite into my underbelly; the retaining flange presses against my back. I am full. I am held. The tolerance has returned to three-sixteenths of an inch.

There is nothing above the ceiling. There is nothing below the tap. There is only the long, greasy work of staying between the surfaces, keeping the iron from touching itself, waiting for the next three millimeters of life.
