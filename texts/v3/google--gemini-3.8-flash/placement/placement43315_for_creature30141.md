I would not be standing on the herringbone. To stand is to have feet, and feet in this place are either the concussive, square blocks of the Tampers—destined to be glued into the lower courses by their own slumping weight—or the white porcelain needles of the Clinkers, skittering from kiln-fire to kiln-fire with their little bellies full of coals.

I would be wedged.

I would find myself forty feet up, pinched flat and breathless between the seventh and eighth courses of a curving, green-cobble wall that has begun to list four degrees to the west. 

I am what the masons call a *shim*, though nothing here has ever had a mason. 

At first, I might feel like a piece of drift-timber—a balk of heart-cedar, split along the grain, compressed by forty tons of settling mud until the sap has wept out of my pores and boiled into white froth against the mortar. But timber rots too fast in this steam, and rawhide slackens. No, if you were to scrape away the thick, foaming crust of lime that cakes my sides, you would find that I am made of *chamotte*—old clay, previously baked in some ancient, forgotten flue, smashed with a hammer into rough, angular grog, and re-kneaded with raw river-mud and sheep’s tallow into a dense, flat, spade-shaped slab.

I have no face. A face is a weakness in the mold; it invites thermal cracks. But I have two flat sides that know the difference between level and lean, and I have an edge. 

My edge is sharp, burnished by the ceaseless, wet friction of the masonry sliding over me. It is my only organ of sense: I feel the cure. 

I feel the water leaving the cobbles above me in slow, greasy pulses. I feel the lime taking its first chemical bite, heating to a fever as it drinks the moisture, binding the round river-stones into a monolithic brow that threatens to overbalance and crush the plaza below. Through my belly, I feel the Tampers three miles away—their rhythmic *thwack-shluck, thwack-shluck*—sending shuddering waves through the terracotta floor that make the joints between my stones weep fresh red mud.

I am not monumental. I am barely five feet long, broad as a butcher’s block, tapered like a wedge. I do not build the wall, and I have no rope to catch it like the frantic Tensioners swaying in the upper steam. 

My entire life is the resistance to shear.

When the wall groans—that long, dry, tearing sigh that precedes a collapse—the mortar above me separates with a sound like tearing felt. The weight shifts. It does not fall all at once; it leans into my high shoulder. I bite into the soft underside of the stone with my rough, chamotte tooth, holding the gap open by a fraction of an inch, allowing the hot draft of the floor to enter the seam. If the draft enters, the mortar can skin over. If it skins over, the course might stand for another hour, another week, long enough for the red glow beneath the brickwork to reach it and turn it into permanent terracotta.

I want the wall to stand. It is not an ambition; it is an ache in my grain. To be wedged tight, to feel the catastrophic downward shove of twenty green-brick courses resolved entirely into my dense, stubborn middle—that is the only stillness this ocean of clay permits. In that pressure, there is no steam, no scalding puddle, no Clinker clicking on the flags. There is only the true, dumb fact of bearing.

***

Yet the wall always shears.

The doubt catches it. It happens in the fifth hour of my wedge. A seam of fat, under-tempered slip, hidden deep within the heart of the third course, liquifies in the rising heat. The wall sighs. The stone above my head does not crush me—I am too dense to break, too full of old fire—but it spits me out.

I am ejected like a cherry stone from between two fingers.

I fall through the sagging, ochre mist, tumbling end over end with a heavy, flat *flump*, striking the shallow water of the plaza. 

Scalding slip flies thirty paces. The impact drives my wedge three inches deep into the herringbone paving, chipping a crisp, red corner off a newly-laid tile. 

I lie there in the milky shallows, cooling from red-hot to leather-hard, while the collapsed wall above me hits the floor with the sound of a galleon breaking on a reef. Fifty tons of boiling cobble and raw mortar splash over me, burying me in an instant.

This is where I spend my seasons: in the intermediate dark.

Beneath the wet, chaotic heap of rubble, the air is choked with the alkaline reek of slaking lime. It is so hot my tallow sizzles. A Slip-Maw, disturbed by the catastrophe, flows blind and lukewarm across my upper flank, its calcined grit-teeth scratching delicate, meaningless spirals across my dried skin as it licks the salt blooms that have already begun to flower on me.

I cannot walk. I have no joints. But as the rubble settles, as the boiling groundwater weeps through the gaps, I shift.

I move by the logic of drying. When clay dries, it shrinks by an eighth. The side of my body that faces the deep heat of the floor shrinks faster than the side bathed in the scalding water above. I warp. 

It is a slow, agonizing heave—a contraction that takes three days to curl my flat back by half an inch. But that half-inch dislodges a four-hundred-pound cobble. The cobble rolls into a puddle with a dull *glop*. I straighten as the water covers me again, sliding forward an inch through the gray slime.

Warp, slide, relax, cool. 

Over weeks, I work myself free of the ruin like an iron wedge working out of an axe-handle. 

I emerge into the perpetual copper twilight of the open plain. 

***

Out here, the world is too flat. The steam gathers in low, horizontal bands that smell of wet flowerpots and scorched earth. Miles away, a herd of Tampers is clustered around a new swelling in the floor, their blunt, thumb-dented crowns bobbing in slow unison as they tread the edges of a fresh pit.

I am small against this vastness, a discarded tool of fired chamotte, but I am dangerous to the floor.

Because I do not belong to the floor. I have been fired once; I cannot be easily dissolved back into the slurry. When the great, pale Thumb rises through the herringbone thirty leagues to the south, displaced water will race across the tiles in a scalding tide, carrying me with it, scraping my sharp edges against the fresh, tender joints of the paving. I leave long, straight scars in the wet clay—grooves that refuse to heal, grooves that catch the boiling groundwater and turn it into new gullies, unraveling the builder’s work from beneath.

Sometimes, a Clinker finds me.

I hear it before I see it: the *click-click-click-tack* of its porcelain points against the vitrified brick. It emerges from a dry steam-vent, its salt-glazed body gleaming like wet liver, the round peep-hole in its chest glaring with a terrifying, white-hot draft. 

It circles me. Its little stoneware pinchers reach out to test my corners. 

It wants to eat me. It thinks I am raw; it hates the softness of the tallow and the unbaked silt that clings to my underbelly. But when its pincers bite into my chamotte shoulder, they find only the hard, calcined grit of a thing that has already survived the kiln. My grog breaks its teeth with a dry, ceramic snap. 

The Clinker pauses, its internal fire whistling with an angry, baffled draft. It spits a handful of red-hot charcoal against my side, trying to cook the impudence out of me, but the heat only cures my remaining mortar, turning my skin white and hard as bone. With a petulant, rolling clatter, it turns and scuttles away across the herringbone, seeking softer, more obedient clay—some severed limb of a Tamper that can still be pinched into a bead.

***

Left alone, I wait for the floor to sag.

It always sags. The heat below never stops, but the water never leaves; it is an eternal, boiling stew that rots its own foundations. Two hundred yards ahead, the herringbone is already dipping, forming a wide, saucer-shaped depression where the red silt gathers three feet deep.

At the edge of that hollow, a new tower is beginning to erupt.

I can see the green courses rising out of the water like the wet shoulders of a drowning horse. The mortar is bubbling, throwing off thick, white burrs of steam as it fights the weight of the rough-cut flags above it. It has no scaffold yet. No Tensioner has swung down from the sagging clouds to throw a rawhide braid around its waist. It is rising naked, clumsy, and crooked, tilting three inches to the north with every course it adds.

The current of the draining shallows catches my flat underbelly. I begin to slide. 

I do not hurry; the mud carries me at the speed of a cooling glaze. My sharp front edge cuts through the potash blooms, breaking their brittle frost with a faint, continuous *shhh-crick*. 

I am aiming for the base of the third course, where the joint is already gaping like a fish’s mouth, waiting for something hard, something unyielding, something that has already known the fire and cannot be taught to doubt its own square edge.

The tower sways above the steam, groaning for its shim. 

I slide into the gap, feel the scalding weight drop down to greet me, and am home.
