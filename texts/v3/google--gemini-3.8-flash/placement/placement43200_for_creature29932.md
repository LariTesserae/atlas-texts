At first, there is no form to it—only the distribution of weight. 

To exist here is not to see or to plan, because sight is frustrated by the sulfurous amber fog and planning requires an edge between now and later that this trench does not possess. It begins simply as an index of pressures against a surface. 

There is the low, horizontal pressure of the water moving south across the basalt slab, never deeper than the first joint of a finger, but relentless in its drag. It smells of hot lard, spent cylinder wash, and the raw, stinging lime that keeps seeping from the aggregate as the green concrete hardens in the dark. There is the vertical strike of the condensation: heavy, oily drops peeling off the rivet heads thirty feet overhead, landing with blunt, flat impacts that hit the skin—or the shell, or the membrane—not like rain, but like small, warm weights dropped from a height.

And beneath everything, the iron shudder of the centrifuges three blocks off, vibrating through the bedrock and into the soles, the belly, the marrow, until the skeleton itself hums at sixty cycles a second.

For a long time, there is no need to be anything more than a shape pressed against the leeward side of an anchor bolt. 

In this basin, being small is an advantage, but being soft is an invitation to be dissolved by the alkalinity of the water. Yet being entirely armored—like the black river crabs whose carapaces tick-tick across the floor in straight, blind vectors—is too rigid. They cannot feel the thermal plumes shifting. They only know what they can pinch, chew, and scrape from the formwork seams until their jaws are gummy with calcium paste. 

No, it is higher up than that, out of the swift drag of the main gutter, where the heat gathers thickest under the copper conduit runs.

Or perhaps lower, wedged into the dead water behind a pump housing.

Let it settle. The body clarifies by what it refuses:

It does not have wings. The bats, suspended in their wet, shivering rows beneath the I-beams, are too close to exhaustion. Their existence is a frantic, sodden metabolism, dropping like sodden rags through the steam plumes with their jaws open, forever swallowing gnats and drain flies just to keep their blood at ninety-eight degrees in the boiling draft. Their hearts beat so fast they blur into the machinery’s hum. There is no stillness in them.

Nor does it belong in the sumps with the mudfish. To be stacked four-deep in the intake pit, snouts jammed into the iron grating, blind and scaleless and coated in caustic mucus, is to be trapped in an endless draught. When the bilge pumps kick on and the pool drops in a violent, gasping gulp, the fish thrash in a dense knot of muscle and white barbels, living only to resist being drawn through the mesh into the impellers. That is an existence entirely defined by current.

The place I find myself is the shelf. 

It is the four-inch ledge where the basalt wall was poured wider to seat the motor plinths, just above the high-water wash of the sheet-runoff, but constantly basted by the condensation that sheets down the vertical formwork lines. 

Here, the body is neither hard nor soft, but something that has compromised with the water. 

Skin like wet calf-leather, hairless and wrinkled from the unbroken ninety-five-degree heat, thick enough that the lye doesn't burn, but sensitive enough to register every shudder of the deck. Four limbs, but low-slung, splayed wide to grip the slick aggregate without sliding. The ears are useless—thickened into blunt, scarred nubs, sealed against the hydraulic scream of the high-pressure lines—so the head rests flat against the concrete to listen with the jaw. 

Through the jaw, the basin speaks in clear, clean physics:
The triple-thump of pump number four’s worn piston rod.
The high, singing whine of an overloaded circuit box thirty yards east.
The hollow, hollow rumble of an empty freight consist braking on the iron above, sending a dusting of dry soot through the street grilles to float like charcoal flour on the water.

In this place, I am one of the scavengers of the margin—not quite the rats, who crowd in frantic, twitching heaps on the motor housings, bickering over crab meat with sulfur-yellow incisors, but something slower, longer, heavier in the haunches. A creature that came down the storm-drains when the forms were still being built, when the timber falsework was still wet from the river, and simply forgot to climb back up.

My days—if the word had any meaning here—are spent wedged in the damp crevice behind the yellow shut-off valve. The iron wheel is warm against my flank, radiating eighty pounds of saturated steam that keeps the marrow loose and easy. 

When hunger comes, it is not a sudden panic; the basin is an open trough. I slip down into the sheet-wash with scarcely a ripple, my flattened tail acting as a counterweight against the current. The water is blood-warm, thick as broth with axle grease and swollen brewer’s grain that has washed through the railbed. 

I do not hunt the fish; they are too deep in the sumps, too fast in their short-stroke thrashing. I wait for the crabs to drift past the plinth. They march with their carapaces square to the flow, scraping at the lime, blind to anything that does not cross their eye-stalks. It is easy work: a heavy, rubbery paw pinned to the top shell to hold it against the concrete, a short, crushing bite at the junction where the leg enters the thorax, and the shell cracks like green wood. The meat inside is hot, salted with mineral lime, tasting faintly of iron filings and diesel. 

Sometimes, the yellow glow of an electric lantern will swing through the fog from the upper catwalks. 

When the men come down in their stiff canvas suits, their faces hidden behind round mica viewports, their clumsy boots making heavy, sucking noises in the inch-deep water, I do not run. Why run? They cannot move fast in their rubber armour. They are aliens here, half-choked by their own breath inside their canvas hoods, their ears plugged with wax, their gloved hands clumsy around their rakes and grease guns. They clear the sumps, they slap a cold smear of tallow onto a bearing, and they climb back into the light before the heat rots the seals on their respirators. 

They leave behind the blue-green crust of their discarded fittings, or the sweet, waxy stump of a yellow grease-pencil dropped from a pocket. I have chewed on those pencil ends; the paraffin tastes of petroleum and lard, a rich, dense grease that sits heavy in the belly for hours.

When they are gone, the iron hatch clangs shut high above, and the basin returns to its equilibrium. 

The light never changes. The water never cools. 

I climb back onto the warm flange of the pump mount, draw my limbs close to my ribs, and tuck my snout beneath my forearms to keep the marble-sized drops from falling directly into my nostrils. The concrete vibrates through my ribs, my belly, my teeth. In the dark behind my closed lids, there are no fields, no river banks, no seasons turning cold and white. There is only the long, flat hiss of the steam loops, the steady sweep of the water toward the grates, and the warmth that never, ever drains away.
