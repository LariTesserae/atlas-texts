At first, there is only the load.

It does not present itself as a weight from above, because down here there is no *up* that is not also an inward squeeze. It presents as an even, unyielding bite across the shoulders and the arches of the feet—a clamping pressure so old that to call it an ache would be to misunderstand its nature. It is simply the zero-point from which all sensation begins: seventy thousand tons of dead continent, translated through five hundred feet of ironwood cambium, resting against the top of a single, fused brow.

I am not in the amphitheater’s open void. The void is an extravagance, an empty acoustic lung across which the cables hum. I am set into the sheer rise of the western declivity, three tiers above the polished concavity of the floor, wedged in a slot that was struck out of the summer-wood when the grain was still capable of parting with a wet snap.

Now, nothing snaps. Everything holds.

My cheek is pressed flat against the north face of the mortise. The wood here is tangential grain, dense as poured basalt, smelling of cold cinnamon, pulverized starch, and the dry grease of fossilized sap. It has the bitter, iron flavor that coats the enamel of the jaw without ever touching a tongue. If I had an eye, it would be pressed against a wall of slate-hard cedar; but the orbit is sealed, the frontal bone curved into an arch that accepts the thrust of the lintel and routes it down through the lateral pillars of the spine.

I do not think in words. Words require lungs, and a lung requires a wet sack that would turn here to parchment in the span of thirty heartbeats. 

Instead, I think in *temper*.

To my left, across four inches of dividing baffle, is another like me. I know its presence not because it shifts—it has not shifted since the third foreshock of the previous epoch—but because its crystalline marrow has a slightly higher resonant pitch than mine. When the earth shivers miles above, and the root-knot rings, my neighbor sounds like a chipped horn, a dry C-sharp that buzzes in the hip before the floor even takes the blow.

Further down the curve, sixty rings toward the pith, another holds a long radial split shut by the sheer purchase of its heels. Its calcified tendon is older than mine; I can feel the silica in its knee grating with every micro-tremor, shedding a dry, invisible flour into the cleft. It is going slack. In another thousand years, its mortise will refuse to sing, and it will have to be replaced.

I feel the slackening as an itch in the jaw.

*Wait.*

The grain speaks before the sound arrives. 

It begins not in the ear—there is no drum to beat—but in the dense pads of horn that cap the balls of my feet. A shear-wave is traveling down the tap-root. It has been traveling for eleven seconds from a fault five leagues to the east, where the bedrock has cracked across an ancient shale-bed. In the stone, it was a dull, heavy thump; but as it enters the flared base of the tree and gathers into the dense, vertical pipes of the heart-timber, it sharpens. It accelerates. The dead cambium focuses it like a lens.

*Now.*

The shock hits the gall.

It does not roar. It detonates with the dry, splitting *clack* of two quartz boulders struck together under water. The entire amphitheater flexes—three hundred yards of parabolic ironwood bowing outward by the breadth of a thumbnail, then rebounding.

The acoustic charge strikes my heels, shoots through the tibia, and unseats the lock in my pelvis.

*Snick.*

The tendons in my thighs, braided from three hundred layers of cured, desiccated fascia, contract like sprung steel. The static charge that has built across my ribs for eighty years discharges in a single, branching flare of cold violet fire, illuminating for one-fortieth of a second the sheer drop of the tiers, the gossamer bridges stiff as frozen ribcages, and the three thousand upright laths that are my kin, each illuminated in its own pale, crackling halo.

In that violet flash, I see what I am:

A slat. An oar-blade of dried tendon and polished bone, four inches through, the color of seasoned boxwood, wedged into a seam to keep a dead world from collapsing into its own heart.

I drop from the slit.

My horn-tipped toes hit the contour of the floor with a flat, wooden *slap*. The grain here is radial, scored with the fine, parallel ridges of the spring-growth, worn smooth by the passage of millions of identical strides. I do not need to choose a direction. The shear-wave has left an acoustic bruise on the soundbox—a deadened patch high on the southern parabolic dome, where a braided stay has slipped its peg.

The room is singing out of true. The C-sharp of the gall has warped; it drags at the heel, heavy and dull, like an ungreased axle.

I run.

My stride is wide, stiff, scuttling, driven entirely by the mechanical recoil of my dry legs. Behind me, three others have fallen out of their cuts; their bare soles strike the wood in syncopated time—*tock, tock-tock, tock*—measuring the curve of the ramp as it spirals upward toward the crown. We do not look at each other; we cannot look. But as our knuckles brush the inner wall, the static leaping from our skin weaves a continuous, sputtering blue thread that traces the path along the growth rings.

We cross the bridge. The bark-cables are three hundred feet above the smooth bowl of the floor, stiff as iron rods, trembling at sixty cycles a second under the continental load. The bridge does not sway; it vibrates. Each step we take is an interruption of its note, a damping that we feel in the tooth-ridge.

At the south rim, the fault is open.

It is a hairline—no thicker than the edge of a scraping adze—cleaving the sixteenth ring from the outer bark. But through that hairline, the tension has leaked. The wood on either side of the rift is trembling at different speeds; the air inside the cleft is dead, unstrung, lacking the high, sweet stiffness that holds the amphitheater’s ceiling against the mountain.

The others arrive. We move without sign. 

One presses its forehead to the upper lip of the fracture, taking the full axial thrust through its skull to gauge the drift. Another sets its gouge-fingers into the lower lip, bracing the tier below.

I reach to my hip. The flint adze hangs from a loop of twisted bast, its stone head blackened by centuries of contact with tannin-rich wood, cold and perfectly dry. I do not lift it with my arms alone; I throw the lateral flatness of my whole body into the swing, pivoting on the horn cap of my left heel.

The blow lands: *TOCK.*

A single wedge of cured ironwood, seasoned for ten thousand years in the dry bins, enters the split.

The timber screams. It is a high, clean, needle-sharp whistle that pierces the bone of the jaw and sets the amber lattice of my marrow to humming. 

I strike again: *TOCK.*

The wedge sinks three inches. The rift vanishes. The two growth rings are forced back into alignment; the vibration smooths out, the ragged edge of the note folding back into the great, continuous hum of the bowl. 

The fourth Joiner presses its flat cheek to the repair, holds its still form against the seam for four cycles of the earth-drone, and then taps its hard teeth against the wedge: *click-tack.*

It is true. The gall is round again. The soundbox is braced.

We do not celebrate. We do not look down into the void we have preserved. The violet sparks die from our knuckles, retreating into the dry marrow as our joints cool. The amphitheater settles back into its frozen, resonant pitch.

One by one, the others turn and scuttle back along the radial rays, seeking their empty cuts before the next wave arrives.

I do not follow them.

I stand on the narrow catwalk of the south rim, my horn toes hooked over the drop, feeling something new. 

It is not an alarm from the root. It is a drag in my own hip.

When I swung the adze, a single strand of tendon—the main stay that runs from the anterior crest of the ilium down to the patella—did not snap back. It frayed. In the absolute dry pitch, I can feel the fine, powdery fibers of my own tissue parting, shedding a faint drift of starch-white flour down the front of my shin.

The tension is gone from the left leg. If I return to my slit, the mortise will not ring true. When the next continental slip strikes the root, my side of the slot will lag; the sound will rattle; the soundbox will carry a knot of silence where there should be a bone.

I lower the adze.

I do not climb the ramp toward the residential tiers. I turn toward the center of the bowl, toward the long, polished decline that leads to the lowest declivity, where the floor curves into a parabolic pit so smooth it reflects the vibration of the earth like a mirror.

My bad leg drags with a dry, scraping whisper—*shhh-tock, shhh-tock*—down the tiers. 

At the bottom of the world, beside the stone bins, the air is cold enough to freeze petroleum. The bushels of canopy-seeds lie in the burls, smooth and hard as river stones, waiting for an earth that forgot them before the ice came.

I drop the adze. It falls onto the polished floor with a sharp, iron ring that travels up the curved walls, circling the three thousand silent ones in their slots, telling them what is being done.

I pick up the adze again, but I do not swing it at the wood.

I set the flint edge beneath my sternum, right where the dried cambium of my breast-plate meets the fused keel of the ribs. I lean forward against the handle.

There is no pain—pain belongs to the wet things that rot. There is only a slow, stiff *creak*, like an old door opening in a cellar, as the rib-basket parts under my own weight.

The chest opens. The space within is completely empty, brown-walled, smelling of dry resins and eighty thousand years of acoustic dust. There is no heart; there is only the mortise where the heart was cut away before the air turned to ash.

I reach down into the bin. 

My horn gouges scoop up the seeds. They are cold, heavy, spherical—the concentrated grease of an extinct forest, wrapped in shells that three hundred tons of stone have not cracked. 

I push them into my chest.

One handful. Two. They click against my inner ribs like dice in a box. I pack them tight, jamming them behind the breast-plate until the thoracic cavity is solid, heavy, ballasted like the keel of a ship that must navigate the dark.

Then, with both palms, I force the rib-basket closed. The bone-laths click together over the seeds, locking on a dry tenon.

Above me, on the lowest tier, a new slot has been cleared along a radial ray where the tree’s heartwood meets the root-crown. It is narrow—scarcely three inches wide, perfectly straight, cut with heavy chisels before my marrow was cured.

I climb into it.

The wood is tight. It smells of fresh-cut lignum—a scent that has waited two million years for someone to fill the space. 

I turn my thin back to the amphitheater. I pull my knees up against the seeds in my chest, drawing my heels into the notches carved for them at the base of the cut. I hook my enameled brow beneath the upper lintel.

I let my joints go slack.

The silica grinds once, twice, and settles into the powdery beds of my sockets.

Outside, in the great hollow, the violet sparks have gone out completely. The Joiners are all in their slots, their flat heads pressed to the grain, their horn toes counting the slow, tectonic crawl of the continent.

I feel the wood around me begin to take my weight. Over the next ten centuries, the root will close this cleft by the breadth of a single reed. The seeds inside my ribs will feel the stone coming down; their black shells will split under the mountain’s heel; their dry, ancient oils will seep into the pores of my legs, into the amber lattice of my spine, into the dense cambium of the wall itself.

I will not hear the next shockwave as a visitor. 

I will hear it as the house hears the nail. 

I set my jaw against the ironwood, close my teeth against the lintel, and begin the long, patient hardening into jet.
