I am not loose in the current. To be loose in the current is to be thrown, and to be thrown is to be spent against the basalt before any kind of grain can set.

I am wedged. 

At first, there is only the sensation of being flattened between two surfaces that have no interest in each other: the base-flange of a four-story vertical condenser and the sheared granite-basalt lip it was bolted to before the valley fell. Three of the four holding studs snapped during the breach, their sheared ends bright as nickel under the brine. The fourth held, but the casing canted three degrees, leaving a crescent-shaped clearance—a gap six inches wide at its belly, narrowing to nothing where the cold iron bites the rock.

That gap is the whole world. It is where the brine has to squeeze through to satisfy the pump's displacement, and because the water is crowded with colloidal iron and colloidal fat, the gap does not stay clean. 

It silts. But it does not silt with earth.

It catches the rags. The kelp-analogues, beaten to ribbons against the intake grills forty feet above, drift through as grease and fibrous tow. The current jams them into the crescent. Then the piston above completes its downstroke: a hundred and twenty tons of forged nickel-steel dropping through twenty feet of travel, arresting against its hydraulic cushion with a sound that does not travel through the water so much as it changes the density of the water for a hundred yards in every direction.

The gap closes by three millimeters. It opens by three millimeters. 

Thirty-eight times a minute.

***

In the beginning, I am only the stuff that fails to escape the stroke.

A stratum of torn kelp-rib, slick with vegetable tallow. Over it, a smear of graphite grease washed out of an open stern-tube. Over that, a drift of fine copper swarf—shavings curled off the armature of a turbine that has been boring its own bearing-bush out of round for three hundred days. 

The downstroke catches us. It drives the iron water through the fibers until the water itself is forced out, leaving the solids dry for a fraction of a millisecond under sixty thousand pounds of pressure—dry in the center of a drowned world, fused by pure friction. When the piston lifts, the tea-colored brine rushes back in, bringing fresh latex, fresh tannin, more silica needles.

The silica needles are what give me a spine, though it is not a spine that bends like a dog’s or a fish’s. It is an internal felt. Millions of them, three microns long, hexagonal, needle-sharp, deposited in chaotic felted sheets like asbestos batting. Every time the heavy iron housing rocks down, it drives the points into the copper shavings. 

The copper deforms; the silica holds. 

After five thousand strokes, the layer has ceased to be silt. It has become a laminated heel, four inches thick, dense as tire-tread, taking the full blow of the three-degree tilt.

I can feel the engine’s work now, not as noise, but as a stiffening. When the machinery works hard—when the steam pressure spikes and the housing groans against the single remaining bolt—the silica needles are forced into intimate, grinding contact with the copper leaf. It produces a continuous, greasy trickle of micro-current: a slow, cold galvanic bleed that travels through my interior like a deep ache. 

The bleed makes the latex curdle and knit. It prevents the tea-water from dissolving me back into soup. I am being forged from the edges inward by a machine that does not know it is building its own replacement shim.

***

By the tenth thousand stroke, the margin has begun to crawl.

It does not crawl toward the light. The light—the sour orange flare of the sodium housings down the rift, the violent violet arcing of the severed cables—is merely an intermittent heat that puckers my outer skin. 

I crawl toward the leak.

From the high-pressure side of the condenser, where the bronze piping has cracked along its brazed seam, a thread of water escapes that does not behave like the rest of the flood. It has been superheated to four hundred degrees under eighty atmospheres; as it hits the cold hydrostatic wall of the abyss, it fails to boil into gas. It forms a greasy, transparent rope—water so compressed it has the viscosity of heavy syrup, clear as cut crystal, sliding sideways across the basalt without mixing.

It smells of zinc and solder. It is violently acidic. 

My front edge—a ragged, spade-shaped flap of vulcanized kelp-rubber, three inches thick and ribbed like the sole of a boot—reaches for that rope. 

The reach is not voluntary. It is simple hygroscopic swelling: the side of my mass that touches the clear rope drinks the hot, mineral-heavy fluid twice as fast as the side touching the cold brine. The cells on that flank engorge, ballooning with dissolved silica, and their expansion forces the leading edge forward across the stone, millimeter by millimeter, like a wedge being driven into a log by a sledge.

When the edge finally crosses the seam of the crack, the syrupy leak strikes the laminated core. 

The sensation is a blinding, structural cramp. 

The acid in the jet attacks the copper leaves, eating them into green sulfate salts, but the silica needles drink the heat and undergo a phase-change: they fuse. They melt at their tips, knitting into a continuous, porous skeleton of vitreous glass that locks the whole front third of my mass into a shape as rigid and unyielding as a cast-iron shoe.

I have stopped being a rag. I have become a casting.

***

Now I have a mouth, though it does not open.

It is an intake gallery: three parallel channels worn through my underbelly where the basalt has been scoured smooth by forty years of sand-wash. The syrupy, superheated leak enters these grooves, travels the length of my body, and exits at the heel, where the 38-hertz hammering of the piston beats it into a white, greasy froth that smells of parboiled leached tallow.

In the middle of the body, where the heat meets the cold iron water of the rift, something else is gathering.

It is the void.

The pump above me has a leaky suction valve. Every twelfth stroke, it draws more water than the cracked flues can supply, and for the tenth of a second, a bubble of true zero forms in the clearance space—a hole in the water, an absence round as a grape, where there is neither brine nor steam nor iron, only the naked geometry of the rift trying to cave in on itself.

Most of these cavitation spheres are swept into the discharge and crushed against the cooling manifolds with that rhythmic, artillery-crack that shakes the kelp-forest. 

One of them did not get swept away. 

It was sucked down into my middle channel, drawn by the siphon of the hot leak. It lodged there, caught between two ribs of fused glass.

The pressure from the abyss—hundreds of fathoms of tea-colored lead—presses on the outside of my rind, trying to crush the grape. But the glass skeleton, tempered by the acid jet and backed by the forty-ton downward heave of the condenser flange, holds the perimeter. 

The void remains. It sits in my center like an eye that cannot see anything because there is nothing inside it to look at.

It is three inches across: a perfect, trembling sphere of pure vacuum, cool as well-water, silent as an unmade tool.

The presence of the void changes the way the sound travels through me. The 40-hertz thrum of the drowned factory no longer passes straight into the rock; it strikes the glass vault around the bubble and rebounds, echoing back through my laminated rubber rind, turning the concussive blows of the reciprocating machinery into an internal, circular singing.

I am no longer just taking the blow. I am ringing with it.

***

Where would you find me?

Look for the fourth bolt—the one that didn't break. 

Look beneath the tilted, rust-caked foot of the No. 3 Condenser, where the water is black-green and thick as tallow, six feet above the drop where the basalt shears down into the bottomless trench. 

You would not see a creature. You would see what looks like an old spill of marine vulcanite, six feet long, three feet wide, black as coal-tar, its margins ragged and gray with the white whiskers of extruded silica needles that have worked their way out through the skin like splinters through an old palm.

Its surface is not soft. If you struck it with a heavy spanner, the tool would bounce with a dull, leaden *thump*, leaving no dent in the cold-worked tallow of the rind.

Only along the back margin—the edge that faces the razor-cut of the rift—would you see that the thing is not dead iron. 

There, where the cold, upward-falling sheets of prismatic water come boiling out of the deep flues, the rind is thin. It pulses. It pulses exactly thirty-eight times a minute, in lock-step with the four-story piston that looms above it in the dark. 

And inside, through the dark tea-colored flesh, if the violet arc from the severed cables should happen to flash across the valley and throw the condenser into brief, white silhouette, you might see the jewel:

The sphere of empty air that never knew a lung. 

It sits in its glass cage, perfectly still while the miles of water churn and thunder around it, kept open by the sheer, unreasoning weight of the machine it supports, and by the silica bones that have learned that the only way to avoid being crushed by the world is to grow around a piece of nothing and hold it open with your teeth.
