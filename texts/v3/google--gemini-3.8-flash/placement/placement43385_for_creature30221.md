To begin with, the instinct is to seek the high, dry timber—to imagine crouching in the rotted control hut with the water-shrews, watching the levers shift their brass shoulders in the gloom. It is the easiest vantage for a mind brought from outside: high enough to keep an eye on the sky, close enough to the outside world to remember rain that didn’t taste of limestone and mutton fat. You want the fast heart. You want the claws that hook into cast-iron studs, the nervous intelligence that counts the seconds between the tilt of a float and the drop of a beam. It feels like survival.

But the shrews are desperate. Their existence is a prolonged, greasy panic, a sprint on the rim of a turbine where a single slipped pad means being converted into pink marrow before the next breath. Their lives are too thin, too borrowed; they have simply brought terrestrial terror into an enclosure that has no use for it. 

So you step down, deeper into the mist.

Perhaps down among the waxy catfish in the dead water behind the flywheel shields. It is tempting to be that heavy, that blind—to press a pale, soft stomach against a foundation timber and let the whole basin shudder through your ribs like a cradle. To feel the trip-hammer fall, not as an alarm, but as an announcement that dinner has been loosened from the rock. There is a terrible, enviable peace in having horn instead of teeth, in passing pulverized slate through a gut lined with gray grease, producing neat, heavy pellets that fall into the sludge without a ripple. 

And yet, even the fish are waiting for an accident. They are scavengers of a machine that does not care if they starve. When a channel scours out a new bed and the quiet eddy fills with high-pressure discharge, they are rolled into the pans like tallow candles, bruised and helpless against the copper mesh. They are passengers.

If you keep descending—past the rocker-arms where the ascidians snap shut with that wet, leathery pop; past the spray zone where the salamanders hang from their ribs like damp wax candles—you begin to realize that the basin’s true nature does not belong to things that dodge the blows, nor to the things that wait for the scraps.

The true nature of this place is the *clearance*. 

It is the four millimeters of tallow-slicked gap between the loose bronze sleeve and the iron pin. It is the film of gray emulsion that prevents the rocker-beam from screaming as it tears through its arc. 

That is where I find myself. 

Not one of the ribbon-leeches, exactly, though I would share their flat, unhurried posture. But I would be the thing that has gone entirely into the grease—an entity whose body is neither tissue nor petroleum, but the emulsion itself, given a slow, dispersed, and intensely localized attention. 

Or perhaps something slightly firmer: an organism made of that very "compressibility of raw dough," wedged directly into the egg-shaped hollow of a primary thrust-bearing, where the heat never drops below eighty degrees. 

I would not have eyes; daylight here is only a flat, gray glaze on the surface of the spray, completely irrelevant to the work of the pit. My sensation would be entirely tactile, hydraulic, and thermal. 

To live inside the sleeve of a four-ton rocker-arm is to inhabit an intimacy so violent that it becomes static. Every four seconds, the shaft rolls toward me with the weight of an entire parish behind it. The pressure does not crush; it merely displaces. My substance—dense as chilled lard, flexible as cured gut—is squeezed out along the microscopic spiral grooves cut into the bronze by three hundred years of turning. As the iron bears down, the temperature spikes: a brief, delicious pulse of frictional warmth that liquefies the sulfurated fats I feed upon. I drink the heat. I drink the microscopic filings of bronze and pig-iron that shed from the faces like fine, bitter silt.

Then the eccentric wheel passes its apex. The beam eases back. The egg-shaped gap opens by the thickness of a thumbnail, drawing a fresh wave of cold, milky lime-water into the housing.

The cold is as good as the fire. When the wash rushes in, I expand back into the clearance, thick and spongy, drawing in the suspended tallow that has drifted down from the upper hoppers. I catch the discarded skins of the leeches, the torn frills of water-mold, the fine flour of ground ashlar. My body acts as a gasket. I keep the iron from touching the bronze; I am the reason the machine does not screech; I am the velvet between its teeth.

Out in the open churn, things are broken, sorted, ground into pebbles, and purged through the eastern grates. The shrews exhaust themselves; the catfish grow huge and sluggish; the black, haycock-sized filter-mass at the sluice strains until it groans, shuddering under the weight of the void it guards. They are all caught in the horizontal rush—from the rock-weep down to the underground dark. 

But in the bearing, there is no forward motion. There is only the cycle: the slow, heavy wobble; the squeeze; the rush of cold water; the brief, scalding kiss of the load. 

I would have forgotten the sky entirely, not with sadness, but because an open ceiling is a flaw in an engine. I would know the world only as an infinite architecture of wet tolerances, an endless balance of pressures, where to be struck is simply to be warmed, and to be squeezed is simply to be held.
