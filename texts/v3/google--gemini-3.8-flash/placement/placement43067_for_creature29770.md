At first, there is only the tension between two kinds of cold. 

One is the cold of the slate, which is ancient, inert, and bottomless. The other is the cold of the copper, which is nervous, thin, and reactive, twitching with every passing shadow like a drawn wire.

I would not be on the open face of the rock. Nothing with a wet eye could survive the scouring glare that turns the sky to black currant. Nor would I be in the wide clefts where the flat-ribbed ones slide, smelling of rancid grease and musk, scouring the mica with their split lips. Their scale is too wide; their ribs are built for the heavy, dragging work of levering through stone.

I would find myself on the dark underbelly of the third gutter, down where the red metal has turned powdery and teal with carbonate, tucked into the hairline gap between the beaten bronze and the quartz basin it feeds.

A body scarcely thicker than the crust of verdigris itself. 

Eight jointed limbs, but not sprawling—tucked tight to the sternum like folded calipers, held together by surface tension and a smear of dense, non-congealing lipid that weeps from the joints. I am something that came up on the chimney years ago, or whose ancestors drifted up as gossamer-borne specks before the needles were ever driven into the hornblende: an aeolian hunter, smaller than the nail of her smallest finger, reduced entirely to hydraulics, eye-clusters, and patience.

My blood does not carry iron; it carries copper and salts that taste of brine and stone-flour, resisting the needle-ice that shatters softer cells. When the shade falls, I do not die; I simply stop moving. The hydraulic pressure in my legs drops to zero. I become an inclusion in the rock, a tiny, hard knot of carbon and dried chitin clinging with microscopic tarsal claws to the underside of her handiwork.

My world is entirely tactile, conducted through the sheet of metal.

Through the copper, I hear her long before she reaches the third basin. She does not step; she leans her bulk into the stone, and the slate lamellae pass the tremor along their cleavage lines like struck tuning forks. Then comes the dull, deadened *thud-clink* of the stone mallet. 

To the open air, the sound has no echo. But through the metal channel, it is an oceanic boom—a bell ringing directly against my shell. The shockwave ripples through the bronze, loosening the microscopic rime-crystals that have formed on the under-edge, dropping them onto my back like grains of salt. 

She is tapping the lip down. She has noticed that the thermal warping of midday lifted the lip a fraction of a millimeter, allowing a bead of melt to weep down the underside rather than dropping into the quartz bowl.

Her finger brushes the rim. 

To me, that finger is a wall of split horn and dried beef, vast, warm, and smelling suffocatingly of sheep fat and stale barley smoke. It blocks the thin wind for three seconds. A wave of humid, mammal-scented air washes into the crevice—air from the valleys, air that holds a ghostly trace of mud and fern-spore, carried up in her lungs and held there until it rots. 

I do not move. If I move, the claw-tips lose their bite on the carbonate crust, and the wind, which is always waiting at the lip of the stone, will shear me off and carry me out over the drop, where there is nothing to cling to for three vertical miles.

When she withdraws her hand, the sun strikes the upper surface of the copper.

The transformation is instantaneous, violent, and silent. The metal groans as its crystals expand. A sudden pulse of conductivity shoots through the shingle, warming my belly while my back is still held fast in thirty-degree frost. 

Then comes the water.

It does not flow in a stream. It comes as a series of heavy, trembling spheres that roll along the upper crease, guided by the hair-cords. But beneath the gutter, where I wait, the condensation forms as a fine, microscopic sweat. 

This is my pasture.

I untuck. The hydraulic fluid warms just enough to crack the joints open with a faint, imperceptible dry scrape. I do not drink the water—it is too cold, a single drop would engulf me and freeze my breathing-slits before it could be shed. Instead, I glean the drift that the moisture carries with it.

The thermal chimney brings everything. Caught in the bead of water that hangs from the copper lip is a broken tibia from an aphid that died three valleys away; the gold, spherical grain of an oak-tree’s pollen, blown up from forests that have names I cannot possess; a shredded scrap of lace-wing membrane, dried to the brittleness of mica.

I drag these out of the surface tension with my pedipalps, chewing the desiccated protein with mandibles that have been ground down by wind-grit, turning the valley’s waste into the thick, dark grease that keeps my joints from locking permanently.

Sometimes, the flat-ribbed rodent passes overhead. 

Its passage is a low, sliding rasp, like an old boot being dragged over gravel. Its belly scales scrape the top of the slate ribbon, millimeters above the copper bridge. It smells of wet iron and cold fat. It does not know I am here; it is looking for the broad crusts, the easy licks of yellow pollen that settle in the troughs. But its fur—stiff, barbed, and greasy—sheds tiny, pale flakes of skin and broken shaft-tips as it squeezes through. I harvest those, too. Every shed fiber is a storehouse of tallow.

My eyes, eight dark beads set in a turret of horn-colored bone, do not look out at the clouds. They do not care for the violet sky or the great drops of air below the hornblende horns. They see only the pitch of the metal, the dark, slick track of the drip, and the shadow of the mallet.

I know the clock better than she does. 

She watches the shadow-line with her clouded hazel eyes, squinting through the crust of her lids. But I feel the shadow ten heartbeats before it reaches the basin. I feel it as a faint, microscopic shivering in the copper—the crystal lattice beginning to draw together, stiffening, getting ready for the snap.

When the needle-shadow of the western horn crosses the third gutter, the heat does not fade; it is cut away as if sliced by an adze.

The copper channel screams—a high, pinging tension that dies immediately into dead cold. The drop of melt hanging from the lip turns from liquid grease to an opaque, clouded bead of rime in the space of a single pulse.

I pull my legs in. One, two, three, four pairs, tucking them under the carapace until I am nothing more than a smooth, brown, six-sided pebble, sealed with grease against the desiccating suck of the wind. 

Below me, the shale lid clatters onto the quartz basin. She is closing it for the night. The sound is flat, muffled by the thin air. Then the wheeze of her lungs—that dry, reedy whistle that matches the slate—recedes toward the stall, toward the three copper pins that are still holding their red, subterranean fire.

The cold moves in. It does not come down from the sky; it rises out of the stone itself, black and iron-heavy. 

My heart stops. It does not need to beat in the dark. The blood in my limbs does not freeze; it simply turns to resin, holding every muscle in a clear, glassy suspension. 

I am wedged between the slate she did not make and the copper she did. When the wind picks up at midnight, sifting its dry ice through the lamellae like flour through a sieve, it plays the edges of the bronze gutters like the reeds of a great, dead organ. 

I feel the music not as sound, but as a rigid, rhythmic shivering that passes through my shell and into the bedrock of the horn. We hold the col together through the night—the stone, the copper, the woman in her skins, and the resin in my legs—waiting for the hornblende to turn yellow, and the first bead of greasy water to roll down the crease.
