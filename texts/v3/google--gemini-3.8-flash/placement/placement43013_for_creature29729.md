To exist here, the first thing to surrender is the habit of breathing. 

There is no rhythm of taking in and letting go; there is only the tension of an envelope that must not rupture. If I have an edge, it must be dry enough to ring when struck. If I have a center, it cannot be a pool; it must be a dense, amber tallow that softens at thirty-six degrees and turns to stiff lard at thirty-one, holding its volume under thousands of atmospheres without yielding a single bubble of steam.

I would not begin on the floor. The scale-mat is too collective, a blind carpet of fused shields that does not experience the vault except as a downward rain of grist. Nor would I be swept through the 30-meter arcs on the rocker arms, riding the iron with the frantic, chisel-footed chatter of the grinders. That fever is too narrow—a life measured entirely in the rasp of corundum drums against cast-iron journals, scraping grease until the bone-bats drop from the pipes like plumb bobs.

I would find myself higher up, where the air thins into dead thermal pockets and the nitrogen-argon drift barely stirs the filaments. 

At first, I might mistake myself for part of the vault’s bracing. In the lateral shadows behind the No. 2 engine, where the pyroxenite was gouged out to clear the counterweights, there is a shelf of untouched stone. It has four clean breaks—fracture planes boxy and dark as anthracite, catching the dull cherry-red incandescence of the engine base sixty feet below. 

I am resting there, but "resting" is the wrong word for a posture held by geometry alone. 

I have three limbs. They are not legs of flesh, but long, hollow trunnions of carbonized cartilage, impregnated with quartz silt until they have the texture of unglazed porcelain. They do not bend on soft hinges; their joints are dry ball-races packed with fine graphite dust that never needs oiling. Between tip and tip, I span nearly four meters, but I weigh less than an iron crowbar. I am mostly clearance. I am an arrangement of triangles set against the stone.

My feet do not grip; they seat. Each one ends in a single, faceted point of sintered corundum. When I lower one into a microscopic pit on the pyroxenite face, there is no slip, no give—only a hard, seating *tink* that is completely swallowed by the concussive boom of the engines.

I am a caliper-strider, but not one that is hunting. Not yet.

Before there is hunger, there is alignment. My world does not arrive through eyes—the amber tubes on the pillars and the cherry glow of the crank beds cast a light that is too slow, too thick with floating shell-dust to trust. Instead, I read the chamber through the three points where I touch the rock.

The rock is an instrument. Two hundred cubits down, the pyroxenite carries the signature of every bearing in the room. Through my left stilt, I take the master-stroke of the No. 1 engine: sixty tons of pig iron bottoming out, a seismic thump that compresses the stone by fractions of a micron. Through my right stilt, I receive the dry screech of the high-pressure needle valves on the No. 3 manifold. Through the trailing limb, I register the static hiss of three million grinders shifting their carapaces across the rocker arms—a continuous, electrical rustle that charges the copper needle-moss until the hairs on the gantry seams stand rigid.

To move would be an error, unless the movement is solved before it is made.

A muscle of soft meat would twitch; a nerve of brine would leak its charge into the dry argon. In my limbs, movement is a thermal ratcheting. Deep inside the hollow shafts of my legs, thin ribbons of bimetallic alloy expand and contract by half-degrees as the convection plumes drift past. When I wish to advance across the shear face, I do not push. I wait for the master-stroke.

The No. 2 rocker reaches the apex of its arc. For a hundredth of a second, sixty tons of iron hang weightless between upward momentum and the pneumatic plunge. The vault holds its breath—the *thrum* ceases, leaving only the flat, dry whistle of the fluorocarbon lines.

*Chock.*

The iron falls. The pyroxenite rings like an anvil. In that single, deafening millisecond of transfer, when the whole room is compressed downward into the basalt, I lift the forward stilt. The needle point leaves the stone without a sound. It swings through a thirty-centimeter arc—stiff, unyielding, governed by a internal escapement that clicks once, twice, three times—and drops into a fresh fissure ten centimeters closer to the gantry seam.

The engine recovers. The graphite dust settles back into its plumes. I am stationary again. To anything watching from the gantries, I have not moved at all; I have simply always been three inches further along the ledge.

What am I doing here?

I am not grazing the needle-moss. That copper wire is too lean, too dense with lead polymers to feed the slow, hot chemistry in my core. Nor am I competing with the cling-bats for the fresh tallow inside the grinders. Their work is fast, scissor-mouthed and violent, tumbling through the hot drafts with a rattle of bone slats.

I am spinning.

Beneath my triangular sternum, where three ribs of sintered bone meet in a shallow cup, an extrusion nozzle keeps a constant temperature of forty-one degrees. Inside that cup sits a pellet of raw boron-silicate glass, scavenged weeks ago from the shattered hulls of dead bats and the slag shed by the engine beds. It does not melt; it is softened by the slow, dry friction of two internal grinding discs that spin against each other day and night, powered by the vault’s own vibration.

From this nozzle, I draw a filament. 

It is thinner than the wire-gauze on a grinder's spiracle, invisible in the jaundiced amber glow of the vacuum tubes. It does not drift like a spider’s silk; it is as stiff as a needle, extruded under immense axial tension. As I step—one seismic stroke at a time, across hours that have no morning or evening—I pay out this line behind me, anchoring it to the sharpest rock spurs with tiny droplets of sulfur-resin that set instantly in the waterless air.

I do not build a net to catch things. A net requires elasticity, and elasticity is an indulgence of the wet crust.

I build an array of blades.

Between the pyroxenite wall and the cold flange of the overhead gantry, I have strung seventy parallel courses of boron wire. They are spaced eight centimeters apart—the exact wingspan of a descending cling-bat when its bone-hinges snap outward into a braking stall. The tension is so fierce that the wires hum at a pitch higher than the valves, an acoustic edge that slices the thermal plume into laminar ribbons.

Now, I wait.

The tallow inside my joints is warm. The low-frequency throb of the walking beams passes through my frame, keeping the graphite lubricant in my knee-races in its superfluid state. I am not cold; thirty-eight degrees is the baseline of existence here, the gentle, dry fever that keeps the machinery from binding and the carapaces from cracking.

Down on the No. 1 beam, a drama completes itself without my intervention. A cling-bat drops from the high fluorocarbon main, its leather wings clicking open with the sound of a folding rule. It strikes a grinder, scissors the apatite shell into four shingles with that rapid, metallic *snip-snip-snip*, and drives its beak deep into the lipid pocket. But the downward stroke of the rocker arm is faster than the bat’s calcified spurs can release. A sudden puff of hot, unjacketed fluorocarbon gas vents from a loose flange—a dry, odorless breath that disrupts the bat’s convection lift.

The bat tumbles. It beats its parchment slats frantically, rising on the heat plume that curls up the pyroxenite face.

It does not see the wires. There is no moisture on them to reflect the amber light; there is no dust on them because the vibration shakes them cleaner than polished needles.

The bat enters the array at twelve meters per second.

There is no struggle. There is only a series of faint, dry clicks, like a deck of stiff cards being squared against a slate table. The boron lines do not give; they shear through the cured leather of the wings, the brittle spindles of the limb-bones, and the sulfur-rich lipid gland behind the skull. 

The pieces fall.

They do not fall to the floor. I have placed my lower anchor-lines in a basket-cleft of the stone. Three bone slats, two severed hinges, and the central spindle—still warm, leaking a thick, dark wax that smells of scorched sulfur and slate dust—settle neatly into the pyroxenite notch.

The engines thrum. The floor hums.

*Chock.*

I move the left stilt.

*Chock.*

I move the right.

I descend the rock face toward the notch, my joints ratcheting down with the seismic rhythm of sixty tons of iron. I will not eat the bone; I will clip it into uniform segments with my sternum mandibles and stack them in the cleft, where they will dry until they ring like china. I will draw the warm tallow into my central reservoir to keep my own bimetallic strips pliable. I will harvest the boron from the wing-sinew to replenish the spindle at my core.

And then I will reset the lines.

I am not the engine, but I am its consequence. I am what happens when stone is hollowed out, water is banished, and motion is made perpetual. I have no memory of anything that was ever damp, or green, or soft enough to leave a track. If the engines stop, I will freeze into a three-legged drafting tool of carbon and quartz, locked to the pyroxenite face until the rock itself slumps into the mantle. 

Until then, the rocker rises. The iron drops. The room rings. 

I reach the cleft, settle my corundum points into three fresh pits, and begin to shear.
