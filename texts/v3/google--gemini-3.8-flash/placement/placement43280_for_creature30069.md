I would not be out on the open flats, running the long spoke of the shadow. 

To run with the runners requires a symmetry of the hips and an elastic moisture in the tendons that belongs only to the young. Out there, five miles from the root, the shadow sweeps wide and fast; a man must jog at a steady, rhythmic lope for six hours just to keep his forehead within the blue edge, all while dragging wicker catch-frames and scraping the noon-tack before the sun catches his heel and cooks the marrow in his shin.

No. If you sought me, you would have to look where the shadow does not run, but turns.

You would find me at the axle.

***

I am seated in the throat of an ancient frost-check—a vertical furrow six paces deep, carved into the shaded flank of a cork spire whose crown disappears three hundred fathoms up into the white glare. 

At the base of the spire, the world does not sweep; it crawls. The shadow here is not a thin, racing band leagues long, but a dense, blunt wedge of indigo cool that pivots around the column like the skirt of a dancer. To stay alive here, I do not need to run. I need only to wake every forty heartbeats, drag my hips three palms to the left along the curved root-shelf, and pull my wicker shields after me. I live in a perpetual, slow circle, chasing the shade around a circumference of two hundred paces, over and over, month after month, wearing a smooth, grease-black trench into the ancient heartwood floor.

My skin has long since ceased to be skin. It is a carapace of layered tallow, ash from the dung-hearths, and the fine, red lignite flour that sifts down continuously from the crown whenever the burl-hawks land. My knees are swollen, calcified knobs; my knuckles are scarred flat from decades of pressing against the grooved floor to leverage my weight. My eyelids are two slits cut in leather, but my ears are wide, funnel-shaped, and naked. 

I am what the runners leave behind when the runner’s gait breaks: the *Trunk-Fast*, or the *Ear of the Hinge*.

My task is not to gather moisture—the runners drop two bladders of morning-sweat into my cleft every third dawn as they sweep past the spire on their wide circuit, tossing them into the dark like men throwing meat to a dog. My task is to keep my jaw against the wood.

***

In my left hand I hold a chisel carved from the serrated jaw of an old borer-grinder; in my mouth, clenched hard between my rear molars, is a rod of cured locustid femur, twelve inches long, its far tip driven two inches deep into a living capillary of the heartwood.

Through the bone between my teeth, the entire continent speaks directly into the base of my skull.

The world above the floor is a deafening, abrasive shriek—the territorial rasping of the dog-sized locustids on the ridges, the tearing hiss of the gliders dropping through the thin thermals, the dry wind shaking the cork spires like hollow organ pipes. But down through the rod, the world is deep, hydraulic, and structural.

I feel the morning dew forty miles north: it arrives as a faint, glassy slackening of the grain, an almost imperceptible easing of tension in the rings as the surface bark drinks its greasy sweat.

I feel the passing of the runners: a rhythm of tiny, synchronized thuds, like the patter of dry seeds dropped on a stretched hide, twenty miles out along the tip of the spire's reach. I can count their strides. I know which of the women is carrying a newborn on her back by the double-strike of her right heel, and I know when a youth is failing by the way his toe drags across the checks.

And beneath them—far beneath them—I feel the white-heads.

***

An auger is moving toward the surface eight leagues to the east-southeast. 

I have felt it for two rotations of the sun. At first it was only a greasy tickle in the root of my jaw—a faint, rotary grind that made the sap-crust in my molars itch. But now it has developed its true tone: a dense, heavy *thrum-thrum-thrum*, the sound of sixty paces of segmented muscle driving an enamel crown through fossilized wood. It is ascending along an ancient resin vein that has been dry for ten thousand seasons.

Where the auger chews, it leaves an emptiness filled only with pressurized, scalding flour. It leaves an eggshell crust. 

If the runners keep their bearing tomorrow, their long shadow-sweep will carry three hundred of them directly across that vein at the height of the noon glare. They will not hear the hum; their ears are plugged with insect-wax against the locustid screams, and their eyes are squinted against the blinding light of the salt-colored plains. They will step onto the crust. The floor will give a dry *huff*, and sixty paces of humanity will drop into the red flour, their skin roasted from their ribs before they can claw three inches toward the air.

I have work to do.

***

I wait for the shadow to bring me around to the eastern face of the pillar. The cork here is deeply furrowed, spongy, smelling of ancient sun-bakes and the dry dung of the burl-hawks that roost three miles above my head.

I reach into the niche where I keep my striker—a heavy club fashioned from the hardened thigh-plate of a ridge-locust, weighted with plugs of petrified gum. 

I set my feet against the grain. I find the acoustic seam—a narrow, pale streak in the cork where an old tap-root was sheared off by a continental shift ages ago, leaving a dense heartwood flute that runs uninterrupted from the base of the spire straight up into the high, sounding hollows of the summit.

I raise the club and strike.

*CLACK-TONG.*

The shock of the blow travels through the meat of my forearms and rattles my collarbones. It is not a loud sound here on the floor; the thin air eats the treble instantly, carrying off the crackle in the dry wind. But the spire itself groans. The three-hundred-fathom cork tower acts as a massive wooden pipe, catching the vibration, amplifying it through its miles of dry, vertical cells, and throwing it out from its summit into the empty stratosphere.

Far out on the savannah of dry heartwood, forty miles away, the sound drops back to earth like a stone dropped into a dry well. The runners will not hear it through their ears; they will feel it strike their solar plexus. 

*One strike:* The spire stands.
*Two strikes:* The runners must shorten their spoke; draw the shadow in three miles closer to the axle.
*Three strikes:* The auger is chewing. The floor ahead is dead.

I swing the club a second time. *CLACK-TONG.* 

I wait ten heartbeats, feeling the return-echo hum through the locustid rod between my teeth. Then I strike a third.

Across the plains, miles away, I can feel through the floor the sudden, ragged disruption of the runners' pace. The seed-patter stops. The line bunches. Then, slowly, the rhythm reforms—angled now, banking inward toward the pillar, peeling away from the death-trap where the worm is turning the earth into hot dust.

***

My hands drop. The shadow has already moved three inches while I was striking; a band of savage, white midday sun has crept across my left knee, instantly crisping the layer of aphid-fat on my kneecap until it smokes and turns bitter.

I grumble, drop the club, and heave myself forward with my knuckles, dragging my withered legs through the black ditch of my own making, slipping back into the cool, indigo slit of the furrow.

Above me, a burl-hawk drops from the cork lip, its leathery ribs locking into place with a dry, wooden rattle that sounds like an iron chest opening. It falls two hundred fathoms in total silence, banking sharply as it hits the radiant wall of the midday heat-front, and begins its long, circular drift over the red savannah, watching for the spindle-crabs to freeze in the grain.

I wipe the grease from my chin. I place my teeth back upon the rod.

Down in the dark, the auger keeps grinding. Its rhythm has changed; it has felt the shudder of my strikes through the capillary walls. It knows I am up here, sitting on the rind. But it is blind, and it has six leagues of stone-hard lignite to chew before it reaches the daylight, and long before its tooth-crowned head breaks the floor, the sun will have turned the pillar three more times, and I will be around on the western flank, waiting for the dew-catchers to throw their bitter skins into the dark.

I close my slit-eyes. The skull-bone vibrates. The wood hums its dry, unbroken note, and the planet turns beneath us, slow and heavy as a grinding wheel.
