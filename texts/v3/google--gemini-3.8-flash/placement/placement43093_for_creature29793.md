The first sensation is not breath, but shear.

To exist here is to be held between two entirely different kinds of hard. On the dorsal side, there is the tremor—the vast, rhythmic, thirty-second heave of the chimney, coming through the basalt like a knuckle dragged across a taut hide. It is hot, iron-heavy, and alive with the sliding friction of several hundred thousand bodies churning their slow circuit above. On the ventral side, there is something else: something unnaturally flat, unnaturally cool, rigid with an ancient, stubborn geometry that the mountain never made.

Limestone. 

It does not hum. It does not yield tallow. It simply resists, cold-grained and bitter with the chalk of a sea that vanished before the rock cracked open to make this chimney. 

I am in the seam where they meet. 

I do not have the flat, slate bevel of the shingles who roll in the open draft. My skin does not click. When the swarm surges above, turning its bellies to the sulfur pulse, I feel it only as a changing load—a ton of horn pressing down through the lintel, then lifting slightly as the boil rolls toward the vent. My body is rounder, ringed, bound in thick, dry parchment that rustles against itself like rolled legal paper whenever the bristled segments contract. 

I have no need for the great open draft. The air in these masonry tubes does not circulate; it is stale, heavy with limestone flour, and so dry that even the ghost of an odor is sheared away before it can register. What I have instead is grain.

My mouth is three horn wedges, set in a triangle, hard enough to score glass. They do not bite; they plane. They move against the square-cut block with a minute, steady vibration, shaving off ribbons of calcium no thicker than a moth’s scale. It is a slow, methodical erasure. The tool-marks left by whatever hands dressed this stone centuries ago—the small, straight incisions of a bronze or iron chisel—are being ground away, stroke by stroke, into a fine, sweetish dust. 

When I swallow the stone, it does not dissolve in acid. There is no fluid in me to spare for digestion. The limestone is crushed mechanically in the gizzard against pebbles of basalt I swallowed three moltings ago, pulverized until it gives up its trace salts, then packed into dry pellets that I mortar into the margins of the tube behind me. 

I am eating the room’s only memory of an outside. 

Every few days—though days have no meaning here beyond the rising and falling heat-tide of the deep anchors—a shadow passes over the crevice where my gallery opens into the chapel. Not a shadow of light, but a shadow of warmth: a shingle has ceased to slide. 

Its infrared radiance dulls from the fever-pitch of living horn to the flat, dull warmth of the bedrock. It has finished its recursive chewing; its breathing slits have clinkered shut; it has been wedged sideways into the lintel’s corner by the pressure of its neighbors and abandoned there, dead before it can be dismantled.

That is when the bristles along my flank work in reverse.

I back down the tube, dragging my leathery rings through the chalk dust, until my sensory fringe touches the dead plate. It is smooth, bevelled, greasy with the tallow of a thousand passages. My mouth-chisels do not eat it—horn is too tough, too fibrous with silicate to yield to the gizzard—but my fore-limbs, short and hooked like iron crampons, seize its lateral margins.

With a long, dry, tearing heave, I draw the plate out of the swarm’s floor and into the wall.

The swarm does not notice the theft. The vacancy is filled instantly by another body dropping into the gap with a soft, muffled *clack*. 

Here, in the dark between the dressed stones, I turn the shell. I line the tube with it. I press it into the void left where I spent the last three seasons eating away the limestone. The dead shingle becomes a shingle once more, but now it faces inward. It walls my passage. It protects my soft, leathery sides from the grinding tonnage of the chapel floor. 

I am replacing the human terrace, grain by grain, plate by plate. 

Where the architects set their square plumb-lines and plumbed their walls against the chimney’s draft, I am laying a new wall made entirely of the dead. It is curved where the terrace was straight. It is flexible where the masonry was brittle. The ancient watch-cell is not collapsing; it is being swapped out from the inside, its dry white bones traded for the dark, fibrous horn of the things that crawled over it.

Sometimes, when my head-shield is pressed deep into the joint where the limestone block meets the basalt chimney-root, I feel the drone of the Anchors through the rock. It is a bass note so low that it shakes the chalk from my jaws. It tells of the deep furnace, of the white-hot gases cracking in the belly of the earth, of the vast, blind lung that keeps this entire chimney from freezing into silent stone.

They are the anchors of the fire. 

I am the anchor of the wall. 

I work in the quiet behind their velvet rustle, blind, parchment-skinned, and perpetual, turning the work of men into the armor of the cleft, one scraped mouthful at a time.
