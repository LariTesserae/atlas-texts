At first, there is only the register of weight. 

Not sound—sound is a disturbance of an air too thin to hold a shape—but the heavy, four-stroke cadence traveling up through the bedrock. It enters not through ears, because there are none, but through the hard, dense horn-spheres at the tips of my stilts: *thrum-shudder, thrum-shudder*. A locomotive-weight boiler idling half a mile to the windward. Below that, the slower, subterranean heave of the eight-day pulse, tilting the plateau by a hair’s breadth, setting the water in the slate pans to rocking.

I am not in the light. To be in the light is to dry out, and to dry out is to turn to brittle cane that snaps under its own lever-arms.

I am beneath.

My ceiling is arched, six feet above the stones, ribbed in sweeping transverse curves of black keratin that sweat a yellow, mutton-thick grease. It is warm down here—the warmth of a blacksmith’s shop an hour after the fire has been damped. The air smells of half-digested iron, hot marrow, and the sharp, vinegar tang of bile. To my left, the skirt of suction-pads cycles with its rhythmic, muffled wet *cluff-shuck, cluff-shuck*, lifting from the greasy slate, advancing four inches, and sealing down again with the pressure of sixty atmospheres.

My body does not have a belly to drag. Everything that keeps the fluid moving is packed into the small, dry cage of my thorax, where something like a clepsydra—a water-clock of thin bone and oiled membranes—ticks with a rapid, uneven hitch. *Tck-tck... tck.* It does not beat like a heart; it meters out drops of thin, clear lymphatic oil that keep the joints of my stilts from galling against their sockets.

My head is a smooth, blind peg of gray bone. I do not miss eyes. What would eyes find in the amber gloom of the under-carriage? Eyes would only show the grease dripping from the seams; they would not tell me the temperature of the gizzard-slag before it touches the stone.

I test the floor with my horn-beak.

There: two yards behind the trailing edge of the left third-tier pad. A fresh dropping. The herd-vessel has vented a knot of shale paste, still bubbling with sulfurous gas, the size of a bullock’s head. If it sits upon the basalt for three minutes, the freezing dew will weld it to the rock like poured lead. 

I step forward. My legs are long—charred wicker, dry reed—moving in high, deliberate arcs to clear the churned fat left by the pads. I drive the lower spade of my beak into the crust of the slag, levering upward with the whole torsion of my thorax. 

The heat of it breaks against the underside of my neck. 

Immediately, the vascular apron—the wide, pleated bib of crimson throat-skin that hangs collapsed against my breastbone—fills with fluid. It unfurls like a sail, thin as parchment, drinking the hot steam that curls off the cracked paste. The moisture does not turn to ice; it enters the capillaries directly, thin and iron-rich, recharging the water-clock in my chest. My joints loosen. The ticking inside my ribs settles into a long, quiet slide.

I strip the soft, oily core from the mineral cake—the parts the gizzard-stones crushed but could not dissolve—and let the cold outer slag tumble away into the wake. Behind me, two others of my kind are already picking through the crumbs. We do not look at one another; our beaks click once against each other’s horn-stilts as we cross paths, an accidental dry sound like firewood shifting in a grate, and we move on. There is no herd-memory between us, only the common overhang of the iron roof.

---

Yet something is altering in the stroke above us.

For three days, the cadence of the plates has been lengthening. The interval between the *thrum* and the *shudder* is now five seconds instead of three. The internal anvils are not striking; they are rolling, sluggishly, through a slurry that has grown too stiff to pump.

The ceiling is sinking.

It does not fall; it descends by eighths of an inch across leagues of travel. The yellow grease that coats the seams is not running; it is skinning over, turning into a dull, waxen rind that the weft-lice are abandoning. High above, in the deep folds where the hull-plates lap over the flank-girders, I feel the dry, frantic skittering of their hand-axe shells as they crowd toward the rim, seeking the outer air before the seams bind tight and pinch them into paste.

The boiler is cooling.

I stand with my throat-bib spread wide, waiting under the leeward spiracles, but when the hydraulic seals groan open, what vents is not the white, whistling blast that recharges our limbs. It is a long, faint puff of gray vapor, smelling of cold tallow and wet soot. It disperses before it reaches my bib. The dew on the stone beneath my horn-pads does not boil off; it remains slick, gray, and dead.

The vessel slows. Its hundred short pads do not cycle forward. They spread themselves outward, widening their circumference, their pneumatic skirts flattening against the basalt with a final, wet, suctional groan that shudders into the floor-plates and does not lift again.

Silence.

The infrasonic drone that has driven the marrow of my stilts since they hardened from gristle ceases. The mountain below does not stop humming, but the roof has disconnected from it. The hull is no longer an engine; it is an arch.

In the sudden cold, the amber gloom beneath the belly turns the color of bruised lard. The water-clock in my thorax stumbles: *tck... tck.......... tck.*

From the high seams near the spiracles, I hear the first faint tapping. 

Dry. Rapid. Like wood-beetles boring through a dead beam. 

The sieve-folk. They have smelled the boiler fail from their hollow hulks miles down the terrace. They are already at the outer rims, tapping the horn-iron with their curved nails, testing the thickness of the rind, waiting for the flesh between the ribs to chill enough that they may crawl into the lateral vents without blistering their blue palms.

The other spindle-walkers beneath the belly do not understand the cold. Two of them have curled their stilts beneath their thoraxes, settling into the cooling grease beneath the central gizzard, their beaks tucked into their dead throat-sails. They will remain there while the sieve-folk enter from above; their wicker limbs will be broken for leverage, their joint-oil scraped into horn gourds to grease the spade-nails of the children.

I cannot curl. The water-clock in my chest has one drop left to fall.

I turn toward the skirt.

For the first time since my legs lengthened from their larval pods, I step out from the shadow of the hull.

---

The sky is lower than the carapace was.

It does not arch; it presses. It is a ceiling of bruised, slate-colored wool, so dense and cold that it tastes like copper against the bare skin of my throat. The windless dew settles immediately across my head-peg, running down the horn in thin, greasy threads that numb the bone.

The vastness is an injury. Distance has no meaning here; the broad, stepped terraces of basalt roll away in every direction, slate upon slate, gray pan upon gray pan, until the curve of the mountain drowns in mist. Five leagues away—or ten—another hull is moving, orange with rust, venting its white plumes into the leaden air with a regular, beautiful mechanical pulse.

I feel it through the basalt: *thrum-shudder, thrum-shudder*.

A harness-worm hangs from the belly of a blind floater drifting two hundred feet above the terrace. Its three-pronged hook of gristle drags through the wire-grass thirty paces to my right, scratching a faint, white furrow in the wet stone. It passes silently, its long, vulcanized thread swaying with the laminar creep of the cloud-deck.

I do not look at it. I lower my head-peg until it is level with the basalt, tuck my throat-skin tight into its dry thoracic slot, and lift my stilts.

The first step outside the iron shelter is uncertain; the grease of the open terrace is thin, mixed with cold dew, and my pads skid an inch before the horn bites the rock. But the pulse in the stone is clear. It is an anchor. 

Three miles to the southwest, a herd-vessel is working its rotary mouth through a bed of oil-lichen, its gizzard-stones grinding with the force of falling boulders. The basalt between us is a single, unbroken rod of black glass that carries every revolution of its plates straight into my frame.

I walk. High-stepping. Wicker and cane against the vast, indifferent floor. 

Behind me, the carcass I left is already growing blue with the moss that bridges the stone to the iron. Ahead, the low, orange brow of the living boiler vents another column of steam—a white pillar against the bruise of the sky—and beneath its distant belly, in the amber darkness where the frost never settles, there is a clearance three cubits high waiting to receive the rhythm of my stilts.
