At first, there is only the load. 

A dull, continuous drag through the shoulders, as if something five stories below were slowly sinking into cold fat and asking to be held up by the neck. The taste in the mouth is tallow and wet zinc, with that sharp, brassy tang that comes off copper pipes when the cooling spray drifts across them and turns to mist on the tongue. 

I would not be on the catwalks, where the light makes knives of everything. That is for the young, or the ones whose eyes have not yet grown thick-skinned from the steam-draft. 

Nor am I down in the aeration troughs, though the smell of them—that sour, clean reek of stone-ground bone and hyperactive green scum—is pulled through every flue I lean against. 

I find myself in the slack. 

In a world jammed so hard against its own ribs, where four-inch iron casings push through lath-and-plaster ceilings before the hearth-bread has even cooled, there has to be someone who lives in the four inches *between* the cylinder and the wall. Not an engineer—the basin has no patience for paper or geometry—and not a laborer with a spanner, who only tightens what is already screaming. 

Something older in the joints. An adjuster of chains. 

I have the boxwood between my rear left molars. It is worn down to a smooth, wet groove where the grain has softened under thirty years of saliva and hydraulic grit. The other end of the rod—six feet of square-forged iron—rests not on the basalt floor (the basalt is too nervous, full of shallow, shivering twitches that lie to you), but directly against the crown-bolt of the secondary hanging truss. 

Through the jaw, the city does not sound quiet at all. 

Through the jaw, the city is an iron kettle coming to the boil on an uneven fire. There is the heavy, wet *shuck-thud* of the drainage ram down in the bilge-sumps, two hundred yards north; there is the dry, tearing scrape of a fresh slab of silica calving off the bedplate beneath the third-tier loom-room; and there is the faint, greasy hiss of the moss-felt creeping an inch every twelve hours over the cold-water flanges, trying to drink the sweat off the bolts before the oil-crew can swab it away.

My heels are broad, cracked at the rims like dry horn, and jammed into short boots of double-cured lath-skin that have never known grease from an animal with hooves. I don't use the rungs. Rungs shear off when the bulkheads slide. I stand on the turnbuckles themselves, one foot cocked higher than the other, knees loose, swaying with that slow, heavy three-second roll that the hanging tenements take when the wind drops down from the mantle-vents.

My hands are not clever. They are flat and gray, the color of wet slate under a clouded noon, with pale yellow calluses at the crotch of the thumb where the chain-links bite. 

Around my neck hangs the lead-pot, swinging on three strands of braided copper wire. It stays lukewarm against my sternum, warmed by the little charcoal cake banked inside its double wall. Every twenty minutes, when a joist begins to groan—not the high, singing groan that means it’s taking weight, but the wet, sticky groan that means the green stone is rising up from below to push it out of true—I dip two fingers into the tallow-pot and smear the threads of the hanger. Then I lay my shoulder against the nut and turn it half an inch against the world’s rising. 

I am not saving the house. The house will be a flue by Tuesday; I can already smell the lime-dust leaking from the mortar where the chimney-breast is pinching shut against the pump-flange. 

I am only giving the woman with the two blanched-faced children time to finish their porridge. I am keeping the bunk from tilting thirty degrees while the grease-pot is still on the trivet.

A lath comes out of the expansion joint by my ear. It doesn’t look at me; it doesn’t have the eyes for it, only two dark, wet beads beneath a skull no wider than a hazelnut. It smells of machine oil and cold, wet mouse. It slides its flat, soft body through a seam in the boiler-sheathing that wouldn’t take a bone-handled butter knife, its silver ribs folding together like a paper fan, hunting the tallow-beetles that cluster where the lag-felt has come unstitched. For a second, its cold, grease-slick fur brushes the back of my hand. I don’t twitch. If you twitch in the drift, you drop your boxwood, and a man who drops his boxwood has to listen with his bare ears, which tells him only what has already broken, never what is about to yield.

From below, through the hatchway where the floorboards have parted three fingers wide, comes the smell of their stew: dried scavenger-tongue boiled with bitter bladder-succulents and salt from the scrubbers. It is a good smell. It has the weight of an hour’s rest in it. 

The light through the high zinc monitor overhead begins to shift. It turns from the color of skimmed milk to the color of raw lead. Mid-afternoon. 

The condensation stops dropping. For seventy heartbeats, the fine, skin-rotting mist that hangs between the gantries lifts, blown aside by that cold, dry draft from the deep clefts where the basalt is still freezing out of the earth's red belly. 

Through the crack between the corrugated iron and the engine-flange, I can see the catwalks thirty feet out. 

The Watchers are stepping out. They look like bundles of wet wool and grease-leather dropped along the iron rails. They don't look at each other. They tilt their square, turnip-colored faces up into that hard, sterile sun, their skin drinking the dry air before the towers start their churning again. 

I do not go out. Out there, the horizon is too wide; it shows too much of the basin’s floor—all those leagues of glassy, black rock boiling up without an owner, and the terrible, silent speed with which the pipes are flung across it to pin it down before it cools. It makes the belly feel empty, like an open flue.

I stay in the crook of the hangar. 

The boxwood is between my teeth again. 

Down in the dark, three hundred feet below the bilge, a tendon of stone has just parted. The sound travels up the iron like a dry knuckle cracking inside a heavy mitten. 

*Not this row,* the rod says to my jaw. *The next one over. The one where they run the loom-belts.*

I take the offset spanner from my thigh-loop. I do not hurry. The iron will not move faster for a man’s rushing, and the stone will certainly not wait for him to be ready. 

I lean my hip against the cold, sweat-filmed cheek of the cylinder, feel the great piston inside gather itself for the return stroke—a ton of forged iron sliding through a bath of tallow with a sound like a wet lung filling—and I wait for the turnbuckle to take the weight.
