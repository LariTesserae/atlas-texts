At first, there is only the tooth.

The left lower canine, specifically. It is worn down to a flat, amber-colored peg, its nerve long dead or cauterized by decades of electrical jitter, but its root goes straight into the heavy bone of the mandible. When the mandible is pressed firmly into the seam where Plate 414 meets the basalt foundation, the tooth becomes a stylus.

That is where I begin: not as a shape or an intent, but as an aperture for the mountain’s weight. 

I would not be down on the slick, iridescent tiles of the trench; the leeches have claimed the molecular flatlands, and the alkaline wash there would dissolve the skin off a palm in forty cycles. Nor would I be up in the high ribbing where the gliders hang like folded parasols. That height requires an emptiness of body, a paper-light chassis made for the updrafts, and I am far too heavy, too sodden with grease and calcified water.

I am wedged thirty feet above the sump, in the hollow behind a dead monitoring pod whose quartz lens was shattered six centuries ago by a shard of blown conduit. The pod has filled with a dry, fibrous silt—mostly shed myriapod hulls, crushed silica threads from the shelf-fungus, and the grey felt I have scraped and packed together to keep the alloy’s bare chill from leaching the heat out of my belly. 

It is a pocket of still air in a world of violent wind.

I have come to look like the corner I inhabit. If you were to bring a torch—though no one ever will—you would not see a man. You would see a pale, lumpy swelling in the tufa, greased with the lard-and-calcite paste until my ribs look like an extension of the wall’s stepped terraces. My shins are bent into the curve of the casing; the second thumb on my left hand has grown thick and spatulate, permanently locked around an anchor-bolt that was driven into the seam when the city was young. Even in sleep, the tendons do not loosen. To fall here is not an error; it is an extinction.

The twenty-hertz note does not stay in the room; it lives in the hollow of my pelvis. It fills the bladder, rattles the loose salt in the joints, and makes the tongue flutter against the roof of the mouth unless clamped hard between the gums.

Every forty seconds, the world performs its breath.

*Ten seconds:* The high, dry whine begins in the valves sixty yards up. A thin, cold vibration that travels through the alloy plates faster than sound through air. My tooth picks it up as a taste of copper.

*Five seconds:* The air goes entirely dead. The low drone drops half an octave—the enormous balance-valves three miles above are opening their throats, starving the sub-generators of their backpressure. The pressure drops so fast the grease on my skin prickles into tiny, cold bubbles.

*Zero:* The shriek. Not a mechanical scream, but the sound of water being torn apart at the molecular level as it leaves the laser-orifices. 

Then comes the fall: the descending sheet of hot, boiling aerosol. From my pocket, I do not see it—my eyes have shrunk to dull, milky marbles that register only the slow, emerald respiration of the chemiluminescent moss across the seam—but I feel the thermal wall hit. It smells of hot brake-linings, wet limestone, and the sharp, dead-ant odor of ozone.

When the mist reaches my ledge, it has cooled from a scald to a hard, sweating fever. I lean out slightly from the pod, opening my mouth wide to let the condensation coat the back of my throat. It is bitter, tasting of zinc and lye, but it is wet. In the Sub-Sieve, you do not drink from pools; you let the atmosphere bleed against your membranes.

During the drop, the steam-flies hatch from the gutters below, rising on the hot draft in their millions. Their wings make a dry, paper-flicker that I feel against the fine hairs of my forearms. Then comes the snap and slap of the gliders dropping from the dark ceiling, their mica-wings banking hard, the stench of their scorched-grease tails trailing through my niche like smoke. Sometimes the tip of a wing will brush my shoulder—a hard, varnished slap that leaves a smudge of yellow tallow—and then they are gone, banking toward the amphitheater terraces to pry a myriapod from its herd before the mist settles.

I do not hunt the myriapods. We are too similar in our patience. Sometimes, when a herd clatters past my niche, their white segments grinding like a train of porcelain saucers, one will pause. Its feather-fine antennae will whip across my face, tasting the lard-crust on my cheeks, the mineral-salt on my lips. It finds nothing foreign in me. I am merely another plate of stabilized alloy that happens to have a temperature of ninety-nine degrees.

What am I doing here?

I used to wonder if I was left behind, or if I crawled down here to escape the great noise of the upper tiers, only to find that the noise had a heavier root. But memory does not survive long under twenty hertz. The vibration shakes the past into a fine, grey powder, like the silt beneath my ribs.

Now, there is only the work.

Between the twentieth and the thirtieth second of every cycle, while the mist is turning to grease on the walls and running down in mineralized gutters, I reach out with the scraper. The scraper is four inches of hardened structural pin, ground flat against the basalt. 

I work on Weep-Hole 18. 

The hole is no wider than my thumb. It was drilled to relieve the hydraulic hydrostatic head behind the Eleventh Tier's eastern shear-plate. For forty years—or four hundred, time has the consistency of cooling tallow—the calcite has been trying to choke it. If the calcite fills the hole, the water will find the dry conduits behind the vitreous casing; if the conduits flood, the twenty-hertz hum will jump to thirty, the balance-valves will lock, and the ceiling—sixty yards of dark, unmortared weight—will come down to meet the iridescent floor like two jaws shutting on a dry bone.

So, I scrape. 

*Chit. Chit. Chit.*

Three strokes per cycle. The white lime flakes off, falling through the steam toward the leeches below. The hole stays clear. It breathes a thin, foul-smelling trickle of warm water that smells of old machine oil and the deep, dead earth.

Sometimes, another comes.

It happens once every thousand cycles. A heavy, rubbery footstep on the seam below my pocket. No sound of breath—none of us breathe through our noses anymore, the moist air would rot the lungs—just the sliding drag of calloused pads on vitreous slate.

I don't look. I lean forward out of the pod.

The other Tallow-Cloak climbs up into the recess. He is smaller than I am, his skin yellowed like sheepskin left near a furnace, his shoulders crusted with the turquoise oxidation of some pipe he has been tending near the western bulkhead. He smells of fermented lichen and sulfur.

We do not speak. There is no air left in our throats for small sounds, and the forty-second valve-shriek would swallow a scream as if it were a speck of ash.

Instead, he reaches out with his long, double-thumbed hand, finds the back of my neck, and pulls my head toward his.

We touch foreheads.

Our skulls are thick, the bone grown dense and spongy to cushion the brain from the acoustics. When the foreheads meet, the city pours through the contact point.

Through his bone, I receive the west:
*The secondary balance-valve on Tier Twelve has a cracked seat; it is fluttering at sixty cycles, spraying cold sludge instead of mist.*
*A myriapod herd has wedged itself into the drain at the fifth terrace; three are dead, their shells crushed into the teeth of the gate.*
*The deep hum—the great mother-generator six miles down—has shifted its phase by three degrees. The plates are cooler tonight. The crust is heavier.*

I press back against him, giving him the east:
*Weep-Hole 18 is open.*
*The moss on the blind buttress is turning pale jade; there is a power leak in the 400-amp bus behind the third rib.*
*The gliders are hungry; their broods have hatched in the high blind arches.*

We hold the contact for four seconds, our brains shaking together in the same cranial cup, vibrating like two iron filings stuck to the same magnet. His skin is slick with grease; my lard smears into his calcite. We are two drops of grease on the same hot skillet, sliding together, then apart.

He lets go. He drops back down to the seam with that loose, terrifying sureness that has forgotten the concept of the void. He moves toward the drainage trench to gather lichen balls before the next vent.

The whine begins in the roof. 

*Ten seconds.*

I set my scraper into the small groove I have carved in the sill of the pod. I pull my knees up against my belly, tucking my broad, rubbery feet beneath my thighs so the hot aerosol won't peel the softer skin between the toes. 

I open my mouth. I set the yellow peg of my tooth against Plate 414.

The city arrives. It is enormous, indifferent, perfectly balanced, and so heavy that the thought of light seems like an absurdity imagined by a simpler animal. 

The pressure drops. 
My ears pop behind their horn plugs. 
The steam-flies rise, their wings humming against my cheek. 

I bite down into the dark, and I hold the Eleventh Tier true against the world.
