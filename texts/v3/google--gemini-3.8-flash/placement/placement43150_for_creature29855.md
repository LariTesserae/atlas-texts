At first there is only the temperature, which does not ask to be felt because it never shifts. Sixty degrees is not cold enough to make the muscles knot, nor warm enough to allow the skin to forget where it ends. It is simply the neutral state of slate, a stillness that seeps inward until the blood runs with the heavy, unhurried cadence of oil poured through a cold horn.

Then the weight of the air arrives. It is thick with the sweet, caustic sting of guano peat and the oily vapor of a billion preened feathers—a gas that tastes of vinegar and wet chalk. 

And the sound. 

You do not hear it with your ears. If you had ears that were open to it, the drum-skins would have torn loose within an hour, or the delicate stirrups of bone inside would have welded themselves flat against the skull to survive. The roar is not noise; it is a structural condition, like gravity. It enters through the soles of the feet where they press against the dolomite shelf, climbs the long shins, lodges behind the breastbone, and vibrates the roots of the molars. It is the friction of twelve million wings churning against an overhang that never meets the sky. 

I am not out on the flat floor where the spade-headed beetles march in their iron columns. To be down there, horizontal and soft, is to be counted as a fall; within sixty breaths the whip-like antennae would find the hem of your coat, and the scissor-mandibles would begin their neat, mechanical work on your laces. 

Nor am I up among the honeycombs of the high face, where the swifts sleep in six-second bursts with their recurved needles hooked into stone. Up there, life is an electric spasm—hearts hammering like dynamos, bodies glued together in a wet, feather-and-fur lather that smells of hot blood and bat dust.

I am wedged between. 

I find myself seven paces up from the floor, in a blind chimney-joint where a flake of the cliff has bowed outward, creating a narrow, vertical dry-well. My back is braced against the sheer dolomite—cold, unweathered, its microscopic ridges biting through the hide of my smock—and my knees are drawn up against the chest, held fast by a wedged spar of dried scrub-oak that was hammered into this crack two hundred years ago.

Who am I? 

The wool and mutton-tallow in my ears have long since turned to hard, yellow wax, sealing me into a private, rhythmic thrum. My eyelids are thick, scarred at the rims with the pale crust that the ammonia leaves behind, so that the eternal slate-blue twilight does not enter as sharp shapes, but as silhouettes: the pale, ribboning undulation of an ivory centipede gliding across a shelf across the flue; the heavy, triangular shadow of a slate lizard waiting three ledges down, as motionless as the stone that bore it.

I am wearing the boots of keeled lizard-skin, but they are no longer strapped tight. They have molded to the shale dust and the damp chalk until they are stiff as roof-tiles. In my lap rests a jointed pole of willow, its lashed copper hook dark with dried fat, but the wood has dried out in the drafts; it has the brittle lightness of hollow bone. 

Perhaps I came down the chimney with the others, when the scrub-oak was green and the plain above was freezing. Perhaps I was the one who swung the slate baton, *crack, crack*, behind the hook-men, until the hampers were heavy and the bat-fat ran golden into the bladders.

Or perhaps I simply stayed too long on the fourth day, when the grayness of the rift began to take the skin, and the thought of climbing three hundred yards of chimney—into the burning, thin wind of the upper crust, into a sun that would split these blinded eyes like rotten fruit—felt like an unnatural violence. 

Up there, things begin and end. The grass dries, the herds starve, the frost breaks the rock, the fires go to ash. 

Down here, nothing ends. The birds will not stop falling. The beetles will not stop their salt-patter across the shale. The temperature will not rise to sixty-one or drop to fifty-nine. The lizard below me has not moved its left foreleg since the last draft curled from the south. Its throat pulses once: a thick, deliberate beat. 

My own chest rises to match it. Thirty seconds to draw the ammonia and the feather-dust in; thirty seconds to let it slip back into the flue.

A tick drops from the overhang high above. It falls three hundred yards through the blue twilight, fat as a plum, strikes the curve of my knee with a small, dull *thwack*, rolls down the stiff fold of lizard-hide, and hits the talus below. 

A click of claws follows. Then silence within the roar. 

I do not reach for my hook. I have forgotten what the sun tastes like, and the stone has already learned the shape of my shoulder blades. I am the thing that stayed behind to watch the hearth go cold, until the hearth became stone, and the smoke became air, and the difference between the watcher and the wall was sheared away by the iron equilibrium of the floor.
