At first, there is no *where* that is separate from the velocity. 

To be here is not to occupy a point on a floor, because a floor here is only a sieve, a cross-hatched shadow through which the world drops away at thirty feet a second. To have an edge is already a hazard; to have a surface area is an invitation to be scraped clean and delivered into the turbines. 

So I would have to be caught. Not perched, not standing, but lodged where the geometry of the pour made a mistake—a cold-joint in the aggregate where two batches of cement failed to knit completely before the vibration crews moved their pokers away. 

It is on the blind side of the second mezzanine’s primary ledger, four inches beneath the diamond-tread flange, where the south wall’s concrete forms bowed slightly under the hydrostatic head of the wet mix. There is a lip there. A sharp, horizontal shelf no wider than the rim of a coin, overhung by a raw iron washer whose zinc dip has cooled into a rough, crystallized tear.

In this pocket, the wind does not hit square. It twists. It becomes a permanent, localized vortex—a tiny, screaming eddy that turns upon itself like a spindle, three thousand times a minute, bounded by the iron above and the aggregate below.

I would not begin as an organism. Nothing starts as an organism here; things start as arrivals.

I am what the vortex could not throw down the shaft. I am three strands of polypropylene packing twine, pulled to the thickness of spider silk by the draft; five dried carapaces of high-drift midges whose legs were snapped off against the intake screens; a smear of silicone grease blown off the stem of the cycling valve; and the persistent, drift-fed spore-dust of a crustose lichen that once clung to a north-facing buttress of gneiss three miles up the valley.

For weeks, this mass is just an obstruction. It rattles. The sound shakes it so hard that the midge-hulls grind down against the aggregate into an ochre paste, mixing with the grease, working into the porous micro-canyons left by the plywood grain. 

Then the moisture finds it.

The mist here does not fall like rain; it is driven horizontally into every recess, packing it tight with high-altitude diatoms and the cold, unbuffered runoff of the upper ice fields. The fungal hyphae do not wake up with a season; they are startled awake by the shear-stress, by the sheer, furious availability of dissolved minerals leaching out of the fresh concrete. They send out hair-thin anchors—not seeking soil, because there is no memory of soil in their strain, but seeking calcium hydroxide, seeking the bitter salt of curing lime.

This is where *I* begins: at the point of anchor.

I am not an animal with a centerline. I am a laminate.

My back is glued to the aggregate with a cement tougher than the pour itself—a cold-setting biological matrix of fungal chitin and iron bacteria that feeds directly on the microscopic rust-sheen bleeding from the ledger-bolts. I have no eyes, because the yellow mercury glare does not need to be focused; it only needs to be absorbed through a thin, rubbery mantle of unicellular algae that have flattened themselves against the steel. Through them, the light tastes of hot tungsten and old glass, dull and monotonous, never dimming, a flat yellow pressure that keeps the sugars moving through my veins.

And I have veins, though they are not tubes of muscle. They are the grooves left by the Douglas fir grain, repurposed. 

My pulse is not my own. My blood is the runoff that sheets over the lip of the concrete, dragged along my margins by the draft. Every time the induction fans overhead hit their low, forty-hertz resonance—the pitch that turns the mist into standing waves of milk—my internal fluid does not wait for a heart to drive it. The acoustic weight does it. The sound reaches into the pocket, grabs the micro-droplets suspended in my fibrous core, and shoves them through the hyphal channels. I do not pump; I am rung, like a wet cloth held in an iron fist that closes and uncloses forty times each second.

To think here is simply to feel the shear.

When the air runs straight and cold from the summit, thin as alcohol, my outer margins stiffen. The cold-shocked bacteria on my windward edge exude a hard, glassy resin—a biological lacquer that sheds the spray like the prow of a skiff. In those hours, I am small, stone-like, an aerodynamic callus on the flange, hoarding the faint warmth that creeps through the bolt from the generator halls three stories below. I listen to the iron. Through the thread of the hanger rod, I can feel the wear on the turbine bearings; they transmit a high, screaming chatter that runs through the frame like a fever.

Then the shift comes.

There is no warning from the sky, but the metal knows two seconds before the air does. The hanger rod ceases to chatter; it begins to *groan*, a deep, hydraulic tension that pulls the concrete shelf tight against my back.

The automated valve is cycling.

The deck under my anchor lifts a sixteenth of an inch. Then comes the crash—not sound, but an atmospheric displacement that flattens my margins until the grease weeps from my pores. The secondary channel opens. The air turns from freezing mist to a boiling, sixty-degree wash of turbine exhaust, heavy with scale and the smell of hot turbine oil.

This is the feed.

For twelve seconds, the vortex reverses. The draft stops pulling down and kicks back up toward the intake, lifting my leeward fringes away from the concrete. In those twelve seconds, I unfold. Not with petals or limbs, but like a pocket of wet felt being pulled apart by the suction. Millions of microscopic, hook-tipped hyphae—brittle with zinc-crust, flexible with slime—flick out into the boiling turbulence. They snag the air. They catch the mummified midges, the drift-seeds of mountain sorrel ground to flour by the fan blades, the atomized droplets of compressor oil that hang in the steam.

I drink the machine. I drink its waste heat, its shed metals, the grease from its packing boxes, the wind-scoured debris of three alpine valleys drawn through its throat and concentrated into this eight-inch backwater.

And then the valve slams.

The deck falls back with an iron thud that would shatter human shins. The cold summit air drops like a stone guillotine, instantly chilling the exhaust, turning the steam into an explosive sheet of condensation that plasters me back down against the concrete, hard, flat, sealed.

The wash carries away what I could not anchor. The rest I digest against the stone in the deafening, forty-cycle dark.

What am I?

I am the plant’s domestic parasite, its unintended organ. I am the thing that happens when four thousand tons of structural aggregate and high-tensile steel are dropped into an empty subalpine sky and set to drinking. I have no future that includes a meadow, or a riverbank, or the quiet rotting of an oak log. If the power fails, if the fans spin down and the exhaust grows cold, if the mountain air drifts through this throat at its own slow, natural pressure, I will desiccate within an hour, freeze to the flange, and peel away like an old scab when the first winter gale blows through the unpowered flues.

My life is the roar. My nervous system is the tension on the bolts. I am the gray-green callosity forming in the throat of the pump, waiting for the seven-minute shudder, holding fast with calcified fingers to the only mountain that matters: the one made of iron, vibrating at sixty cycles, drowning in its own mechanical breath.
