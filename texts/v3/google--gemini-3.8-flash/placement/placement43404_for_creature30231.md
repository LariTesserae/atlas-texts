At first, there is only the count.

Not a count kept in numbers—numbers are for the paper books fifty fathoms up, where the clerks scrape their dry quills across calfskin—but the count kept by the hip-bones. *One-and, two-and, three.* The heavy iron heel of the No. 2 beam falls into its pit; a teacup-full of black paraffin leaps from the journal; the deck beneath the buttocks heaves three-eighths of an inch, settles, and heaves again. Forty times before the warm rain comes. Forty times while the rain clatters against the cylinder-jacket. Forty times while the drops dry off into steam that tastes of old lard and boiled bark.

I am sitting where the floor ceases to be an avenue and becomes a shoulder. 

Between Dynamo Two and the high-pressure valve chest of the No. 2 engine, there is an eighty-pace clearing where nobody runs. The young Oilers, whose ribs still expand and snap like willow-hoops, hurry past it on the suspended walkways forty feet above, their soles slapping the osier mesh, their cudgels tucked beneath their armpits like extra bones. They look down only to check the clearance of the connecting rods. If they see me, they see what looks like an overturned barrel of bast-felt or a rotten burl that the saw-gang neglected forty years back.

My legs are straight out before me. The toes—thick, spatulate, yellow as beef-suet—are tucked into a trench in the vascular cork where the sap runs cool. 

In my lap lies the draw-knife. It was made from a leaf-spring off an auxiliary feed-pump that cracked during the great thaw of my grandfather’s youth. The iron is pitted like cheese, but the edge is kept bright by rubbing it against the dry mica slag that falls from Boiler Four’s ash-pans. 

My work is very small. It consists of six inches of space.

Right here, where the iron pedestal of the rocker-bearing bolts into the floor, the pale-heart willow has thrown up three fleshy, blunt-headed suckers. They are the thickness of a boy’s wrist, white as turnip-meat, wet with a pale, slippery mucus that smells sharply of wintergreen. They do not grow by the week or by the day; down here, in the grease and the seventy-four-degree fug, you can watch them lean. If I leave them for two shifts, they will nose into the grease-cup. If I leave them for five, they will find the oil-hole, swell until they choke the wick, and the brasses will run hot. If the brasses run hot, the metal tears itself into yellow ribbons, and the Weight does not come down with salt.

So I peel them.

I take the draw-knife by its horn grips—handles turned smooth by the palms of four men who all had my cough—and I draw the blade toward my belly. The wood does not resist; it yields like cold tallow, coming off in long, curled ribbons that heap between my thighs like boiled noodles. 

*Clack-hiss-clack.* The No. 2 beam takes its stroke. The mist blows across my shaven head, warm and heavy as a wet towel dropped from a wash-tub. A five-second spat of rain hits the top of my ears—those thick, gristly folds that long ago grew over the holes to keep the dynamo-shriek from turning the brain into custard. I don't hear the water. I feel the slap of it.

Beside my right calf, something small and cool scurries through the shavings. A whip-tailed vole, pink-eyed and bald as an elbow, stops with its snout an inch from my ankle. It twitches its whiskers against the bark-cloth of my apron. It is wondering if I am meat yet.

I do not kick it. Kicking takes steam from the ribs, and my ribs are already crowded by the brass-rot. I simply let the flat of the draw-knife fall across its hindquarters with a dull *thwack*. It does not squeak—nothing down here wastes breath on screaming except the marmosets—it merely skitters away into the dark beneath the condenser pipes, where a huntsman the width of a wash-basin is waiting on the warm flange of the exhaust valve.

A boy would have killed it for the skin. I don't need skins. The bast-felt apron I wear was beaten out when my knees still bent; it has soaked up forty years of cylinder-wash and willow-resin until it is stiff as boiler-iron. It holds my shape even when I slouch. It is my shell, better than the iron plates of the boiler-ticks, because it does not crack when the counterweights drift an inch too close.

A twitch travels up through the floor. It is not the forty-beat. It is a sharp, telegraphic rattle: *tack-tack-tack... tap.*

Someone with a birch cudgel is hammering the cold-water main fifty yards east. 

I drop my left palm flat against the damp deck to catch the iron-marrow. The pulse comes clear through the wood: *The packing on Dynamo Nine is throwing fire.*

A young man's trouble. Let the boys with the split-cane tongs climb into the armature-pit to beat out the swifts. Let them choke on the blue smoke of the commutators. My hands stay on the horn grips of the draw-knife. My breath stays inside my barrel-ribs, wheezing out through my teeth in that short, rhythmic bark that matches the low-pressure pump’s exhaust: *huff... huff... huff.*

A second sucker has grown three lines since the midday rain. It is reaching toward the cold iron cheek of the pillow-block. I place the blade against its neck.

I do not cut it.

Not yet. I like the cool push of it against the steel. The tree is very strong; it pushed up through twenty feet of municipal limestone two hundred years ago, and it has not forgotten how to lift. It pushes against my blade with the slow, blind curiosity of an infant’s finger searching for a mother’s nipple. It is hungry for the oil that seeps from the trunnion. It is hungry for the salt in my skin.

Yesterday—or three rains ago, the time is greasy down here—I noticed that the dirt beneath my big toe had stopped being dirt. 

My toes have lived in this groove for so many shifts that the willow-bast has curled over the nails like new wax over a plum. When I try to lift the foot, there is a small, wet resistance, like pulling a boot out of river-silt. A web of white threads, fine as the down on a swift-chick’s throat, has laced itself through the calloused horn of my sole. They do not hurt. They are slightly warm. They have the salt-itch to them, the way a healing scald itches under the lard-dressing.

If I took the draw-knife and turned the blade downward, I could sever them with two strokes. A clean slice through the cambium, a handful of peat-dust rubbed into the yellow flesh to stop the weeping, and I could haul myself up by the hand-rail and walk sixty paces to the Oven-Beds to sleep among the hot ashes behind Boiler Four.

I look at the hand-rail. The iron is black, pitted, and running with wet condensation. The osier ladder leading up to the hammock-girth has five rungs broken; the willow has swallowed them, turning them into pale, fat knobs that would turn under a heavy foot. Up there, the air is eighty-five degrees and smells of other men’s sweat and the sour mash of swift-eggs. Up there, you must hold the ropes with your toes even while you dream, or the high-pressure relief valve will blow you out of your cradle into the teeth of the bevel gears.

Here, the air is seventy-four. The deck is soft.

A troop of marmosets comes tumbling along the high steam-line, grey and wet-slick, their long tails hooked around the lagging. They stop above me, chattering their high, silent shrieks, their black eyes blinking down through the red ember-glow of the boiler-grates. One of them drops an empty tick-shell; it bounces off the slope of my bast apron with a dry *clatter* and rolls into the oil-sump, where the leeches stir the dark water with their flat, red ribbons.

The sucker under my knife has grown another fraction. Its blunt head is touching the brass of the bearing-cap now. It trembles with every revolution of the shaft—*clack-hiss-clack*—drinking the hot vibration through its skin.

I lay the knife across my shins. 

The horn handles are cold now; my palms have been off them for twenty beats. My hands find my knees instead. The joints are the size of tea-kettles, swollen and smooth, yellow with the tallow-rot that takes every man who sits in the sump past his fiftieth year. They do not bend anymore, but they fit neatly into the hollow between the root-limbs. They look very much like the two black calluses on the dynamo’s rocker-arm—the ones the boys scrape for the lamp-wax every Tuesday morning.

The rain comes again. Sudden, loud, warm. It washes the gray midges from my forehead down into my beard. It smells of the Upper City—of horse-dung and wet chimneys washed down through three hundred yards of brick shafts—diluted by three acres of wet leaves and the black cylinder-oil that never stops falling from the rafters.

I lean my head back against the iron flank of the bearing-pedestal. 

The metal is forty strokes a minute. It hits the base of my skull like a heavy, familiar fist: *one-and, two-and, three.* My lungs bark their little puff of brass-steam into the dark, precisely on the off-beat, between the stroke of the piston and the lift of the exhaust valve. It is a good stroke. It has held the water out of the lower districts for two hundred years; it will hold it out tonight.

Beneath my heels, the white threads are tightening. They are no longer fine as down; they have found the meat behind the ankle-tendon, where the skin is thin and soft from forty years of grease-wash. They enter with a small, sharp sweetness, like the prick of a hot needle dipped in tallow.

I do not reach for the knife. 

The draw-knife is an Oiler’s tool, and Oilers have to run when the cudgels beat the water-mains. 

I am becoming something heavier. The No. 2 beam needs twenty gallons of paraffin an hour to keep its brasses from screaming, and the Upper City is stingy with its salt. The tree will give what the box does not bring down. It takes iron; it takes oil; it takes the barrel-ribs that have done with their coughing.

I close my eyes. Behind the thick lids, the red mica light from Boiler Four is only a dull, steady pulse, forty beats to the minute, forty beats to the rain, forty beats until the next Weight drops through the brick. 

The wood is cool against my shins. The iron is hot against my neck. Between the two of them, I find I am seated very firmly indeed.
