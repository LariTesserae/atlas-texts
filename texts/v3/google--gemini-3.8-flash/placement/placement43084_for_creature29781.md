At first, there is only the seam.

It is not an edge you can see, because sight down here is a smear of exhausted red and grease-haze, but an edge you measure with the skin of your flank. To the left, less than the length of a forefinger away, the rock face is forty degrees: an unyielding, basaltic cold that smells of crushed iron ore and wet cellar dirt, running with an unbroken film of seep-water that tastes of lime and sulfur. To the right, four inches out, the lagging of the expansion column radiates a dry, solid two hundred: an iron furnace that smells of scorched engine fat and ancient river-mud baked to brick.

Between them is the draft. 

It does not blow. It rises, slow and syrup-thick, displaced by the heavy cold sinking down the fracture and drawn upward by the thermal draft of the cylinder. It is ninety degrees in the channel. It smells of boiled rye, old sweat, neatsfoot oil, and the dry, sweetish decay of wet engine-waste.

I am in the waste.

I did not fall here, and I was not built here. I gathered. 

If you parted the tangled wads of uncarded cotton—the yellowed wiping-waste tossed down the vents after cleaning the crankpins of the surface mills—you would not find an animal with ribs or a clear throat. You would find something that has taken the shape of the sling. A flattened, pale knot of tallow-crust, river-silt, and fine brass swarf, bound together by a web of pale mycelium that feeds on the lard-oil soaked into the threads. 

I am soft where the bedding touches the oilcloth, where the sweat from his shoulder drains into the cotton. But where my edges spill over the rim of the sling toward the cold basalt, I am calcified: a brittle, scalloped rind like the scald-moss, pale as suet, drinking the lime from the seep-water before it can reach the hot iron and scream.

I wake when the accumulator drops. 

It is not a sound; the ears here were never made for air. It is a hydraulic shock that travels through the two steel pitons driven into the dynamite cracks above my head. The iron sings *thrum-chug*, the oilcloth hammock sways two inches to the left, and the sudden slam of air pressure compresses the cotton waste until every pocket of trapped vapor sighs. 

When the air pressure spikes, the man turns.

He does not know I am distinct from the bedding. To him, the sling is simply his nest—it has grown thick, heavy, and felted over the months, stiffened with boiler-grease and shedding a fine, powdery dandruff of dry sulfur and cotton-lint. When he curls his knees to his chin, his bare shoulder—blistered, yellowish, cross-hatched with old scald-scars that have healed into slick white gristle—presses directly into my softest margin.

His skin is very hot, but it is a wet, animal heat, different from the dead baking of the column. It carries the salt of the rye bread and the sour tang of the catarrh rattling in his bronchial pipes. 

I drink the salt.

My fibers—microscopic, white as the limp brake-ferns on the upper grates, threaded through the weave of the engine-waste—creep into the pores of his neatsfoot trousers where the oil has gone gummy. I do not bite; there is nothing to bite with. I only absorb what sloughs off: the gray flakes of dead skin stripped by the sulfur air, the hair that falls from his calves where the lead wire of his clogs chafes the bone, the gray scum that collects in the hollow of his collarbone while he sleeps.

When he rises, the sling lightens. The ash-wood clogs hit the rock floor with a dull, wooden *tock* that I feel through the basalt. 

He stands in the neutral zone, narrow as an eel, his chest heaving twice to clear the valve-scale from his throat before he steps out into the catwalk. As he turns sideways to squeeze between the second header and the wall, a draft of wild steam rolls back into the cleft. 

It hits my outer rind. The scald does not hurt; it is food. The sulfur vapor crystallizes instantly against my pale rosettes, dusting them with a fine, yellow frost. The lime in the droplet-mist cements the cotton threads deeper into the rock cracks, anchoring the nest so the next concussion from the foundry above cannot shake it loose.

While he is out on the iron, scraping the copper traps with his broken file, the cleft belongs to the slow things.

A flatworm, three inches of colorless lard, slides over the cold lip of the basalt, leaving a glistening thread of slime that smells of canal mud. It senses the moderate warmth of the sling and turns toward it, undulating its paper-thin margins. 

I wait for it. Not with jaws, but with the loose fibers of the waste. 

When it touches the cotton, the dry mineral grease and the fine filings of lathe-turnings snag its cilia. It thrashes once—a silent, wet ripple—and then the sulfur-crust on my outer margin begins to drink it down, dissolving the horn-shaving body into grease and nitrogen, pulling the river-ghost into the cotton to feed the moss-spores that keep the sling thick and warm against the basalt.

Later, the four beats come.

*Clang. Clang. Clang. Clang.*

The scrap pipe against the stone. The vibration runs down through the fracture like an electric needle, humming in the pitons, setting every strand of the greasy bedding to a faint, dry shimmer. 

The mills above are throttling. The deep vibration in the teeth begins to slacken, dropping from a hard, grinding drone to a low, shuddering idle. The hiss of the leaks softens; the air in the vault clears from white scald to a heavy, greasy gloom lit only by the carbon filaments.

He comes back sideways, dragging his ash clogs through the black graphite puddles. 

He is carrying something. In his hand, wrapped in grease-paper that has gone gray from his fingers, is the heel of the sour rye from the noon hamper. He does not eat it all. He sits on the ledge of the cleft, his back inches from the weeping cold stone, his horn-calloused hands resting on his knees, and chews slowly, his jaw moving with a mechanical, rhythmic patience.

A shower of heavy crumbs falls between his clogs, down into the wadded waste beneath the oilcloth.

They are cool and hard, tasting of dark grain, river-water, and the flour-dust of a world sixty feet above that has sun and horses. 

My fibers reach for them through the dark of the sling, winding around the rye-crusts, pulling them down into the grease, burying them beneath the lard-threads and the shed sulfur, sealing them into the body of the bed before the slugs can scent the starch.

He sighs—a wet, rattling whistle through his nose—and swings his legs up into the oilcloth. 

The hammock groans. The lead wire on his clogs clinks against the rock. He pulls the grease-rag over his face, his forehead resting against the basalt where the cold seep cools his temple, while his shoulder sinks deep into my yielding, tallow-soft center.

The vault enters its twenty-minute silence. 

The column behind us ticks as the iron contracts half an inch against its stone saddle: *tick... tink.* 

We rock together in the neutral zone, neither burning nor freezing, suspended between the steam that would strip his flesh and the stone that would take his heat, while beneath his ribs the thick lungs work like bellows, and beneath his spine, I knit the cotton tighter around his bones.
