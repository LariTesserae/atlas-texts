At first there is only the sensation of grain under the ribs—not against them, but running parallel to them, so that the rhythm of the heartwood’s swelling and the rhythm of the chest’s expansion are difficult to tell apart.

I am not in the graft-pockets where the families sit facing one another across inverted drops. There is too much domestic warmth there, too much salt from the bowls of wood-grub, too many fingers testing the hemp. Nor am I high on the single guy-lines where the spinners work their grey paste, though I know the taste of that wet fiber; the smell of it comes down the mist every time the upper sky vents its amber heat.

I am lower, where the light is already turning the color of deep river water, but not so low that the vinegar-steam of the mothers scorches the throat. 

I am lodged in a lap-joint.

A three-way meeting where an ash strut fifty spans across was cleft with hornbeam wedges nine centuries ago to admit the tongue of a creeping willow. The joint never quite took. It did not fail—if it had failed, the shelf four leagues above would have tilted into the void like a dropped leaf—but the wood of the willow had an oily, bitter tannin that the ash refused to drink. A gap remains. It is five feet high at its widest, tapering to a hairline seam where the two timbers grind together with a sound like wet slate being ground against leather.

Inside this pocket, gravity does not know which trunk to favor. 

If I shift my weight toward the ash, my heels find purchase on the furrowed bark and the world pulls gently toward the northeast, toward a swollen knot the size of a hill that anchors the third tier. If I roll toward the willow, my shoulder becomes my foot; the pull swings sixty degrees to the south, soft and slow, like the drag of deep water against a trailing hand.

For a long time I thought I was a splicer who had stayed too long at an inspection. There are bone scrapers tied to my waist with two-ply bast, and a pouch of dry lichen that has long since turned to dust. There are the scars on my shins where the adze slipped during some forgotten tightening. My big toes have the thick, thumb-like curve of those who swing through the empty leagues without a lanyard; the nails are worn down to horn nubs from gripping the damp cambium.

Yet I do not go back up.

When the vibrations come through the wood—the tap-tap-tap of a fossilized mallet ten miles off, asking after the tension of the lateral braces—I feel the strike in the root of my molars, but I do not reach for my own hammer to answer. I press my forehead into the cleft where the sap gathers and I listen to how the blow decays. 

The splicers above listen for the note: whether it is true, whether it is high, whether the rope is about to part. They hear the timber as a road or a roof. 

I hear what the wood does to the sound after the note is gone. 

In the gap where the ash and the willow refuse to graft, the vibration does not travel in a straight beam. It enters the open air of the pocket, turns three times in the non-Euclidean twist where the timbers shear, and doubles back on itself. A single mallet-strike becomes a low, continuous murmur that lasts for four hours, circulating around the rim of the cleft like a bee inside a pitcher-plant. It rubs against the damp walls until it softens into heat. 

That heat is what I live on.

My skin has stopped shedding. The humidity is so constant, the ambient sap so rich with the tallow of drowned scutes and the ferment of ancient leaf-mold, that the boundary between where the hide ends and the bark begins has grown uncertain. Where my calves rest against the willow, fine, pale filaments—not hair, but something closer to the mycelial threads that line the scute-beds—have crept out from the pores. They do not hurt. When the world takes its great hydraulic breath, lifting three fathoms on the surge of afternoon sap, the threads tighten. They draw a pale, sweet liquor from the cambium straight into my veins, tasting of copper and green malt.

Perhaps I am a Tensioner who forgot the stones. 

The elders at the spatial faults cast their pebbles and count the minutes until the loop returns them from behind. But here, the loop does not return anything solid. If I spit into the dark angle where the two trunks cross, the drop does not fall, nor does it swing around my shoulder. It hangs. It remains suspended three inches from my face, a trembling, pearlescent bead of water that catches the green-gray glow of the abyss. Over days, it grows, fed by my breath and the slow weeping of the ash, until it is as large as a melon. 

Through its curve, I can see the lower tiers. 

They are so far down they seem not to be made of wood at all, but of green smoke arranged in parallel ribs. Occasionally, across that vast, luminous nothing, a planum drifts through the lens of the droplet—a smudge of milk-white skin undulating with seven rhythmic heartbeats, its skirts trailing through the vapor. It looks no larger than a grain of meal, yet through the curvature of the hanging water, I can see the capillaries pulsing in its margins.

I realize I am not maintaining the joint. 

The joint is maintaining the fold.

If the splicers above knew this seam had not closed, they would come down with their sledges and their wedges of dry ash. They would drive the wood home until the fibers crushed together and the resin flowed to seal the breach. They would lay three corpses in the trench to rot down into living glue, and the gap would vanish into a smooth, scarred knuckle of continuous heartwood.

And if they did that, the triangle would straighten. The pocket would collapse. The five leagues of empty, leaning air that depend upon this crooked hinge would flatten out like a tightened sheet, snapping the guy-lines across twelve horizons.

They think the world is held together by its joinery. They think it is the tightness of the lashings, the ancient tongue-and-groove craft of the ancestors, the endless vigilance of the ash-wedges that keeps the sky from dropping into the green floor.

They do not know that the world needs its unhealed places. 

It needs the joints that were botched in the dawn of the work—the timbers that met at an impossible slant and could neither agree nor pull apart, creating pockets of soft, sideways gravity where the air can breathe without falling. It needs the gaps where the craft failed, where the wood was too stubborn to graft, leaving a little room for the void to pool like rainwater in a hollow stump.

I am the thing that sits in the refusal.

My hands are still human, but when I stretch my fingers into the hairline crack between the trunks, I no longer feel the coldness of wood as an object outside myself. I feel the massive, slow-motion agony of the ash as it tries to shear past the willow, the millions of pounds of lateral thrust held back by a quarter-inch of friction. 

I reach into my pouch, find a pinch of the old, dry lichen from the upper shelves, and pack it gently into the shear-line. Not to heal it. Not to join them. 

Just enough to keep the two giants from touching, so that the space between them can go on dreaming its quiet, tilted afternoon for another thousand years.
