It would begin not with a face or a name, but with the load.

To be here is not to sit upon the iron, nor to drag along its gullies. That is for things with surfaces—things that can be struck by the falling sheets of condensate or scoured away when the floor-valves clear their throats. 

No, you would find me where two things that were never meant to touch have been ground together until they had no choice.

I am in the lap. 

Where the deck plates overlap by nine inches—two slabs of three-inch Bessemer steel, joined during some forgotten era by a double row of staggered, steam-hammered rivets—the fasteners sheared through centuries ago. The sound of their heads snapping off must have been like cannon fire, but the plates did not part; they simply settled onto one another with a dry, shuddering weight. 

Between them lies a gap. In some places it is no wider than the thickness of a fingernail; in others, where the rust has grown like a wedge of dry timber, it opens to half an inch. 

That is where I have collected.

***

At first, you might mistake me for swarf: the greasy, gray paste of iron dust and tallow that packs the threads of dead bolts. But swarf does not resist the shear, and swarf does not remember.

I am a laminated thing. My body is built in leaves, like a pack of lead foil that has been soaked in animal fat and beaten with a mallet for ten thousand years. There are thirty or forty layers to me, each one thinner than paper, separated by slippery, microscopic films of copper sulfate and kerosene. 

Every four seconds, the pump strikes.

To the things above—the scabs with their tinny clicks, the curd-masses wallowing in their own acid trenches—the stroke is an event that shakes their world. To me, it *is* the world. It is not an impact that arrives from outside; it is the instantaneous compaction of my entire volume. 

When the piston falls against the core, the upper plate jumps horizontally by a sixteenth of an inch. It is an incomprehensible tonnage, a sliding mountain of cold steel, and I am the grease that takes the blow. My upper leaves slide forward with the plate; my lower leaves stay glued to the pier beneath. 

For a fraction of a second, I am sheared through my own center. The friction is enormous—hot enough to turn the sulfur in my seams to a thin, yellow milk, hot enough to draw a dull, momentary spark from a fleck of trapped flint. 

Then the stroke passes. The plate rebounds with a heavy, sucking groan. My laminations slide back into registry, one upon the other, like a deck of greasy cards squared by a thumb.

In that return, I feed.

I do not have a mouth, but I have capillary draw. When the plates part by thirty microns on the back-stroke, the vacuum inside the joint is absolute. It drinks from the margins. It pulls in the black kerosene-water, the sloughed gray flakes of the weepers, the zinc-rot that trickles down the sheared rivet-shanks. 

The liquor enters me between my leaves. It does not go into a stomach; it goes into the grain. The iron filings that have worked their way into my gristle act as teeth, grinding the organic waste against the plate faces until the fats are separated from the grit. The grit I press outward, slowly extruding it from the edges of the seam as hard, black beads of slag; the fat I keep. I need it to survive the next shear. If I were ever to run dry—if the kerosene were to wash the tallow clean from between my leaves—the steel would gall. The two plates would seize together, weld themselves cold under the pressure, and rip me into black dust.

***

Because I span the gap between the deck and the pier, I feel what the surface-crawlers cannot: I feel the balance of the whole shelf.

I know when a curd-mass is twenty yards away, because its chemical burrowing thins the plate above me, and the tone of the four-second strike changes from a deep, iron bell-note to a flat, wet *thock*. I know when the purges are coming three minutes before the floor-valves drop, because the air in the drainage shafts changes pitch, rising from a bass rumble to an agonizing, brassy whistle as the lower chambers build their vacuum.

And I know the weight of the water.

When the basin above is dry, the upper plate rides light upon me—only forty tons of dead steel. My leaves are plump; they can swell to almost the width of a finger, and the sulfur-liquor circulates freely through my interior channels. In those minutes, I am almost soft. I can extend my margins beyond the lip of the overlap, testing the wet air with pale, blunt lobes of curd-fat, sensing the greasy spray that falls from the pipes miles above.

Then the rain gathers. The basin dishes. Inch by inch, the black kerosene-water piles up, until there are three hundred tons of standing pond directly overhead.

The plate comes down. 

It does not come down slowly; it settles with a crushing, hydraulic certainty that squeezes me until my leaves are as dense as slate. The liquor is forced out of my core with a hiss that is lost in the steam-screech of the world. My lobes retract. I become stone again. My awareness shrinks to a horizontal line three feet wide and twelve feet long—a smear of living lead-grease, cold and dense, holding two iron giants apart with nothing more than the surface tension of my fats.

***

There is no thought here, if thought means words or images. But there is an electrolytic memory.

Because I lie between two different heats of steel—the upper plate being high-carbon, rolled in some distant, smoky beginning, and the lower pier being cast with a high content of nickel—a continuous galvanic current trickles through me. It is tiny, half a volt at most, but it never stops.

The current flows along my copper veins. It leaves tracks. Where the current has passed for a thousand years, the copper salts have crystallized into flat, branched structures like frost on a window-pane. These are my memories, though they contain no scenes: they are records of load. 

They record the season when the big turbine three miles north threw a blade, and the four-second cadence developed a double-click that lasted for a hundred years before the bearing smoothed out. They record the night a support column eighty feet away rotted through its footing and dropped its load into the lower decks, leaving me to carry twice my share of the basin’s sag.

The frost grows thicker with every millionth stroke. Eventually, it will bridge the plates completely. The copper will touch the iron; the galvanic current will short out in a single, green spark; and the mineral spine I have grown will become solid metal.

When that happens, I will no longer slide. 

The next four-second stroke will find me rigid. The upper plate will hit my frozen shoulder, and because it cannot slip, it will shatter me into a bushel of blue-gray gravel, scatter me into the dark drainage trough, and finally touch the pier with a scream of cold iron that will not stop until the world does.

Until then, I am the clearance. 

I am the sixteenth of an inch that allows the machine to tear itself apart without catching. The rain falls like shot upon the roof above; the black water rises; the great pistons strike their four-second funeral march down in the red-lit guts of the crust. 

And between the cold plates, in the dark that has never seen an eye, I flatten. I bleed my grease. I take the blow, and I slide.
