At first, the sense is only of weight and moisture—not the sensation of falling into water, but of having always been soaked through, like an old wool sleeve left out in the grass until its threads have forgotten where they were spun. 

There is no reason to look up. The sky is only a white dilution of the steam, and nothing will ever drop from it that weighs more than a raindrop. So the attention stays downward, spread over several square yards of slick, slate-gray floor.

I would not be the thin things, though their lives have an enviable economy. To be a leaf of boiled tripe, tasting the iron in the rock with the whole surface of one’s belly, is too close to being the rock itself; there is no space between the hunger and the floor, no gap where a state of being can turn around and notice itself. Nor could I be the white gristle in the galleries below the moss. That cold, branching marrow is too industrious, too caught up in its subterranean masonry, turning the drowned ribs of ferns into sheets of grey flint. It works as an archive works—tireless, blind, and indifferent to its own holdings.

No, I would find myself higher up, on one of the wide, low swells of basalt where the film of water is only deep enough to reach the knuckles—if there were knuckles. 

I am one of the gray bulks. 

It takes a long time to know this, because there are so few messages sent from one end of the frame to the other. A sensation at the flank—the cool touch of a drifting seed-sac, perhaps the size of a pigeon’s egg, bumping against the hide—takes twenty minutes to travel inward to where the blood moves. The blood does not race; it is as warm and slack as the pool it wades in, driven by a heart that contracts not out of terror or desire, but from the simple, mechanical fatigue of having stayed open for too long. 

*Thump.* 

Then the silence comes back, filled with the minute *chink* of mineral water trickling over my shoulder. The water splits around my neck, parting into two quiet braids that run down the leather of my sides, carrying away the chalky crumbs of lime that my skin continuously sheds into the pool. 

I am not young, but here the word has no leverage. My back has collected enough fine sediment in its folds that a small colony of liverworts has taken hold between two dorsal wrinkles, drinking my heat and the daylight in equal measure. I can feel them only as a faint, velvety drag when I shift, which happens once every three or four days, when the stone beneath my chest has surrendered all its warmth to my gut and I must slide forward six inches to find a fresh seam in the rock.

The movement is an embarrassment of effort. It requires the great leather apron of the belly to gather itself into a single wave, lifting forty stones of waterlogged meat and gristle against the suction of the wet slate. It makes a sound like a wet boot being pulled from deep mud: *sclup*. Then the stillness settles again, instantly healing the tear in the water-skin.

The seed-sacs are good. They taste of nothing except cold oil and river silt, but they coat the horn combs of my mouth with a grease that keeps the volcanic sulfur from burning the throat. When three or four of them drift into the underslung pouch of my lower lip, I do not snap. I simply wait until their hulls soften in the warm froth of my breath, then let them slide down the dark gullet toward the stones that tumble slowly in my crop.

And yet, despite the perfection of the circuit—the stone feeding the fern, the fern dropping the seed, the seed feeding the bulk, the bulk weeping lime back into the stone—there is a place where the circle snags.

It is the cleft near the southern lip. 

I have spent forty years traveling toward it. Not with urgency—I have spent whole seasons stranded on a single shelf of basalt, watching the pale vellum-things graze toward me and drift away—but with a steady, hydraulic bias. The current of the basin seeps in that direction, toward the low lip where the water finally slips over the edge to vaporize into the white gulf below; but it is more than the current. 

It is the smell of the black wood.

The living plants here have no scent beyond the bitter, metallic tang of their condensation. But the haft of the adze, wedged into that dark tooth of basalt, smells of another place. It smells of resin that was heated over an open flame five hundred years ago. It smells of dry smoke, of earth that was once allowed to become completely parched, of the oil from fingers that had joints, that flexed and gripped and let go. 

I am pressed against it now. 

My left flank fills the cleft, wedged so tightly against the living rock that the film of runoff must go around us both. My hide, with its rough, pimpled tubercles, has molded itself around the plaited bark cordage that lashes the adze-head to the wood. I can feel the regular, crossed ridges of the weave pressed deep into my fat. 

The stone of the adze is dolerite, very dense, very cold at its core, but where it touches my ribs it has begun to take my temperature. It no longer feels like an intrusion; it feels like an exceptionally hard rib that has grown outside my own skin.

Sometimes, in the long hour between the daylight fading and the night-mist settling its thicker wool over the pool, my spiracles will open with a faint, rubbery hiss. The chest expands three inches. The bark cordage groans softly against my skin. 

In that forty-minute breath, I draw in the steam from the rock, the smell of the copper fissures, and the faintest, dying vapor of the human sweat that soaked into the oily heartwood before the mountain was quiet. The heart beats once against the adze. The adze does not move. It is gripped by the basalt, gripped by the mineral crust, and gripped by my own slow, cooling side.

Between us, we are making something that cannot be undone. The lime from my skin is filling the gaps in the plaiting; the silica in the water is turning my outer skin to slate. In another century, my breath will lengthen until it forgets to return. The liverworts will march across the saddle of my spine and meet the moss on the other side. 

There will be no skeleton to find. There will only be a rounded shoulder of dark basalt, slightly higher than the others, with a soot-black haft protruding from its flank like a splinter that the mountain has swallowed, digested, and decided to keep.
