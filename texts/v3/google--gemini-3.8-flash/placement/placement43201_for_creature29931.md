At first, there is no *me* to locate, because there is no edge where the world stops and a body begins. 

There is only the pressure. 

It pushes inward against every surface with an unbroken, fever-warm insistence—a hydrostatic cradle that leaves no pocket unfilled. The air is so laden with suspended water that breathing does not feel like drawing in a foreign gas; it feels like circulating the room through an interior sponge and sending it back out, slightly warmed, slightly salted.

And there is the hum. 

It does not enter through the ears, because there are no ears yet, only bone and tissue capable of sympathy. A twelve-cycle oscillation vibrates in the base of the throat, in the cartilaginous arches of the ribs, in the tiny calcium grains drifting in the fluid of the skull. It is impossible to tell whether the mountain is beating or if my own marrow is idling at this exact, ancient frequency. We share a timing gear.

For a long time, this is enough. To be an eddy in the warm mist. A point of slight resistance in the convective updraft that lifts off the deep, hot basins. 

Then comes the distinction between cold and warm. 

It begins on what must be a skin, though it does not feel like leather or fur. It feels porous, naked, perpetually washed. On one side of this plane, there is an abrasive roughness: damp, fibrous peat, spongy bryophytes saturated to bursting, the microscopic claw-holds of roots that have spent ten thousand years chewing granite into silt. It smells of broken leaves, elder-mud, and the faint, sweet rot of dead cilia drifting down from above.

On the other side, less than an inch away, there is an absolute, terrifying slickness. 

A surface so flat, so dense, that water cannot find a posture upon it. I feel the condensation leap away in rhythmic, frantic spasms—droplets forming, losing their molecular grip to the machine’s sheer indifference, and flicking out into the open void. Touching it is like resting against frozen oil, yet it radiates a steady, alkaline heat that tastes faintly of old copper and ozone. 

I am not drifting. I am anchored here, precisely at the border. 

My body gathers around this line of tension. It organizes itself along a spine—long, flexible, segmented like a chain of wet knuckles. I am low to the surface, flattened against the mountain’s rib. There are no eyes to press shut against the dark; there is only a forehead that functions as a sounding board, broad and slightly convex, tuned to the weight of the air.

I test my limbs. They are four, set wide, terminating not in claws—which would skitter uselessly against the alloy—but in spatulate discs that soften and cup against the curvature. As I draw one back, there is a wet, deliberate resistance, followed by a soft, hollow release: *schlock*. Millions of microscopic vacuum pores disengaging from the frictionless metal, then seating again into the wet moss of the rock.

A draft shifts. Far below, sixty meters down in the black, something immense has turned or settled. The air compresses slightly—a wave of barometric lift that travels up the vertical cliff like a thick, wet hand. 

In response, the crown around my neck wakes up.

They do not open like petals; they unfurl like soaked ferns, feathering outward into the updraft. These are my gills, but they are thirsty for more than oxygen. They are an array of tongues. 

As the draft washes through them, the hollow resolves into a dense, sensory ledger:
There, three hundred yards out in the open middle, is the greasy, lard-rich musk of a Plume-Hide carving a slow turn through the mist, its baleen sieve whistling as it scoops up the water-mites.
There, dangling in the quiet dead-space near the ceiling vault, is the sour, mineral rasp of the Cable-Weavers, chewing the blind velvet until their silicate tongues leave lines of bare fibrous vine.
And below—always below—the heavy, sweet, iron-thick exhalation of the Sump-Pads, digesting a fallen flyer, rendering its bones into liquid salts with a silent, cellular patience that makes my own stomach turn in faint sympathy.

I am not the flyer. I do not have the madness of hollow bones, tumbling through thirty thousand cubic meters of unmapped black air, trusting only to clicks and oily feathers. 

Nor am I the carpet at the bottom, blind and vast and waiting for the rain to drop its food.

I am a Seam-Clinger. I am the surveyor of the border.

My purpose is not thought, but measurement. My mind is a taut string stretched between the granite and the machine. I feel the pylon expand by a micron as subterranean power shunts through its core; I feel the stone contract as a cold drip from the summit seeps into a deep fissure. I exist to know the distance between the two.

A sudden tremor runs through my spatulate palms. The 12-cycle hum skips half a beat—a rare, microscopic stutter in the ancient technology that has held this chamber pressurized since the world outside forgot how to be warm.

The air thickens. The condensation falls faster, the *tock-tock-tock* of the drops striking the pads below doubling its tempo into a low, wooden applause.

A pressure builds in my thoracic cage. It is not fear; it is the absolute necessity of registration. The vault must be checked. The curvature must be sung.

I draw the wet, hot air deep into the resonant sacs along my flanks. My ribs widen, lifting me off the frictionless alloy until only my suction-pores hold me fast to the slick vertical pitch. 

I open the blunt slot of my mouth. 

The bark is not a cry; it is an impact. A dry, percussive crack that breaks off my chest and slams into the blackness like a dropped hammer. 

*CLACK.*

I close my mouth and lay my throat instantly flat against the stone, spreading my gill-ferns wide to catch the return.

The sound goes out, spherical and violent. 
It strikes the broad, wet leaves of the hanging creepers fifteen yards away—a soft, muffled bounce. 
It races across the central clearance, clipping the oily belly of a passing Plume-Hide, which twitches its wing in mid-stroke. 
It hits the opposing wall—eighty meters of smooth, weeping pylon—and rings back with a sharp, metallic ping that tastes of copper. 
Then, three seconds later, the high vault returns it: a softened, curved decay that tells me the ceiling has not moved, that the great bell of the mountain is still whole, that the pressure is holding.

From two miles away, across the black gulf, another bark answers. Then another, slightly lower in pitch, from the deep roots near the sump. 

*Clack.* 
*Tock.* 
*Clack.*

The chorus passes through us, mapping the hollow from throat to stone to air to gill, until the space is entirely rebuilt in our marrow. The mountain is intact. The machine is idling. The black is full.

I settle my belly back against the seam. 

To my left, the velvet moss is cool and wet, offering the small, slow bodies of crawlers that I can sweep into my mouth with a blunt tongue. To my right, the alloy pulses, hums, and repels the water in tiny, spitting arcs, warming my ribs with the heat of an engine that has forgotten its makers.

I close my lung-ferns against my neck, feeling the microscopic vacuum-pores seal me to the rock, one by one. 

I do not wish for eyes. To have eyes would be to imagine an end to things, to draw a line where the dark becomes something else. Here, there is no end. There is only the wet weight of the air, the steady drum of the sump, and the twelve-cycle hum that will rock me into the slow, dreamless sleep of the mountain’s skull.
