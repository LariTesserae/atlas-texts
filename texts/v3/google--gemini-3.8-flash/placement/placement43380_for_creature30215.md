Not in the open water. To be out in the churn without armor or anchor is simply to be converted into mineral flour, shredded against a bastion before any thought can take shape.

And not down in the tightest seams, either—not crushed twenty-deep beneath the carapaces of the wedge-crabs, where existence is reduced to the single, thrumming reflex of closing one’s shell against the hydraulic ram until the calcite fuses with the granite.

Where I find myself is in the slack-water behind a broken riser—one of the cyclopean steps that once led up to an esplanade, now battered into the shape of a kneeling ox. 

Here, the shear-current splits. A violent plume of water curls upward off the stone lip, creating an eddy behind it that doesn’t rip, but turns slowly, like a heavy wheel of tallow. The water in this pocket is thick with the ground-down dust of the world—gray, milky, bitterly cold, smelling of crushed feldspar and ancient brine. 

At first, I don’t know what I am made of. There is no warmth in me, which is proper; warmth here would be a waste, an open wound through which the sea would bleed your strength in seconds. My body feels long and low to the stone, pressed down by the immense column of water overhead until my underside conforms to the slight, scalloped hollows left by forgotten chisels. 

I am not swimming. Swimming is for the ribbons, frantic and thin-ribbed, vibrating themselves to death in the open light. I am clinging. My weight is enormous, but it isn’t the dead, rolling mass of the armored scavengers. There are points of articulation along my sides—short, jointed spurs of dark, horn-capped cartilage that find the micro-fissures in the granite and bite in. When the current pulses, I do not resist it with muscle; I lock the spurs into the stone, letting the sheer tensile strength of the joints take the strain until my whole frame groans like a dry timbership in a gale.

I have no eyes, yet the world is not dark. It is loud—not in the ear, for I have none, but along the dense keel of bone that runs from my snout to the root of my tail. 

Every feature of the plateau rings like a struck bell:
To the left, a dull, heavy *thrum-thrum-thrum* where a braided hawser of tube-worms whips against the flank of a trench. 
Directly above, the sharp, needle-like hiss of cavitation bubbles tearing themselves open against the rounded brow of the riser—a sound like grease hitting a hot plate, continuous and cold.
And far out across the plaza, the rolling thunder of loose ashlar blocks being kicked along the submerged avenues, tumbling into each other with hollow, subterranean impacts that shake the marrow of the stone beneath me.

I am not waiting for prey. The water brings everything.

From the margins of my head—which is broad and wedge-shaped, like the head of a cold chisel—I extend narrow, flexible filaments into the edge of the spinning eddy. They do not snatch; they drift, tasting the drift-wash. There is the salt-bitter trace of cracked crab carapace; the faint, oily slick of a shredded swimmer; the cold, dead iron of the deep upwelling. 

When a chunk of something dense enough to sink tumbles through the slack-water—a knot of sheared worm-cable, coated in tallow-fat—I don't dart out. I haul myself forward an inch at a time, ratcheting my lateral spurs along the rock, dragging my gray, mineralized belly across the stone with a dull *skratch*. My mouth is beneath me, a tough, transverse slot lined with plates of self-sharpening horn. I pin the tallow-knot against the granite and shear it flat, swallowing it along with a mouthful of coarse quartz grit. The grit stays in my gut, tumbling there, grinding the meat into paste.

Yet I am not just an animal of the bottom. There is something else in the structure of my limbs, something redundant and strange that the current hasn't entirely ground away.

Behind the heavy gripping spurs, tucked into protected pockets along my flanks, are shorter, thinner appendages. They have no claws. They are tipped with wide, spatulate pads, leathery and sensitive to the micron. 

As the eddy shifts and the current momentarily pins me dead-flat against the step, one of these smaller limbs creeps out from beneath my flank. It doesn't seek food. It reaches back into the narrow angle where the riser meets the tread—the joint line, packed with gray silt and the crushed fragments of ribbon-eggs.

The spatulate tip slides into the gap. It begins to stroke the stone.

*Scritch. Scritch.*

It clears the fine sand from the seam. It feels along the hidden margin of the megalith, tracing the joint back toward the dark heart of the platform. And there, deep in the crevice where the scouring wash has never reached, the tip of my limb finds something that sends a sharp, vibrating thrill through my entire spine:

An edge.

Not the bulbous, dissolved tallow-curve of the outer rock, but an actual ninety-degree corner. A line so sharp it could cut rawhide, leveled and squared by some hand that moved before this water was ever lifted into the sky.

My body goes rigid against the current. My palps retract. The spurs lock into the stone with an involuntary, mechanical clack.

I stay there for hours—perhaps days, for time in the cold has no seam—holding that hidden corner with my smallest limb. In the roaring madness of the shear-winds overhead, where schools of blind ribbons shred each other in the gray pewter dusk and the twenty-foot rollers tumble toward the abyss, I do not move. 

I am a thing made of cartilage and cold iron, an armored leech that has outlived the reason for its own shape. But as the immense weight of the drowned world hums through the rock, my limb continues its slow, rhythmic stroke along the concealed line, clearing the silt, keeping the seam true, testing whether the mountain still holds.
