At first, I would not be a person at all, or not one with an edge where the body stops and the terrace begins. 

I would be the dull ache in the heel where the calcium has begun to knit into the marrow. 

You would find me low down—not in the upper daylight of the roof-carpet, where the blind moths drop into the squabbling beaks of the drakes, and not out in the boiling troughs where the hairless dogs go paddling after grubs. I would be wedged into the crook of a tier that was split open three tides ago: a narrow, sweating pocket where the lime is still the pale pink of scalded lard, sighing out thin, vinegar-scented plumes from a vent that refuses to die.

My knees are drawn up against my chest. They do not bend well anymore. The mineral gets into the synovial grease after forty or fifty blister-cuttings; the knees turn to chalk, the elbows click like adze-heads, and the flesh at the nape of the neck thickens into a grey, hairless hump that smells faintly of river-dog tallow. 

I am an old finisher, or perhaps simply what an adze-man becomes when his wrists can no longer take the recoil of river-slate against fresh stone. 

Before me, stuck upright in the soft floor like a marker, is an adze-handle cut from the rib of a bull-dog, its broad blade bound with the dried gut of an eel. But I do not pick it up. My hands are filled with something softer: a wooden-thick bowl of tallow mixed with pulverized bone-ash and river-silt. 

Around me, the noise does not reach my head as sound. It reaches me as a shiver through the travertine. 

Three terraces up, the boys are breaking a fresh blister. I feel it through the meat of my buttocks: *thud, thud, split.* The rock there is young—soft as turnip—and their slate edges are biting cleanly. Farther off, a mile into the pewter fog, there is a rhythmic, shuddering thrum that never dies; it is the migration of the flightless drakes shifting their roosts, millions of webbed pads slapping the damp mud roofs in a wet, heavy surf. 

My work is not the breaking. The young men break; they shout from their bellies, their greasy skate-grey chests heaving, steam spraying from their mouths into the pewter air as they rip the hot rind away before it crystallizes. 

My work is to sit in the raw wound while the heat is still coming out.

I take a handful of the bone-paste. It is warm—everything is warm, always, the warmth of an animal’s inner groin—and I drive it into the hair-fractures with the heels of my thumbs. If you leave the cracks open, the steam will eat the lime-wash from the inside out within three sleeps; the roof above will grow spongy and sag down into the stew-pot. So I follow the fissure. I follow it with the blunt pads of my fingers, which have lost their prints to the alkaline brine decades ago, smoothed down into round, grey pegs of horn. 

A river-dog is wedged into the niche beside me. Its white, rubbery flank heaves against my calf, its ear-valves pulsing shut and open with each wet snort. It is swollen with grease, stupid with the continuous heat. When my shoulder stiffens, I rest the weight of my arm upon its neck; it does not move, only groans softly from the wet meat in its throat, its dewlaps pooling like spilled curd over my bare feet. We do not look at each other. There is no reason to look. The light does not change; the fog neither rises nor falls. The world is this niche, the dog’s white flank, and the steam that smells of sour soup.

In my lap lies an eel-skin pouch containing three long pegs. They are human femurs, boiled clean, split lengthwise, and scraped with slate until the edges are true. One of them belonged to an adze-man who worked the terrace below this one when I was still agile enough to carry a hide-basket of river-clay. I know the bone by the crook in the lesser trochanter—he had taken a bad slip on an unworked step when the crust was thin, and the calcium had healed in a thick, knotty sleeve. 

Now he is straight. He is dry. He has been through the flues until the last of the salt-grease was baked from his pores. 

I reach into the soft, unformed corner of the cell, where the new stone meets the floor of the dome above, and I drive him home with three short strokes of a slate hammer. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

He enters the soft lime without splintering. The mineral seizes him immediately; in two days, the crust will have drunk his lime into its own, and the roof above will not shift, even when the drakes squabble across it fifty strong. 

I do not think of him as dead. To think of *dead* requires that there be a place where things go to rot and be forgotten under grass, and here there is no under. There is only the floor pushing up, and the meat living upon it, and the bone holding the two together. He has simply stopped barking across the alleys and has become an anchor for a clay ramp where the children run with their scorpions.

A child comes now, crawling on belly and palms through the low oval of the hatchway. 

She does not speak—the din outside is too wide, too thick for voices in a low cellar. Instead, she brings the flat of her hand down against the newly pasted lime: *slap-slap, pause, slap.* 

The vibration travels through the stone, through the dog’s body, into my own hip. 

*More grease?* the hand asks through the stone. 

I press my knuckles into the floor three times. 

*Bring the oil of the gut-pots,* I tell her through the mineral. *The vent is dry.*

She leaves a smear of grey bird-fat on the sill as she backs out into the alleys. She does not look at me, and I do not look at her face. I look at her heels—thick, wide-padded, yellow with sulfur crust at the edges, built for this rock, built to grip the greasy slope without skidding into the sea-trench. 

When she is gone, I lean my forehead against the wall I have just caulked. 

The stone is alive. It is not stone such as other worlds might know—cold, ancient, patient things that outlast the hand that touches them. This rock is only three days out of the deep. It is soft, hot, irritable; it groans as the shelf below expands. I can feel the blister six cubits down gathering its breath, the mineral water hissing through the capillary lime, looking for a way out through my knees, through the floor, through the dog's belly. 

In an hour, they will break it. The adzes will clatter against the white crust, the drakes will screech from the domes, the blind moths will whirl through the warm steam, and someone will come to drag me into the next hollow before the rock seizes hard as flint. 

I am the thing that sits between the stone while it is still meat and the stone when it becomes a house. 

I wipe my hands on the dog's flank, take up another peg of bone, and wait for the floor to rise.
