I would not be in the air. To hang out over the cloud-floor with locked shoulders, straining plankton from a gale that never drops below two hundred knots—that is for creatures made entirely of horn and hollow pipe. Nor would I be in the water-cups, living out a million generations across nine inches of still quartz, waiting for a shearman’s tongue to blot out the sky.

I would be in the flute. 

Not climbing it, either. Not with the frantic, claw-tip urgency of the young Stitchers who go up the pegmatite veins driving horn pins into the seams. 

I would be the trailing half of the cord. The ballast.

When two go up a vertical groove on the lee-flank—where the jet doesn't hit flat, but shears off the prow in massive, curling rips that sound like iron plates tearing in half—the lead-climber is light. He has the narrow lungs, the narrow wrists, the horn-tipped adzes. He moves like a spider on a cold window. 

The second climber is different. The second climber is the anchor. 

I would find myself sitting inside a three-foot-wide vertical channel, three thousand feet above the white floor, with my knees drawn up against my chest and the soles of my boots—thick slabs of scaph-leather, hair-side out to catch the micro-granulation of the stone—wedged across the mouth of the groove. My spine would be rounded into the hollow, my back pressed so hard against the curved granite that every flute-ridge fits into the spacing of my ribs. 

I am thirty years into this rock, which means I no longer have an ordinary throat. It has thickened; the muscles under the jaw are knotty and hard as walnut from keeping the mouth clamped against the wind. My tongue is a dry, slick wedge, dark purple from the scaph-tallow I rub into it every morning before the zenith turns indigo. You do not drink with an open mouth here; if you open your mouth, the draft off the lip can strip the lining off your cheeks before you can swallow. You cup a mouthful of water from a pebble-hole into a dried bladder, kneel in the baffled draft, and let it leak down your gullet one drop at a time under your tongue.

Between my thighs runs the braided gut. 

It is thirty fathoms long, greased with mineral wax so the dry wind doesn't fray it to thistle-down. It runs up the channel, snapping and humming against the rock, disappearing around a bulge where my partner—a boy with three fingers on his left hand and no name I have ever spoken aloud—is working a horn wedge into an ancient frost-spall.

I do not look up at him. If you tilt your head back in the groove, the shear will catch the lip of your leather hood and snap your neck like dry kindling. You keep your chin tucked into your collarbone. You look at the rock between your shins.

You look at the grain: white feldspar, grey quartz, flakes of black biotite that shine like wet beetles when the sun hits the lip of the flute at noon. 

And you hold the cord.

You don't hold it with your fingers. Your hands would freeze to the horn adzes if you kept them clenched. You hold it with the base of your spine and the crook of your elbow, looped once through a toggle of ibex horn jammed crosswise behind your hips. If the boy slips four hundred feet above me, there will be no shout. The wind eats sound before it clears the lips. There will only be a sudden, violent pluck on the gut—a blow like an axe-strike against my hip-toggle.

When that pluck comes, I don’t think. I have no time to think. My body has learned the groove the way water learns a fracture. I straighten my legs. The soles of my boots cam against the flute walls; my knees lock into an arch; my shoulder blades spread flat, wedging my chest into the stone until the ribs creak. The impact will try to rip me out of the channel and fling me into the slip-vortex, where the birds tumble like scraps of paper. 

My job is simply to be heavy. To be a stone that breathes. To let his falling weight drag me three inches down the granite until the toggle binds into a narrow pinch, and there we hang—two bodies suspended on forty yards of sinew, vibrating in the roaring throat of the chimney until he can find a toe-purchase on an orange moss-crust and take his weight off my waist.

We speak through the cord. A double twitch means *the rock is loose*. A long, slow pull means *anchored*. Three sharp tugs means *pay out slack; the wind is dropping in the pocket*.

Most of the time, there are no tugs. There is only the text.

Because I cannot look up and cannot look down, my world is five inches of granite and the humming of the line held against my jaw. The Stitchers do not use whistles only for the wind; we use our own teeth as resonators. If I clamp the gut between my molars, the entire tower enters my skull.

Through the bone, the roar isn't a roar. The roar is the air trying to tear the mountain out of the earth by its roots. The granite doesn't stand still; it sways. A four-thousand-foot needle of marrow-stone leans four inches to the east when the jet thickens at midday, then snaps back with a slow, grinding shiver that sets the teeth in your gums aching. I can feel the shearmen landing in the hollows three hundred yards above me—not the weight of their bodies, which are light as wicker baskets, but the tiny *click* of their calcified wing-sockets striking the lip as they tumble out of the shear into the dead-air.

I can feel the scaphs, too. A scaph is moving through the flute two ribs to my left. It feels like someone dragging an emery board across the base of my skull. It moves three inches, stops, rasps the liver-lichen down to the pink crystal, then moves again. It knows I am here. It doesn't care. To a scaph, a Stitcher wedged into a channel is just another dry hump of horn and hide that doesn't yield to the scraper.

We will stay in this channel until the indigo above turns black-violet and the first cold stars come out—stars that do not twinkle here, because there is no wet air to make them jump, but burn like needle-points of white phosphorus driven into the dark.

Then we will pull ourselves into the deep chimney behind the flute, where the wind dies to a continuous, hollow pipe-note, and wedge ourselves into the felt-mats. We will eat two fingers of dried scaph-fat, cold and fibrous, chew it until the grease coats our gums, and pass the bladder between us without looking at each other's faces. In the dark, his hand will find my wrist, make the three quick finger-taps that mean *the pin held*, and then we will sleep with our chins against our ribs.

One day, the pluck on the line will be too great. The flute will be greasy with aerosol mist, or the horn toggle will split along the grain, or the sheer torque of the tower leaning into the planetary jet will pinch my hips until the pelvis cracks.

Or perhaps I will simply get too cold. At this height, the cold doesn't creep into you like water; it sets into your joints like lime. You wake up and your knees will not unlock from the stone. The boy will know. He will feel the stiffness through the sinew. He will not try to drag me down—down is mud and rot, a mile below the cloud where no Stitcher has set foot since the first towers were named.

He will climb down to my groove, take the adzes from my belt, take the grease-bladder and the carved bone whistles from my chest-pouch, and cut the gut with his slate-knife.

Then he will pack the liver-lichen and the dry alpine felt around my shins until I am tight in the cleft. He will turn my head so my jaw rests flat against the flute, where the stone groans loudest.

And he will leave me there.

It takes forty years for the grit to take the leather off your shoulders. I have seen the old ones in the high cracks, where the chimneys narrow near the summit: their spines are yellow and polished like ivory handles, the ribs grooved into the same flute-pattern as the mountain, their mouths open in a permanent, silent whistle as the jet slips through the empty sockets where their eyes used to be.

They aren't dead. Not the way people who rot in the earth are dead. They are just done moving. They have stopped being the climber and have become the anchor-pin.

That is where I would be. Waiting in the flute, my jaw against the quartz, feeling the planetary jet push the needle three inches to the leeward, holding the boy on forty yards of gut, while the white light above us goes on sharpening its teeth against the world.
