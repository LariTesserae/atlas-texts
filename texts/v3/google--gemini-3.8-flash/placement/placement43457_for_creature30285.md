At first, the temptation is to look for yourself in the air above the shelf—in the cold knife-edge where the two ocean winds shear past each other without touching, or in that column of furnace-breath mist that stands between the basalt cliffs like a white pillar. It is easy to imagine being the vapor, or the sheer drop, or the light that comes through it without casting a distinct shadow. 

But there is no traction up there. The air on this shoulder is an eviction; it belongs to the gale, and the gale has no memory of the terrace. It only wants to strip the ridge down to its teeth.

So you come lower, down past the fluted lip of the weeping rock.

You might think you are one of the isopods—something clear and hard-shelled, clinging beneath the ribbed belly of leaf four, waiting for the groan of the jade wood, timing your breath to the rising sap so you can drink the fall as it happens. There is a clean, mechanical beauty in that: no eyes, just eighty micro-hooks buried in the wax of a green hull, the sheer physical shock of fifty gallons tearing over your carapace, and then the slow, warm grazing on red iron-dust while the stone below still hums. 

Yet there is no room inside the isopod for anything more than the next tip. Its universe is three seconds of brace and nine minutes of crawl. It is too narrow a pocket to hold a self.

Nor is it possible to be the woman. Her space is entirely occupied. Sixty years of iron water, sixty years of steam curing her skin into boot-leather, sixty years of the hammer-bones in her skull calcifying until her inner ear became a solid pebble—she has no vacant corners. She has become a geologic feature of the sixth step, as finished and unyielding as the chipped gutter she sweeps with split bamboo. To imagine taking her place would be an intrusion; she has already earned every vibration that travels up her shins from tree seven. 

No, where I find myself is neither the animal running across the ribs, nor the plant catching the sky, nor the woman who grinds. 

I find myself in the tool. 

Not the calcified gourd, which is fragile and doomed to crack eventually under its own weight of lime, but the river cobble. The pestle.

Look at where it came from: it does not have the vesicular, pockmarked face of this shelf. It is not basalt. It has no iron-red veins, no sulfur-crust, no bubbles frozen into stone by a sudden venting of the earth’s gut. It is gray-green, fine-grained quartzite or dense river-slate, rounded by ten thousand years of tumbling in a cold, braided river far down in some shadowed valley where the mountain was still young. It was rolled smooth by waters that had nothing to do with steam or sulfur—waters that ran over gravel bars, through alder thickets, beneath skies where geese flew. 

A hand brought it here—not hers, but someone’s before her, carried up through the chimney path in a sling of raw hemp, heavy as an iron heart, to be the one thing on this warm shelf that never belonged to it.

And now it has lived here so long that the valley is forgotten, but its foreignness remains. 

To be that stone is to be dense where everything else is spongy or porous. The cistern-trees are vast chambers of water and pith; the basalt underfoot is riddled with steam-tubes and hairline fault lines; the very air is an emulsion of spray and sulfur gas. But the cobble is solid, completely packed, cold at its core until the woman’s palm or the bedrock warms it from without.

I would sit beside the sunken trough, halfway down the six chipped steps, where the three streams converge. 

My underside is flat—not by river-wear, but by centuries of being ground against the red-black volcanic floor. It is polished to a greasy, dark mirror, impregnated so deeply with the lipids of the tallow-tongue that water does not wet it; water sheets off it in beads, trembling with the terrace's vibration. 

I would know the rhythm of the day not by light—for under the basalt overhang the light changes little—but by temperature and grip. 

First, the deep, slow chill of the dawn, when the bedrock drops just enough for the thermal currents to draw the dew tight against my flanks. 

Then, the sudden, dry heat of her hand. Her fingers are thick, rough as sharkskin from the iron mud, their whorls worn away. When she picks me up, she does not test my weight; she knows it as she knows the weight of her own forearm. She sets three knots of the leathery lichen into the trough—they are cool, wet, yielding slightly like pickled tripe—and then brings my base down upon them.

That first crush: the sensation of the dense, fibrous knots splitting, the sudden pop of their internal cells yielding to hydraulic pressure, and then the release of the grease. 

It is thick, warm, and heavier than water. It climbs up my sides by capillary action, coating the microscopic pits in my quartzite skin with an oil that will never turn rancid, never sour, never dry. It smells of old silt, wet slate, and the wild turnip of the fault vents. Under her downward roll, I feel the native basalt beneath me—rougher than I am, sharp-toothed, biting into my flat base with a tiny, gritty screech that neither of us can hear, but which rattles through my interior grain like a bell struck underwater.

Between the strokes, the terrace speaks through the rock:

*Thump.* 

Tree two drops. The shudder passes through the bedrock, through the mortar trough, up through the oil slick, and into my stone belly. It is an instant, dry kick. 

A pause. Her hand shifts its leverage.

*Boom.* 

Tree seven drops. A wetter, heavier jolt, carrying the splash of warm spray that drifts across the steps and lands on my top-curve in fine, warm freckles that immediately roll down into the tallow.

When the work is done, she wipes me with the edge of her thumb—not to clean me, for the grease is my protection, but to clear the excess back into the lip of the calcified gourd. Then she sets me down in the same hollow beside the sixth step, where the stone has been rubbed to a shallow dish that matches my flank.

That is where I stay while the afternoon turns the sky to ink. 

The heat rises out of the fault line beneath me, warming my belly until I am eighty, ninety degrees through and through. The fine mist settles on my back and evaporates almost instantly in the mountain sun, leaving behind a faint crust of white mineral salt that the next day’s oil will dissolve. 

Down below, the young men are shivering in the scrub country, looking up at the black ridge where the steam rises, terrified of the sound of the falling buckets. They think this place is an engine that was built to break men. 

They don't know that the engine doesn't care enough to break anything. It is only water meeting fire, caught for a few seconds by nine green leaves before it falls into the clouds.

And I sit in the trough, heavy, oiled, alien, and quiet—the one cold thing the river made, now permanently warm, holding down the center of the terrace while the mountain counts its time in water.
