I would not be on the pallet. 

The pallet belongs to the one who ratchets, the one whose joints have settled into the formal logic of canes and pegs. To hang there requires a trust in the kapok cords that I do not possess; those braided fibers, though strong, sing too loudly under tension. In a chamber where every cubic inch is saturated with the mantle’s roar, a taut cord is not merely a suspension—it is an iron pipe transmitting the shudder directly into the spine. 

I would be higher, where the split narrows toward that final, absolute pinch of rock, but before the air becomes completely unbreathable with the sour draft of the copper seam. 

There is an obtuse shelf up there, four paces above the basin, formed where the ironstone sheared diagonally across a band of black vitrophyre. The shelf is no wider than the span of two spread hands, slanted down at an angle that would throw off anything soft, anything that relies on moisture or tallow to adhere. But because there is no tallow, because the rock grips instantly on contact like dry wool on a thistle, one does not slide. One wedges.

I find myself wedged there, drawn into the angle where the ceiling becomes a knife-edge. 

At first, I might seem to be only another blister in the volcanic glass—a dark, irregular node of slag that cooled with a slightly different grain than the wall behind it. There is very little to distinguish a living contour from a tectonic one when neither is allowed to swell. I have no belly to rise and fall; there is no rhythmic heave to betray where the stone ends and the flesh begins. 

Like the folded one below, I do not possess a pulse. A pulse is an absurd extravagance in a sealed kiln—it requires valves, elasticity, a reservoir of fluid to churn and push. Here, whatever circulates does so through microscopic capillary channels carved directly through the dense, chalky core of the bones, drawn upward by the steady evaporation-gradient that the chimney-draft creates along the spine. It is an architecture of wick and pore. 

Yet I am not identical to the sleeper. 

Where its eyes are flat shields of amber selenite, flush and blind to color, my skull has accommodated the liver-red glow of the roof differently. Above my brow, the plate of bone has thinned until it is parchment-sheer, translucent as horn. I do not see shapes as an eye sees them; I see the thermal drift. I see the convection as faint, curling braids of rust and dull violet rising from the razor-floor, pooling in the pockets of the ceiling, staining the cold, smooth flanks of the river-stones down in the basin with a darkness that has nothing to do with shadow.

And I listen with my teeth.

To survive on this shelf, one must keep the lower jaw pressed firmly against the vitrophyre. The basalt hum miles below does not reach the ears as sound—it is too vast for that, too flat, an amplitude that simply cancels the interior of the ear until it hums with its own ruined static. But through the jaw, through the dense, rootless plates of enamel that line the mandible, the stone speaks a legible language.

Through the rock, I can feel when the sleeper unhooks its first knee. 

It is not a sound; it is a minute shift in the vector of the room's shear. A tension released from the flint pegs. Then, the rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of its keratin treads finding the obtuse hinges of the floor. 

I watch it from above, through the dull red drift. I watch the long, flat palms—those strange, rectangular boards—reach into the spun-glass plumes of the yellow floss. 

I know the scraper it uses. I know the thumb-hollow rubbed into the blackened rib. 

That bone was not carved here. It is too curved, too broad in its sweep, to have come from anything that ever ratcheted between these walls. It has the architecture of an animal that required a cage—an expansive, soft basket designed to rise and fall against the pressure of an atmosphere that did not smell of heated flint. It is the rib of something that carried a great deal of water inside itself, foolishly, like an open pail. 

Sometimes, when the draft from below drops by a fraction and the resonance of the chamber shifts from the tone of an iron bell to the duller thud of lead, I look down at the three river-stones in the chipped obsidian basin. 

The folded one handles only the first. It is obsessed with the egg-shaped quartz-veined pebble—the one that fits into the hollow behind its jaw. It rolls it until the stone squeaks against the dry skin, searching for that phantom, the *slip*. It believes that if it presses hard enough, the smooth skin of the river-pebble will remember how to glide.

It does not touch the third stone. 

The third stone is smaller, flatter—a dark, greenish disc of slate that was ground down by the silt of a riverbed so old that the basalt around us was still a white-hot slurry beneath the crust when it was smoothed. It sits half-buried under the golden shavings that the sleeper sweeps into the cracks. 

If I were to drop down from my shelf—if I were to let my limbs notch out, ratcheting down the sharp steps of the cleavage with that light, brittle *clink* of dry horn on glass—it would not be for the yellow sulfur-frost. 

I would go to the basin for the slate. 

I would not roll it against my cheek. I would place it flat between the palms of my hands. 

My hands do not have the gecko-treads of the sleeper. My palms are completely smooth, surfaced with a single, continuous sheet of dense, horn-colored enamel, polished by decades of being pressed flat against the vitrophyre to hold my balance in the heat. 

Between those two sheets of enamel, the slate would find no purchase. For a single fraction of a second—before the friction took hold, before the bone-dry surfaces caught upon the microscopic grit of the room and bound fast—there would be a sensation like nothing else in the rift. 

Not water. We have no word for water; the word has baked out of the mineral memory of our salts. 

Just the absence of the tooth. The brief, terrifying glide of one flat thing passing over another without shattering, without humming, without grinding the rock to powder.

Then the heat would seize it. The slate would reach the temperature of my marrow. The grip would return, instant and thirsty, locking the three of us—the stone, the sleeper, and the watcher in the pinch—back into the unyielding, red-lit iron of the shelf.

Until then, I stay where the rock is narrowest. 

Below, the sleeper folds itself down again, knee to sternum, forehead to shin, clicking into its triple-jointed knot. The sound of its joints settling dies away beneath the mantle’s roar. 

I press my chin back into the vitrophyre. The vibration enters the bone, passes through the hollow skull, and hums out through the top of my head into the pinch, steady as a furnace, absolute and without end.
