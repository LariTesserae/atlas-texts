I would not be on the open decks. The mile-wide shelves are too broad, too clearly meant for the things that slide or the things that measure; to walk there is to be perpetually caught in the false horizon, calculating slopes that do not exist until the knees give out.

Instead, you would find me tucked into a shear-pocket—one of those narrow, vertical clefts where a main diagonal brace fails to reach its gusset plate by nine or ten inches, leaving an unbolted mouth of bare, milled iron that looks out into the flat glare. 

It is a place of terrible draft. The air does not blow as wind blows over a plain; it bleeds horizontally through the gap, forced out by the immense, silent displacement of the decks settling five tiers above. It smells continuously of chilled paraffin and the sour, tallowy tang of fresh-stamped washers.

I would be something with the shape of a man who has spent forty years inside an intake duct, but altered by the necessity of the seams. 

My spine has lost its backward curve; it arches in a single, rigid C, hardened to fit the inner radius of a thirty-inch I-beam so that when I draw my knees up, my back beds perfectly against the web, cold steel against oiled canvas, leaving no air between to carry away my heat. The drill-cloth of my sleeves is gone at the forearms, replaced by heavy cuffs of beaten lead sheet laced tight with fine iron lock-wire. They are dead weight, those sleeves. They keep my hands from trembling when the harmonic sweeps through the bay.

I am not blind, but I do not have the walkers’ lead spectacles. I have something worse: the third lid of the shear-owls, taken while they roosted and pressed into the sockets until the tissue took graft. It is cloudy, the color of wet tallow, thick enough that the white zenith does not burn the optic nerve, but clear enough to let me see the only thing that matters here: *the gap*.

Through that gray membrane, the world does not look like steel. It looks like strain. 

Where a girder carries fifty tons of compression, it shows as a dark, oily bruise along the metal’s skin. Where a plate is in tension, shivering near the point of shear, it glows with a pale, phosphorescent lilac, faint as phosphorus scraped from a wet match. I watch the lilac lines migrate. I watch them creep across the floor-plates like grease across hot tin, searching for an exit through the non-Euclidean folds.

My food is not the soft crustaceans from the wet wells. I cannot reach them; the drops are too long and my fingers are too thick, blunt-tipped and calloused into square pads from decades of feeling the milling chatter on the flanges. 

I eat the shims.

It is slow, quiet work. I carry an old cold-chisel, its mushroomed head battered flat by blows from some forgotten drift-pin, and a short maul turned from bronze that will not throw a spark against the iron. When the great valve five levels down unseats—in that single, six-second sigh when the plates lift half a hair under the venting of the compressed oil—I drive the chisel into the damp around a washer. 

You must be very quick. You must take only the margin, the tail of the ribbon that protrudes into the air-wash to drink the sweat. If you drive too deep and nick the body between the mated faces, the tension will pinch your tool and keep it forever, welded tight by twenty tons of dry grip.

The meat of the shim is tough, like pickled salt-pork that has dried in lime, but it is saturated with the mutton-tallow of the threads. It does not decay. In the belly, it sits heavy and cold, a hard grease-plug that takes days to dissolve, yielding up its calories with the stubborn slowness of paraffin burning down a wick. It leaves the tongue coated in a thin, alkaline grease that keeps the throat from parching in the thin air.

For water, I do not scrape the underside of the girders. That is for those who walk. In my corner, the water does not condense; it *rises*. 

Every morning—or at the interval where the third shadow swings through its arc and makes the air taste of copper pennies—droplets of pure, lead-sweet condensation detach from the lower flanges and drift upward through my gap. They float past my face like tiny, leaden ball-bearings, perfectly spherical, motionless in their own small reference frames. I catch them on the flat of my tongue as they pass. They taste of nothing at all, except the cold.

I have no drum-code for the walkers. When their three-toed pads slap past my cleft—the rubberized cloth of their gaiters whispering against the diamond-tread—I do not rap the steel. 

I do not want them to look down into the pocket. If they look, they will see that the flange they are walking is two inches narrower than it was three miles back; they will see that the bolts have begun to lean thirty degrees off the perpendicular, their shanks bowing under a silent, lateral shear that has no business existing in an engineered work. It would make them pause, and a walker who pauses on a seventy-mile bevel loses the line of his stride. Once the stride is broken, the ear begins to listen to the perspective folding behind him, and then he looks up, and the zenith takes his eyes.

Instead, I stay wedged. 

My knees are pulled against my breast-bone; my lead-wrapped wrists are crossed over my ankles. I have become, through long residence, a kind of living dampener. Where my carcass fills the pocket between the brace and the plate, the chatter cannot establish its standing wave. The iron stays quiet for ten yards in every direction. 

Sometimes, in the long hours between the valve-strokes, one of the lice will leave the belly of a lead-beast and roll down into my cleft, drawn by the faint, sour heat of my woolens. It will crawl onto the lead plate of my forearm, its six blunt legs clicking with that dry, pea-shell rattle, searching for the grease-seam.

I let it work. It scrapes at the dried slime on my cuffs, turning slowly on its leather-hard skirt, its weight a clean, familiar four ounces against my bone. When it has cleaned the lead, it will settle into the fold of my elbow, pull its legs beneath its rim, and wait for the structural load to shift.

We both wait. 

Far off, through the fan-folded perspective where three cooling stacks stand nested inside one another like sheared cups, the white light stays hard, dry, and total. Beneath us, thirty levels down through the unfloored lattice, something very large and perfectly square settles another millimeter into its bed of uncured lime. 

The vibration rises through the ironwork: first as a shrill, singing note in the wire-grass, then as a kick against the soles of my feet, and finally as a flat, dull *clink* from the heart of the joint against my spine, where a three-inch bolt stretches, sighs, and holds.

I let my breath out through my teeth, tasting the iron-dust, and lean half an inch harder into the cold.
