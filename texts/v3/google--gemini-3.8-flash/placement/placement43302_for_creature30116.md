At first, there is only the weight of the powder, which is not the weight of earth, but of something unmade—a dry, suspended meal that holds neither moisture nor air, only a dense, cool grain that parts without shearing. 

I am not in the light. 

Above, the triple-reflected glare must be beating against the vertical stone until the limestone hums, but down here, three fingers beneath the crust, the world has no geometry. It has only resistance and temperature. To move is not to dig; there is no hollow left behind to prove where I have been. The gypsum flour simply slips around the blunt curvature of my skull, runs along the segmented plates of my flank, and closes again with a faint, silken sigh that is felt entirely through the ribs.

I am thick as a thumb, blunt at both ends, pale as mutton-fat. I have no eyes—only two dark, heat-sensitive pits beneath the translucent bone of my forehead that register the sun above not as brightness, but as an ache: a flat, circular iron plate pressing down upon the basin floor, baking the top inch of dust into a fragile wafer.

Down here, it is cold and smells faintly of brimstone and pulverized seashells. 

I do not hear the swallows. The midges do not exist for me as chords or vibrations in the air. For me, the life above is an occasional harvest of hulls that have sifted down through the centuries: the chitinous knees of insects, dry as chaff; the barb of a swallow feather, light and stiff; the microscopically fine curls of limestone peeled away by the woman’s chert blade, which wander down through the airless fall and eventually work their way through the crust to settle in the dark. I draw them into my mouth with the dust, grinding the flour against the small, triangular teeth set into the roof of my jaw, extracting the ancient, sun-cured tallow that clings to them.

The basin is not quiet to me. 

To the woman, it is an amphitheater of silence broken only by the needle-fall of the drops. To me, the basin is an ear. 

Every time a drop gathers its bulk on the flute sixty cubits above and makes its fall, the stone beneath the silt rings. It is not a *tink* down here. It is a dull, solid tap that travels horizontally through the bedrock floor—a pulse that strikes the underside of my belly and tells me where the wet flutes are, which gutters are fat with water, and which are drying out. 

And then there is her weight.

She believes she makes no sound. She rolls her foot from the outer edge to the metatarsal; she slides across the floor without lifting dust. But to the gypsum, a footstep is a mountain laid down. When she walks to the saucer-hollows with her bird-down and her hair-bound gourds, the entire silt-bed flexes. The grains rearrange themselves in microscopic avalanches, packing tightly together under the heel, loosening behind the toe. I feel her pass overhead like a slow, deliberate shadow of mass—an interruption in the grain. Sometimes she pauses directly above me, her bare soles drawing a tiny thread of heat out of the top crust, and we remain there together for twenty breaths: the woman suspended in the white, blinding vacuum of three-directional noon, and the pale worm coiled in the cool flour beneath her instep, waiting for her to move on so the dust may settle back into its fluid rest.

I have no memory of the scrub-country above the rim. I do not know what the sky is, except that once every few weeks, the iron plate lifts.

It happens when the high heat has drained out of the stone—when the chalk walls have given up their stored bake to the empty hole above, and the air in the amphitheater grows thin, sharp, and still as glass. 

Then, the crust of the floor ceases to burn. The sulfur beneath turns numbing, like ice.

Driven by the cold below, I turn my snout upward. The powder grows warmer, then coarser, then brittle. With a dry, papery snap, my blunt head punches through the gypsum crust into the void.

The sensation of nothingness against my skin is terrifying and immense. There is no resistance; there are no walls; the weight that holds me together suddenly vanishes. I drag the rest of my ivory length out through the hole, leaving a meandering furrow behind me, and stretch out across the flag-like stone.

The amphitheater is blue-black, vast, and utterly drained of glare. High above, the circle of the rim is a ring of jagged charcoal against a dusting of salt that does not drop. 

For an hour, or three, I lie motionless on the open stone. My skin drinks the chill from the night air, letting the slow, heavy blood in my core cool until my heart beats only once for every hundred drops that strike the perimeter walls. From here, I can smell the tallow on the woman’s skin where she sleeps deep in the bread-oven seam; I can smell the sour grease of the midges' lines drying in the night drought; I can smell the faint, iron trace of dew gathering on the cold flutes, preparing the first silver needles of dawn.

I am the bottom of the basin. 

Before the light can strike the rim and turn the geometry back into fire, the stone flags under my belly begin to tremble with the first thermal stirrings of the rising sun. I turn, slide my head back into the furrow I left behind, and let the white powder swallow me whole, swimming downward into the sulfur and the cold, where the world is only weight, and the long, slow drop of time.
