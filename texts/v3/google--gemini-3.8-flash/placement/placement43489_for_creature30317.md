It starts not with a face or a name, but with the acoustic pressure of the floor.

Everything here is a transmitter. If you press yourself flat—whether you have bellies, sternums, or jointed sternites—the city does not feel like stone. It feels like the stretched skin of a dry drum, vibrating at frequencies so low they bypass the ears and register directly as an ache in the marrow, a dry shivering in the fat along the spine.

At first, I think I must be down in the dark: one of the grinders, those great ivory screws turning through the chalk sub-strata, blind and heavy, chewing the dead streets back into flour. The rhythm down there is comforting—a slow, masticating circularity, where destruction is so neatly paired with the slick wet trails of new foundation that time stops having any edge. There is no grief in being broken down if you are immediately smoothed into an archway.

But there is no light in the grinder-tunnels, and I have too much heat on my skin. 

It is a dry, vertical heat, coming down like an iron press from the noon sky and rising in equal measure from the crystalline pavement. That eliminates the cart-lizards, too. Their life is an endurance of burdens, a slow, trancelike hauling under the bite of their guidance-midges, their bellies dragging close to the salt-dust. I do not feel that heavy, unbaked dough in my feet. I do not feel the drag of sledge-runners on polished calc-spar. 

Nor am I one of the weavers. Their existence is too hurried, too micro-focused: the frantic regurgitation of foaming slurry, the rapid-fire troweling with the serrated foreleg, the endless, shivering race against the sun before the paste flashes into rock. They never look out. They have no eyes to look out with, only those trembling quills that read the wind for shear-lines.

I am higher than the lizards, but more still than the weavers.

I find myself wedged. 

Not trapped, but held—precisely, dryly, securely—within the acoustic throat of a southern spire, forty cubits above the viaduct. 

Here, the fluted architecture has been bored through by intentional chimneys, or perhaps by an ancient grinder whose channel was preserved and thickened by the smoothers until it became a vertical flue. The midday air does not sit here; it roars upward. The sun bakes the south-facing facade until the bone-pale wall is hot enough to pop a spindle-seed, and the cooler air from the subterranean vaults is sucked violently up through this narrow bore, creating a permanent, rising draft that smells of warm wax, chalk-flour, and bitter plant grease.

My body has grown to fit this throat. 

I am long, segmented, and flattened like an ash-shingle, wedged across the diameter of the flue. My sides are not soft; they are fringed with stiff, comb-like arrays of calcified bristles that bite into the concentric trowel-ridges of the weavers’ mortar. I do not move for days, perhaps for seasons. There is no need. The upward gale holds me in place, floating my weight against my anchors.

I am not stone, though the salt has crusted on my dorsal plates until you could not tell my skin from the wall without chipping it. I am alive in the spare, desiccated way this basin permits life: my fluids are heavy as motor oil, held tight behind wax-sealed spiracles, never a bead surrendered to the wind. 

My diet is what the draft brings me. The thermal carries an invisible bounty: winged, papery seeds cast off by the gray scrub on the terraces; microscopic flakes of shed reptile scale; dried pelts of midges; and, once every few hours, the heavy, aerosolized droplets of euphorbia lipid shed by the felt-moths as they bank sharply through the alleys below. My mouthparts are not jaws; they are a set of three layered, silken sieves that open into the draft, collecting the drift, tasting the city’s metabolism in the dust. I know which sector is thriving by the bitterness of the pollen; I know when an ox-lizard has died ten bridges away by the dry, salt-meal dust that coats my bristles twenty minutes later.

Yet my purpose—if a thing without a designer can be said to have purpose—is not merely to feed. 

It is to be the reed.

The wind does not pass through this flue smoothly. My flattened body, tilted slightly against the updraft, acts as an acoustic baffle. As the thermal wind rises or falls with the wandering of the sun across the salt-flat, the air screams over my serrated edges. 

If the day is too hot, if the southern parapet begins to dry too rapidly and threaten micro-fissuring, the updraft strengthens. The pressure beneath me swells. By reflex—an involuntary arching of my dorsal plates, a slight splaying of my lateral combs—I alter the aperture of the chimney. 

The note changes.

It shifts from a hollow, rushing hiss to a dry, piercer whine—an infrasonic whistle that travels downward through the crystalline heart of the tower, vibrating through sixty feet of solid cast calcium into the foundation.

Below, in the blinding light, forty thousand weavers stop. They do not know why they stop; they only know that the stone beneath their tarsi is humming with a thin, rapid pulse that makes their antennae ache. Without looking, without choosing, they turn in unison toward the leeward face. Their gizzards begin to churn their alkaline saliva faster. They begin to trowel an extra layer of resin-paste over the western joints, thickening the counter-buttress before the heat-stress can crack the bone-pale masonry.

When the wind settles at dusk, and the cold begins to pool in the basin like invisible water, the updraft dies. I flatten myself against the chimney floor, retracting my sieves, drawing my legs beneath my salt-crusted shield. The flue goes silent. The weavers retreat into the brood-hollows.

I do not sleep, because sleep belongs to wet creatures whose brains must clear away the poisons of soft blood. Here, I simply cool down. My black grease thickens. The rhythmic murmur of the city slows to a faint, papery click as the stone contracts under the stars. 

I am a single notch in the flute. I am part of the tower’s hearing, its automatic throat, suspended in the wind between the unworked earth miles below and the dry, white brilliance of the sky.
