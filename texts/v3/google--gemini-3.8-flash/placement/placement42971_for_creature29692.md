At first, there is only the sensation of load.

Not weight in the terrestrial sense—not the downward drag of an atmosphere pressing on wet clay—but the uniform, lateral torque of an ocean that wants to fall through the bottom of the world and is being squeezed through iron instead. The water does not yield; it transfers force instantly, like a single, continental block of cold quartz.

If I look for my edges, I do not find skin. Skin is a porous, leaking boundary meant for mud and seasons. What I find is a long, narrow line of compression. 

I am suspended along one of the vertical stays. 

Not the titanium wire itself, but something that has grown into its twist, coiled around its high-tensile core over centuries until the steel strand and the biological armature are indistinguishable under the cold, flat light. My form is tall and three-sided, like an elongated surveying prism or a three-fluted drill bit, fifteen fathoms from crown to heel, perfectly vertical. I offer no flat surface to the horizontal current; the screaming shear off the hornfels ridges strikes my leading edge and splits into two laminar sheets, glassy and silent, leaving no wake to betray where the water ends and I begin.

My substance is not flesh. It is cold-cast vitreous horn, banded with alternating laminations of dense bio-calcite and the microscopic bronze flakes cast off by the siphon collars miles to the north. Where the bronze dust has settled into my grain, it has oxidized not into green rot, but into a hard, electrical skin that glimmers with the dull, greasy sheen of unpolished lead. 

I have no eyes, because light here is not information; it is merely an illumination of empty geometry. To see five miles through water as clear as cut air is useless when there is nowhere to hide and nothing that needs to be recognized by color. 

Instead, I am hollow. 

Down the center of my core runs an open, oil-filled flume that matches the vertical cable precisely. Through this chamber passes the 18-hertz hum of the planetary siphon, magnified by the acoustic resonance of the wire. It enters my base as a physical shudder, runs the full length of my structural ribs, and exits through my tapered crown. 

This is my pulse. I have no chambered heart; I have no need of one. The rotating shafts anchored into the basalt thousands of feet below are my myocardium. Every revolution of their bronze impellers forces an acoustic wave through the wire, flexing the microscopic check-valves within my flume, driving my mineral-rich hemolymph upward through my flutes in hard, mechanical spurts. If the machines were to seize, my blood would halt in its conduits within three seconds, freezing where it stood, locked in the bitter brine. 

I am tied to their unlubricated turning as intimately as a piston is tied to a crank.

Between the titanium cable and my inner sheath, there is no friction. A micro-layer of hydrostatic grease—secreted by dense, glandular pores that draw lipids from the water’s drifting flocs—keeps me buoyant, balanced precisely between the upward lift of the displaced brine and the downward pull of the cable’s anchoring stud.

I do not swim. I do not crawl. 

When I move, it is an adjustment of trim. By constricting the lower ring of my flutes, I change my drag coefficient against the current. The hydraulic pressure of the ocean against my tapered facets does the rest: I slide. 

I can slide four miles downward in an hour, descending along the vertical wire without a sound, passing through the pale noon daylight into the hard, ink-veined azure of the mid-depths, down toward where the diorite terraces drop off into the black. I pass the caliper-jaws where they hang like iron plumb bobs; their lateral organs register my laminar slipstream as nothing more than a thickening of the cable itself. I pass through the drifting shoals of chime-shrimp, their transparent carapaces clinking against my lead-colored plates like hail against a lighthouse window. 

My purpose—if a thing born of engineering and brine can possess purpose—is tension.

The cables do not remain taut on their own. The immense hydraulic drag of the rising currents exerts a harmonic flutter upon them, a high-frequency vibration that, left unchecked, would shear the mounting studs out of the basalt bedrock like rotten teeth. 

I am a dampener. 

Where the cable’s resonance begins to spike into violent, standing waves, I find the node. I settle there, clamping my three-fluted flanks against the steel braid with hydraulic foot-collars. My mass absorbs the lateral whip; my internal chambers turn the mechanical chatter into heat—not much, only fractions of a degree, but enough to create a thin, rising thermal boundary layer that slips upward along my flanks, carrying away the micro-crystals of salt that threaten to foul my grooves.

While I hold the cable, I feed.

I do not hunt the ribbonfish. I do not scythe the water. I lower thin, stiffened outriggers—rods of fibrous hornfels-chitin no thicker than a needle, fifteen yards long—into the slipstream. They do not bend; they are braced with internal mineral struts that lock under load. As the sheared water roars past them, they trap the metallic wash of the upper basin: microscopic splinters of titanium sheared from the siphon intake cowlings, dense particles of apatite dust cast off by the jaws of the hunters, and the rich, oily slicks left behind when a leviathan scrapes its opalescent flank against a basalt fin.

Capillary gutters running along the outriggers draw this material inward, directly into my central core, where it is sorted not by digestion, but by density. 

The oils are sent to my slip-rings to keep my passage along the wire frictionless. 

The iron and titanium are drawn into my outer plating, laid down in microscopic, overlapping shingle-patterns by slow-moving rows of mantle-cells that work with the silent precision of cold lathes. 

The soft, biological fats are burned slowly in the resonant chambers, oxidized by the high-pressure oxygen forced out of the cavitating water by the turbines.

I am an animal made of the debris of an abandoned engine, maintaining the rigging of a world that was never completed.

Sometimes, a leviathan passes close. 

It does not touch the cable—it knows the cable’s song too well, knows the wire would slice through its chalk-gray cartilage like a wire through lard. But its bulk displaces a mountain of water. As it glides past, a thousand tons of blind, calculating gray mass surfing the 18-hertz gradient, the pressure wave hits me like an iron beam. 

My plates creak. The horn laminae groan against the steel cable. The oil in my central flume flashes with cold, triboluminescent light—a brief, blue internal spark born of extreme shear that illuminates my ribs from within, casting a three-sided, geometric shadow through the diamond-clear water, twenty fathoms down onto the naked face of a submerged basalt needle.

The leviathan’s white, lidless vent-eye does not turn to look at me. It does not need to. It feels the dampener holding the node; it uses the dead spot I create in the acoustic field to pivot its starboard plane, banking silently into the deep slipstream toward the next siphon head five miles distant.

When it is gone, the blue spark fades. The light returns to its fixed, unchanging pale daylight. 

The light penetrates straight down, unyielding, revealing every fracture of the stone, every tooth of the impeller housings, every taut, singing wire that pins this drowned highland to the earth. 

There is nothing to wait for. There is no weather coming. Far above, beyond miles of clear ice-water, the pale sun will swing across its narrow arc, but down here the 18-hertz tone does not waver by a single cycle. 

I loosen my lower collar by a fraction of a millimeter. The current takes me, and I begin the long, smooth slide upward toward the sunlit reaches, riding the hum of the steel, cold and metallic and completely at home in the invariant roar.
