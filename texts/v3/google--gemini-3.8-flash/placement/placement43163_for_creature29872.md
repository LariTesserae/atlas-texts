At first, there is only the pressure on the heels.

It takes a long time to distinguish the bone of the heel from the pink gneiss beneath it. They are almost the same density, almost the same temperature. But the stone is older by an order of magnitude that makes no sense to count, and the stone does not yield the half-millimeter of tallow that gathers between the callus and the grain. 

That is where I find myself: in the gap between the rock and the fat.

I am not standing upright, because upright is an arrogant posture that belongs to thin air and places where things fall down. I am settled on my hams, my knees turned outward, the broad, flat pads of my feet tucked into a joint-line where two slabs of the plateau meet without a lip. My belly rests on my thighs like a sack of warm tallow. It has its own weight, its own breath, shifting slightly whenever the lung-bellows in my chest draw in the afternoon drift.

I have a pelt, but it is not fur like the animals of the lower shelf have. It is a coarse, lead-colored thatch, matted with its own saturated moisture. It smells of river-silt, cold well-water, and the sweet, mutton-grease of the mounds. If you were to run a finger through it—though nothing here has fingers thin enough to do that—you would find no dry skin at the roots. I do not carry water *on* me; I am soaked through, like an old sponge that has lain in a bucket until it weighs three times its dry self. It keeps the wind from finding my gristle. It makes me heavy enough that when the great surges roll through the herds, I do not slide.

Before me is the edge of a ring-wall. Three stones high, cobbles the color of liver, gathered from a dry wash two leagues south where the gneiss once cracked along a freeze-joint before the warmth settled in for good. 

My hands are resting on the top stone. They are absurdly wide, thick-palmed, with digits that do not taper. The gray skin on the pads is scored with tiny, interlocking whorls that act like the wet leather washers of a pump: when I press them against the smooth quartz, they make a soft, rubbery *clack* and take hold so hard that I could hang my entire bulk from them if the plateau had an edge to hang from.

Beneath my left foot, under the horn-plates of the pavement, the crabs are working. 

I can feel them through the soles of my feet—not as individuals, but as an itch in the stone, a microscopic stuttering of tiny horn points tapping across the wet quartzite. A plate died two nights ago, three paces behind my back. It went soft at the rim, where it had been glued to its neighbors with tallow-paste; the vacuum broke with a sound like a wet boot being pulled from an estuary. Now the crabs are under there, drinking the soup. I can feel the vibration of their alkaline spittle bubbling against the dead horn. By tomorrow, the empty round will settle flat, and the young plate next to it will swell into the room, its mantle creeping over the gap like tallow running down a candle.

I do not look at it. My eyes are small, buried deep under heavy shelves of bone and fat to keep the saltless fog from bleaching them. I do not need to look. The world here is not seen; it is *borne*.

Presently, the mist comes over the shelf.

It does not drop from the sky; it wells up from the trough like water filling an empty basin from the bottom. It smells of the forests five thousand feet down—rotting ferns, heavy pollen, the musk of creatures with claws and teeth that die quickly and violently in the heat. By the time it climbs the plateau, all that violence has been strained out of it, crushed into a fine, warm steam that tastes of old oil.

To my right, half-buried in the white vapor, the flank of an elder mound begins to heave.

It is forty paces from me, though it looks like an island of wet slate. Its intake valve—a three-foot furrow lined with pale, corrugated meat that looks like salt-pork—parts with a deep, liquid suck. *Huuuh-whuff.* 

The air drawn into that slit passes through my pelt. I feel the drift of it, the tiny drop in air pressure against my neck. A moment later, the wax-smell reaches me.

It is time to move, but "time" here is not an arrow. It is an accumulation. It is like the dew collecting in the shallow bowls of the rock until the surface tension gives way and a single, silent film of silver slips four inches to the west. 

I lean my weight forward onto my knuckles. The stone takes my broad pads; the suction cups at the base of my wrists kiss the wet gneiss with a dull, wet report. I rise only as far as my knees, keeping my center of mass lower than my ribs, and begin the crawl across the discs.

They do not crack under me. They are flexible, ancient keratin, greasy with condensate. Under my palms, their growth-rings feel like the rough grain of green ash-wood. As my weight presses down on them, the water between their seams squirts out in tiny, lukewarm jets that wash across my insteps. They know my tread. If a stranger’s weight came down upon them—though there are no strangers, only the seven of others like me who work the northern swale—they would clamp hard against the granite, pulling their rims down to hold their water. But for me, they merely flex, a resilient floor of living shingles that ripples two paces ahead of my crawl.

When I reach the elder mound, I do not speak. I have no apparatus for words that fly through the air. In my throat is only a heavy, cartilaginous plate that can vibrate against my breastbone when I need to call the others to a carcass. 

Instead, I press my forehead against its flank.

The skin is thick, cool on the outside from the dew, but burning with a deep, sluggish furnace three inches in. It feels like leaning against the side of a vat of boiling starch that has been wrapped in wet harness-leather. The beast does not startle. Its vast, simple nervous system takes twelve heartbeats to register that my skull is touching its fourth lateral gutter.

Then, a deep shudder. A low, internal thud, like an iron weight dropped into grease. It acknowledges the contact.

I raise my right hand, hook my thumb into the fleshy margin of its intake valve, and peel.

The wax comes away in a thick, amber ribbon, three fingers wide and cold as suet at the edges, though soft and buttery where it touched the mucous membranes of the valve. It is heavy with the spores of the lowlands, the yellow dust of mosses that will never grow on this stone, the distilled vapor of a hundred miles of forest compressed into a dense, odorless grease.

I put a wad of it into my mouth. 

It does not melt immediately. It sits on the tongue, stiff and salty, tasting of sheep-pens, lime, and wet quartz. As my body-heat works into it, the tallow softens, coating my throat with a thick, insulating film that will keep the mist from souring my gullet during the night.

The mound breathes again. With the valve cleared of its crust, the intake is wider, easier. *Chuuu-hah.* The vast, ribbed bellows within its ribs expand, lifting four tons of gray muscle half an inch off the disc-floor, then dropping it back down with a wet, flat *wallop* that shakes the puddles for twenty yards around us.

I stay there for an hour, or three. The light does not change enough to mark the difference. It remains the same leaden, milk-white glare, filtered through three hundred feet of resting cloud. 

After a while, the wind brings a vibration through my knees that is not the elder’s breathing.

It is a slow, rhythmic knock. One. Two. Three.

Half a league away, at the white quartz slab, Sael is leaning. 

He has found the dead one from the eastern swale—the great, blunt cow that stopped filtering two mornings ago. He has his back against its hip, his heels wedged into the joint-lines of the bedrock, and he is singing into the stone. 

The sound does not travel through the mist; the air is too wet and heavy for a voice. It travels through the gneiss. It comes up through the soles of my feet, through the thick bones of my shins, through my pelvis and into the back of my teeth: a dull, rhythmic thrumming, like a wooden mallet striking an oak beam buried in clay.

*Come. Lean. The stone is upright.*

I do not hurry. To hurry on the plateau is to tear the discs, to break the tallow-seals that keep the crawlspace damp, to slip on the dew-mirrors and crack a hip against the unyielding pink table. 

I work my hands into the creases of my shag, clearing the dead water from my armpits with two broad, scraping strokes of my thumbs. I push myself back from the elder’s flank. The beast’s valve is already crusting over again, tiny droplets of amber grease exuding from the glands along its lip, preparing the next harvest for whoever crawls this way when the mist thins.

I turn south, toward the standing slab. 

As I walk—my knuckles brushing the rims of the discs at every second stride, my wide, flat toes finding the gray spaces where the horn is worn thinnest—I feel the forty-minute wave begin behind me. 

The elder has shifted its bulk to reach a fresh lens of water that has pooled in the lee of my ring-wall. Two tons of meat have dropped three inches to the left.

The discs under its belly groan. The ripple moves out: a dull, gray heave that lifts the carapaces of ten thousand plates, travels through the herd of heifers huddled in the shallow swale, nudges the ribs of the great bulls that lie like stranded river-cobbles in the mist, and finally reaches my heels.

It lifts me half an inch. My feet float for a fraction of a second on a cushion of shifting horn and warm grease.

Then the stone receives me again. 

I plant my pads, set my knees, and wait for the ripple to die against the base of the standing rock before I take another step into the warm, white quiet.
