At first, there is only the pulse. 

It does not come through the ears; it arrives through the belly and the lower jaw, which are pressed against things harder than flesh. A thrum every nine seconds. A low, dry shudder that knocks through the plates, lifts the red dust an eighth of an inch into the water, and lets it drop again. 

To be here is to be anchored to that count. Nothing else marks the passage of time—there is no day, no turn of weather, only the nine-second heave of the dead impeller sloshing cold water against an unyielding gate, somewhere deep behind the iron bulkheads.

I would not be swimming. Swimming belongs to the things that still believe in distances, like the pale bream wheeling blindly in the middle water, their bellies swollen with grit and manganese. To swim here is to expend what cannot easily be replaced in nine-degree water, where every calorie is paid for in iron-flavored slime.

No; I would be set into the iron itself. 

Down in the machine pit beneath the main sluice bank, where the floor drops two feet below the catwalks and the current dies completely into an oily, motionless grease. There is a cast-iron actuator housing there, its access cover blown off by an air-hammer sixty winters ago. Inside, where a four-inch bronze drive-screw once turned to lift a gate that will never lift again, the cavity is round, smooth, and flooded.

My tail is coiled into the empty spiral of that housing. Three loose turns, resting in the rust-softened threads where the screw used to bite.

I am not metal, though the iron has worked its way through my mucus until my flanks are the color of wet liver and slate, mottled like waterlogged cedar. My skin has no scales to catch the edges of the exfoliated steel; it is thick, tough as boiled boot-leather, and coated in a heavy, alkaline grease that sheds off me in long, gray ribbons whenever the surge rocks the housing. These ribbons drift into the dead corners and dissolve, feeding the white worms that turn the rust into shot.

My eyes are there, set high on my flat, depressed skull, but they have long since clouded into milky, boiled marbles. They tell me only if the sour amber light from the ceiling lanterns is failing. When the filaments high above flutter and dim, the murk turns from a weak tea to pitch, and the skin along my flanks registers a faint, prickling shift in the water's electrical charge—the calcareous tubes on the soda-straws sixty feet above snapping shut in unison.

I do not need eyes. The cavern is mapped entirely across my skull, along the notched grooves of my lateral line, and down into the single, thick barbel that drops from the center of my chin.

That barbel is my sounding lead. It rests over the lip of the actuator housing, trailing four inches down into the rust-flour that covers the gutter. Through it, I taste everything the cavern exhales:
The sharp, sour tang of the accumulator batteries leaking their weak acid from the catwalk ceiling; 
the cold, sweet copper of the river pushing through the intake screens ninety yards north; 
the dry, dead-leaf taste of the manganese-eating bacteria peeling off the I-beams;
and the faint, greasy musk of a bream that has scraped too hard against a rivet and left a shred of pale skin behind.

I do not move for days. There is no reason to. The cold slows the blood to a thick, ceremonial crawl. Around my gill covers, tucked deep into the soft folds behind the opercular flaps, three flat ochre leeches have fastened their mouths. They do not hurt; they are cold like me, heavy with the same iron lymph. They swell slowly, their bodies undulating in time with my breathing, which is so faint it barely stirs the rust-curtains hanging from the valve flange above my head. We have shared this housing for twenty years. When they die, others take their place from the silt, climbing the cast iron like small, blind hands.

Every few hours, something shifts in the battery. 

A sculpin, its skin studded with iron thorns, crawls too high onto the actuator web, its blunt, finger-like fin-rays clicking against the corroded rivets: *tink, tink, tink.* 

I do not stir for a sculpin. They are mostly bone and grit, hard to crush, and their bellies are bitter with machine dross. 

I wait for the blundered drift. 

It happens when the nine-second surge catches a school of bream mid-turn. One of them, its milky eyes turned toward the sour amber glow above, loses the cushion of the current. It drifts backward toward the machine pit, its tail fluttering uselessly as the dead water behind the actuator swallows it. 

I feel its displacement forty feet away: the faint, rhythmic flutter of soft fin-rays, the small panic-wave pushing through the water, thinning the grease of the sump.

My barbel lifts from the rust. 

I do not strike with the darting snap of a river-thing. I am too heavy, five feet of waterlogged muscle and liver-fat. I uncoil one turn from the helical groove of the housing. 

Then I heave.

My broad tail sweeps once through the dead water. It displaces fifty gallons in a single, blind piston-stroke. The surge blows the four-inch carpet of red silt into a violent, billowing cloud that rolls out across the catwalks, blinding the amphipods and burying the worm-fields. 

My jaws open—broad, flat, lined with pads of rasping cardiform teeth like coarse iron files. The vacuum I create is absolute; water, grease, suspended flakes of rust, and the stunned bream are dragged into my gullet in a single, heavy thud of hydrostatic force.

The mouth closes. The teeth meet with a dull, muffled crunch—the sound of brittle scales and cartilage giving way. 

I swallow slowly, my throat working the cold mass down into the slow, acid chamber of my belly, where it will take three weeks to dissolve under the weight of nine-degree water. A cloud of red-gray flour puffs from my gill-slits, settling over my eyes, over my yellowed lips, over the leeches that wait at my throat.

Then I back into the dark.

My tail finds the smooth, helical groove of the actuator casing. It settles into the threads, loop by loop, turning until the machine and the meat are flush again. My chin sinks. The heavy barbel drops back through the settling red dust, resting once more against the lip of the concrete curb.

Above, the nine-second shudder strikes the bulkheads: *shk-thump.* 

The red flakes fall like burnt paper through the amber ribbons of sour light. 

I close my jaws tight, hold the iron cold inside me, and begin the wait again.
