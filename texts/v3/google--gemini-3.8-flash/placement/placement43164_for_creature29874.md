At first, there would be the mistake of looking for edges. 

You would expect the cold shock of an arrival—the sudden line where you end and the water begins, the prickle of skin contracting against a chill, the immediate, rhythmic panic of ribs lifting to draw what cannot be drawn. 

None of that happens. The water is already the temperature of the blood you expected to have, so there is no boundary to mark where the medium gives way to the creature. It does not enter through a mouth; it simply continues through. For a long time, the only indication that you exist at all is a slow, alkaline sweetness, like almond milk left out in a warm kitchen, and the total lack of falling.

You are not sinking. Or rather, you are sinking, but at the rate that silt settles through the felted tapestries of the maiden-moss: an inch every few days, slow enough that the motion feels like an architecture rather than an event. 

You look for hands, but what responds is wider than fingers. It is a margin. When you reach, you do not grasp; you displace. A broad, yielding expanse of yourself ripples, and the water parts with the thick reluctance of warm oil. 

Are you one of the plies? You look upward through yourself, expecting the forty-cubit folios of cartilage, waiting to feel the snap of leather pages shutting upon a meal of iron and sun. But you do not rise toward the bronze mist. You lack the buoyancy of their gas-filled spines. You are heavier than the light, yet far too loose to join the scribers down in the dark, clicking their microscopic libraries into the granite whalebacks. 

No, you are caught in the middle country—the long, suspension-bridge world between the upper steam and the leaden vellums of the bottom. 

Here, the maiden-moss hangs in curtains forty feet tall, held aloft by its small, internal breaths of methane. You drift into one of these curtains. You expect to become tangled, to struggle against the fibers, but the weed does not catch. It welcomes. It has the same soft, fatty density as your own outer layers. You press into it, and where your mass meets the weave, the moss yields with a slow, sighing collapse, releasing a cluster of tiny, fragrant bubbles. Bruised mint. Hot shale. The scent passes through you not as an odor in the nostrils, but as an ache that loosens your tissues, thinning you out further into the weave.

And it is here, inside the moss, that you begin to know what you are.

You are not an animal with a single purpose, nor a plant rooted in the old stone. You are a *passage*. 

You have formed around an old center that never dissolved. In the middle of your mass, tucked into a fold of tallow-soft pulp, rests something small and hard: one of the seeds dropped ten thousand seasons ago from the crown of a ribbon-bark that grew before the meniscus learned its curve. The seed has not rotted—nothing rots here—nor has it sprouted. It has simply waited, resting in the milk-warm bath, until your tissues gathered around it like silt gathering around a pebble in a slow river.

You are its husk, its vessel, its slow and living drift.

Your body is pale, translucent at the fringes like parboiled horn, broadening out into five or six soft, undulating skirts that have no bones. You do not hunt. You do not need to. The water is so thick with dissolved lime, starch, and the pale snow of falling vegetable lint that living is merely a matter of permitting the broth to soak through your folds. Every hour or two, a deeper shudder runs through your margins—a slow, peristaltic wave that presses the water out through thousands of microscopic pores, leaving behind a fine, sweet paste that lines your interior.

You do not see the tableland, not with eyes. But you *hear* it, if hearing is the word for what the sternum does when the high ground shifts.

Far below, three leagues through the amber twilight, a granite ridge groans against the weight of the sea. It is not a sound; it is a thud that strikes the center of the seed inside you like a hammer wrapped in wool. An instant later, the weir-bells answer. 

You feel the light before it reaches your skirts: a sudden, cold thrill of turquoise pulsing through the water from the stone pavements below. The siphonophores are counting the strain. Then, from miles to the west, where the meniscus bulges against the desert sky, comes the answering pulse—a faint, sour lemon-yellow that washes through the maiden-moss, turning the pale green fibers to old gold.

In that pulse, you read the balance. The tableland has tilted by half a millimeter. The iron-black current that loops across the lake floor has quickened its braid. 

Because you are loose, because you have no anchor in the rock and no bladder to haul you to the mist, you belong to the current. It catches the lower skirts of your body—those pale, tallowy ruffles that trail ten feet below your core—and tugs you out of the maiden-moss. 

You slide from the weed with a wet, soft slither. The gas pearls rise around you in a constellation of tiny, ascending lanterns, smelling of mint and warm grease.

Now you are moving. Not swiftly, but with the steady, unswerving dignity of the deep water. The ribbon-bark trunks slide past on either side like the pillars of an endless, submerged nave, their smooth, branchless barks scarred with the circular feeding-rings of things that passed this way when the world was narrower. 

A ply drifts overhead, seventy cubits of ribbed cartilage rippling in three independent rhythms. As its shadow falls over you, the water cools by the fraction of a degree, and your skirts contract slightly, drawing closer to the ancient seed in your chest. The light turns to grape-skin purple. Above you, the ply’s pages snap shut—*thump-slap*—a muffled, wet impact that travels through your jelly and makes the seed ring softly, like a brass bell struck underwater.

You are going toward the edge. You do not fear it, because fear is an invention of creatures that can be broken by falling, and here there is no fall. There is only the long slope, the rising Meniscus, and the strange, diamond-patterned net that trembles within the skin of the water.

As the current carries you along the granite knuckle of a final, filed-down crest, the water pales. The amber deep gives way to a blinding, razor-edged brilliance. It is the light of the dry world, the lower sky, tearing through the curved glass of the ocean’s margin.

Here, the water does not end; it arches. You can see the great wall of blue and bronze bulging outward over the drop, holding back leagues of depth with nothing more than its own impossible skin and the glassy lace of the creature that lives within it.

The current slows as it approaches that vast, curved lens. You come to rest against a cushion of maiden-moss that has lodged in the lee of a stone lintel—one of the old works, its thumb-wide spiral channels clogged with plum-colored ferns.

You settle into the crook between the ancient cut stone and the living water-wall. 

From here, through the three-mile curve of the meniscus, you can look down. Far below, through the distortion of the curved liquid, the dry deserts of the planet lie spread like a cracked yellow hide, scorched, silent, and terribly empty. You see the dust storms crawling across the plains like tiny gray caterpillars. You see the jagged, splintered teeth of mountains that were never rounded by water, sharp enough to cut the eye.

A fine, dry wind beats against the exterior of the meniscus, kilometers thick, making the glass-clear sinews of the rim-net hum with a high, thin vibration that tastes faintly of salt and lime.

You tuck yourself deeper into the moss. The seed within you is cool, heavy, and infinitely patient. 

You are not the mind that built the drains, nor the hand that carved the lintel. You are not the terrible net that holds the sea upon the mountain’s shoulder, straining its sinews against the void. You are the thing that rests in the lap of their enormous labor. 

You will stay here for an age, or two, or three, rocking in the milk-warm alkali, breathing the mint-scented gas as it pearls up from the weed, watching the turquoise and lemon thoughts of the weir-folk flicker across the deep floor behind you, while before your face, through kilometers of curved and weeping glass, the dry world burns itself away to dust, waiting for the mantle to slip.
