It begins not as a creature or an object, but as a condition of clearance. 

A gap. Not the large one behind the transformer where the ribcage rises and falls against the housing, but one of the others—narrower, forgotten even by the dog, where two unbolted channel irons lie parallel on the floor framing with three-eighths of an inch of stagnant air trapped between their flanges. 

To be here is, first, to have no verticality. The ceiling is not an idea that applies; there is only the underside of things. The underside of the tread-plate with its diamond pattern pressed backward into the iron like an imprint in lead; the underside of the cable tray, sagging slightly under three hundred pounds of neoprene and cross-linked polyethylene; the belly of the brine pipe, sweating through its seams where the black bitumen was brushed on too thin.

At first, you might think you are the grease itself. That thick, amber-black grease—lithium-based, smelling of petroleum distillates and heavy soap—pumped into the pillow blocks of the cooling fan bearings before the power was cut. It does not go anywhere. It gathers along the lip of the seal, takes the imprint of the dustless air, and holds it. 

Or perhaps the moisture. But the moisture is too busy. It is hurried by the gradients, dragged from the warm casings up toward the ceiling beams, condensed, dropped, licked away, sweated out again. The moisture has an itinerary, a desperate little circle that repeats every twenty seconds. 

No, to find oneself here is to be slower than the water, but less permanent than the iron. 

Look lower, where the tread-plates stop. 

There, beneath the open framing, where the construction timber was left behind because nobody had room to turn a crowbar to pry it out: two-by-fours of rough-sawn Douglas fir, soaked through with the alkaline wash from the final grout pour. They have not dried. In this cellar, wood does not rot, because the spores of the dry-rot fungi require higher oxygen and an atmosphere that isn’t saturated with ozone from the terminal blocks. Instead, the wood undergoes a cold, chemical petrifaction. The lime from the cement has leached into the soft summerwood rings, replacing the sap with calcium carbonate, turning the fibers white, chalky, and brittle as dried bone.

I am in the grain of that timber. 

Not the whole plank—that is too large, too dead—but the boundary layer where the alkaline fir touches the zinc primer of the lower flange. 

It is a place where three things meet: the continuous, low-amplitude shudder of the sixty-cycle transformer, the damp lime soaking upward through capillary pull, and the stray voltage from an ungrounded terminal board overhead that bleeds four micro-amps into the steel every time the pneumatic valve *chuffs*.

Out of that tri-part contact, something is assembling. 

It doesn't have an ancestry. It doesn't have the lineage of the dog, whose ancestors chased badgers across Yorkshire hedgerows or slept by the coal fires of watchmen’s shacks. It has no memories of grass, because no part of it was ever above the curb level of the city above. It is native only to the convergence.

It starts as a crust. 

A crust that spreads very slowly along the underside of the flange, using the vibration to walk. The sixty-cycle hum is an engine of locomotion for anything small enough: microscopic flakes of zinc oxide and silica, tumbled against one another by the tremor, drift toward the low-pressure eddy behind the flange bolt. There they settle. They don't form a dead mineral scab; they are glued by an exuded mucus that has the consistency of thinned shellac—secreted by the white, hair-like mycelia that have crossed over from the horsehair felt, but altered now, hybridized with the sizing and the grease until they are no longer purely vegetable.

It has an edge that feels. 

Not with nerves, but with conductivity. Where the crust is moist with the brine-sweat, it carries the four micro-amps. Where it dries out against the warm backdraft of the transformer, the current drops to zero. That is its sense of world: not light and dark, not sound and silence, but *conductive* and *open-circuit*. 

It likes the margin where the circuit is nearly closed. It creeps toward the dog’s rear hock.

The dog does not feel it, because it moves at the speed of lime curing under water—a millimeter every three weeks. But the dog’s hock, tucked hard against the iron channel, has shed a tiny pile of dander and grey hair-stubs where the skin is calloused. 

That is the feast. 

Not meat—nothing here can handle meat—but the keratin. The tough, sulfurous protein of the dog’s old life, shed as it turns into something slate-colored and mechanical. The crust absorbs the hair, dissolving the keratin with an enzyme that smells intensely of stale turnip and battery acid. It leaves the pigment behind, incorporating the dark granules into its own body until the crust, which was chalk-white, turns the dull, leaden charcoal of the room.

If you looked down with a glass through the floor framing, past the bundles of lead-sheathed cable, you would see it now not as a growth, but as an *embossment*.

It has begun to mimic the hardware. 

Because it grows along the vibration lines—the nodal points where the steel hums least—it does not form round colonies like bread mold or the smooth brackets of bracket-fungus. It forms straight, parallel ridges. It looks like a poorly cast gasket. It looks like the bead of vulcanized rubber that squeezes out when a pipe-flange is drawn down too tight with an air-wrench.

It is three inches from the dog’s left pad.

The dog shifts. The *tink-tink* of its squared-off claws on the steel plate is an earthquake through the channel iron. A shower of dried scale falls from the web of the beam, dusting the crust with iron oxide. The crust doesn't reject it; it takes the red powder into its wet margin, turning brick-brown at the lip, thickening its wall.

I am becoming the thing that will remain when the dog’s thirty-second drink fails.

Because eventually, the water will stop. A compressor on the fifty-fourth level will trip; a brine line will freeze solid and cease to sweat; or the marine lamp will finally burn through its thick carbon filament with a tiny *pop*, and the amber circle on the floor will vanish into the absolute blue-black of the deep ground. 

When the dog stops breathing—when its shallow, sixty-cycle chest finally hitches on a regulator-click and does not draw back—its warmth will bleed out into the Portland cement in less than six hours. The transformer will keep humming, but the thermal seam will collapse. The cold will take the space behind the housing.

Then the dog will be mine.

Not with the violence of maggots—there are no flies here, no beetles, no bluebottles to lay eggs in the corners of the lips. The dog will not bloat. The air is too cold and too dry for the gut bacteria to do their soft, wet work; they will chill into dormancy and die in the acid of their own wastes.

Instead, the crust will come off the channel iron. 

It will climb the calloused hock first, following the oil track left by the grease on the fur. It will advance through the shingles of the slate-gray coat like frost spreading through a pile of iron shavings. It will take three years to cross the ribs. It will lay down a fine, tough film of calcium silicate and mineralized rubber over the leather of the nose, through the broken whiskers, across the clouded lenses of the eyes that watched the red indicators for so long.

It will turn the animal into an exact, hollow cast of itself—a pipe-fitting made of hair and salt, gasketed to the deck by its own calluses, perfectly level, completely silent.

Until then, I am only the cold strip between the bolts. 

I am the tongue of lead-colored matter that waits for the counterweight to clink. I do not have a voice, but I have a tension. When the room drops its three beads of water—*one, two, three*—and the dog swallows them with that dry, leathery click of its palate, a faint, four-micro-amp twitch runs from the timber, through the zinc, across my margin, and into the tip of its black claw. 

A greeting between two things that were never planned, converging under the work, neither of us knowing which of us is the machine, and which is only what happens when the door is shut.
