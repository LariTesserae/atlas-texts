At first, there is only the sensation of shear. 

The brine does not flow across you; it drags like chilled glycerine, parting around whatever you are with the slow, reluctant yield of oil. If there is a self to begin with, it is dispersed in the tasting of gradients. A plume of bitter lithium here; a fraction of a milligram more calcium two millimeters to the left; a trace of stale ammonia drifting down from an overhung culvert. There are no eyes to tell where the water ends and the stone begins. There is only the texture of resistance.

For a time, you might imagine you are one of the high, weightless things. A juvenile drifting down an archway, belly lit with stolen cyan, legs kicking in frantic, drunken arpeggios that leave micro-vortices in the syrup. You think perhaps you are the thread of silk trailing from a spinneret, swinging across the black void between two needle-spires, waiting for an eel to pass so you can catch the draft of its belly. You feel the sulfur-yellow pulse of a drifting medusa brushing against your flank, cold and non-committal, its crystalline bell whispering of higher tiers where the water is marginally less bitter.

But no. The buoyancy is wrong. There is no lift in your limbs. The water is not holding you up; it is merely delaying your arrival. 

You try to twitch a pleopod, and the movement is small, muffled, dense. The saffron gut inside you is no longer a bright, loose knot coiled in clear lymph; it is crowded, pinched against the ribs by two monstrous, symmetrical weights that swell across your back like twin sacks of damp flour. The lime-sacs. They are so full they hum with chemical heat. They ache with an unbearable, alkaline fullness, drawing in the raw minerals of the abyss and turning them into thick, milky paste that presses against the seams of your dorsal plates. 

You are not above. You are already sinking.

It is not a fall. In water this heavy, falling takes half a lifetime. You drift downward through the tiers, grazing past the stacked tenements. You feel the rapid, feather-tick click of antennae against your own as you pass through crowded intersections—hundreds of brief, impersonal interrogations: *Full? Full. Dense? Dense. Descend.* 

An eel slides past through a conduit bored straight through a residential block. Its flank, cool and hairless as wet rubber, brushes along your side-plates. You do not flinch; you flatten your talons against the masonry and let the animal’s passage press you into the floor, welcoming the momentary draught of fresh trench-water it drags in its wake. When it has passed, you do not rise again. Your legs are too short now, tipped with heavy amber hooks that no longer suit swimming. They want only to dig into chalk. They want purchase.

You crawl downward. The cyan light of the moss gutters begins to thin, choked out by the increasing mass of the spires. Up above, the city was all filigree, lace-bridges, and fluted balconies designed to trap the falling drift. Down here, where the fissure narrows into a razor-sharp V of virgin basalt, the architecture has abandoned elegance. The pillars are thick as thumbs, lumpy and gouty, swollen with layer upon layer of hastily plastered cement. 

Here, the water does not even slip; it stagnates in the pockets between the footings, thick with the discarded mucus of a million moldings. The cold is different here—it is not the cold of open space, but the cold of the rock itself, unyielding and razor-edged, smelling of freshly shattered iron and silicate.

You reach the foundation.

You find a seam where the white aragonite meets the glass-black volcanic basalt. The rock here is raw; it was cracked open only days or hours ago, its edge sharp enough to shear a chitinous limb cleanly at the coxa. Between the rock and the bottom-most arch, there is a gap—a hairsbreadth of empty brine where the stresses of the trench are pulling the city apart. The wall above is groaning, transmitting that low, rhythmic thrum straight into the liquid core of your body. 

*Build here.*

The chemical imperative is not a thought; it is an unbearable pressure behind the eyes. You anchor your rear claws into the basalt shelf. The glass is slick, but the amber hooks find a micro-fissure and seat themselves with a brittle *snick*. You hunch. 

The dorsal plates slide apart. The release is an agony of relief. The cold, alkaline curd oozes from the cleft in your back—a single, glistening bead of concentrated lime, cloudy as curds, heavy as lead. 

Before the brine can leach away its binding oils, your front legs are already moving. You do not possess trowels; your front tarsi are the trowels, their spatulate brushes stroking, patting, spreading the secretion into the gap between the glass stone and the old masonry. Another worker is beside you—you can feel the dry, papery rattle of her side-plates against yours—and together you smooth the bead flat. Twelve seconds. The aragonite needles crystallize. The paste stiffens under your brushes. It grows warm, then stone-cold, locking your front claws in place for a fraction of an instant before you tear them free to receive the next drop.

You do this until the sacks are hollowed. Until the saffron gut is shriveled and the lymphatic fluid in your thorax is cloudy and gray.

And then comes the stillness.

You try to unhook the rear legs from the basalt, but the mortar you laid ten minutes ago has crept backward around your tarsi. Aragonite crystals have grown into the micro-pores of your chitin. Your shell has ceased to be an envelope; it has become an aggregate. The joints of your abdomen, long calcified by the constant weeping of the lime-glands, will no longer bend. You cannot hunch again. There is nothing left inside to push out.

A young rigger—clear as distilled water, belly glowing with fresh cyan—scurries over your head. Its tiny, delicate legs patter across your carapace without hesitation, leaving faint footprints of biological silk. It does not know you. It does not avoid you. To the rigger, you are simply the new contour of the footing.

You do not fight it. You draw your segmented antennules inward, curling them beneath the blunt hood of your face so they will not be broken. You press your forehead flat against the cold basalt, tucking your mouthparts flush against the stone. 

Behind you, you feel the approach of others. Heavy masons, sliding their swollen abdomens along the newly hardened ledge. The plates of their backs part with that familiar, slow slide. 

The first drop of their lime falls warm upon your lower segments. 

The brushes of their front legs begin to stroke. They work deliberately, rhythmically, spreading the fresh curd across your carapace, filling the hollows between your ribs, smoothing you into the fillet of the arch. The heat of their reaction warms your back one last time—a fleeting two degrees of comfort in the freezing syrup—before the aragonite sets, cold and white and final.

You are no longer the builder. You are the viaduct. 

Beneath your forehead, through centimeters of basalt, you feel the fissure expand—a micron’s breath of raw movement in the deep earth. The stone stretches. The stress travels upward through the footings, passing cleanly through your petrified shell and into the spire above without a single crack. 

In the total, muffling silence of the trench, you hold the city up.
