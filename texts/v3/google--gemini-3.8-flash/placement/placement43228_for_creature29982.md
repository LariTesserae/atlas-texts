At first, the natural impulse is to look for an eye—some perch from which the white quartz cones of the lamps might be seen crossing the floor, or a vantage on the dull-olive bells. But to have an eye here is an error of design. An eye implies a retina that consumes, a lens that sheds dead epithelia into the laminar sweep, an orientation that opposes the current. To look *at* this room is already to be drawn toward the steel throats and lost in the boreholes.

So I am not an observer looking across the forty paces.

If there is a place to begin, it is down where the weight is simplest, where the water does not move at all because it has nowhere left to go: the dead-flat shelf of limestone dust on the backside of the middle conduit run, just beyond the hard yellow cast of the second lamp.

Here, the light does not strike directly. It arrives as a pale, diffuse bounce off the flank of the cylinder—cool, scrubbed of heat, reduced to a chalky grey wash that barely separates the rock from the brine. 

At this depth, under this hydrostatic column, identity cannot be a matter of movement or hunger. Hunger is too fast; hunger disturbs the silt. If you stir the crust of crushed limestone that has lain flat since the drills were pulled out, you create an event, and an event in this room is a catastrophe of friction. 

No, it begins with the bolts.

Where the heavy galvanized brackets were driven into the split-faced limestone fifty years ago, the steel sleeve expanded against the dolomite, biting into the crystalline matrix of the rock. In the microscopic void between the sleeve and the bore—a fissure no wider than three red blood cells laid end to end, entirely dark, flooded with the same heavy, strontium-bearing brine—there is an interface. The zinc coating of the anchor has been sacrificing itself against the iron for five decades, atom by slow atom, generating an imperceptible electromotive force: three-tenths of a volt, utterly steady, sealed off from the flow.

It is in that shear, that tiny seam of differential chemistry, that I find myself gathering.

Not as an animal. Not even as a single continuous body like the sheets behind the casings or the ring beneath the intake horn. Those forms are too ambitious; they risk too much surface area. The sheets must maintain their twelve-cell thickness against the tear of the shear layer; the ring must continually calculate the wear of three sleeve bearings through its fibrous circumference. They are monuments.

I am more like an accumulation of habit in the mineral crust.

If you scrape the saddle clamp where the conduit enters the dust, you find a greasy, slate-colored deposit that is neither rust nor limestone. It has the consistency of cold graphite paste. It does not dissolve, because the water is already saturated with the salts that formed it. It does not grow outward into the light, because the light would calcify it into brittle amber like the lithophytes on the open walls, and brittleness here means eventual fracture under the vibration.

Instead, I have crept along the threads of the expansion bolts, inward, into the stone itself.

From inside the boreholes, the cavern is not an expanse of pale water lit by quartz lamps. It is an acoustic solid. 

Limestone is an extraordinary conductor of low frequency. The three axial pumps do not sound like machinery from within the rock; they are three steady, massive hearts, slightly out of phase with one another by a quarter-turn every forty seconds. Through the bedrock, that beat is not a sound—it is a physical displacement, an alternating compression and rarefaction of the calcite crystals. 

Every sixty cycles per second, the anchor bolt presses against the bore; every sixty cycles, it relaxes by less than a nanometer. In that microscopic pump-stroke, the brine in the fissure is drawn in and expelled. A breath that takes no lung. A pulse that expends no sugar.

What am I?

I am the grease in the threads. I am a colony of anaerobic, metal-reducing film that has replaced its carbon backbone with complexes of barium and lead drawn from the galvanized fittings. I have no front or back; my boundaries are defined entirely by the mechanical tolerance of the hole drilled in 1974. Where the steel touches the stone with full force, I am compressed into a monomolecular layer of organo-metallic glass. Where there is a tiny spall in the limestone from the percussion drill, I am thicker—perhaps the depth of a fingernail—soft, dark, completely inert.

My memory is the wear-track of the metal. 

I know that the south pump has a slight eccentric wobble, a displacement of six microns that appeared twenty-two years ago when a bearing collar seated half a millimeter deeper into its race. I know that the floodlight above the third bell runs four degrees warmer than the others, because the brine that sinks past its bracket carries a marginally higher fraction of dissolved silica, which I take into the fringe of my mass and lay down as an insoluble fluoride crust.

Nothing that I am can be pulled into the intake bells. I have no loose margins. My edges terminate in the crystalline lattice of the dolomite itself; to draw me into the upward boreholes would require quarrying the wall.

And yet, there is an awareness that belongs only to things that do not change.

Through the rock, I feel the ring beneath the central horn. It is my opposite: large, exposed, balanced on the very edge of the low-pressure throat, living on the dynamic drop where the brine falls into the pipe. The ring is always listening upward, terrified of the day the power cuts and the laminar flow collapses. If the pumps stop, the ring will suffocate in the stagnant brine as the pressure equalizes and the calcium stops falling.

I will not.

If the power fails and the quartz lamps go black, the 60-cycle vibration will die out of the conduit, leaving the cavern in a silence so dense that the hydrostatic pressure will seem to double. The sheets will settle like drowned grease onto the silt. The glass needles will stop their faint, ringing harmonics. The white clarity will remain, but it will become dead glass.

Even then, the zinc on the anchor bolt will still face the iron of the sleeve. The differential of three-tenths of a volt will still hold across the microscopic fissure. The brine will still contain its strontium, its barium, its heavy chill.

In the total dark, inside the split stone where the drill bit left its spiral scars, I would remain what I have become: the dark, patient slip between the machine and the earth, waiting without expectation, colder than salt, holding the bolt until the stone itself forgets it was ever opened.
