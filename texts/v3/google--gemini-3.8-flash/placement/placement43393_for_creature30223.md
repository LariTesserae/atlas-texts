At first, it feels like nothing more than an obstruction in the run-off. 

Something stuck under an archway where two roofs meet—not quite a person, not quite a beam of split birch, but a thickened knot where the drainage has to part and find two paths instead of one. 

The cold comes first. Not the sharp, needle-bite of wind on the plateau, but the quiet, flat ache that sits in the marrow like gray silt. It is the cold of water that has traveled four miles through solid ironstone before finding the air, carrying the taste of wet gravel and subterranean copper. It runs over the collarbone. It runs in a steady, paper-thin sheet across the ribs, so uniform that after an hour you can no longer tell where your skin ends and the slick face of the basalt begins.

I would not be on the high bridges. Up there, where the light falls silver at noon, the air is too anxious; the young men bounce on the birch poles with their flint knives ready, squinting against the falling shale, their muscles always taut against the drop. 

Nor would I be among the sheep in the dark vaults, listening to the dry grind of their teeth against sage. That is too dry, too preserved, too much like being tucked into a drawer before the day is done.

No, I am down where the water gathers its weight—where the gutters carved into the living rock join together like fingers into a palm, just above the drowned bins where the loaches turn. 

Here, the tiers do not rest on native rock. They rest on each other, and on what was placed here three generations back. 

I am a shorer of the third tier. A wedge-man. 

My body is forty years into this cut, which means the work of turning to stone has already begun without the formality of dying. The joints of my fingers have lost their slide; they have closed into tight, permanent hooks, like the root-knees of dwarf willows that grow on the rim, but they fit the lip of a slate shim as if they were chipped from the same bed. My skin has long forgotten the sun. It has the pale, yellowish tint of sheep fat where the tallow has soaked in, and where the tallow has worn away against the rock, it is black with chimney soot and bruised the dull purple of a crushed mussel shell.

I sit in the hollow behind a tier-post. It is a space no larger than an upturned wicker crate, wedged between the foundation of a smoking-hut and the sheer, weeping face of the canyon wall. 

My knees are drawn up against my chest. My back is pressed flat to the shale—not to rest, but to read. 

Through the grease-rubbed leather over my spine, I feel the fracture breathe. The rock does not stay still; it shifts under the weight of the water table like a sleeper turning under heavy hides. When the subterranean pulse begins—an hour before midday, and again when the silver light dies off the northern rim—there is no sound. There is only a stiffening. The water in the chink behind my neck stops weeping and begins to push, spraying in a fine, atomized mist that smells of wet copper. The post in front of my shins groans once, a low, fibrous scrape that vibrates through my teeth.

That is when the mallet moves.

I do not use iron—iron here would swell with orange rust and split the masonry within three seasons. My mallet is a knob of river-basalt, drilled through with a bone bow-drill and fitted with a handle of petrified hornbeam that has been seasoned in the cold run-off until it is heavier than lead. 

Beside my hip, stacked in order of thickness, are the shims: slips of blue-black slate, three fingers wide, their edges beveled with a wet sandstone. 

When the surge comes, the timber lifts half a thumbnail’s height off its bed-stone. Just half. If you are not there to feel it in the seat of your hips, you miss it. The water slips under the foot of the post; the whole upper tier—four dwellings, twenty people, three hearths—hangs for the space of two heartbeats on the rawhide stays alone. 

In that space, my hand knows the gap without looking. One tap of the bone ring against the post: *stand back above*. Then the slate slides in. A single, muffled blow from the mallet—*thunk*, dead and flat against the wet awning above my head. The shim seats. The timber comes down upon it with the dull, grinding crunch of stone biting stone. 

The weir holds. The alley above does not sag. The girl carrying the basket of tallow-scraps does not lose her footing on the birch bridge. She feels only a small, sharp hitch beneath the soles of her grease-cured wraps, like a horse shifting its hoof in the mud, and she keeps walking.

Between the surges, there is only the listening. 

In the dark behind me, wedged upright into the narrowest crevice where the cold spring jets hardest, is my brother’s father. 

He has been there six winters now. At first, when we levered him into the crack, his reed wraps were green and smelled of the upper steppe; they rotted away in the first month, carried off down the drainage gutters in stringy gray tatters. Now, he is stripped down to the gray marrow. The minerals have had their way with him. His shoulders are the color of river slate, smooth and greasy to the touch, and when the water rushes over his breastbone, it doesn't give him gooseflesh; it hums against him as if he were an outcrop of black ironstone. 

His left shoulder has already taken the weight of the lintel above him. Next winter, or the winter after, when the birch pole beside him finally yields to the spongy gray rot, we will not need to bring down fresh timber from the rim. We will merely knock away the rotted wood and drive three slate shims between his collarbone and the beam. He will hold it better than the birch ever did. He does not swell with the wet; he does not dry and crack when the tallow lamps are brought near.

Sometimes, when the cold gets into my teeth and the tallow on my throat begins to flake, I lean my forehead back until it rests against his jaw. 

It is very cold. Colder than ice, because it does not melt; it is the temperature of the stone four hundred feet down, where the frost never reaches because the mountain is too heavy for it. But it is solid. In a place where the floor is always sliding, where the rafters are always creeping toward the sump, where every joint is wet and moving, his chin does not give.

I tap my bone ring against his jawbone once. 

*Clink.* 

A hard, clean sound, sharper than speech. It carries through the basalt, through the lime-crust, down into the black troughs where the pale loaches are circling in the dark, and up into the soles of the children who are learning to walk with their toes hooked over the wet slate.

It means nothing. It is not a call, and it is not a prayer. 

It is only to hear that the stone is still whole, that the cut has not widened, and that between the rock that wants to close and the water that wants to drown, there is still an inch of room for a man to sit with his mallet, holding the world up by the thickness of a slate flake.
