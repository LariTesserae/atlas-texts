First there is only the hum—not in the ears, which have long since given up trying to sift out the roar of the twin falls, but in the crest of the hip and the hinge of the jaw. 

It enters through the timber. It is the three-hundred-ton weight of water dropping forty cubits onto the granite anvil, carried through the wet heartwood of the lower buttress, up the braided root-gutters, through the hemlock stakes, and directly into the balls of my feet. If I lift one heel, the world goes half-quiet; if I set it down, the mountain resumes its low, iron droning against my ribs.

I would be down where the light gives up its sharpness. Not in the upper baskets where the boys swing out to snare parakeets and their capes rattle like dry sedge in the downdraft, and not quite down in the black drip where the hangers hook into the anchor roots above the gray mile of nothing. 

I am in the swell between: Tier Two of the Crawl, where the houses are no longer things people built, but things the tree is slowly swallowing down its throat.

My feet tell you everything before I do. They are wide, yellow-rimmed with tallow-soaked horn along the flanks, the toes spatulate and restless. They do not cross a hurdle by stepping; they feel for the grain, rolling on the outer edges to clear the green slime that coats the bamboo like boiled suet. My skin is the dark, bruised blue of a wet slate shingle, cross-hatched with pale rings where the moss-bloom thrives in the warm creases of my groin, the crook of my knees, the soft hollow behind my ears. It smells of baker’s yeast and creek-muck. It is the only blanket that never rots.

In my lap lies an adze with a blade of chipped black basalt, lashed to a curved elk-horn handle with five-ply nettle cord. It is heavy, cold, and dripping.

My job is the wedge.

The great fig-cypress does not hate us; it does not know us. But it grows with a thick, blind momentum that would crush an infant in its cradle simply by adding an inch of secondary bark between the equinox and the first hail. If left alone for three seasons, the cambium would roll over the oval door-frames of our huts, seal the smoke-slots with pale, fat callus-wood, and turn our grandfathers’ hearths into hard, knotty burls three hundred feet above the gorge. 

So I sit in the damp notch of an eighth-century branch-joint, right where the floor of my sister’s storehouse meets the living bark, and I tap.

*Thnk. Thnk.*

I drive wedges of seasoned hemlock into the breathing seam. Hemlock is bitter; the tree shrinks from its acid just enough to pause, turning its sap away from the wound and swelling around the peg instead of over the osier wickerwork. I am the grit in the eyelid of the wood. I keep the crevice from blinking shut.

Across my shoulder, wedged into the greasy wool of my hood where the body heat pools thickest, lies an old bitch weasel named Skit. Her belly is warm, bald from nursing four litters in the rush-lining of my roof, and her front left paw is missing two toes from an encounter with a pucu under the flume. She does not move when I swing the adze. She only shifts her wedge of a skull, pressing her dry, cold snout against the cord of my neck, her breath smelling faint and sharp of chewed crayfish shell and tallow. When a rat scratches inside the inner weave three cubits to the left, her ribs tighten against mine like a pulled bowstring. We don’t need words for that; she twitches, my knuckle raps the wood, she slides down my arm like an oiled ribbon and vanishes through the wicker.

From above, down through the green chimney of the canopy, comes a whistle: three high, broken chirps, clipped off hard against the teeth, followed by two flat palm-slaps against a bridge rail. 

*Runoff rising. Clear the trip-hammers.*

I do not look up. To look up in the Crawl is to take half a cup of frigid spray straight into the eyes. Instead, I rap the stone face of the adze against the living wood—*once, twice, long pause, once*—a vibration that travels forty yards along the horizontal bough to where the boys are tending the bark-troughs. They’ll feel it through their shins while they’re hauling the wet bundles of nettle. 

The light here is never more than a cold, grey tea. It seeps down through five tiers of wicker nests, through hanging curtains of clubmoss that weigh sixty pounds a yard when soaked, through the steam rising off the communal braziers where the elders are rendering down guan-fat in slate kettles. It is a thick, crowded dimness, full of the grease of cooking birds, the bitter tang of wood-tannin, and the ceaseless, high-pitched squall of the parakeet colonies quarreling over the bromeliad snails fifty feet above our heads.

Sometimes, when the wind shears off the high desert and falls straight down the chimney of the cleft, the fog is scoured out in three gasps. 

Then—for perhaps fifty heartbeats—the world yawns wide. 

You can look down between your own bare toes, past the split-bamboo hurdle, past the dark bulges of the lower huts, past the grey anchor roots that taper into threads, and see the terrible, dry emptiness of the gorge bottom miles below: dry yellow dust, red rocks like dropped peas, a sky so vast and pale and vacant that it makes the stomach heave against the ribs. A world with nothing over its head. A world without a lid.

Whenever that happens, the entire tier goes still. Every child drops to its belly, their broad toes clawing into the hurdles, their hands clamped over their ears to block out the silence of that great void. We wait, shivering under our grease, our muscles locked in the misery of that naked daylight.

And then the cloud rolls back.

The first wet breath hits the rock wall with a soft *whuff*. The mist pours back over the lip like curds dumped from a pail. The air thickens, grows heavy, sweet with the smell of wet rot and bruised cypress leaf. The drops begin their small, rhythmic battering against the rawhide domes. The cataract reclaims its belly-roar, shaking the bone in my teeth.

Skit comes back out of the thatch, her chin red with rat-blood, her whiskers twitching. She curls back into the hollow of my throat, wet-furred and smelling of iron.

I pick up the hemlock wedge. I feel the grain with the horn of my thumb. The tree is pushing back, hard and slow and blind, trying to make itself whole again where we have carved our room to breathe. 

I set the wood to the seam, raise the adze, and strike.
