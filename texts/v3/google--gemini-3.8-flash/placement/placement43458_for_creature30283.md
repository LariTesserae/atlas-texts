At first, there is only the sensation of being held in a pair of calipers.

The stone is at my back—not flat, but hollowed out by the small of my spine, the way water hollows a pebble over an age it will not remember. The knees are drawn up so tight against the breastbone that the ribs and the kneecaps tick against one another whenever the draft gusts hard enough to shift the weight of my vest. 

That is the first thing: the vest. It does not yield. It is made of the thick rind of the three-year spindle-succulent, boiled in bitter tallow until it ceased to be a plant and became something closer to boiled leather or horn, then cross-stitched with the stiff grey beards of the ceiling moss. When I draw in air, the vest does not expand with me; my lungs must find room inside it, pushing sideways into the hollows beneath my armpits, small and cautious, like mice turning in a larder. To breathe deeply here would be an obscenity, a great vulgar spilling of wet breath into a chimney that is always drinking.

My chin rests on the shelf where my two shins cross. From here, looking down between my ankles, there is seventy cubits of amber shadow before the next balcony, and beneath that, another, and then the dark talus where the drawers crouch over their holes like ticks on a dog’s belly.

I am not a drawer. I do not have the heavy, squatting shoulders of the men who work the winches, whose arms are scarred white from the thrashing of the great leathery tails when a frog comes up out of the basalt slips half-drowned in its own slime. 

Nor do I have the narrow, bird-snatching thumbs of the boys who run the upper rigging, the ones whose knuckles are always scabbed with lichen-burn from clambering into the martin-bottles before the dawn dial strikes.

My thumbs are broad, flat, and hard as horn scrapers. They are the thumbs of someone who pares.

Beside my right heel, nestled in the hollow where two shale slabs meet, sits the flat basalt mortar and the three adzes: one of chert, flaked fine as a dried moth’s wing; one of grey river-slate that must have come over the lip of the mesa fifty generations back, before the trails fell into the salt; and the smallest one, the thumb-knife, carved from the breastbone of an old male bustard.

All around me, the tier is sleeping its dry sleep. 

To a stranger looking down from the crack four thousand cubits up, the street would look like a woodyard where bundles of dry laths have been stood against the huts to season. There are twelve of us on this terrace alone, all folded into the same triangular crouch, our chins on our knees, our braids greased down with cricket-fat until they look like blackened sinew ropes dried in the sun. We do not twitch. Even the old woman two doors down—the one whose left knee was crushed when the roof beam of the seed-granary slipped during the great drought twelve seasons ago—does not shift. She has learned the mountain’s first law: *to move is to smoke.* To fidget is to throw a handful of your own blood to the wind.

So we sit. The draft strokes our ears, smelling faintly of the deep earth: burnt sulphur, the cold tallow of the vents, and that low, unmistakable stench of frog-skin that means the haulers have struck three leagues down.

I wait for the yellow tooth.

It begins not with light, but with a change in the wood.

The house behind me is framed with the dead ribs of the spindle-tree—not the young, waxy stems we use for weaving, but the heartwood of the old giants that grow out of the wet floor vents, wood so dense that when it dies it does not rot; it turns into a kind of reddish iron that rings like a pot when struck. As the sun reaches the rim of the cart-track above, four thousand cubits through the cleft, the draft in the chimney speeds up. It warms by half a breath. The spindle-poles in the lintel expand with a tiny, musical *clink*, like a bead of glass dropped onto slate.

My eyes open. I do not look up. You never look up into the slot; the light there will burn the center out of your eye before you can blink, leaving a purple scab in your vision that lasts three moons.

I look down at my feet.

The yellow edge hits the communal floor first, down on the third tier where the broad threshing slab lies like an altar. A square of violent, screaming gold, so bright it hurts the bridge of the nose through closed lids.

Then the terrace drops.

It is not an order. No one clicks a bone. But the whole town comes off its haunches at once, like a flock of plovers rising from a shingle beach when the shadow of a hawk passes. 

The heavy vests clatter together—a thousand beetle wing-cases knocking on the stone like hail on dried hides. The sound goes *tlac-tlac-tlac-tlac* down the curve of the belly, bouncing from the tenth tier to the fifth, until the amphitheater is humming like an overturned bee-skep.

I reach out my flat thumbs.

The boy from the sixth terrace is already there, his bare toes gripping the lip of my balcony, his sinew tether trailing behind him like a thin grey snake. He has three martin-chicks tucked into the bib of his vest, their naked pink heads poking out through the lichen fringe, their yellow beaks gaping for the grease-mash. In his right hand he carries what I have waited all morning for: a gourd of the bitter scrapings.

He doesn't speak. He drops the gourd into the basalt hollow between my feet, clicks his tongue twice against the back of his teeth—*k’tik*—to say he needs the awls sharpened before the beam climbs past the roof of the water-house, and then he is gone, swinging down the ladder-pole by his heels, his brown legs flashing in the amber bounce-light.

I pick up the bustard-knife.

The gourd is warm. It is always warm. It comes from the lowest bins, where the cliff-mice live in the warm chaff from the seed-pounders. It is full of the dross: the discarded wing-cases of the ground-beetles, the dried skin that peels from the legs of the frogs when the drawers rub them with mineral salt, and the fat, oily rinds of the seeds that have begun to turn rancid in the dry bins.

This is my trade: I am the *seamer*. 

The drawers bring the water up; the martin-boys bring the grey paste from the high skies; the women weave the rattle-skirts. But the city itself—these ten tiers of dry-stacked shale that hang like a wasps’ nest over nothing—would slide into the salt plains by tomorrow noon if someone did not tend the seams.

I take a lump of the fat, hard as tallow, and lay it on the slate. With the chert blade, I begin to shave it.

The light is on the fifth tier now. It is moving fast. We have perhaps thirty breaths before it hits my ledge, and while it is here, the fat will run like oil. Once it leaves, the cold draft will come behind it and freeze the resin into flint within fifty heartbeats.

I work the paste with a little of the fine, red sand that drifts down from the ceiling vaults. You must know the balance with your fingers, not your eyes: too much sand, and the chinking will crumble under the heel of a climbing boy; too much grease, and the beetles will eat it out from between the stones during the dark hours, leaving the rooms open to the sulfur draft.

It must smell like three things: the bitter oil of the spindle, the sharp iron of fresh dung, and the dry grease of the tallow-pits. When it smells of all three, it is sweet.

The light hits me.

It is like being struck across the shoulders with a flat cedar plank. The cold that sits perpetually in the hollow of my throat is burned away in an instant; my skin feels as though it were being drawn tight over my cheekbones by four cords of sinew. The mites in the lichen of my vest begin to run, tiny red specks scurrying out to the ends of the grey moss fibers to die in the glare. I lift my face just enough to let the beam strike the grease on my chin.

For these sixty heartbeats, I am not a thing carved from boxwood. I have a pulse in my wrists. I can see the blue veins inside the translucent horn of my scraper; I can see the dust-devils dancing in the column of light above the threshing floor, where four boys are running circles on the stone to crush the dried seed-pods under their calloused heels, their laughter coming up the rock like the snapping of dry kindling: *kh-kh-kh-kh.*

I don’t look at them. I lean down into the crack between my own door-sill and the ledge of the terrace below.

The seam has opened two fingers wide since yesterday. The mountain is breathing out; the crack has widened as the thermal air swells the rock. 

I take the hot paste, glistening and translucent like dark honey in the sun-glare, and drive it deep into the dark with the flat of the bustard-bone. I force it home until the shale bites back against the tool, until the grease squirts through the vent below and I hear the soft *fwt* of it landing on the terrace of the family beneath me. They will not mind; they will scrape it off their roof with their fingernails and use it to rub into their children’s hair against the ticks.

"Hold," I say to the stone. 

I don't say it in the throat. I say it with the marrow-whistle between my teeth, three sharp, flat trills that signify *it is seated.*

A girl on the ladder two spans away catches the note. She stops, balanced on one toe on the worn cedar rung, a basket of dried cricket-cake balanced on her hip. She looks down at the black line I have just filled, tilts her head like a martin on a twig, and taps her thigh-bone twice with her knuckles. *Good.*

Then the light lifts.

It doesn't fade; it simply steps up, like an old man climbing a ladder. It leaves my ledge. It takes the terrace above. 

The heat goes with it as cleanly as if a skin had been peeled from my back. The cold, grey draft from the floor vents—the old, sour breath of the deep earth—rushes back into the hollow to take its place.

The boy from the sixth terrace drops down beside me again, his hands empty now, his mouth glistening with the grey pap he has been sharing with the chicks. He takes the bustard-knife from my fingers, smells the edge to see that I have cleaned the grit from it, and slips it into the loop of his vest.

"The third frog was dry," he clicks at me, using the low palate-sound that doesn't carry beyond the lintel. "They brought it up three spans. Its throat was like an old shoe. The drawer says we will eat seed-meal for two moons."

I do not answer him. I am already drawing my knees back up toward my chin.

The vest stiffens as the tallow cools. The lichen fibers, which had softened for an hour in the sun, dry into a coarse fur that prickles against the salt-crust on my collarbone. My shins find their old cross-notch against the stone.

Down below, the frenzy is already folding itself away. The tools are slipping back into the leather loops; the children are being hauled back by their tethers, kicking against the shale walls like spiders on silk; the old men are sitting back down beside their piles of adzed spindle-wood, their eyes half-shut against the copper twilight.

The great column of dust in the center of the pocket slows its spin. It becomes a drift, then a settling, then a grey skin that covers the threshing floor until you cannot tell stone from man.

I put my chin down.

Through the bone of my jaw, resting on my kneecaps, I can feel the whole cliff. I can feel the sixteen tiers above me, their weight pressing down through the dry-stack until the shale groans in its resin seams. I can feel the winches on the bottom slope, where the drawers are greasing the hide ropes with tallow that has just stopped smoking. And deeper still, ten thousand cubits into the root of the mountain where the air is as hot as blood and smells of wet gravel, I can feel the slow, heavy shift of the great frogs turning in their beds of silt, waiting for the earth to sweat again.

I close my eyelids until there is only a slit of copper left.

The draft sighs through the spindle-trees. The bone cards clatter once, twice, together.

I am seated. The crack is full. The mountain can pull all it likes.
