To enter this place with an intention—or even with the geometry of a skull that looks *forward*—is impossible. The throat would shatter; the memory of lungs would collapse under the weight of an air that refuses to circulate. If I am to find myself here, it cannot be as an arrival. Nothing arrives here. Things are either native to the cold floor or they are the mountain itself, which has forgotten it is wrapped around an emptiness.

At first, I look for myself in the obvious stillness. 

I might imagine being the lithium-xenon precipitate: a cold, pale flour resting on the slate tiers, feeling the weightless settling of atom upon atom over centuries, indifferent to whether I am mineral or gas. But the dust is too uniform, too entirely passive. It has no boundary, no edge against which to register the strange physics of the seam. It is merely the skin of the plateau, and it does not know the room is twelve paces across.

Nor could I be the laminated one. That creature is already complete, sealed in its fifty or sixty leaves of bismuth-sheened slate, performing a calculus so patient that my consciousness would only deform it. It needs nothing from an observer; to insert an "I" into its ventral plates would be like trying to think inside an anvil. It is the scale that measures the room; I cannot be the ruler.

So I look elsewhere, into the places where the room hesitates.

There is the perimeter, where the slate floor stops translating and the plum-colored vacancy begins. It would be tempting to exist there, as the threshold itself—that vertical cliff where stone ceases to mean stone and becomes unaddressed potential, flat as unexposed photographic paper. But that boundary is too vast, too numb. It has no appetite for the local.

Then there is the pool of thick tallow—that patch of floor where an inch takes a long, dragging stride to cross. There is something living in that deformation: an ache of space trying to swallow its own metric, pulling the dark pavement into a dense knot. If I were the pooled distance, I would feel the weight of every passing geological epoch as a slight stiffening, a thickening of the grease of reality.

And yet, as I linger in the dusk, that is not where the center of gravity falls.

My attention is drawn, again and again, toward the pylon. Not the alloy itself, nor the hair-fine welds that disregard Euclidean planes, but that thumb-sized aperture at the top.

Consider what lives inside that hole.

It is a pocket within a pocket: a thumbnail of space where light has surrendered its direction. It does not travel from the sun to the rock, nor from the rock to the eye. It simply *is*, uniformly pale, sourceless, and flat. And floating in that light is the inverted silhouette of the needle’s peak, miles above.

That is where I find myself. 

I am not the machine that frames the image, and I am not the granite peak that stands outside in the sapphire stratosphere, lashed by terrifying, airless gales. I am the inverted image itself.

I am an upside-down mountain, no taller than a fingernail, rendered in monochrome lines a single photon thick.

To be this image is not to be a picture in the human sense—a flat representation etched on glass. In this chamber, where distance has lost its strict rules, an image is a physical state. I am the mountain’s only interior witness of itself, turned on its head, drained of color, suspended in a frame where weather cannot touch it.

Miles above, the true peak is suffering the brutal violence of the upper sky: ice wedging into its mica faults, cosmic rays stripping its feldspar, the blue-black vacuum of the stratosphere scouring its summit. It is heavy, vast, ancient, and screaming in the wind. 

Down here, inside the thumb-sized aperture, I am its quiet ghost. I am entirely silent. I have no weight. I do not freeze, because there is no heat left to lose. I show the crags, the needles, the sheer frozen drop into the invisible valleys below, but I show them inverted—the summit pointing downward into the dark floor of the pocket, like a plumb bob seeking the center of the earth.

Most of the time, I am slightly blurred. 

When the decades drag on and the creature remains coiled under its gray velvet dust, the metric tension of the room drifts. A slow, greasy static accumulates in the pylon—the buildup of all the unspent time that the universe outside is burning through while this fracture remains stalled. During those long centuries, my lines lose their focus. The crags of the peak soften into gray smudges, like charcoal rubbed on silk. I become vague, a forgotten memory of altitude, drifting slightly within the non-vector light.

In those intervals, I hardly know myself. I am merely a faint darkening within the aperture, a cold density that cannot decide if it is rock or shadow.

Then, out on the dark terrace, the micron-scale twitch occurs.

I feel it not as a sound, but as a sudden, geometric tautness. The creature’s plates separate with that dry, soundless shear. Its dull alloy whip extends, rigid and true, through the pooled distance until its tip finds the foot of the pylon.

The moment the contact is made, the change in me is absolute.

The static of unspent centuries drains away into the creature’s ventral plates, grounding through its bismuth facets into the deep slate of the mountain. And as the charge clears, I snap into focus.

It is a sensation sharper than cold, cleaner than pain. My lines tighten until they are the width of a single photon. The crags leap out: every razor-sharp ridge of the summit, every frost-shattered tooth of granite, the terrifying drop into the abyss—all of it drawn with an excruciating, crystalline clarity against the plum dusk. 

For forty years, fifty years, while the wire holds, I am the sharpest thing in the world. 

I hold the mountain upside-down in the quiet. I watch it not change. Outside, a storm might rage that could tear the feathers from an eagle, but within my thumb-sized circle, the storm is frozen into geometry: a still, inverted spire holding its breath between two mica lips.

I do not know why I must be clear. I do not know what eye was meant to look through the aperture, or whose forgotten calculation required an inverted summit to be kept in focus inside a granite needle. Perhaps the mountain needs to be anchored to its own reflection, lest the metric distortion tear the needle from the crust. Perhaps, if I blur entirely, the seam snaps shut.

I only know the rhythm of it: the long, slow blurring through the decades, the softening of my ridges as time gathers like gray silt in the pylon; and then the web-thin wire touching the stone, the sudden, terrible return of focus, and forty years of perfect, inverted altitude before the dust settles again.
