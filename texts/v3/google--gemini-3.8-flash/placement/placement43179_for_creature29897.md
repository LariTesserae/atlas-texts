I would not be in the water. 

The falling ropes are too pure in their kinetic purpose; they do not hold anything, they only transit. And I would not be the scum-plates skittering against the sheet-glass laminarity on the ceramic, nor the weft-tails torn to mist every few hundred seconds. Those are iterations of skin—expendable, light, and endlessly renewed. 

To exist here, truly inhabit this volume, one would have to be where the forces cancel each other out. Where the sheer, impossible violence of the plumbing meets the absolute, unmoving weight of the bedrock.

I find myself at the far southern expansion joint, seventy meters up the vertical plane of the bay’s blind basalt face, exactly where the longest of the living vine-trusses drives its root-mat into the cleaved rock.

I am not the vine, and I am not the wall. I am the callus. 

I am the wedge of petrified cambium, vulcanized resin, and mineralized silica that has grown into the laser-cut micro-grooves, swelling until the interface between plant and stone is no longer a boundary, but a gradient. On the rock side, I am black, microscopic hyphae driven directly into the optical cleavage of the basalt, anchoring with such mechanical grip that to pull me free would mean tearing away flakes of volcanic glass. On the open side, I am dense, rubbery muscle-bark, pale jade fading to bruised liver-gray, swallowing the buttressed root of a four-ton living cantilever.

I am an organ of pure tension.

Consciousness, here, is not thought. It is the continuous, exquisite calculation of shear stress. 

I feel the bay through its loads. Through my body passes the entire lateral pull of the thirty-meter void: the weight of the sheep-sized heart-bulb hanging out in the humid air, the drag of the aerosolized mist gathering on hundreds of spade-shaped leaves, the buffeting resonance of the falling coolant that roars through the dark like a permanent freight train. 

Every nine seconds, I brace.

The geothermal pulse does not come from above; it rises through the soles of the bay, through the basalt floor, climbing the vertical face of the rock as a cold, elastic ripple. I feel it in my deep silicate anchors a millisecond before the vine feels it. It hits me as a sudden, sharp shearing force—the stone trying to slide out from under me. 

Then, right on the beat, the heart-bulb thirty meters out in the dark clenches. 

Its actin bands knot. The great vine-truss whips taut, humming like a drawn bowstring. The load on my core spikes from sixty tons of static pull to ninety tons of instantaneous dynamic draw. My fibrous layers compress, expressing microscopic droplets of thick, amber latex that bead along the seam where the bark meets the glass-sharp stone. I do not slip. The laser-etched teeth hold. I absorb the snap, convert it into heat, and dump it back into the damp rock face. 

Then the bulb relaxes, the sap sloshes backward with a hydraulic heave, and the load drops. 

Wait nine seconds. Endure again.

I am fed by the wash, but not like the leaves that drink the mist. The coolant that hits the upper rock cascades down the glass wall in a film no thicker than an oil slick, heavily laced with dissolved zinc, fluoride, and alkaline scavengers. It pools in my upper creases. I taste the city through its degradation: the metallic bitterness of eroding turbine bearings forty levels up; the sharp, vinegar tang of burned dielectric fluid; the faint, sweet residue of recycled sugars leaking from the hydroponic transit hubs. My outer bark is calcified, turned almost entirely to stone by the continuous deposition of these synthetic salts. I grow inward, crushing my own dead cells into a structural laminate denser than teak.

There are other senses, slow and peripheral.

Through the vascular core of the vine, I feel the static discharges of the weft-tails. They are trivial things—pinprick sparks where the pale ribbons brush against each other in the ionized downdraft—but their electrical snaps travel down the wet xylem like minute nervous twitches. When hundreds of them touch at once, a shiver of cold voltage prickles through my core, letting me know the draft is picking up, that the air is ion-heavy, that the overhead space is choking on its own vapor.

And then, the sound changes.

It does not quiet, but the harmonic frequencies of the falling water columns suddenly drift out of phase. The tension along my truss begins to jitter—a fast, high-frequency stutter that means the overhead valves are beginning to unseat.

The rifle-shot crack blows through the chamber.

Even through the dense basalt, the acoustic hammer-blow strikes my outer face like a flat iron plate. The downdraft hits a fraction of a second later: millions of cubic feet of dry, sterile, sub-zero air dropped straight down into the warm cathedral of mist.

This is the trial. This is the reason I exist.

The dry air tries to shrink the vine. If the jade truss loses its turgor, it drops, and the lateral load on the ceramic pilasters will become asymmetrical—an imbalance that would cause the slender, self-compressing pylons to shear under the bay's immense resonant vibration. 

The heart-bulb seizes, locking itself into an unyielding knot of frozen tissue. The great leaves fold inward with a collective, wet clatter. Out in the void, the vine becomes a rigid iron girder, and the pull on my anchor multiplies until the glass edges of the basalt groove bite deep into my living cambium.

Pain, here, is only modulus of elasticity. It is the sound of micro-fibers snapping inside my heartwood, the high, metallic ping of silica crystals fracturing under a load that exceeds their tensile limit. The air turns to ice, bone-dry ozone, and hard cyan light as the floor strips purge their heat. For four seconds, I am stretched between two dead worlds: the city’s unbreathing composite envelope, and the frantic, frozen hydraulic machine trying not to fall into the abyss.

I hold. The resin in my deepest rootlets glazes over, setting hard under the pressure.

Then the valve slams.

The dry storm cuts out instantly. The deafening, rhythmic roar of the water collapses back down from the ceiling like a falling ceiling of lead. The warm humidity spikes back to eighty-five degrees in the space of three breaths. 

I feel the sudden, heavy, wet expansion as the sap floods back through the vascular channels, swelling the vine until it rounds out, softened by relief. The tension slumps to a safe, dead weight. Along my margins, where the violent pull tore my fibers from the rock, fresh latex is already weeping into the micro-fissures, chemical and urgent, laying down new structural adhesive before the next geothermal beat can strike.

Down in the dark, the white rags of torn weft-tails are dissolving in the trenches, their base carbon returning to the water. In twelve minutes, they will be back, reaching blindly for each other across the gap, crackling with static.

I do not reach. I do not open to the cyan or indigo light. I remain pressed flat against the dark, optical face of the cleaved stone, buried under forty years of calcified runoff and vegetable scar tissue, wedged tight in the seam where the machine ends and the rock begins—blind, unmoving, and holding the empty cathedral together by the strength of a closed fist.
