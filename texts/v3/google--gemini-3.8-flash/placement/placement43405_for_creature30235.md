To enter this world with a face, or even the memory of an upright spine, would be an error instantly corrected by the water. 

If I began as something with lungs, the bruised butter-twilight would not even grant me the grace of an inhale; the first drag of that warm, yellow-curded flour would cement the windpipe before the knees could find the stone. If I began with ears, the sheer vertical weight of the roar—that unending collapse of geometry—would pulp the drums into grease within the first two yards of descent. 

So whatever I am, it must shed that architecture on the way down. It must surrender the vertical. The soup takes the limbs first, snapping them backward like dry straws and planing the splinters down to meal; then it takes the chest, collapsing the vault of the ribs until the organs learn to flatten, spreading laterally like batter spilled across a hot plate. 

Down here, *down* is the only direction that does not flay you. 

I would not find myself in the churn. The churn is pure dispersion, a frantic, sterile boiling that creates nothing and remembers nothing. Nor could I remain in the black hair-pelt, whipping and tearing in the lee of the shelves; that is too restless, too raw with the phantom twitching of shredded muscle that has forgotten it is dead. 

I would drop lower. Past the yellow clay-spouts, past the sheets of leaden condensation, through the violence of the middle-broth where water has the tensile snap of plate glass—until the roar suddenly ceases to be a sound and becomes a solid. 

And there, in the paper-thin silence beneath the gale, I would settle.

***

At first, I might think I am the tallow. 

There is a strange, quiet comfort in being the tallow: the grey, mineral grease packed into the dead angle of a square cistern, insulated from the tearing shear above. It would be easy to be that soft, ancient fat, cradling the young—the translucent fingers with their little bellies full of grinding-plates, their sand-bladders shivering like compass needles in an iron mine. I could be the mute, nourishing paste they chew, the slickness that lets them slide without friction, the buffer between their lard-pale skin and the feverish basalt below.

But the tallow is too passive. It gathers; it does not choose. It has no grip. 

And in a world where velocity is an engine of absolute erasure, grip is the only form of intention that exists.

I am not the young, either. Their work has not yet been demanded of them. They still possess the luxury of internal organs, the soft, blind luxury of floating in grease, knowing the world only as a rhythmic drumming in their bladders. They do not yet know the price of the boundary layer.

To know the boundary layer, you must have an edge. 

***

I find myself not along the spiral channels—where the older, finished wedges take their centuries-long laps, polished to an oiled obsidian mirror by the ceaseless grit—but at the lip. At the corner. 

In a world planed flat by five billion years of friction, a right angle is a blasphemy against the current. A corner creates a vortex; a vortex creates lift; lift is death. If a cistern is square, it is because something has fought the hydrodynamics of the entire planet to make it so. 

I am the one holding the corner.

I am smaller than the mature wedges that roam the flats—barely three fingers wide, heavy as a slug of spent uranium, my back not yet hardened into the black mirror-horn of the elders. My carapace is still rough, a porous, calcified crust of quartz dust, sulfur-scabs, and shed grit, caught in the slow, chemical bake of my own excretions. It is dull, the color of a wet slate shingle, still tacky to the touch where the minerals are knitting.

Above me—not an inch away—the torrent screams at two hundred miles an hour. It drags at the chamfer of my trailing edge with the strength of a team of horses, trying to slip a microscopic fingernail of water under my skirt. If it manages to pry up even a single micron of my margin, the boundary layer will fail; the zero-velocity sheet will tear like tissue paper, and I will be launched into the soup, rolled and pulverized until I am black hair snagged on the bedrock two hundred miles to the east.

So I do not move. 

The wanderers move by millimeters a century, loosening their rims to let the planet shove them along the groove. I cannot afford to loosen. I am wedged into the ninety-degree turn where the soapstone wall meets the scouring floor. 

My underside is not skin; it is a vacuum-gasket of dense, fibrous gristle, soaked in the mantle-heat that seeps up through the basalt. Through it, I taste the rock. The basalt tastes of old iron, heavy and fever-warm, thrumming with the lateral gossip of forty million other wedges clamped to the slate: 

*Click. Click-click.* 
*Firm.* 
*Firm.* 
*Held.*

I do not answer with a click of my own. I have no room to vibrate. To vibrate is to break the hydraulic seal. 

Instead, my work is the press.

Under the continuous, agonizing down-force of the ocean falling over itself, my leaden belly is slowly extruding the green sludge. It comes from the hair-roots tangled beneath my skirt, from the tallow washed over the lip by the eddy, from the pulverized shells of my ancestors drifting down as fine, grey flour. I take it into the fold between my soft margin and the stone. I knead it with the micro-shiver of my ventral plates, working the air-curds and the water out of it, compressing it until the molecules lock, until the sludge stops being mud and begins to cure into green-black soapstone.

I am the thumb. 

The indentation in the rim of the cistern—that rhythmic, apricot-sized hollow the user spoke of—is not something stamped into the stone by an artisan who walked away. 

It is my seat. It is the mold of my own body, pressed into the curing mineral by the sheer, unblinking weight of the sky. 

I will sit in this corner for four thousand years. The slurry will grind across my back, stripping the rough calcified horn away, grain by quartz grain, until the dull slate gives way to the oily, basalt mirror-sheen of the elders. The water will try to find a seam in me, and finding none, will polish me into the stone itself. 

By the third millennium, the green soapstone will have risen an eighth of an inch around my skirt. It will seal me in. My edges will no longer need to hold vacuum, because the stone I have excreted will have become the vacuum. My pale muscle will slowly surrender its water to the mantle-heat; the sand in my bladders will settle into the bedrock fissures; the quartz in my shell will fuse with the quartz of the curb. 

I will stop being the builder and become the stub.

Another wedge, young and lard-pale, climbing out of the cistern when the tallow runs thin, will crawl up onto the lip. It will slide its chamfered edge into the smooth, apricot-sized hollow left by my back. It will find the angle true. It will find the vortex damped, the eddy turned away, the corner held against the fury of the boil.

It will drop its leaden skirt into the depression I spent three ages smoothing. It will set its vacuum against my ghost. 

And through the five-billion-year-old basalt, through the roots of the black hair, through the fossilized horn of my own shell, it will send the only word the continent understands:

*Here.*
*Firm.*
*Still held.*
