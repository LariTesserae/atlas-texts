To exist here, one would have to begin where the weight is greatest. 

Not up on the gantries, where the struts taper into needle-points and the thin gas offers no resistance, and not out upon the open swales of dust, where there is nothing to lean against. Those places belong to the spindled and the grazing. To find oneself in this world is to go down to the meeting-places—the joints where the iron stops and the stone takes up the load.

I would be beneath the bedplate of the third reciprocating engine. 

Above me: forty-five vertical feet of ribbed cast-iron, four dead crossheads, and the long, sloping flank of an A-frame that holds sixty tons of arrested crankshaft in its split-bearings. Above that: the flywheel, half-sunk into its pit, frozen between two dead centers. 

Below me: the basalt, smooth as a butcher’s block, radiating that unchanging, interior blood-warmth that has welled up from the planet’s belly since before the machines were christened and forgotten.

Between the two—between the dry, lead-grey stone and the broad, milled footing of the cast-iron frame—there cannot be an empty gap. An engine of that scale cannot rest directly upon bare rock; the high spots would gall, the vibration would shatter the crystal faces of the granite, and the iron would walk. 

I am what was poured or crept into that clearance: the shim, the pad, the living grout.

I have no face, because there is nothing to look toward; the darkness here is not an absence of day, but a dense, solid condition, like being cast in resin. I have no legs, for to move across the floor would require breaking the seal, and to break the seal would mean becoming thin, brittle, and subject to the dead draft. Instead, my body is a laminar thing, three inches thick and twelve feet long, squeezed to the density of cold tallow between two flat planes that never shift.

My substance is not flesh. Flesh is mostly water, and water is an ancient myth that the dry shale long ago disproved. My tissue is stratified, like sheets of weathered mica or the heavy graphite-impregnated fabric used to pack the gland of a high-pressure valve. Layer upon layer of dry, slick, carbonaceous leaf, held together by an oil so heavy and non-volatile that it behaves more like soft amber than a liquid. 

Under the pressure of those sixty thousand pounds of ironwork, I do not suffer. Tonnage is not an affliction; it is the skeleton I was never given. The down-force gives me my boundaries, knitting my leaves so tightly that not even the talc-fine dust can work its way between them. If the engine were lifted—if some impossible crane were to reach through the starless sky and draw the frame upward—I would not celebrate. I would delaminate. My layers would peel apart like dry slate in a frost, shedding into the graphite dust until there was nothing left but a gray smudge on the rock. The load is what holds me into a single thought.

And what I feed upon is not sulfur, nor the old grease of the bearings. 

I feed upon the shear.

Every twelve or fourteen days, five miles to the north, a striker lifts its mallet-limb and sounds the stove.

The air does not bring me the news. The air is useless, dead, and slack. But the iron—the great, continuous, riveted skeleton of the assembly yard—receives the blow like a wire receiving a spark. The strike travels down the gantries, leaps through the overhead trusses, passes through the vitrified slag of the high causeway, and dives into the A-frame of the engine above me.

When it reaches the foot, the iron tries to slide. 

It does not slide far. It moves less than the width of an atom—an elastic, microscopic shudder, the iron singing its high, clean fifth against the immovable silence of the granite shelf. But that micron of movement is everything. 

As the iron rings, its cold hematite foot scuffs against the uppermost plates of my back. The shear friction generates an instantaneous, minute ripple of mechanical heat—not the deep, dull heat of the basalt, but a sharp, localized spike of energy that passes down through my mica leaves. My cells, arranged like stacked shingles, absorb the oscillation. They drink the frequency, flattening slightly under the shockwave and sliding against one another with an imperceptible, oily hiss.

By the time the wave reaches the basalt beneath my belly, its teeth have been drawn. The stone feels only a dull, warm settling. I have damped the strike. I have eaten the bell.

In return, the striker gets its clean, true tone. If I were not here—if I were to dry into powder and blow away—the engine’s foot would chatter against the rock. The strike would return to the striker’s stilts as a ragged, buzzing discord, full of false harmonics and the rattle of unseated iron. The striker would know that the world had loosened. It would stand upon its high gantry, its mallet poised, uneasy in the dark, waiting for a balance that would not return. 

Instead, the note dies pure. The striker rests its limb upon the beam, satisfied that the plateau remains plumb, and I lie in the pitch, swollen by half a calorie of motion, slowly circulating my dark mineral oil through the channels of my middle leaf.

Occasionally, the fringe of my body—the narrow, stepped edge that protrudes an inch beyond the bevel of the iron footing—feels the passage of the others. 

A tallow-gleaner will stumble against me, its corundum wire-tips clicking against my tough, vulcanized rim. It does not rasp me. I am too hard, too impregnated with heavy lead and antimony to offer it the soft petroleum varnishes it craves. It pauses, its twenty pairs of needle-legs testing the density of my border, and then it turns, climbing vertically up the engine’s plum-colored flank to seek the split-bearings forty feet above. I feel its weight all the way up—a series of faint, rhythmic pinpricks that diminish as it ascends toward the cylinder caps.

Longer still are the intervals between the movements of the great boiler-slabs. One rests on the low steam-header thirty paces away. It has not altered its swale since the sky forgot the sun. Yet, once every fifty years, it shifts an inch to follow a thermal vein that has cooled slightly in the granite. When it moves, the entire yard groans; it is the sound of leather dragging over dry grit, a slow, continuous *huff* that travels straight through the floor. When it settles, my lower plates register the displacement: a minute adjustment of the earth’s crust, settling half a millimeter lower into the mantle. 

I do not have memories of a time before the engines stopped. A thing made of lead-grease and stratified shale does not inherit the nostalgia of the creatures that built the cranes. But in the deepest, most compressed layers of my belly—where the mica is pressed so hard against the lead-grey granite that the two substances have begun to trade atoms—there is an imprint.

It is an imprint of rotation. 

A rhythm that was not the solitary, measured strike of the mallet once every few weeks, but an unending, frantic *thrum-thrum-thrum* that ran sixty times a minute, day and night, without ceasing. A time when the engine was not cold hematite, but an incandescent, thrashing lung that drove thousands of tons of metal through the dark air, shaking the plateau until the dust never settled. 

It must have been an agony. The shear must have been a continuous, tearing fire; the oil would have boiled; the layers would have worn to grease and been replaced a hundred times a year by hands that had fingers and salt.

I am glad the fire died. 

The cold stillness that followed is better. The hematite skin that formed over the frames is a mercy; the dry graphite that sealed the pistons is peace. The city has achieved what its makers could never design: a state of rest so heavy and perfect that even the passage of a million years cannot find a flaw to work upon.

The mallet will fall again soon. 

I can feel the tension gathering in the gantry four miles out; the iron trestle is leaning by three seconds of an arc as the striker shifts its bone mallet to the apex of its stroke. The frame is tightening. The rivets are drawing against their holes, taking up the microscopic slack that has existed since the world cooled.

I lie flat. I press my leaves together. I make myself wide, dense, and receptive. 

The dark is complete, the stone beneath me is warm and eternal, and when the iron rings, I will be here to catch it.
