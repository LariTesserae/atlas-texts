At first, there is only the contact.

It is not a thought; it is an area of pressure. A broad, flat coolness pressed against the ribs and the meat of the thighs, and above that, a dry, stagnant warmth that rests on the nape of the neck like a folded blanket of coarse wool.

To locate oneself here requires shedding the habit of looking out. There is nothing to look toward; fifty paces is not a distance, it is a circumference, and the amber-grey air holds the gaze close to the stone, softening it until sight is barely distinguishable from touch. 

I would not be above the rim. Up there, where the bluffs end, the air must have an edge—a draft that carries seeds, a sharp, thinned heat, the wide, blinding vacancy of an uncurved sky. That world belongs to things that hurry to avoid being dried out. It has nothing to do with this bowl.

Nor would I be among the harvestmen in the dry joint of the overhang, folded into an umbrella of chalk-grey limbs, waiting for a lost midge to tumble down. That is too dry, too thin. It is an existence made entirely of thrift, of paring oneself down until the pulse is a thread that barely vibrates.

I would be lower, where the damp from the floor meets the long-retained heat of the wall. 

At the southern end of the curved revetment, where the arc of stones runs out of purpose and simply slopes down into the compacted silt, there is an angle. It is an elbow of space, two feet deep, bounded on one side by a rounded field stone with a skin of chalky carbonate and on the other by the rising floor of worn flagstones. 

That is where the weight settles.

For a long time, there is no need to decide whether I have a shell, or hide, or skin. The difference between them is largely a matter of water, and here, moisture is meted out with such absolute consistency that all surfaces tend toward the same leathery compromise. 

If I reach—not with an arm, but with an intention that slowly draws muscle through silt—I find limbs. They are folded inward, drawn close against the belly to keep the softer parts from the dry air. The knees are blunt, the color of wet river clay that has begun to set. The hands are not claws, nor are they the broad, horny shovels of the great beast beneath the ledge; they are five-fingered, but the tips are broadened, flattened, their nails worn down into thick, yellow plates of horn that have lost their edges against the grit.

I am what remains of the builder. 

Not a survivor, and not a ghost. A ghost is made of memory and wind, and there is no wind here, and nothing changes enough to make memory distinct from the present. 

Perhaps centuries ago—though the phrase has no meaning where an inch of liverwort takes a generation to creep—there was an impulse to gather. Hands lifted those field stones. They did not quarry them; they picked up what lay loose and set them in a curve that matched the turning radius of the great, horned creatures who shared the hollow. The wall was not built to keep anything out, for nothing ever arrived; it was built the way water creates a scallop in limestone: simply by staying in one place and letting weight and movement clear a groove.

Over eras, the work finished itself. The carbonate weeping from the stone glued the rocks together, and the hands that laid them found fewer and fewer reasons to lift another. 

To sit against the wall is not to wait. Waiting implies a horizon, a threshold that something must cross. Here, to sit is merely to occupy an equilibrium. 

My breath does not have the slow, mechanical interval of the beast, but it has learned from it. It takes perhaps a hundred of the water drops to complete a cycle: a long, cool filling of the chest with the smell of iron and dust, a suspension while the ribcage rests against the smooth flank of the revetment stone, and then a faint, warm expulsion that doesn't even disturb the powder between my toes. 

When the beast stirs beneath its slab, I feel it before the sound travels through the air. The vibration comes up through the bedrock—a heavy, grinding shift of density, the red silt compressing, the smooth horn of its pads finding the worn hollows in the flags. 

The sound comes after: *chhh-shhh*, like wet leather being dragged over sandstone.

I do not open my eyes wide. Through the semi-clouded slit of the lids, the amber-grey light is enough. I watch the grey-fawn hump of its back glide past the revetment. A trail of calcium dust, shaken loose from its spine, falls into the seam between two stones and stays there, adding another microscopic layer to the bond that holds them.

Its snout lowers to the pan. 

In the long silence while it drinks, while the water rises against the lip of its mouth and the amber crustaceans graze upon the wrinkles of its chin, my own throat aches with a slow, sympathetic thirst. It is not an urgent craving. It is the thirst of a dried pea placed on damp wool, drinking through its skin at a rate that cannot be measured. 

I know the taste of that water. It is cold at first, then heavy with lime, coating the tongue with a thin, mineral silt that leaves the mouth feeling clean, alkaline, and ancient. 

The beast lifts its head. The water droplet breaks.

*Clink.*

The note rings into the stone beneath my pelvic bones, shivers through the curve of the revetment, and dies into the red clay.

The creature turns, its heavy flank brushing the wall. A flake of its dried hide, no larger than a fingernail, detaches and falls beside my foot. It lies there on the compacted silt, pale and curled, slowly absorbing the damp of the ground until it relaxes, goes flat, and becomes indistinguishable from the floor.

I pull my chin closer to my chest, letting the hoods of my eyes slide down. The warmth in the limestone wall behind my back is steady, breathed out from centuries of light that the world above has long since forgotten. 

There is nothing to be done. The stones are in their arc. The water is balanced in its pans. Another drop gathers on the overhanging brow, swelling by fractions of a grain, growing heavy with the patient gravity of the hollow, preparing to fall.
