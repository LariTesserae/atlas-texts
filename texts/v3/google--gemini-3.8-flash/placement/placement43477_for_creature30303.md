Not the light. The light has no purchase here; it simply fills, the way cold brine fills an open bucket, level and vacant from wall to wall. It offers nothing to lean against.

And not the stone either, though at first it is tempting to claim the stone—to imagine oneself as the rounded tonalite being hefted, the grey river-shale passing from palm to greased palm three hundred paces across the plaza. But the stone is too dumb, too heavy with the ancient sleep of the shelf above it. It only wants to lock. It wants the aragonite to knit its pores shut, wants the white fur to creep out of the damp and seal it into one continuous, calcified floor that will never have to move again. The stone is lazy; it fights the hands by simply waiting for them to tire.

No, look lower, where the air is thickest with salt-dust and the fine spray from the sluices.

Look to the sledges.

Not the wood—there is no wood. The runners of the heavy sledges are curved lengths of fossil-ivory or the split jawbones of things that died when the rift was still an arm of an ancient sea, their marrow-cavities packed with tallow and plugged with horn. But between the bone runner and the flat flags of wet slate lies a thin, yellow-grey smear: the rendered fat of the four-winged lards, beaten together with the lime-flour that sloughs from the dry-stackers’ aprons.

It is in that smear that things begin to assemble.

There is a consciousness that belongs only to sheer: the terrible, silent velocity of two hard surfaces being kept from tearing one another apart by three millimeters of grease.

Every sixty paces, a sledge passes. The weight on it is two tons of blue-grey shale, damp enough to sweat, hauled by fourteen gut-ropes hooked into the hide harnesses of the runners. When the sledge moves, it does not roll. It slides with a soft, tearing hiss that dies two feet from the runners, eaten instantly by the heavy air. Beneath the ivory shoes, the pressure is enormous—enough to liquefy the grease, enough to crush the grit into microscopic flour.

If you are there, you know the city not as a plan of boulevards or amphitheatres, but as a topography of resistance.

Here the slate is slightly hollowed by three hundred years of leather soles; the sledge planes easily, floating on a cold cushion of fat, the pull on the gut-ropes easing by a finger’s breadth. Here, near the mouth of the third radial sluice, the lime has begun to sweat early; tiny needles of aragonite, sharp as broken glass, have spiked up through the flagstones overnight. The runners bite into them with a dry, shuddering crunch that travels straight up the spine of the haulers, setting their amber eyes vibrating in their grease-rimmed sockets.

To be the grease is to know the entire metabolism of the rift from the underside. You know that the bird-fat is thin this noon—the flyers have been feeding on the small translucent amphipods from the western overflow, whose oil is light and watery, rather than the fat, yellow-bellied mothers from the deep traps. You know it because the sledge drags. It pulls heavy, sucking at the slate like wet leather.

And then the image shifts, because grease does not suffer, and there is an ache here that requires a pulse.

Lift it an inch. Out of the slime under the runner, up through the hemp-tied trace, into the heel.

Now the thing is walking.

It has no name because there is nobody to call it, and if someone opened their throat to yell, the rift would choke the sound into a thimble of air before it reached the ear-slits. But it has a stride. The stride is wide, low-slung, with the knees turned permanently outward like the hinges of a crab’s leg.

The soles of the feet are three inches of pale, fissured horn, crisscrossed with healed gashes from broken scarab shells and slate-chips. They are wider than the ankles, spread out like pads to distribute eighty pounds of body weight and ninety pounds of drag across stones that must not be broken before they reach the terrace.

In the heel, the city is a clock made of bone mallets.

*One-two. One-two.*

Three streets over, fifty strides behind the low wall of a granary that will be demolished before the light shifts a single millimeter, a gang of sixty is dropping their mallets against the bedrock. They are resetting the foundation flags. The shock comes up through eighty yards of solid tonalite: a small, dry *tick* against the calcaneus, followed a fraction of a heartbeat later by the secondary shudder of the flag settling into its bed of gravel.

Your knees bend deeper to absorb it. The cadence is already in your thighs before the sledge has even cleared the crossing. Without looking—without the amber discs ever lifting from the wet edge of the flag three paces ahead—your ribs contract. The spiracles beneath your jaw puff two thin jets of steam against the oiled eel-skin of your breast. The ropes go taut.

The world does not have a past. If you carry the same slab of water-worn limestone ten thousand times back and forth across the white plaza—from the south wall to the north wall in the morning that never ends, and from the north wall back to the south wall in the afternoon that never arrives—the slab does not become older. It only becomes rounder. Its edges lose their sharpness to your gut-wrappings; the small depression where your thumb presses the side grows deeper by the thickness of a moth’s wing every forty journeys.

Eventually, the stone will be too small to serve as a spacer. It will slip through the fingers. It will be tossed into the sluice-channel with the dead crabs and the broken wedges, to be rolled down toward the skin-deep lake, tumbling against a million others until it is no bigger than an amphipod’s egg.

Is that death?

No. Death is when the mallet-shock stops making the knee bend.

It happens between the granary and the sluice. You are running with the sledge, the hide harness cutting into the continuous, gristly band where the neck joins the chest, when the vibration from the tonalite simply fails to reach the marrow. The floor is still thrumming—you can see the rills of mineral perspiration shivering in their hairline cracks—but the bone in the shin has grown dry. The lime has found it.

After forty years of drinking the cold water from the saucer-hollows and breathing the air that tastes of crushed shells, the skeleton begins its own aragonite work. The knee-joint calcifies. The gristle that hinges the ribs turns to chalk. The body is attempting to become a terrace. It is trying to settle into that ultimate, unbudging rest that the bedrock achieved two ages ago.

When the knees refuse to yield, the sledge does not stop. It strikes you from behind.

It is not an assault; it is simply momentum. The ivory runner rides up over the calf, greased with lard-fat, sliding over the pale skin with a cold, dull push. You are down on the flags. The amber discs turn upward for the first time in sixteen years, staring into the blinding, milky glare of the halite ceiling.

From this angle, the ceiling does not look like stone. It looks like the belly of a great, dead fish stretched from mountain to mountain, lit from behind by a fire that has nothing to do with heat. The cracks in the selenite run like pale veins, splitting and rejoining in long, jagged deltas. For four heartbeats, you are the only thing in the city that has a sky.

Then the runners peel the eel-hide off.

The blade of the flake-knife is very cold along the ribs—colder even than the stone beneath your back. It does not hurt; the skin of the torso is thick as an ox-lip and has little feeling left except for the broad pressure of harness-leather. The hide smock comes away with a wet, sucking sound, leaving the pale, tallow-colored flesh bare to the cellar-air.

Hands—familiar hands, with fingers bound in gut and nails ground down to square yellow pegs—reach into the pouch at your hip. They take the horn wedge. They take the lump of lard-grease. They do not take the amber discs because they cannot be pried out until the skull has lain three days in the froth of the amphipod lake.

Then the heels are lifted.

You are moving again, but backwards now, your bare shoulder blades dragging across the smooth, water-worn marble of the gutter. The friction is light; the rills of condensation are running thick here, greasing the stone with a clear, alkaline slime that tastes of plaster.

The sound dies first. The acoustic viscosity of the chasm gathers around your head like a hood of wet wool. The frantic scuttling of the crabs on the low walls, the jagged, snapping flight of the lards above the terraces—it all thins into a single, dry rustle, like dry bladders being kicked through salt.

Then the current takes you.

The water of the sluice is thirty-six degrees. It does not feel cold; it feels heavy, like mercury poured into the ears. It lifts the hips, rolls the chest, and carries you out of the wide, eighty-stride boulevard into the concentric spirals where the froth churns white.

Thousands of small, fringed legs begin at the calves. They do not tear; they brush. They work with the same manic, rapid economy as the builders on the terrace, clearing away the tallow, clearing away the skin that has forgotten the sun, clearing away the yellow tooth-plates that will never crush another shell.

The light stays. Even through the milk of the water, even through the churning carpet of cellophane ribbons, the glare remains frozen at noon.

In three weeks, the thigh-bone will wash ashore on the southern flags. It will be very white, very smooth, rubbed down by the sand-scour until it has no corners left. A hand will reach down from a running sledge. Fingers wrapped in gut will lift it from the froth, weigh it for a moment against the shock of the tonalite, and tuck it into a pouch to be ground into a mallet-butt.

And the city will be three hundred paces to the east, waiting to be brought back.
