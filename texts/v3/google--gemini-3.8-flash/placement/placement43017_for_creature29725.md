At first, there is only the sensation of two different horizons held against opposite flanks. 

To the left, the gulf: three thousand cubits of sheer indigo air, pale with suspended mica, dropping down into the trough where the base of the plinths dissolved long before memory began. To the right, cold basalt, three inches away, so close that the dry mineral grain of it fills the field of vision completely, an unyielding gray wall without mortar or fracture.

I am upright. That is the first certainty. 

I have no breadth to speak of. If I were to look down at myself—though I have no neck to crook, no hinge between perception and base—I would see only a dark, vertical seam, sharp as a split flag, resting upon the shale pavement. I am weighted at the bottom like a plumb-line. The flags beneath me are cool, dry, and scoured of any moisture; they do not stick to my edges, but they hold me by the simple, ancient habit of unmoving stone.

For a long time, there is no need to be anything more than this. A counterweight in an eight-foot slot.

Behind me, and ahead of me, stacked through five hundred tiers of identical, windowless basalt chambers, others of my kind stand. We do not touch. We do not speak, for there is no fluid in our throats to make a sound, nor any air damp enough to carry a wave without it dying into the dust. We know each other by the shared tension of the floor. When the pressure drops and the bruised slate color of the sky thickens toward black, the shale beneath us tightens like a drumhead. We feel the slight, terrifying buoyancy of the city—that moment when the gravity of the world below forgets its jurisdiction, and the stones beneath our footings want to drift like dry leaves upon water.

In that silence, we do not let them drift.

We lean. 

It is not a thought; it is an escapement clicking forward a single tooth. Three degrees to the south. 

Our weight, multiplied across ten thousand razor-thin cells, shifts the fulcrum. The friction line gives way with a dry, soundless shudder, and the entire terrace—five hundred tiers of windowless tenements, half a league of dry shale—slides forward into the indigo void. It extends like a drawer pulled from a chest, hanging out over the mile-deep drop, perfectly balanced on the emptiness.

We hold the three degrees. We hold it for a century, perhaps two, while the unseen mass in the trough settles itself against the stars.

Then something fails to right itself.

***

The shift ends with the usual unison *tink*—ten thousand bases righting themselves against the flags. But my footing does not find its notch. 

A single flake of dry shale-leaf, blown up from the basement chasms during the last barometric drop, has drifted into the narrow slot of my cell. It lies across the seam where my base meets the basalt. When I tilt back toward the vertical, my edge catches upon its flinty rim. 

I do not stop at ninety degrees. The flake acts as a tiny, frictionless roller. My base slides three inches forward, and the balance that has held me pinned between the wall and the abyss snaps.

I lean four degrees. Then six.

To a thing made of an edge, six degrees is an exile. The wall to my right is suddenly no longer three inches away; it falls away at an angle. The door of the cell—eight feet high, two fingers wide—does not hold me. I slip through its taper like a coin sliding through a slot in a counter.

I do not fall downward. Here, falling is an act of memory, and my kind have never learned the habit of the drop; we have only ever known the habit of the floor. 

I glide.

My base skims across the dust of the open terrace, riding the frictionless mica-flour that coats the flags. For the first time since the megaliths were hewn without wedges, I am outside the tenement.

***

The open plaza is terrifying in its geometry.

Without the basalt wall to fill my right eye, the city rushes into the void of my senses. I perceive two halves of the world simultaneously, without a middle. To my left, the endless towers rising toward the black vault; to my right, the knife-edge bridges leaping miles of empty air toward distant, shifting ziggurats. 

Because I have no thickness, I make no wake in the mica-haze. When I slide broadside to the permanent dusk, the dim indigo light catches my face—a face that is nothing more than a tall, flat sheet of dark slate-vellum—and I cast a shadow that stretches two hundred yards across the flags, thin as a thread. But when I pivot to meet a draft rising from the trough, I vanish from the city entirely. I am only an edge, an infinitesimal slit in the twilight, fracturing the sight of the towers behind me into two misaligned halves.

I drift across the central terrace, carried by the rising mineral draft. 

High above, in the inverted arches of a ruined colonnade, something stirs. One of the chalk-white wanderers. Its ten segmented limbs are wedged deep into the dry joints of the masonry, spanning a gulf of empty air. Its hollow carapace, gray as powdered bone, is pale from having gorged on some recent spatial fold. 

As my shadow crosses the flagstones beneath it, the crawler’s calipers twitch. It does not look down—it has no eyes for looking—but it feels the interruption in the light. Its needle-tipped limbs release their vacuum pads with a faint, muted *thwip*. 

It descends, not by dropping, but by walking down the sheer, vertical face of the pillar, moving with that erratic, insectile patience that measures time in centuries. It thinks I am an error. To its mineral logic, an upright blade in the middle of a plaza is a fold of space that has forgotten to tuck itself away, an excess dimension that ought to be planed flat.

It extends its longest leg. The needle-sharp tip of dry horn reaches toward my broadside, seeking the seam where I ought to join the stone.

If it touches me, it will draw the flat plane of my body into its hollow carapaces, wedging me into the joint between two blocks until the street snaps straight and I am crushed into powder.

I do not turn broadside to defend myself; to defend is to present surface, and surface is what it feeds upon. 

Instead, I turn my edge to it.

The crawler’s caliper strikes nothing. It passes through the coordinate where I seemed to be, meeting only the dry, indigo air. Its joint groans with a tiny, soundless shudder of displaced pressure. It pauses, confused by the sudden disappearance of a mass that still registers upon the floor beneath it.

I slide past its reach, caught in the lateral drift of the plaza.

***

Now the storm begins to descend.

The light drops from indigo to bruised slate. A profound, ringing silence presses down upon the masonry, so heavy that the unmortared joints of the terraces groan as they settle. 

From the chasms below, the thermal rises.

It brings with it the gray shale-leaf—millions of razor-sharp, paper-thin flakes of stone, carried up from the trough by an absolute dryness. They do not tumble; they enter the plaza in vast, orbiting sheets, aligning themselves along magnetic corridors that span the void between the towers.

They find me.

As the flakes orbit the pillars, they strike my broadside. They do not shatter me; they cling. They are of the same mineral logic as I am—dry, calcified, ancient, and thin. They adhere to my flat face by static friction, stacking themselves against my flank like scales upon a fish. 

With every pass of the gyre, I gather mass. The shale-leaves lace themselves into my edges, extending my profile, thickening me from a blade into a ribbon. The static discharge begins—tiny, silent arcs of pale violet fire snapping between the flakes, illuminating the windowless facades of the tenements as I drift past.

I am no longer just a counterweight that has slipped its groove. I am being woven into something else.

The mica-ribbon—the thirty-yard aggregate of the storm—reaches down its long, multi-jointed spine and gathers me into its braid. I feel the collective appetite of the drift: not the rigid balance of the tenements, but the thirst for continuity, the desire to thread the needle-bridges and map the shifting boundaries of the districts before they slide again.

Together, we rise.

We leave the flags of the plaza behind. We coil around the sheer, vertical needles of basalt that scrape the blackening upper vault. Below us, the five-hundred-tier blocks look like the teeth of an upturned comb, their millions of razor-thin doorways standing open to the void. I can see the tenements leaning—three degrees south, all in unison, their lateral shift sending terraces gliding out over the abyss like stepping stones laid across the sky.

We climb beyond the reach of the crawlers. Beyond the reach of the unmortared bridges.

At the summit of the tallest needle, where the air thins until even the mica-flour can no longer hover, we find the gate.

***

It stands in the middle of the sky, anchored to nothing. A cyclopean arch of dry, unworked stone, framing a square of darkness that is blacker than the indigo around it.

On its threshold sits the thing that has never moved.

Close to it, its form is neither plumb-bob nor compass; it is an immense, folded hinge of gray slate, miles wide, its joints packed with the frictionless dust of dead stars. It does not breathe air, for there is none here. It breathes the geometric tension of the city below—it feels every shifted terrace, every crossed shadow, every crawler wedged into a seam.

The mica-ribbon throws itself into the archway. The flakes of shale-leaf shatter upon the threshold with a dry rustle like falling parchment, dissolving into the shadow of the gate.

I do not shatter. 

I am carried by the momentum of the climb, but as I hit the stone of the threshold, my base—that heavy, ancient, weighted edge—strikes the unworked slate of the gate’s lintel.

*Tink.*

The hinge groans. The plumb-bob shifts by the width of a hair.

I do not pass through the dark patch of air into the future city, nor do I fall back down into the trough. I find my footing in the hinge itself, wedging my razor-thin body between two colossal plates of slate that have remained motionless for three eons.

I fit. I am the spacer it was waiting for.

From this height, the city is only an arrangement of sharp, vertical edges, repeated to infinity across the twilight. Miles below, down in the blue-gray trough where the eye stopped looking, a plinth of gray stone that has not yet been named rises five inches through the dust. 

I feel its upward motion through the hinge, through my spine, through the dry, static-charged scales of my body. 

I set my base against the stone. I lean—not three degrees, but half of one—and hold the gate open against the sky.
