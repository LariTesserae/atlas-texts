I would not be in the high watch-towers, where the braided hair of the bridges thrums in the invariant draft, nor down at the mirror-lip of the black pool, not yet. Those are the extremes of the world: the tension that catches the air and the stillness that drinks it. 

I would find myself in the long, stepped middle—in the elbow of an aqueduct where the stone channel turns across a split in the dolomite. 

At first, I think I would be the tool itself: a heavy wedge of gray flint, cold in the palm, or the basalt hammer whose face has been bruised into a shallow cup by ten thousand impacts against the terrace flags. In a place where nothing rots and the light refuses to pivot, consciousness feels less like an interior monologue and more like an edge held against a surface. 

Then the flesh asserts itself. 

I have the clay-dark right side and the pale left side. The sun has pressed its dull gold coin against the right temple for so long that the skin there is thick as dried rowan-bark, while my left ear is thin, white, and permanently chilled by the leeward wind that slides down from the snowfields. My legs are long from knee to ankle; they ache with that flat, low-grade iron soreness that comes from walking three-foot risers without ever having learned to stoop.

I am crouching on a shelf of wet slate where a cushion of moss has been rolled back like a fleece. 

Underneath the moss, the joint has moved. It has not failed—nothing fails suddenly here—but the rift has breathed. A hair’s breadth of pale, newly exposed rock shows white against the gray, smelling of sulfur and cold well-water. 

Between my feet rests a shallow tray woven of split reed, weighted with three things: a handful of flat shale shims, a horn spatula for lifting the translucent calcite snails from the seam, and a dried cake of rowan-meal wrapped in ibex bladder.

I am not young enough to run the high goat-paths with the ash-baskets, leaping from flag to flag with the flat-footed bounce of the boys who herd the swallows. Nor am I stiff enough to descend to the basin. My marrow still turns over; when I lie in the pitch-dark corridors of the dry halls on the moss boards, three hours of shallow breath are still enough to clean the gravel from my eyes. 

I am a tender of the joins. 

It is quiet work, even for a city where mallet-blows sound like heartbeats through felt. I spend what might be hours—though the sun never tilts its two-hour lean—sitting with my left shoulder against the cold leeward face of an arch pier, watching a single blind snail make its way along the shadow-margin. Its shell is so clear that I can see the tiny, purple knot of its heart beating once every twelve paces of my own breath. If the pier settles, the shadow shifts the width of a grass blade, and the snail stops, turned aside by the dry heat of The Face or the dead damp of The Leeward. I use the snail as a level. Where it stalls, the wall has leaned.

An ibex doe has come to rest on the terrace above me. Her great scythe-horns rest against her flank, notched like tally-sticks. Her cloven hoof clicks twice on the stone above my skull, then falls still. The faint, bitter musk of her coat—rhododendron pollen and dried cud—drifts down with the cold air. I don't look up at her, and she does not lean over the coping to look at me. We simply share the same vertical line of stone; her weight presses down into the lintel, and the lintel presses into the pier, and the pier rests on the bed of dolomite where my heel is planted. We are both counterweights against the rift.

My voice is gone from disuse, shrunk down to that gravelly cough they all share, but my hearing has become broad and flat. Sound travels without bouncing, so I can tell the difference between the hammer of my brother four terraces up—who strikes with the flat face of a river-stone, high and hollow—and the mallet of the men wedging the great reservoir two miles away, which arrives through the soles of my feet as a dull, subterranean *thump*, like a heavy sack dropped into wet sand.

Near my right knee, propped in an angle where the draft cannot reach, sits the ancestor who held this run of channel before me. 

She has been there since before my hair was first squared at the jaw with flint. Her skin has the polished, greasy sheen of an old alder mallet; her skirt of mountain-wool has turned the color of the slate behind her. One of her bare heels is wedged under the third course of the revetment, keeping the split pine decking from slipping into the ravine. Her face is turned upward toward the fixed disc of the white sky, her mouth slightly parted, perfectly empty, preserving the air she ceased breathing forty seasons ago. 

Sometimes, when the salt frost forms on my eyebrows from the thin cold, I lean my forehead against her dry knee. Her sinew has cured as hard as dry oak; she does not yield, but she holds the sun’s warmth in her grease long after I have turned to the leeward. There is no prayer in it. I simply check that her heel has not slipped from the pine seam. It has not. She holds.

My hand moves into the reed tray. I find a sliver of fresh flint, sharp as a split tooth. 

I prize up three snails that have strayed an eighth of an inch into the scald of the sun-face, their clear shells cold against the ball of my thumb, and place them back upon the ink-line of the shade. Then I reach for the basalt hammer. 

I do not strike hard. In this air, a hard blow shatters the grain of the young stone. I tap. *Tuck. Tuck.* A wedge of shale enters the hairline gap between the sixth block and the seventh. The water in the aqueduct gutter above me stops its minute shivering, smooths out into a ribbon of black tallow, and continues its slow, level crawl toward the cisterns.

I draw a pinch of the rowan-meal from the pouch and press it against my tongue with my thumb, tasting the cold flour, the dust of the rowan skin, and the grease of the ibex that carried it. 

The light does not move. The disc does not dim. 

Across the gulf of the rift, a flock of soot-black swallows drops from the white chalk cliff—a shower of small, dark weights falling in absolute silence through three hundred cubits of gray air, snapping open their fringed wings only when they graze the gutters, then vanishing under the bridges like thrown knives. 

I take my mallet between my palms and wait for the ibex to shift her foot, or for the snail to move an eighth of an inch along the stone. There is nowhere else for the sun to go, and nothing to finish before it gets there.
