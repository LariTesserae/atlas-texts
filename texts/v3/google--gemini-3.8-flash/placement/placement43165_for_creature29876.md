It begins with the temperature of the foot.

Not cold in the way snow is cold—there is no bite or numbness to it—but cold the way a slate slab taken from a cellar wall is cold: an unyielding, mineral extraction of heat that enters the sole and rises straight up the marrow without ever shivering. 

At first, there is only the sensation of being wide and thin. You might think you are the film itself—the clear sheet of mountain sweat that clings to the earthenware by skin-tension alone, shivering when the thin upland drafts sweep down from the ridge. You feel the microscopic drag of the comb-scratches left by whatever scraped the clay before it baked; you feel the tiny, hard ridges of crushed shell, the sharp micro-cliffs of flint shims, the absolute flatness where a lens-creature has passed and left its glaze of dense silica. You are spread over leagues of shallow pans, whispering down millions of stepped lips, falling an inch at a time, clicking softly as you drop.

But water doesn't remember where it came from, and this presence does.

So you pull inward, away from the wide glare. The light here is too pure; it doesn't illuminate things so much as plane them down into flat, blinding facets of pink and white. Beneath the glare, in the three-inch troughs where the current stalls, the silence is heavier. There is a desire to fold yourself into the seams—to soften, to become the curd-like ribbon that drinks the bitter lime runoff and sweats it back out as knife-sharp mortar. To press an eyeless, calcified brow into a corner where a frost-fissure is trying to open, to swell with wet dust and seal it shut forever, leaving behind only the blunt, pale print of an absent thumb.

Yet that is too still. That is the work of an organ, not an occupant. 

The weight gathers higher up. It lifts out of the basins, levering upward on joints that make no sound unless they move, and when they move, it is the sound of two dry river stones rubbed together in the palm of a hand.

I am up on the stilts. 

Standing here, twice the height of a man who no longer exists, the horizon does not curve; it breaks across a million tiny rectangular steps, climbing toward a sky so pale and blue it looks like bleached linen. The air at this height has an iron, electrical tang—the taste of lightning that never quite discharges, held perpetually in check by the cold.

My feet are broad, round pads of slate-gray hide, porous and damp. They do not balance on the edges; they never touch the knife-sharp rims where the water folds. They settle squarely into the centers of the pans, filling the floor from margin to margin, pressing down onto the millimeter of water until it squirts out in a thin, continuous halo around the rim of the sole. 

The walk is not a journey. There is no lower valley to reach—the mud down there would cling, rot, and poison the tendon-hinges with moss. There is only the traverse. Three hundred basins to the north, turning before the grade steepens into a flume, stepping diagonally across the stepped weirs where the water glides fastest. 

Every few hours, I lower the head. The neck is a counterweighted spar of overlapping shingles, bound with gray cord that never dries out because it drinks the mist rising from the basins. The muzzle is blunt, a shovel of split slate. I drag it along the pink clay bottom of a pan, feeling the scrape of the combs, scooping up the thin, opalescent mica-discs that have grown too heavy with quartz-dust to slide over the weir. They dissolve against the roof of the mouth without taste—only the clean, silken grit of crushed rock and the bite of raw lime.

And then, lift the head again. Clear the eyes—not eyes of jelly, which would cook in this glare, but two polished flakes of dark horn set deep under the brow-plates, registering only the stark contrast of ink-black shadow and porcelain-white wash.

Sometimes, far across the terracing—three leagues away, where the tiers blur into a single shimmering pink slope—another set of stilts moves. It takes an hour to watch it lift one foot and place it down. It leaves no ripple behind it; by the time the pad lifts, the water has already smoothed itself flat again, perfectly level, waiting for the condensation of the next cold front. We do not call to one another. There is nothing to say that isn’t already being said by the trillion liquid clicks beneath our feet.

Lately, though, the left knee-joint has begun to take on lime. 

It does not hurt—pain belongs to things with blood and fat—but it has grown heavier. When the leg swings forward, the tendon drags against a crust of calcium that the mountain’s breath has deposited between the plates. It rasps with the sound of a dry trowel. 

Yesterday, I did not complete the step into the next cistern. I stood for three sun-crossings with one foot raised an inch above the weir, watching the water weep over the lip beneath me. The mica-lenses drifted under the shadow of my pad, pausing there in the cool darkness before sliding over the edge. In the corner of the basin where my heel rested, a pale cord had already crept out from the mortar seam, testing the leathery edge of the pad with its blind, chalky snout. It was tasting the slate of my shins. It was recognizing the stone.

Soon, perhaps before the shadow of the upper ridge reaches this tier, the joint will not bend at all. 

I will place the raised foot down, exactly in the center of the rectangular pan. The four pads will seat themselves firmly into the earthenware, displacing the water evenly to all four margins. I will lower the head until the slate chin rests on the crest of the weir, bridging the gap between the upper terrace and the lower. 

The air bubbles will continue to *gluck* in the dark ceramic pipes below. The thin discs will slide across my back, polishing the slate shingles until they shine like the rest of the mountain. And the blind ribbons of lime will come up from the joints, millimeter by millimeter, packing themselves into the spaces between my ribs, filling the hollows of my knees, until the rasping stops entirely—until the water that condenses out of the blue sky can slide down my spine, smooth and unbroken, dropping without a splash into the cistern I used to walk through.
