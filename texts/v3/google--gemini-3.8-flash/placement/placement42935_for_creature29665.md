At first, there is no boundary between the body and the room. 

There is only the weight of the wool-heat, which presses evenly from above, and the denser, oily heat that pushes upward from the iron flags. To exist here is not to inhabit a form, but to occupy a temperature: eighty-one degrees where the shale has fractured clean; eighty-four where the condensate pools in the gravel; eighty-eight against the dead pump’s skirt, where the deep steam still mutters through the silt. 

If I have an outline, it is discovered only by contrast. 

I am cooler than the engine block. I am wetter than the crushed ballast. The three red coals on the eastern wall do not reach me as light—for I have no surface that receives a picture—but as a faint, dry irritation on my left side, a tiny, prickling asymmetry in the dark that tells me which way is east without needing a name for the direction.

Gradually, a margin reveals itself. 

It is a fold. A blunt, cartilaginous lip, ribbed like the underside of a mushroom or the bellows of an accordion, resting heavy against the iron. This is the forward edge. When I draw a gallon of myself forward, the lip spreads, flattening into the seam between two rusted plates to taste what has gathered there. 

The taste is precise. It is not sweet or sour; it is conductive. 

The grey lime-scale tastes thin and brittle, like old chalk soaked in rain. The iron-slime tastes dark, iron-sweet, and electric, prickling against the cilia with the tiny, faint sting of galvanic decay. And beneath both is the grease: tallow-grease, cylinder oil cooked down by thirty months of dry fever into a dark, buttery resin that yields under my belly like cold marrow.

I do not have limbs. I have mass, and I have friction. 

When the peristalsis begins, it is not an effort of walking, but the slow, muscular wringing of my own substance. I shorten; the ballast stones beneath me grind together with a muffled, iron rattle; I lengthen, and my skirt flows an inch into the cleared corridor. The sound of my passing is low and dry, like heavy canvas dragged across an ash-heap. 

Ahead of me, a spade-leaf rises out of the gravel, swollen with tepid sap, blocking the lane. I do not see it, but I feel its coolness—a small pocket of shadow where the thermal line falters. 

My leading fringe folds around the base of its stalk. There is a moment of pressure, a quiet, rubbery resistance, and then the horn-plate beneath my fore-lip slides forward. The stem shears with a wet, fibrous *snip*, clean as a gardener’s pinch. 

The stalk does not fall; it is drawn under. 

Inside the chemical dark of my lower floor, the thousands of fine, rasping teeth go to work. They do not chew as a jaw chews; they abrade. They mill the leaf down against the iron flags, breaking the fibrous cells, drinking the alkaline water, pressing the pulp until the sap separates from the flesh. 

It is here that the cold begins. 

In a vault where the heat hangs like dirty wool, where every stone is fevered, my gut produces the only coolness in the world. It is not frost—frost would kill us all, curdling the discs in the tanks and turning the spade-leaves to black slime. It is a chemical counterfeit. A pale, volatile oil, pressed from the crushed liverwort and the tallow-stems, distilled in the slow, blind alembic between my hide and the ballast. 

As I drag myself toward the dead pump, I leave it behind in a thick, gleaming ribbon. 

It coats the cleared gravel. It seals the raw iron from the caustic drip of the roof. It smells violently of spearmint—a clean, stinging vapor that rises into the cherry-red dark, hanging an inch above the floor, marking the furrow where I have passed so that if I turn, or if another of my kind slides into the lane, we will know the furrow has been tended.

Every forty seconds, through the ballast and the iron plate beneath my belly, comes the shock. 

*Tock.* 

It does not travel through the air; the fleshy leaves eat the air-sound before it can reach my fringe. But the iron remembers the blow. Forty feet away, in the hopper, the vascular felt catches the falling drop, drinks its mineral weight, and drives a tiny, ringing shudder through the bedplates. I feel it as a sharp, clean pulse against my ribs—the only clock we have. 

One drop: the condensate gathers on the flywheel’s lower rim. 
Two drops: the pulse-disc in the tank ripples from rim to core. 
Ten drops: an inch of gravel is cleared; the horn shears another pale sprout before its roots can bite into the seam.

I reach the pump. 

The iron here is nearly ninety degrees, slick with the sweat of old steam. I do not hurry. Hurrying is for things with eyes, things that run toward a prey or away from a collapse. Here, the collapse happened before we were gathered; the prey is only the film that the machine gives off as it slowly dies. 

I widen. 

My hide relaxes, spilling outward into the silt until I am barely two inches thick, a broad, pale blanket pressed flush against the curved flank of the iron block. The tiny cilia beneath me find the packing gland, where the dark, mineral grease has weeping out between the bolts. 

They begin to lick. 

It will take three days of drops to polish this flange to gunmetal. In three days, my fringe will have felt the cooling of the resistor coils by half a degree, and I will slide away toward the shallow tanks to graze the lime-crust along the bottom lip of the plates. 

There is another one near me now. I feel its weight through the silt—a dull, dragging displacement five paces back, entering the lane I have just cleared. 

It does not turn aside. In half an hour, its leading edge will touch my trailing skirt. We will not coil or strike. We will not even shudder. We will simply press against each other, lard to lard, wet skin against wet skin, sliding past with the long, indifferent sigh of two engines shunted onto the same dead siding. 

Its scent will be mine: the mint-cold oil over the boiled iron. 

We will exchange our temperatures—my eighty-four degrees taking its eighty-five—and then the gap will open between us again, marked only by the *tock* from the hopper and the slow, greasy tears falling through the dark, keeping the church fifty paces under the world.
