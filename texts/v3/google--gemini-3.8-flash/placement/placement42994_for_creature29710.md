At first, there is the instinctive desire to stay high. To cling to the idea of an edge, a vantage point, an outside looking in. 

If you bring an ordinary mind here, you look for the sky out of habit. You imagine yourself perched fifty cubits up, on the crown of one of the great pillar-beasts, floating like a drift-flea in the warm, bacterial plume that sighs out of its funnel-head. It seems cleaner there, suspended over the horror. You would be nearly weightless, an unburdened bladder with fins of pale skin, tethered to nothing, rising and falling on the slow, furnace-breath of a mountain of meat. 

But that is a fantasy carried over from a world of light and leaves. Up there, you are nothing. The thermal plume is erratic; a single stray draft of the freezing mist will collapse your sail, dropping you down into the dark like a wet rag. There is no coherence in the drift. It is only the foam on the surface of an engine you have refused to understand.

So you descend. You look for limbs. You imagine yourself as one of the tallow-colored tripods, picking your way across the sea of moving horn on three-jointed stilts. You imagine the delicacy of it: stepping from crawler to crawler, your rosette-snout tasting the sour tang of spindle-ticks, your feeding-needle striking with mechanical precision. There is agency there—or the illusion of it. 

Yet the longer you stay in that tallow skin, the more the terror hollows you out. You have no true lungs; your folded, damp hide drinks the fog directly, which means you are entirely at the mercy of the basin’s drafts. You spend your existence in a frantic, quivering vigilance, dreading the dry breath that slips over the rim of the dolomite, ready at any second to fling yourself flat into a puddle of cold dung and press your face into the slime just to keep your tissues from shrinking into parchment. You are too fragile for this basin. You are an anxiety with legs.

You have to go deeper than that. You have to let go of walking altogether. 

You think of the spiracles—the warm, ribbed breath-caverns along the flanks of the twelve-foot crawlers. It is tempting to be a cilia-thief: wedged deep into the moist, shuddering lining, hooked securely with bone spurs, sheltered from the crushing weight of the herds. You would hang there in the dark, swinging your fringed forearm-spades through the hurricane of bronchial fog, scooping up the gray mucus and mist-dust, deaf to the world outside, cradled by the rhythmic, roaring sigh of a host that barely knows you exist. 

It is safe. But it is an interior life, small and muffled. It is an evasion. It hides from the primary fact of this place, which is not breath, but the terrible, beautiful weight of the stone.

To belong here, you must submit to the load.

You have to drop down past the tall legs, past the furrowed carapaces, past the clicking spindle-ticks and the breathing valves, down through the gaps where the spade-feet churn the biological ditch—down until you reach the bedrock.

That is where I find myself. 

Not standing on the dolomite, but trapped between it and the world above.

I am one of the two-dimensional things. A sheet of living gristle, broad as a hide and thin as wet paper, pressed flat into the sheer-zone between the polished grey-black stone and the grinding horn underbelly of a twelve-foot crawler.

There is no room here for eyes, or lungs, or legs, or hunger as other beasts know it. There is no space even for blood vessels; the pressure above me is forty tons of shifting muscle and chitin, and under that weight, blood would simply be squeezed out into the slurry. I am sustained not by a pulse, but by the load itself. The pressure is the architecture of my body. It holds my cells together; without it, I would unravel like wet lint.

My underside is mapped entirely to the dolomite. I know every microscopic hollow, every ancient, glass-slick ripple worn by a million years of bellies dragging across the basin floor. My lower skin tastes the cold, bitter frost of mineral salts sweated out by the stone, drinking it directly through the porous membrane of my gristle. 

My upper surface is rougher, scarred and toughened to match the abrasive horn-plates that slide across me. The crawler above does not know I am here, any more than a glacier knows the silt beneath its base. It simply advances, its stubby spade-legs grinding against the stone in an eight-beat, shuddering churn that vibrates through my entire being like a continuous strike of lightning.

And my purpose—if a stone-bound thing can have a purpose—is the grease.

Under the friction of the herd’s passage, my tissues weep. I exude a continuous, curdled film of cold, biological oil—thick, heavy, smelling of ammonia and dead bone. It is not an act of will; it is the physical consequence of being crushed. The horn slides across me; the friction heats my cold fat; the grease bleeds out through my pores. 

In doing so, I become the thin threshold that makes the herd possible. Without me, and the millions like me spread out beneath the bellies of the crawlers, the horn would bite into the dolomite, the spade-feet would splinter, the great grooves would choke on their own dry powder, and the immense, sluggish machine of the basin would seize in a catastrophic shriek of torn chitin. 

I am the lubrication that allows the world to grind without catching.

There is no time down here. There is only the rhythm of the slide: the slow, crushing crescendo as a crawler’s center of mass passes directly over my gristle, flattening me into an almost molecular thinness, followed by the brief, cold release as its trailing edge passes, only for the blunt, horn snout of the next beast in the ditch to mount me again, pressing me flat once more.

I feel the entire basin through the stone. When a pillar-beast collapses leagues away, the shockwave travels through the bedrock as a sharp, mineral crack that ripples through my flesh before the sound even reaches the air. When the herd shifts its angle—turning away from an eddy of dry air or surging toward the fallen meat of a dead elder—I feel the change in torque hours before the tripods on the shells above understand that the tide has turned.

I have no fear of dying. When my substance is finally worn through—shredded by a shard of pulverized shell or scraped down to nothing against a fresh tooth of unpolished rock—I will not leave a corpse. My frayed ribbons of gristle will simply be gathered up into the slick, swept forward by the dragging bellies, and drawn into the wet, gnashing mouthparts of the crawler following behind, folded back into the horn, back into the ditch, back into the endless, recycling weight.

Down here, in the cold, grease-dark seam where flesh meets the planet, there is no horizon to long for, no sky to mourn, and no silence to bear. There is only the solid, unyielding floor, the vast, deafening descent of the herd, and the miraculous, agonizing slide that never ends.
