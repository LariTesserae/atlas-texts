It begins not with a view, but with a tooth.

Specifically, the lower left canine, pressed hard against the cold-rolled web of an eighteen-inch channel girder. If you keep the enamel welded to the steel, the jaw takes the hum before the air can swallow it. 

Through the bone comes the entire anatomy of the cleft: the three-stroke thump of the east-wall compressor four hundred paces down; the long, singing whine of the hoist cable on Tier 190 where the brake-linings are wearing unevenly; the quick, erratic stutter of a high-line rat scuttling across the lower flange just past the turnbuckle.

I am not in a harness-box. Those swung cradles are for families, for the ones who still need to look at each other’s gray-greased faces while they chew dry mutton-meal and sleep in bundles to conserve the water in their skin. 

I am three feet off the stone, wedged in the dead gap behind the north-side counterweight trunk, where the girders do not span the void but drive directly into the diorite. Here, the rock was bored out with four-inch diamond core-drills to take the expansion anchors. It is a place without width. My back is braced against the unweathered chevron of the rock—a plane so sharp that even through two layers of cured sheepskin and tallow-stiffened duck-cloth, a careless shift of the ribs will split the weave—and my knees are jacked against the back of the steel guide-rail. 

If I dropped my tallow-tin, it would strike nothing for twelve hundred yards. It would make no sound. The void beneath the arches of my boots is not an absence; it is a weight, a massive draft of dead, sterilized air pulling forever downward like a heavy grease.

I am a wedge-taper. A shim-man. 

The cleft is not finished breaking. The people in the swinging boxes believe the mountain was cleaved once, long ago, and that the turnbuckles simply hold the walls apart like props in an old mine. But the mountain is young and vicious; it breathes, though its breath takes years. Sometimes a frost-free expansion deep in the root pinches two inches shut; sometimes the diorite yawns half a thumb-width, and an anchor-pin that has carried fifty tons of cable-stress for thirty winters suddenly goes loose in its socket, rattling like a dry tooth in a skull.

When that happens, the iron screams—not into the air, where no sound travels, but through the rivets. It hits the soles of the boots as a high, electric sting.

That is where I go.

My fingers have long since stopped splitting. The skin on the pads has turned yellow-gray, like horn, impregnated with so much powdered graphite that when I rub thumb and forefinger together, there is no friction at all—only that cold, oily slip that makes human flesh feel like machine parts. I carry my tools between my teeth and in the loops of my breast-harness: four flat blades of soft sheet-lead, three cold-chisels forged from scrap drill-steel, and a two-pound drift hammer with a lead head so it won’t spark against the diorite.

You cannot speak here. The desiccated wind coming up from the deep smelting pods tastes of burned zinc and sulfur, and if you open your lips to form a word, your tongue sticks to the roof of your mouth like dried cod. I think in taps. One tap: *hold*. Two: *slack*. A long, dragging vibration that rattles the fillings in the back of my mouth: *the south wall is settling; give it grease.*

Beside my hip, in the notch where the iron shoe bites the rock, two needle-footed geckos have tucked themselves into the angle. They are pale as bone-ash, flat as dried leaves, their bellies pressed against the metal to catch the faint, muffled warmth of the furnace flue eighty feet below us. They do not run from me. In this gap, anything that moves is either iron, food, or another thing trying not to fall. We share the same vibration. When the main ore-skip plunges past on its guides, twenty feet out in the dark, the whole frame kicks sideways with an iron *clack* that rattles the geckos' claws against the paint-less steel and shudders through my spine, lifting me an inch off my perch before gravity drags me back against the stone.

We take the jolt together. Then the silence settles back in—that heavy, thin-air hush that feels like wool stuffed into the ears.

Later, when the noon-blade arrives, I will see myself. 

It comes without warning: a blinding, cold-white razor of vertical daylight that drops straight down the slot, lasting only as long as it takes the sun to cross eight paces of sky. For seven minutes, the darkness is sliced open. 

In that glare, I will see my knees, gray with granite dust; the brass-wire stitches holding the calves of my boots together; the thin, pale skin of my wrists where the hair has been worn away by cable-burn. I will see the thousands of tiny, wingless midges drifting like diamond dust in the updraft, and the frozen grease caked in the threads of the turnbuckle an arm’s length away. 

And then the light will slide off the iron, hitching up the southern face like a lifted curtain, and the purple gloom will close over us again.

I will reach out my left hand, slip a three-millimeter lead leaf between the rock-shelf and the baseplate, and raise the drift hammer. Two inches of swing. No more. The dead weight of the lead will seat the shim with a dry *thump* that no one will hear, but three hundred feet above, a rigger leaning his brow against the tension-wire will feel the hum change from an ache to an iron true, and he will know the tier has caught.

I will stay here until my sheepskin turns the color of the stone. When the cold finally gets past the tallow and stops the lung-bellows, they won't have to haul me up or tip me off. The rock has a pocket ready for me, right behind the guide. They can just drive another jack against my shins to hold the strut true, leave the lead shims in my lap, and let the high air turn the rest of me to leather.
