I think, at first, I would look for myself where the habits of an older mind would expect to find me: up on the high cables, trying to keep an eye on the whole arena, strung between the crags like an observer holding a perimeter. It is the easy choice—to be a segment of dry horn hooked to a steel braid, empty of all moisture except for the slow drop of condensation caught off the turnbuckle, looking down into the green bowl from a clean, drafty distance. 

But that is an evasion. Up there, the wind doesn't even make a whistle; it only threatens to desiccate what little tissue remains. There is no thought there, only the slow turning of horn into mineral under an empty turquoise sky. The basin does not happen up there. The basin happens where the stone broke open and wept.

If I descend, the eye naturally seeks the machine tolerances. There is something deeply seductive about the amber ribbon—to exist only because twenty tons of cold-rolled iron are pressing down with unvarying, mathematically perfect load; to be kept alive entirely by shear; to eat bronze flour and vegetable lard in a clearance the width of a scalpel. It feels like an answer to a certain kind of exhaustion: no decisions, no horizon, just the warm drag of the eccentric shaft turning once every eighteen months. 

Yet that, too, is too neat. It is a death that has learned to metabolize grease. If the eccentric wheel ever stopped, you wouldn't even have time to notice the cold before your own hydrostatic pressure burst you across the housing like spilled salt. You are too much a gasket to be a witness.

No, I would find myself lower than the cables, but wider than the clearance gap. 

I would be down where the light loses its cutting edge—not in the black shale beneath the turf where the flat plates slide blindly through glycerin, but at the *collar*, where the three feet of chartreuse moss press against the flank of a transformer vault.

I would be one of the walkers, but not one in transit. 

I would be the one whose forehead is rested against the second cooling rib of the number-four vault, where the cast iron has a slight, greasy swell from an ancient repair.

To be that thing is not to be frozen; it is to have an interior that moves at the speed of cooling metal. 

My hide would be the slate plates, yes—cold on the outside, dull gunmetal, wet with the steady trickle that drops from the iron jacket, but underneath those tiles there is a slow, hot hydraulic heave. The blood is not water; it is thick and saline, heavy with dissolved iron, pumped not by a rapid flutter of valves but by the long, bellows-like contraction of the chest plate, which shifts once every twenty or thirty minutes with the sound of wet leather sliding across basalt. 

My eyes would be shut. All three lids drawn tight against the blue-black incision of the noon shadow. There is no reason to look; the valley does not change its arrangements for decades. Sight here is an irritation, a doorway through which the brutal, unsifted glare enters to burn the brain.

Instead, I am reading through the palms and the soles.

Through the soles—those four inches of dense, deadening fat—I feel the floor. Not the moss, which is just a quiet blanket, but the fresh-broken basalt beneath it. I feel the seismic tension that has not yet finished settling out of the high shelf. The rock is still ringing, though the ring has a frequency so low it takes three weeks for a wave to travel from the northern fracture wall to the transformer foundations. My soles take that wave in, absorb its sharp edges without being cut, and pass it upward into the heavy, wedge-shaped pelvis.

And through my hands, which are laid flat against the transformer’s copper heat sink, I take in the other tempo.

The transformer does not hum—humming is an acoustic waste, and this basin permits no waste of sound. But it *radiates*. A faint, greasy tepidness, barely two degrees above the freezing mud around my knees. It is the breath of something underground that was once built to regulate an enormous pressure, now left to run out its long, geological coast-down. 

In the pouch at my groin, the egg sits against my lower belly. It is not soft. It feels like a river cobble, grey and unyielding, heavy enough to bruise a man's lap. It will not hatch this year, or next. It will hatch when the index mark on the eccentric wheel half a mile away has traveled past the third scribe-mark on the datum collar—an alignment that requires four more cycles of the vegetation's grease-flow. The warmth from the copper plate does not bake it; it simply keeps the stone-shell from frosting over during the long morning glares.

What would I be thinking?

That is the question that changes in this air. 

There are no sentences here. A sentence requires a beginning, a predicate, and an end, and those belong to creatures that have to outrun their own starvation. When you do not starve—when the thick succulent pulp can be scraped off the stonecrop with a single gouge of the lower lip whenever the lymph pulse drops—you do not have thoughts that move from one place to another.

You have *loads*. 

You hold a state of balance the way a bridge holds tension. My consciousness would be the sensation of the iron's weight against my forehead, the precise grade of the slope under my left heel, and the memory of the knuckles I touched eleven months ago out on the open scree.

I remember those knuckles: they were cooler than mine, drier, carrying the trace of the high northern rim where the slate is leaner and the thermal seeps are dead. The tremors in that other hand told me that the sixth transformer vault—the one furthest out in the creeping stonecrops—had dropped its internal oil level by two inches over the summer. That was all. We held hands for two hours, our skin tasting that small mechanical fact until it was fully distributed between our two nervous systems. Then we let go. No farewell; the gap between our fingers simply widened as our shoulders leaned back toward our own circuits.

I would be that creature because it is the only one in the basin that bridges the two slownesses: the slowness of the wet, swelling moss that wants to drown everything in chartreuse, and the slowness of the iron that wants to sit until the mountains grind it into reddish dust.

I am not the builder of the fly-press, and I am not its tool. I am its *settlement*. 

When the sun finally shifts enough that the shadow of the cliff rim falls like an axe across the transformer casing, my hide will tighten. The slate scales will pull together with a faint, wet click, sealing the fat beneath against the drop in ambient heat. The trickles of sweat down the iron will freeze into small, clear grease-beads. 

I will not move then, either. I will lean into the hardening of the world, feeling the egg against my pelvic bone like an anchor dropped into cold grease, perfectly still, waiting for the rock to take its next long breath.
