I would not be found on the high parapets where the eagles sit, nor among the elders who measure the mouthfuls of slate-water from the cisterns. Those places have too much sky in them, or too much ledger-work. 

If I am anywhere, I am down in the fourth tier, where the mountain’s shoulder presses hardest against the back of the city—in the crawlway between the living rock and the double-skin wall of an ancient granary.

It is a narrow wedge of space, no wider than the span of my ribs, smelling of dry sheep-grease, ammonia, and the mineral chill of shale that hasn't seen the sun since the fault cracked open. My knees are pulled up near my chin. The position is not uncomfortable; my joints were shaped by forty years of flagstones, the cartilage hardened, the hips set slightly wide like the splayed rafters of a cellar. 

My skin would have the texture of smoked hide, stiff at the knuckles and yellowed around the nails where diorite flour has worked its way beneath the quick and died there. My left ear is mostly quiet—the maul took that when I was sixteen, tapping the green river-pebbles against the quarry faces until the high ringing simply swallowed the lower sounds of water and wind. But my right ear is greedy. 

I would be a wedge-tender. 

It is not a title anyone speaks, because there are no titles worth wasting breath on when three clicks of the tongue against the back teeth will tell a mason where to strike. But the work is this: between the square-dressed facing stones and the millions of tons of loose scree that want to slide down and fill the valley, there must be something that gives. If a wall is too stiff, the winter ice finds the tooth of it and jacks it out into the alley like a rotten tusk. If it is too loose, the scree turns to grease in the afternoon thaw and bellies the tier until the whole terrace slumps into the courtyard below.

My work is in the dark of the drainage flutes. I carry a pouch of split mountain-sheep horn, shaved flat and soaked in tallow until it is tough as old hoof, and a flat mallet of river-green stone worn down to the size of a kid's skull.

When the frost begins to bind the upper gutters at midday, I slide into the weep-slots behind the pens. My calves are braced against the dressed basalt of the street-face; my shoulders are wedged into the cold, shifting rubble of the mountain itself. I stay very still until my blood slows down to the pace of the rock.

The blood is heavy, thick as crushed juniper berries. It makes a slow, dry drumming behind my temple—*thump, thump, rest*—that matches the weight of the terrace above my head. Through the soles of my raw-hide shoes, I can feel the beasts shifting in the pen ten feet out: the vast, dull thud of a *chal* dropping its belly to the flags, its cud-grind vibrating through four courses of unmortared masonry. 

Beneath that, there is the mountain’s own voice. It does not speak in slides or roars; it speaks in gravel-dribbles. A single pebble rolling six inches inside the dark behind me. A sharp, dry *tink* like an eagle’s talon against flint—that is an ice-lens forming in a joint three yards deep.

When I hear that *tink*, I do not hurry. Hurrying brings sweat, and sweat is a salt thief that freezes against the ribs. I reach into the pouch by touch, feel the grain of the horn wedges with my calloused thumb, find one with the proper bevel, and slide it between two slabs of diorite where the pressure has begun to bite too hard. I tap it twice with the green stone. 

*Tock. Tock.*

The sound is short, flat, swallowed instantly by the loose shale. The horn takes the squeeze. The stone sighs—a tiny puff of dry grey dust that hits my cheek like cold flour—and settles a fraction of a hair. The balance holds for another three seasons. 

Sometimes a *pith* will come through the flue, its belly fat and low to the stone, its slate-colored fur brushing my knuckles as it slides past. It does not fear me any more than it fears a lintel. We are both things that live in the gaps where the daylight cannot cut. If its flank is cold and damp, I know the drainage above is pooling; if its whiskers are dry and white with lime-crust, the draft is clear through to the outer face. I learn more from the grease on its ribs than a Seeper learns from an hour with his ear to the basalt.

When the light fails—not gradually, but all at once, as the western horn of the ridge bites the bottom of the sun—the cold comes through the wall like an iron spade. 

Then I back out of the flue, feet first, dragging the maul by its leather thong. 

I come out into the lower alley behind the communal fold. The air out here is blue, sharp enough to peel the mucus from the nostrils, smelling of dried-dung smoke and the hot, rancid grease of three hundred beasts standing rump-to-shoulder against the wind. 

My daughter is there, or perhaps my brother’s girl—it matters little whose blood it is, since all our ribs are bent the same way. She does not greet me. She holds out a wooden bowl with two thumbs over the rim: three swallows of water, grey with rock-meal, and a lump of cold tallow mixed with ground grass-seed. I take it with both hands, my thumbs feeling the greasy rim of the wood, and let the fat sit on my tongue until the slow heat of my mouth turns it to oil. 

Then I go down into the stall. 

I do not light a wick. Fat is for the belly and the stone wedges, not for the eyes. I find the grandfather beast—the big, one-horned bull who has survived nine shearings, his fleece matted into grey, felted plates that smell of ancient bog-moss and dung. I crawl under his flank while he is still on his knees, before he folds his great hocks under his ribs.

His belly drops down beside me, heavy as a fallen wall, hot as an oven stone that has sat all day in the noon glare. The fleece is greasy enough that it sticks to the wool of my cowl. His breath comes in long, wet groans, rocking my shoulder with every lung-stroke, his cud-chew a wet, rhythmic grinding that shakes the shale beneath my back.

I pull my feet up into the skirt of my felt cloak. My forehead rests against his ribs; I can hear the fermentation working in his first stomach, a bubbling, sour heat that keeps the frost from riming my beard. 

Above us, forty tiers of dry stone rise toward the raw peaks. Millions of tons of broken diorite, held together by nothing more than the angle of their falling and the horn wedges driven into their seams. The mountain wants them flat. It wants them down in the salt-fog where the forgotten things are. It has all the centuries to wait, and it will have them in the end. 

Every stone knows this. The beasts know it in their broad, flat hooves. 

I close my right eye—the good one—and leave the deaf one turned to the mountain. My thumbs are tucked deep into the bull’s wool to keep them from the iron air. Before the sleep takes me, I feel the scree behind the rear wall shift once: a single grain of basalt, no larger than a lentil, sliding an inch down the dark slope, finding a new crevice, and coming to rest against the horn. 

It is enough. The wall will stand until morning.
