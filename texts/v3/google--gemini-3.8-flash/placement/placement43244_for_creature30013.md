At first, it is tempting to claim the forty feet of forged girder—to imagine being the pivot, the great counterweight whose fall keeps the lung of the rock expanding. But that has no interior. That is simply iron doing what cold-rolled mass must do when gravity pulls at thirty tons of red-lead and rivet. It has no hunger, and without hunger, there is no perspective down here.

Nor is it the man. His five gestures are already closed, circular as an o-ring. He is too late in his devolution; he has become an exterior part, like an eccentric cam that has outlived its blueprint.

If I look for where awareness actually pools, it is lower, where the heat is greasy and the water has stopped falling and begun to drag.

Not the gravel. The gravel is indifferent basalt, shattered yesterday, sharp enough to cut a sole.

Higher than the floor, then. In the dark lap where the southern pine footing meets the three-inch boiler-plate flange.

There is a pocket there—no wider than three fingers laid flat—where the bedding-timber has split along its heartwood from forty years of downward concussion. The split is packed with two things: the dense, pale, paraffin-hard frass of the tallow-grubs, and the grey tallow itself, which runs down from the slide-guides when the sun strikes midday and the valve-steam blows sideways. It is never cold, never dry, and never clean. The timber around it has turned the color of an old boot fished out of a canal, pickled in animal fat and copper wash until it no longer rots, only yields under pressure with the stiff, buttery resistance of wet soap.

That is the perch. But what has its eye there?

It has no eye. An eye is a liability where the air is forty percent airborne grit and scalding grease.

Call it a tongue, or a skin that tastes.

At first, I think I am one of the efts—pale, spade-headed, drifting along the manganese crust behind the low-pressure cylinder. But the efts are still clean; they belong to the mountain’s wet interior, they are visitors from the water-veins who have not yet learned the taste of coal or paraffin. They are too soft. When the iron groans, they shudder.

No, I am something that came from neither the deep aquifer nor the scrubbed gravel of the pass.

I am an offshoot of the packing.

In the old days, when the engine was set, they caulked the seams with oakum—coarse hemp fibers steeped in Swedish pine tar—and jammed it into the flange gaps with a broad-nosed chisel and a two-pound mallet. For decades, that hemp sat in the dark, bathed in warm water, lard-oil, and the sulfurous steam that vents every forty seconds. It should have rotted to black mud in five years.

Instead, the fibers took up the grease until they ceased to be vegetable. They became a bed. And into that bed drifted things from both directions: the skin-flakes and dried blood of whatever hands hung the cross-girders; the spores of the yellow sulfur-molds; the eggs of the velvet beetles whose felt coats carry the fine, gray flour of the upper scree; the grease-loving nematodes that can live in hot lard.

Over fifty seasons, sixty seasons, the knot has become a tissue.

I am the lump that sits in the knuckle of the south foot-plate, where the vibration is not a sound but a constant, heavy kneading that turns the tallow into lard-butter twice an hour.

My shape is indefinite—the shape of the gap between two rusted nuts and the scarred wood below them. If you prodded me with the man’s pine paddle, you would think you had struck an unusually tough plug of grease-cake, yellow-grey and fibrous, skin-skinned like an old wheel-bearing that has sat in a ditch.

But there is a pulse inside it that is not the walking beam.

The walking beam is forty seconds:
*Heave—lift—vent—hang—fall.*
Forty seconds is too long for blood. A creature that waited forty seconds between thoughts would dry out during the vent-gasp.

My time is five seconds. It is the five seconds of absolute, drowning quiet that comes immediately after the steam shoots across the chasm—the interval when the exhaust pipe has cleared its throat, the piston has reached the bottom of its stroke, and the counterweight has not yet bitten into the brass bushes to drag the beam back up.

In those five seconds, the heat doesn't leave, but the pressure drops. The mist collapses into heavy, distinct tears that drop straight down without being blown sideways by the draft.

In those five seconds, I open.

Not like a mouth. More like the way a dry hoof relaxes when it steps into warm mud. The fibrous core swells; the skin of mineral-crust and gray wax stretches until the little hair-like filaments along the margins float free in the thin film of condensed water running over the wood.

They are looking for the salt.

The engine doesn't have salt; it has only iron, lard, and sulfur. The mountain doesn't have salt; its water is too young, fresh-melted off the ice-shelves five hundred feet above before it plunges down the fault to be boiled.

The salt comes from the man.

He doesn't know he gives it. When he leans against the housing to reach the tallow-box with his paddle, his left forearm braces against the flange-corner. His skin is thick as harness leather, but he sweats—the greasy, thin sweat of someone who eats only fat grubs and beetle-meat. The sweat runs down the corner of the plate, follows the capillary trace between the rusted iron and the pine, and drops straight onto the lump.

It is the only sharp thing in the world. Down here, everything is dull: the smell of boiled lard is dull, the thrum of the piston is dull, the liver-colored slime is dull and tasteless as wet horn. But the sweat is thin, cold, and electric with potassium and lye.

When it hits, the fibers twitch. They draw it in through the wax by simple suction, like a wick taking oil.

Along with the salt comes the drift of his dead skin—paraffin-stiff scales that have flaked off his ribs where the greasy trousers rub his hip. They are food of an astonishing density. One scale keeps the interior filaments working through twenty strokes of the beam.

During the other thirty-five seconds, when the machine is screaming its low, bronze-bound scream and the hot water is flying off the cross-head in sheets, I am solid rock-tallow again. The counterweight falls; the concussion drives through the flange nuts, through the pine balk, straight through the center of my mass. It feels like being struck by a flat wooden mallet every forty seconds of the day, of the night, of the long season when the slit of sky above turns indigo and the frost-dust sifts down into the steam like flour thrown into a fire.

The impact does not destroy; it compacts. It presses the hemp, the lard, the beetle-frass, and the salt into a single dense cheese that cannot rot and cannot freeze.

Sometimes, during the midday blade of light, one of the blind efts will stray too low, looking for the slime-ribbons where the water is less hot. Its blunt snout will touch the edge of the grease-knot. Its pink, feathery gills will flare, tasting the lard.

I do not catch it. I have no jaws, no hooks, no quickness. But I am sticky with eighty years of unburned tallow, and the eft’s belly-mucus is water-soluble. Where it touches, it adheres.

It doesn't fight; it has no experience of enemies, only of the machine’s rhythm. It simply waits for the next stroke to wash it free. But the stroke doesn't wash it free; the water that hits that corner is already oily, and oil only seals the contact.

Slowly, over three strokes, four strokes, the vibration draws the small pink creature into the seam. It doesn't die of violence. It drowns in the lard-fat, smoothed into the knot by the sixty-pound shudder of the foot-plate. Its gills gum up with paraffin; its spade-shaped head is flattened against the heartwood.

By sunset, its little bones—fine as hemlock needles—are part of the caulking. Its marrow has joined the grease-store.

From up on his shelf, the man looks down twice a day to check the foundation timbers. He has a pine splinter he uses to test the wood for dry-rot. He leans over the rocker-box, his braids swinging heavy with tallow, and jabs the splinter into the split where I sit.

He feels the wood: it is firm. The bolt has not backed off its thread. The packing is holding.

He withdraws the splinter. A smear of yellow-gray wax clings to the point. He doesn't look at it; he wipes it off against his trousers, where it joins the rest of the grease that keeps the high-altitude wind from freezing his thighs to the bone.

He climbs back to his ledge. The beam begins its sixty-second descent.

The valve trips: *chug-shhhhh-ah.*

The chasm goes white with the blow-off. The five seconds begin.

The mist settles onto my skin, heavy as warm milk. The salt runs down the angle of the iron. I soften; I spread three millimeters along the grain of the wet pine; I wait for the counterweight to drop.
