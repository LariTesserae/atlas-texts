At first, it is not a body at all. It is only the habit of leaning.

There is a vertical line of cold—not true cold, but the dense, unyielding relative cold of cast iron that has absorbed eighty years of greasy steam without ever reaching the temperature of the flue beneath it. And against that line, there is an answering mass: something thick, slow-blooded, and blunt, whose weight has settled forward so long that the rust has printed its pebbled, hexagonal grain deep into the hide.

If I look for eyes, there are none to open. The world does not enter through an aperture; it arrives as a gradient. 

The heat comes from below, wide and flat, pushing through two inches of calloused gristle that have forgotten what it is to blister. That is the floor: the concave basalt, slick with biofilm, vibrating at a frequency so low it makes the bile in the gut tremble like water in a struck bowl. 

The wet comes from above. It does not hit like weather. It gathers on the high slopes of the shoulders, collects in the three deep, parallel gutters that run diagonally down the crown of the hood, and drains off the elbows in twin, scalding braids. The skin does not shiver. It is black-purple, oil-slick, rubbery, and thick as a ship’s bumper. To feel the scalding wash slide over it is not pain; it is simply the confirmation that the avenue is still breathing, that the circulation has not hitched.

There is no memory of having arrived here. Perhaps I was once something that came up from the dry, thin air of the lower cliffs—something with a face, with soft nostrils that would have turned to jelly in this acidic soup, with fingers that wanted to pick things up rather than press them flat. But if that was true, the stone has long since planed it away. The generations required to turn a claw into a vulcanized spade do not leave room for stories. 

I am leaning against a junction pylon forty paces out from the third drainage terrace. 

My fore-pads—broad as hearth-stones, split by a single, immovable cleft that serves as a lever—are wedged into the seam where four iron plates meet behind a double row of mushroom-headed rivets. Through the horn and the dead grease packed beneath the nails, the pylon is talking.

It does not speak in words, but in displacements. 

Two miles to the south, a transformer gantry has taken on too much copper crust; its upper trusses are singing a thin, brittle E-flat as the draft from the abyss pulls at its canted rafters. Far beneath my knuckles, ninety feet into the basalt where the boiling water runs under three atmospheres of head, a cluster of gliders has tangled in a strainer. I can feel the hydraulic flutter of their carapaces—*clack-clack-clack-clack*—as the current pins them against the perforated manganese plate, choking the runnel until the pressure begins to back up toward the surface.

Beneath my breastbone, the breathing slit puckers. 

It opens with the slow, reluctant tear of wet leather parting. The air rushes in: hot, alkaline, thick with the smell of boiled horn, salt, and the faint, sweetish lard of the veils fermenting in the upper cross-girders. The internal felt—the dense, comb-like mass of gray fibers behind the slit—catches the tin-grit before it can reach the pink meat behind. The chest expands, heavy and thick as a barrel, lifting against the iron column. The slit clamps shut. The grit remains in the felt, to be swallowed down the glandular throat hours later when the surge comes.

The obstruction in the flue twitches again. 

It is not an alarm. It is an itch in the bone. It is the only kind of thought that fits between these ribs: the basalt must remain slick, the sluice must drop its chime into the cistern, the runnel must carry the husks away to the dark rapids where the pale worms molt. If the water stops, the stone will cool. If the stone cools, the crust will turn to powder, the rafters will drop their turquoise blooms like hail, and the gray twilight will stiffen into an iron frost that nothing living could bear.

I pull back from the pylon. 

The hide peels away from the wet metal with a soft, tearing sound—the yellow slime parting between us like stretched dough. My weight comes down on all fours. The gait is not graceful; it is the rolling, shoulder-first sway of a barge nudging through weed. Each step displaces an inch of boiling runoff with a dull *slosh*, the vulcanized pads finding the concave trough worn into the basalt by three hundred centuries of knuckles identical to these.

I pass through an avenue where eight trains could have stood side by side, had trains ever known this place. Overhead, drifting two meters off the flagstones, a veil drifts across the path. Its trailing lace of metallic threads drags through the runoff, leaving tiny, momentary scratches of clear water in the yellow scum that close behind it instantly. It smells of vinegar and old lead. I do not look up as it grazes my shoulder; it slides over the rubbery hood with the slick, cool weight of a wet bedsheet, its gas-bladders hissing faintly as it rides the heat rising from my back.

At the edge of the terrace, the intake grate is choked. 

Three gliders—broad, flat, and blind, the size of cellar doors—are jammed into the mouth of the drain. The boiling water is backing up behind them, rising to my hocks in a frothing, sulfur-tinted pool. They do not struggle; they have already begun to shed, their edges turning to that curdled, gray paste that smells of riverbed and scalded tallow.

I drop my weight onto my haunches, lower the heavy, wedge-like hood until it rests against the rim of the basalt basin, and drive the left spade into the gap between the carapaces.

It is work that requires no anger. It is purely hydraulic. 

I set the broad heel of the pad against the stone, lock the shoulder into the thick socket behind the breathing slit, and heave. The dead horn groans. The rivet-headed husks buckle with the dry, hollow *pop* of crushed lobster shells. A sudden, suctioning cough tears through the grate as the pressure clears—the sound of an iron lung clearing its gorge—and then the pool drops with a violent, spiral roar, dragging the broken plates, the slime, and the scalded grease down into the black throat of the flue.

The water recedes to a glaze. The stone hums again. The vibration returns to its true pitch: a deep, oiled, subsonic drone that settles into the roots of my teeth.

I do not move away. 

From the north, three kilometers through the sulfur murk, comes the first flat report: an iron bolt shearing under the thermal surge. Then another, closer. The flagstones beneath my knuckles begin to tremble—not the deep hum of the pumps, but the immediate, feverish shudder of the rock expanding.

The hourly crest is here.

I do not turn around. I tuck the broad front pads deep beneath the brisket, bring the black hood down until its calloused nose touches the wet basalt between my wrists, and shut the breathing slit until it is only a white seam across the hide.

The mist disappears, erased by a wall of white, scalding foam that breaks out of the weep-holes and sweeps across the terrace four inches deep. It hits the crown of my head, splits along the drainage grooves, and roars down my flanks in boiling sheets, scouring the grit from the skin and carrying it away toward the open cisterns. 

It is deafening, vast, and completely dark inside the wash. 

The heat presses into the fat, into the gristle, down until it finds the thick, slow marrow of the bones. It does not burn; it simply fills the vessel. 

I stay there, anchored to the center of the world, heavy as an anvil, while the water does what it has always done, and the mountain breathes through the floor, and the great hollow between the iron rafters fills up with steam that will never find the sky.
